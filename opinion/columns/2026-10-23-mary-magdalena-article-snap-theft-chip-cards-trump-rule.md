At the grocery checkout, a family with children hands over an EBT card to buy groceries. The card is rejected. Thieves have already copied its magnetic stripe and drained the account. The family did not commit the theft. It carried the card the government gave it.

That distinction should never have required an investigation.

This week, the Department of Agriculture’s Food and Nutrition Service proposed a rule requiring all 50 states to replace magnetic-stripe SNAP cards with cards containing digital microchips and tap-to-pay technology. Public comment will remain open for 60 days. Once the rule is formally enacted, states will have two years to complete the transition. The proposal would also establish federal “SNAP EBT Operating Standards” designed for ongoing revision, making fraud prevention a system to maintain rather than a one-time card swap.

The proposed chip rule is good. Its lateness is the indictment.

A week is a strange unit of government accountability. In this case, it is also the interval between exposing a preventable theft scheme and proposing the repair.

One week after ProPublica reported that $470 million in SNAP benefits had been stolen from recipients the previous year, largely because the cards remained vulnerable to skimmers, USDA announced the proposal. Two USDA officials said the reporting had been shared throughout the department. The rule had been in the works since 2023 and had been delayed repeatedly. The reasons included severe understaffing at USDA after Department of Government Efficiency cuts and a backlog of regulations prioritized by the White House budget office.

This was not a policy born of foresight. It was a repair pulled from the queue after the cost of delay had been made painfully visible. The administration had the fix and the draft rule three years ago. It did not move. The White House, USDA and Vance’s office did not immediately respond to requests for comment.

Credit and debit cards have used microchips for more than a decade. California adopted them and reduced theft of SNAP and other public benefits by 83 percent in two years. More than 40 states have not added chips, leaving hundreds of thousands of lower-income Americans to reach grocery-store checkout lines only to discover that thieves have drained the food assistance they were counting on. [Earlier coverage of the proposed chip-and-tap-to-pay rollout](/articles/2026-10-26-usda-proposes-requiring-chip-enabled-snap-cards-and-tap-to-pay-terminals/) made the case: the cards themselves are the bare minimum. California had already shown the way.

A government can move when it chooses. This one did not choose until the damage became news.

Now comes the funding trap. The estimated total federal and state cost of the transition is $283 million, set against more than $600 million in SNAP and other benefits stolen from existing cards every year. One-time transition cost. Recurring annual theft. On the government’s own numbers, the repair costs less than half the theft.

The arithmetic is not close.

The Senate’s version of this year’s Farm Bill includes money for chip-card conversion. The House version does not. President Donald Trump’s One Big Beautiful Bill Act also slashed states’ funding for administering SNAP. [Federal funding cuts have already slowed states’ shift to chip-enabled EBT cards](/articles/2026-10-14-federal-funding-cuts-slow-states-shift-to-chip-enabled-ebt-cards/), leaving the new mandate to land on constrained state budgets. The mandate cannot be funded by another round of state staff cuts.

Donald, your administration demanded a national transition while the law you signed cut the state money needed to make it. You are a small man with a large pen. Your signature made the checkout line pay for your delay. You do not get to wear the chip rule like a medal.

Then there was the fraud czar.

Vice President JD Vance, whose roles include serving as the administration’s “fraud czar,” spent years portraying SNAP recipients as suspects. He said, “There are people who have Lamborghinis who currently receive SNAP benefits.” In his 2016 memoir, *Hillbilly Elegy*, he wrote that people in Kentucky and Ohio were largely “hardworking, except of course for the many food stamp recipients who show little interest in honest work.” A review by ProPublica of dozens of speeches, social media posts and news accounts found that Trump administration officials consistently framed recipients as perpetrators of fraud.

On Wednesday, while barnstorming in Ohio for Republican candidates, Vance called a press conference and emphasized that low-income families are often the victims of SNAP fraud. From a stage in southern Ohio this week, he then reversed course. SNAP, he said, was one of America’s most important social-service programs. He praised its role in ensuring that children could “have a full belly at the end of every single night” and called it “one of the most important and sacred things that our tax dollars go to.”

That was not a clarification. It was a rhetorical about-face. It was not ideological conversion. It was emergency repair of public rhetoric after the victims’ stories became impossible to dodge. The policy finally caught up with the reality his old rhetoric denied.

In Oregon cases, groups allegedly stole EBT account numbers and PINs, used them to buy bulk quantities of energy drinks and baby formula, and resold the goods for more than $3 million. Those are not struggling recipients. Those are organized thieves running a business. Vance himself described parents at checkout lines expecting to feed their children being told that thieves had already taken the money.

For years you made a family with too little food sound criminal while organized thieves copied magnetic stripes and drained what those families needed to eat. Your throat closes around “hardworking” when you read your own sentence again. The turn in your gut is yours when the checkout line comes back. Picture your own mother at that checkout line with an empty food budget. She does not have a Lamborghini. She has a card thieves copied. You needed only to look at her to see the difference. You looked elsewhere.

California shows that the technology works. Oregon shows the scale of the criminal enterprise. There is no respectable reason to keep families carrying the risk of electronic theft while the government searches for a cheaper solution.

For years, Washington called the victims the problem. Now it calls them victims and proposes a rule. That is not leadership. It is the minimum correction forced by exposure. I will not call it a victory. The chip upgrade is overdue. The people who delayed it do not get the credit.
