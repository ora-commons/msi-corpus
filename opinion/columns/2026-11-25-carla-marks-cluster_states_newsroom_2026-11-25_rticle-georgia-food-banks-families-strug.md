The bags are lighter and the lines are longer because Congress cut food aid while grocery prices climbed, and volunteers at a DeKalb County mosque are now being asked to make up the difference. Neither is an accident. This week, [News From The States reported](https://www.newsfromthestates.com/article/georgia-food-banks-families-struggle-stretch-aid-holiday-season) on Enough to Share, a volunteer-run pantry at the Atlanta Masjid of Al-Islam. It began in 2017 when five volunteers pooled $20 each for a dollar-store run serving about 30 families. It now serves roughly 240 families every two weeks. A bag that once covered three or four family meals now covers about two.

That is not an empty warehouse or a failed harvest. It is the dollar buying less while the line gets longer. Nassar Madyun, a volunteer who manages Enough to Share’s order with its food bank, used to buy 12,000 pounds of food for about $400. The same $400 now buys roughly 5,000 pounds. Charity was handed the gap.

Give the volunteers their due and give the other side its sentence. Grocery prices are genuinely higher. That tightens family budgets and nonprofit budgets alike. States do make paperwork errors. The people at Enough to Share are doing remarkable work with almost nothing. All of that is true.

It is also the story as it is told sincerely. It just is not the story the numbers tell.

Jon West, interim president and CEO of the Atlanta Community Food Bank, which distributes food to hundreds of organizations across 29 metro Atlanta and North Georgia counties, sees both ends of the squeeze. “Those dollars just aren’t going as far for us either,” he says. More families need help. Nonprofits can buy less with the money they have. About 30 percent of the people who came through the food bank network during the preceding three months had never used a food bank before.

Food insecurity is also higher in rural Georgia. That is partly why aid is harder to reach there. There are fewer providers. In Haralson County, Wesley Myrick of the Georgia Interfaith Public Policy Center says one or two organizations may serve the entire county. They are “bombarded as soon as they offer it.” Families must travel farther, spend more on fuel, and arrive at the one open door all at once. Every extra mile for a lighter bag is a toll the policy quietly installed. Friction is not the weather. It is a design choice with a gas price.

The missing third meal does not disappear into somebody else’s earnings. It simply is not bought. The cost did not vanish. It moved—from federal benefits into family budgets, from nonprofit purchasing power into smaller orders, and from public provision into volunteer time. That is how a public decision becomes a private scramble.

The money trail is short. The law the administration pushed through last year, H.R.1, cut SNAP funding by about 20 percent, or roughly $187 billion over the coming decade. Joseph Llobrera, senior director of research on food assistance at the Center on Budget and Policy Priorities, says the change has already taken 5.5 million people off the program, a 13 percent drop. Georgia’s SNAP participation has fallen by nearly 10 percent, about 150,000 people, and by roughly 8 percent among households with children.

Those households with children under 14 are exempt from the law’s stricter work requirements. They cannot fail a test they never take. Yet their participation still fell. Something else is doing the subtracting, and the law’s paperwork machinery is documented doing exactly this kind of subtracting.

First the benefit shrank. Then the survivors had to prove it. Elaine Waxman, a senior fellow at the Urban Institute, says states are scrambling to avoid taking on more of SNAP’s administrative cost if they fail to reduce payment-error rates quickly. Those rates measure paperwork and administrative mistakes, not fraud. To protect their budgets, states demanded more documentation. The documentation created backlogs. Eligible families fell off the rolls.

Every purge books as a savings in somebody’s ledger. If it is something other than the paperwork squeeze, name it. The same pattern is visible in [a federal proposal that could end food stamps for more than 73,000 New Jersey households](/articles/2026-11-13-federal-proposal-could-end-food-stamps-for-more-than-73-000-new-jersey-household/), while families here are already [cutting back on groceries to offset housing, energy and insurance costs](/articles/2026-11-24-families-cut-back-on-groceries-to-offset-housing-energy-and-insurance-costs/).

So here is the fix that requires nothing of the people who made the decision: donate more. It is also the fix that fails before it starts.

One volunteer pantry feeds 240 families twice a month against roughly 150,000 fewer Georgians on SNAP. A bag covering two meals is not a bridge over a $187 billion hole. It is a patch on a hole that is still growing. The pantry must raise its own money, buy at supermarkets, and ask local greenhouses and farmers for whatever else it can find. Applause costs nothing. Praise is cheaper than groceries.

Charity is magnificent. It is not a budget. Gratitude is not a financing model. A mosque can be the front door of a food network. It should not have to be the whole safety net.

I am not against the market, and I am not against charity. I am against the arrangement where a mosque has to become the insurance policy.

The economy is a set of human choices, not the weather. So what do we build?

Start with something this country already built, measured, and then took apart. In 2021, Congress expanded the child tax credit, made the payments monthly and nearly universal, and the Census Bureau’s Supplemental Poverty Measure recorded child poverty falling 46 percent in a single year, from 9.7 percent to 5.2 percent. It lifted 2.9 million children out of poverty, about 2.1 million of them because of the expansion. Then the expansion lapsed at the beginning of 2022, and child poverty shot back up.

That is not an anecdote imported from a small northern country. It is a switch flipped on, flipped off, and measured here, in this country, in families with children like those standing in that line. The cash support works. We have the receipts. What we lack is the decision.

First, restore SNAP’s purchasing power and make enrollment boring. Government already collects much of the information needed to identify eligible families. Use tax filings, school enrollment, hospital discharges, and licensing records to pre-fill forms, renew benefits, and flag changes. Fund the caseworkers who resolve errors. Let families correct a mistake instead of assembling proof from zero. The state should find eligible families. Families should not have to audition.

Second, build coverage that does not depend on which nonprofit happened to be founded in which county. Put a nutrition site in every county, with mobile distribution where distance makes a fixed site useless. Use public or community operators where no local nonprofit can. Fund it through stable federal and state money so the doors stay open. A benefit reserved for the visibly desperate is easy to starve. A public floor is harder to pretend does not exist.

Universalism is not decoration. It is maintenance. Benefits available only to households judged poor become poor benefits, and the rest of the country can pretend they are somebody else’s problem. A benefit that reaches every family gives the middle class a reason to defend it, much as the interstate did. The floor holds when it belongs to everybody.

America already has part of the ownership grammar. About 900 rural electric cooperatives serve 42 million people. About 145 million Americans belong to credit unions. North Dakota has run a state-owned bank since 1919. None of these is a Scandinavian import. They are American proof that people can own infrastructure and capital together.

But shared ownership does not create administrative capacity by magic.

I grew up between two arrangements. In Minnesota, my grandfather sat on dairy and electric cooperative boards, and my grandmother ran a Lutheran church basement like a pantry: casseroles, rides to the clinic, quiet envelopes of cash when a farm payment came up short. It was the best institution in the county. It was never, not once, a food policy. Mutual aid is a complement to a floor, not the floor itself. When the casserole is the plan, that is not community strength—it is a hole where the floor used to be, with someone standing in it, holding a casserole.

On my mother’s side, Gothenburg had the welfare state. Same work ethic. Different plumbing. The plumbing here has to be built, and building it takes longer than announcing it.

American labor mostly bargains firm by firm. Nordic systems bargain across whole industries and pair that bargaining with capable public administration. We have fifty state systems, weak political parties, campaign finance that rewards concentrated money, and a long history of using racial division to split working families before anyone reaches the ballot. High trust is not free. It is partly the product of institutions that keep delivering.

Denmark argues about percentages. Here, the fight is over whether the floor exists at all. Automatic enrollment asks the tax and benefits systems to talk to each other, where the legal and technical work is unfinished. A universal child benefit must survive an appropriations fight every year. A program that must be reauthorized annually is a program with a fuse in it.

The workable version is not a federal marble palace. It is the boring pieces. Make the credit automatic where the data already connects. Let states that want to top it up do so. Write the benefit so filling out the form is not the means test. Repair the incentive before the machine eats the floor.

At Enough to Share, Brenda Rasheed says the mood in the line has not changed. She sees “the gratitude on faces, the relief on faces.” Of course they do. The gratitude is real. It is also not a budget. The bag is two meals instead of four. The line is longer. Thirty percent of the people coming through the food bank network over the last three months were new to it.

Prices are weather. The cut is a vote.

Change the vote—and build the floor. Restore SNAP’s purchasing power. Make eligibility automatic. Put one nutrition site in every county, with mobile distribution where distance matters. Make the child benefit universal and durable. Let the mosque be the front door, not the whole safety net.

Charity can hold the line. The country can build the floor.
