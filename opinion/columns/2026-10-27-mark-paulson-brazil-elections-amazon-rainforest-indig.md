The woman at the north end of County Z brought me a Stihl chainsaw with a dead coil and an electric bill she wanted to talk about while I fixed it. She has a heat pump and a half-ton truck and no charger she can reach from her house, and her bill went up again this year. She wanted to know whether anybody was going to do anything about it.

I told her what I know about how the co-op works. I have been a member of Adams-Columbia Electric Cooperative since I was a boy. The bill comes in the mailbox with the annual meeting notice, and the board that decides what gets built on her line and on mine is elected by the households that live on it. That is not a small thing. In 1936 fewer than eleven percent of American farms had electricity, and the companies that owned the wire did not think rural Wisconsin was worth the run. The federal government thought otherwise and made a plain decision: twenty-five-year loans at three percent, routed not to the investor-owned utilities but to member-owned, nonprofit cooperatives. By 1950 about eighty percent of American farms had service. The instrument did not work because people are sentimental about farms. It worked because the customers ended up owning the thing.

The same bet is being made right now in South America, and it is the same bet because the question underneath it is the question she asked me: who owns the thing, and who carries the cost when the money runs out.

The Amazon exhaled on Sunday, and the money arrived on Monday.

The Netherlands put $500 million into the Tropical Forest Forever Facility on Monday, a Brazil-backed fund launched at last year's United Nations climate summit to pay developing countries to keep forests standing. The pledge raised commitments to $7.8 billion, still $2.2 billion short of the fund's $10 billion year-end target. Prospective donors had held off while the election was unsettled. Claudio Angelo, the Climate Observatory's head of international policy, called the Dutch pledge a vote of confidence in Lula's victory. WWF Brazil credited Lula with strengthening Brazil's leadership internationally and said maintaining that leadership is what mobilizes other countries and raises the ambition of the commitments themselves. The $10 billion target is a floor, not the finish line.

Official results put Lula at 51.78 percent of the runoff vote against Flávio Bolsonaro's 48.21 percent, a 4.4-million-vote margin after he trailed in the first round ([official results](https://mainstreetindependent.com/articles/2026-10-25-official-results-show-lula-wins-brazil-runoff-with-more-than-51-of-the-vote/)). For the fund, that is the good half of the story.

Lula defeated Flávio Bolsonaro. He did not defeat Bolsonaroism.

Lula's government announced last week the lowest Amazon deforestation rate on record. A forest is not protected by ceremony. It survives through enforcement. Now comes the harder part.

Think about what the fund is actually built to do. It pays a country to keep a forest standing, and the money is supposed to reach the people who live with that forest, including the nations whose land it is. My co-op runs the same logic down a line in Wisconsin. The people downstream of the wire own the wire. Wendell Berry's argument in *The Unsettling of America* is that a community's economy is its membership, and that you can take the membership out well before you take the money out. The Tropical Forest Forever Facility is a bet that Brazil's version of that membership ends up holding the forest money. Right now the bet is unhedged, because Brazil is holding the forest, the fund, the oil, and a Congress that would rather it did not. Credibility, however, does not eliminate the danger. It identifies where the danger now resides.

Brazil's Climate Observatory, a coalition of 172 organizations, says seven in ten incoming lawmakers oppose the government's climate and environmental agenda, and it points to measures already weakening pesticide rules, Indigenous land rights, and environmental licensing. The Observatory has urged Lula to broaden his alliances and strengthen his ties to Congress. The [Liberal Party emerged as the largest force in Congress](https://mainstreetindependent.com/articles/2026-10-26-brazil-s-liberal-party-emerges-largest-in-congress-after-lula-s-51-8-runoff-win/), and Lula's own supporters warn that the new chamber is increasingly stacked with legislators who want the safeguards gone and with the agribusiness caucus. The Dutch money was paid for standing forest, and standing forest depends on enforcement, and seven in ten of the people who write the enforcement rules oppose enforcement. The agribusiness caucus is not an abstraction up here. In this county the ground is in Heartland Farms' name: about 27,000 acres across five counties, roughly 9,000 acres of potatoes a year under contract for Frito-Lay, about a hundred center pivots.

Presidential victory is not legislative control. A president can inspect forests, enforce laws and coordinate international aid. He cannot simply legislate around a hostile chamber, and he cannot buy a Brazilian congressman a single vote with Dutch money.

Here is where it lands on a household bill. The Wisconsin Public Service Commission sets the terms my neighbors and I have to live inside: who pays for a new line, how a farm or a householder with solar gets paid back, and where a transmission corridor may cross. Some of those dockets have been fought over for years — Cardinal-Hickory Creek, the net-metering parameters under docket 5-UR-109. And the ground for building is getting harder, not easier. A June 2024 count found at least 450 counties across 44 states with significant restrictions on renewable siting by the end of that year, up 16 percent in a single year, and the running tally of projects killed or delayed since 2013 stands at 760, with 117 rejections in 2024 alone. A national laboratory survey that year put roughly a third of utility-scale wind and solar projects cancelled and half of them delayed six months or more. Then H.R. 1 took back the unspent balance of the $5 billion NEVI program, and the economics of putting a charger on Highway 13 between Wisconsin Rapids and the Dells never worked the way the map promised.

When the money is right and the control sits with the people on the line, a rural household gets generation, and a wire, and a charger, and the cost is spread across the members who use it. When the money is wrong or the control has been captured, the member gets the promise and the bill. That is my neighbor's electric bill. That is the vote she will cast.

Flávio Bolsonaro made clear where his policies would have gone, and that is what stood on the other side of the arithmetic. He denied climate change while claiming support for environmental protection, offered few specifics on forest protection or financing, called for "modernizing" environmental laws, and proposed giving Amazon landowners more decision-making power. Environmentalists warned that those changes would weaken the safeguards and accelerate deforestation. His father, Jair Bolsonaro, governed from 2019 through 2022, and deforestation surged as his administration weakened environmental enforcement agencies and loosened the regulations. The instruments that produced the record Lula announced last week are the instruments Flávio was promising to loosen further.

And the victory carried the fingerprints of the people whose rights are tied to the forest more tightly than anybody else's. The Articulation of Indigenous Peoples of Brazil worked with organizations that went to court to secure Indigenous voters' access to the polls; together with the court rulings and government transportation for remote communities, that work helped make the runoff win possible. APIB said Lula's victory reflected "the strength of grassroots mobilization in defense of democracy, life and Indigenous peoples' rights." The Amazon's political geography changed because Indigenous voters were not merely courted as a constituency; their organizations helped determine who would govern.

One hand delivered Indigenous voters to the polls. The other is trying to write their rights out of law.

That is the whole argument in one election, and it is the argument my co-op makes on a smaller scale: the people who live with the resource have to hold the money that manages it, or somebody else manages it for them. The Forest Forever Facility was built on that premise, and it has to survive the chamber that just showed up.

The stakes are not the election. David Lapola, a Brazilian scientist and lead author of a chapter on so-called tipping points in an upcoming United Nations climate report, said it plain: "The Amazon can breathe a sigh of relief!" About 60 percent of the Amazon lies in Brazil. The forest absorbs vast quantities of carbon dioxide, and scientists continue to debate the threshold beyond which the dense, humid rainforest could shift toward a drier ecosystem, with irreversible consequences for biodiversity and the climate.

I keep a notebook. Twelve years now of the ice going out on Petenwell, the rut starting, the first mosquitoes, how long the syrup season ran. The ice on Petenwell has gone out eleven days earlier than the first winter I wrote it down. The state's own numbers say the same thing in their way: the climate assessment done by the Wisconsin DNR and the Nelson Institute at UW–Madison has Wisconsin about three degrees Fahrenheit warmer since 1950 and seventeen percent wetter, with 2010 through 2019 the wettest decade since the records begin around 1900. The long series is not a promise either. Lake Mendota's ice record, the longest continuous one in North America, has run since 1855, and the lake now loses about twenty-nine days of ice to what the mid-1800s had. Aldo Leopold's sand counties are Wisconsin counties, and this one is one of them. The tipping point is still a question. The direction is not.

Flávio's refusal to concede cannot change the arithmetic. He said he would ask Brazil's electoral court to nullify the results before they are certified on December 18. He carries a severe burden: evidence of fraud sufficient to overturn a 4.4-million-vote margin, and persuasion of the court before the deadline. Early signals from Brazil's institutions and regional leaders, including opposition state presidents who oppose Lula, suggest the outcome will be accepted at home and abroad. A legal challenge cannot turn 48.21 percent into 51.78 percent. Flávio Bolsonaro can keep the election dispute alive. The more consequential contest is already underway.

The decisive contradiction is offshore oil. Lula supports efforts to drill for oil in offshore waters near the Amazon. The contradiction is fiscal before it is symbolic. The money the world is paying for standing forest and the money from drilling beside the forest both arrive in the same treasury, which means one set of people has to be persuaded that the second one is not worth taking. Put another way, the Tropical Forest Forever Facility could become green wrapping around oil. Lower annual deforestation figures are not enough if energy policy makes sustained destruction the more attractive transaction.

And nobody should wait for the oil question to resolve itself at some future election. U.S. crude production averaged 12.9 million barrels a day in 2023, a record, and kept climbing after that — 13.2 million in 2024, a monthly record of 13.6 million in July 2025 — under two parties and two presidents, each of them claiming the credit. Federal-lands production reached 1.7 million barrels a day in 2024, about 13 percent of the national total and six times the 2010 level. The reason those numbers move the way they do is not elections. It is leases, royalty terms, capital, and permitting. The long story is leases granted in 2017 producing oil in 2023. Same on the electric side, which is where it comes back to my neighbor's bill: what gets built on her line depends on the permit, the capital, and who owns the line, and those three settle it long before any ballot does.

The Netherlands put $500 million behind Brazil's promise. The Indigenous organizers made that promise politically real. Now Lula has to convert a 4.4-million-vote reprieve into governing reality: alliances in the chamber he does not control, Indigenous rights written into law instead of speeches, the oil question settled before somebody else settles it for him, and the next tranche of forest money tied to measurable gains at home rather than to promises.

The race is over. The defense of the Amazon begins now — and the forest has bought Lula time. He must now earn it.
