A war closes a strait, and nine months later the world's largest maker of solar panels posts its biggest export year on record. That is the entire shape of 2026, and it is not the shape anyone planned for. It is also a story that lands on a farm bill in Adams County, Wisconsin, whether or not anyone in Ankara or Beijing says so.

The numbers are not subtle. In the first nine months of this year, Chinese exports of electric vehicles rose 61%, wind turbine shipments jumped 45%, battery exports climbed 39%, and overseas solar sales grew 22%. Export value for electric vehicles hit $72.8 billion, up from $39.7 billion last year. Shipments to Southeast Asia — a region that just watched a fifth of the world's oil and gas supply become a war zone — rose nearly 160%, by the tally of the think tank Ember. Once installed, this year's exports will likely avert about 450 million metric tons of carbon dioxide annually, roughly Brazil's entire annual footprint. The Centre for Research on Energy and Clean Air estimates the clean tech China ships in 2026 will eventually avert about 8,400 million metric tons over the lifetime of the equipment — roughly 24% of annual global emissions, more than the United States and India emit combined. Lauri Myllyvirta, an analyst at CREA, calls it "a pivot point for the outlook of global energy and global emissions." Independent experts said the estimate was in line with the scale of what China is actually exporting, though they could not validate the underlying data — a caveat worth holding onto.

Read those numbers with the Strait of Hormuz in mind, and then read them again with your own electric bill in hand. The countries that import fuel — Laos, Thailand, Vietnam, the Philippines, Indonesia — got hit first and hardest by the war, because they are the ones buying. Their consumers are paying more for electricity and fuel right now. So they are doing the only rational thing available: installing rooftop solar, buying electric vehicles, taking the incentives their governments are now offering. The Philippines became the second-largest destination for Chinese solar in the first quarter of 2026; Indonesia has pledged 100 gigawatts of solar by 2029. "There is a paradigm shift happening," said Yu Sun Chin of Zero Carbon Analytics. This is not climate virtue. It is energy security, priced in by a war, not by a summit.

Which is precisely the lesson a Wisconsin reader should take personally, because we have been running this experiment from the other end for forty years. My own electric service comes from Adams-Columbia Electric Cooperative — the largest rural electric cooperative in Wisconsin, headquartered in Friendship, member-owned since the Rural Electrification Act of 1936 told the investor-owned utilities that the market could go hang. The co-op's power flows through Dairyland, and Dairyland's economics flow through fuel: natural gas sets the marginal price, and when gas prices lurch because a war hits a strait, my rate pays for it. Cheaper Chinese panels and batteries entering the global market are, from the membership meeting in Friendship, pure upside — the equipment cost of a member's rooftop array, a farm's solar offset, the cooperative's next generation project all falls. That part of the story I'll take, and I'll say so plainly.

But note who is now supplying the hardware of my own self-reliance. The whole point of the co-op form was that the membership owned the means of its own power — wire, substation, vote, one member one vote. Now the panels, the inverters, the batteries, and increasingly the transformers and controllers coming into rural America are stamped Made in China, and the warranty, the standards, the spare-parts supply, and the firmware are written in Beijing. We swapped dependence on a Persian Gulf we did not control for dependence on a supply chain we do not control. The landlord changed. The tenancy did not.

That is the wariness a small-engine mechanic recognizes instantly, because it is the same wariness I have toward the diagnostic software that locks my bench out of a Deere tractor I have paid for. It is the masculine contradiction running through the whole trade story: the rhetoric of self-reliance, sold to us by people who have quietly sold the means of self-reliance offshore. The farmer who can't calibrate his own baler and the county that can't source a transformer without a foreign purchase order are victims of the same transaction.

Here is where I part company with the green lobby's reading of the CREA report. "Averted emissions" is an accountant's invention — a spreadsheet deduction for pollution that will never be measured, resting on assumptions the report's own reviewers admit they cannot validate. Call it a hypothesis, not a monument. What survives every model is the one hard fact in the exercise: the hardware is Chinese. If the clean energy future arrives, it arrives stamped Made in China, and the invoice is payable in dependence.

And the same China is still building coal plants at home while its diplomats recite green scripture abroad, as we reported earlier this year. Muyi Yang of Ember has the right mechanical analogy — replacing a ship's engine while it's still at sea — but nobody should mistake an export record for a domestic reckoning. Emissions cut in Jakarta count exactly as much as emissions cut in Adams County. Still, the two facts sit together: China is running the shipyard and sinking the ship.

Which brings us to America's answer: tariffs. President Trump's higher duties were supposed to stop the flood. Instead, Chinese clean-tech exports to the U.S. rose 13% in January through September. Myllyvirta says demand was "much stronger than I expected." That number is the whole trade war in one line — Washington's heaviest lever, and the machines kept coming. We saw this gathering in April, when we noted that China was gaining from the Iran war as global energy interest turned away from fossil fuels; what has changed since is the scale. The clean-energy pivot is no longer a forecast debated in conference halls; it is a shipment schedule.

The question that schedule poses for us is not whether panels are good — they are, and cheap ones are better — but whether Adams County ends this decade as a member of its own energy economy or as a market for somebody else's. The money that leaves for foreign equipment is the same money that used to circle: the hardware store, the electrician in Friendship, the shop that sells and services the inverters. Every dollar of hardware we cannot build, service, or firmware-open is a dollar of Main Street that doesn't happen, and a tax base that thins accordingly. A county that imports its energy future wholesale is a county renting its own grid.

In November, Turkey will host the U.N. climate talks, and electrification will be the leading topic. The delegations will arrive with pledges and timelines. Meanwhile the actual transition will be driven by shipping manifests and insurance premiums — by buyers who no longer care whether the panels are green, only that they are cheap, available, and independent of a strait that can be closed by a missile.

I'd go further than the experts who say renewed interest in clean energy will outlast the war. The war didn't create the transition; it revealed one that was already economically inevitable and removed the last excuse for going slowly. What it also revealed, in the only language this county has ever fully trusted, is the bill. The question left for Adams County is not whether the panels are coming. They're coming. The question is whether we own the means of the switch, or merely pay for it.
