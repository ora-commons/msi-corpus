Gregory Aymond put a confessed abuser in front of Catholic schoolboys. That sentence is the whole case, and every word of it is documented; the record is public, and it fits in one hand. In 2012 a young woman reported to the church what Paul Hart, one of its priests, had done to her when she was seventeen and had met him through his ministry. Hart confessed it — to his own superiors, inside the church's own confidential investigation. An advisory board working with the archbishop reviewed the file and recommended removing Hart from public ministry, and it said why in plain terms: under the church's own canon law as amended in 2002, the girl he touched had been a child. Aymond read that recommendation and overrode it on a technicality — the canon-law age of consent in the early 1990s, when the contact occurred, was sixteen; Louisiana's was seventeen. Five years later, when Brother Martin High School, an all-boys' Catholic school in New Orleans, asked for a chaplain, the archbishop sent Hart to the students, and sent him with a blessing, in writing: "Please know that I not only appreciate your ministry but will pray for you that God will use you to be a sign of pastoral care to our young people." You read that recommendation, Archbishop, and you sent him anyway.

Hold the blessing beside the letters the office claims. The gospel you preach speaks of exactly this with no softness: whoever causes one of these little ones who believe in me to sin, it would be better for him to have a great millstone fastened around his neck and to be drowned in the depth of the sea. See that you do not despise one of these little ones. I desire mercy, not sacrifice. As you did it to one of the least of these, you did it to me. And when religious men kept the form of piety while feeding on the people the form existed to protect, he named them whitewashed tombs — beautiful outside, full of dead men's bones within. So hear it plainly, from inside the house. You knew. Your own advisory board told you, and told you why. You overrode it, set the man among boys, and asked God to bless the arrangement. That is not a lapse in judgment. That is evil. The word is not too strong; the gospel you preach uses it for less. It is betrayal, and it is cruel, and the tradition you serve has another word for it: sin — and I write that word inside the household that earned it. I have knelt my whole life in parishes of this same communion; the confidential process that shielded Hart was built by my own church, and the chancery instinct that calls concealment prudence is mine before it is anyone else's.

Richard Trahant is the attorney who ended it. His work for abuse claimants inside the archdiocese's 2020 bankruptcy gave him access to the documents chronicling Hart's confession, and in late 2021 he told Brother Martin's principal that Hart carried "a credible allegation from [his] past that involved a minor." Conscience permitted the warning; a confidentiality order forbade the details, and he kept them inside the order. The archdiocese soon supplied the school the specifics itself. Brother Martin asked for Hart's removal from its campus. Hart retired in January 2022 — nine months before his death — while the archdiocese told a Times-Picayune reporter he was leaving to fight "struggles with treatment for brain cancer." To the bankruptcy court's investigators he called the reporting on his departure "so bogus that it looked … almost made up."

Then the bill arrived, addressed to the wrong man. Judge Meredith Grabill fined Trahant $400,000 — $460,000 once interest was counted — ruling that his warning had set off a chain of events amounting to a violation of the confidentiality order, and she expelled four of his clients, four survivors, from the committee negotiating the settlement. Consider what was actually enforced: a confidentiality order written to protect abuse survivors' files, enforced against the one man who used those files to get an abuser away from children. That is cruel. It is also, on the investigation's own findings, mistaken — a sanction on conduct that did not occur. The inquiry Grabill herself ordered determined that Brother Martin had learned the specifics of Hart's record from the archdiocese, not from Trahant, and conceded there was evidence supporting his denial that he had ever fed the press. He appealed all the way up, and in June the Supreme Court [let the fine stand](/articles/2026-07-01-supreme-court-leaves-400-000-fine-in-place-against-lawyer-who-flagged-priest-s-p/) when it declined to hear the case — so he paid it. Here is what the order cost in fact: a lawyer paid $460,000 for warning a school, and four survivors lost their own advocate on the committee deciding what their wounds were worth.

The rot is not only the chancery's. Institutions of every kind, sacred and secular, in every political camp, have protected their own names against their own wounded, and the court that punished the truth-teller here was no one's chancery. If a public school board had done what this chancery did, I would write the same column; if any court anywhere fines the person who exposes abuse while the institution that produced it pays nothing, I name that wherever I find it. And my own Church hid men like Hart for decades — moved them, covered for them, blessed them — and this bankruptcy exists because that pattern came to New Orleans. I have knelt in parishes where the names that needed saying were replaced with music, and I do not get to stand outside this and point.

On November 6 the vindication came — four years after the man it concerned died, nine years after the blessing. Archbishop James Checchio, Aymond's successor, added twenty-seven names to the archdiocese's list of credibly accused clergy, and Hart's name is on it now, with three bankruptcy claims standing against him, in a list of 106. The church's own list settles what Hart spent his last years denying. Credit where the record gives it: Checchio did what his predecessor would not. But he called the update a step toward "accountability and healing," and the survivors of this bankruptcy had already called the arithmetic [a farce](/articles/2026-08-28-new-orleans-catholic-abuse-survivors-call-bankruptcy-amounts-a-farce/) before the December 2025 agreement put $305 million on the table. Trahant called his exoneration "the very definition of a Pyrrhic victory" — "Sure, it's exoneration — yet only after I paid a fortune" — and then said the truer thing: "But if the question is: was it worth it? The answer is yes, and I would do it again tomorrow." Of Hart: "He was lying. And … now they've admitted [it]." Accountability is not a list filed nine years late. It is an apology with a name signed to it, the $460,000 returned to the man who paid it for the truth, and every file Hart's silence protected opened to the light. James Adams, one of the survivors the court struck from the settlement committee, said the rest plainly: "At least now, everyone knows that we were wrongly removed." And: "The court sanctioned an innocent lawyer and his clients." He asked the new archbishop for the apology and the refund. That is the whole of what justice requires here, and it has not yet been done.

Romero preached in 1977 that a church is obliged to denounce what he called *pecado social* — social sin: "Cuando el régimen económico, político y social establece privilegios para unos pocos y deja a otros muchos en condiciones infrahumanas, hay pecado social y la Iglesia tiene la obligación profética de denunciarlo." When the order of things establishes privileges for a few and leaves the many in inhuman conditions, there is social sin, and the Church has the prophetic obligation to denounce it. He asked the question this story answers: "A church that does not provoke any crisis, a gospel that does not unsettle, a word of God that does not get under anyone's skin … what gospel is that?" A gospel that does not touch the real sin of the society in which it is proclaimed is no gospel at all. It is a letter of recommendation written to the man your board told you to remove. And a church that desired mercy would count mercy for the wounded and justice from the institution, not the other way around.

Archbishop, hear me. What you did was betrayal — of the girl who reported in 2012 and was answered with a technicality, of the schoolchildren you set him among, of the office you held — and no clock assembled out of a 1990s canon absolves it. The door is not closed to you, and it is not mysterious about what it asks. You are retired, not beyond it. You can say in public, while you are alive to say it, what you did and why you overrode your own board — the door costs only the truth. You can apologize, in the church's own voice, to the woman who reported in 2012 and to Richard Trahant, and ask your successor to return the $460,000 to the man who paid it for the truth. You can treat the canon law of 2002 — your church's own law, which already knew she was a child — as binding rather than optional. You can walk into the archive room in Baton Rouge when the files arrive, sit at the table, and read your own 2017 letter to Hart aloud where anyone can hear it. Repentance does not expire. Neither does the mercy that waits behind it.

I repair broken things for a living — chairs, pipes, doors, and once in a while a heart — and I know the difference between what a shop can mend and what only truth can. In Baton Rouge a public archive waits for the boxes the church has promised to send: the files of 106 men, open to anyone with the legs to walk in who wants to read what the chancery spent years keeping dark. Files outlast the men who sign the orders that bury them. The fine was built on the claim that these pages were secret; when they open, anyone can read how much a man paid for daring to come near the truth.

The texts the archbishop's office claims are not ambiguous about children, and they are not ambiguous about what remains once the listing is finally done: "These you ought to have done, without neglecting the others."
