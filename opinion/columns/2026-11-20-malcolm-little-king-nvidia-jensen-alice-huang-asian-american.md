The Alice Huang spy allegation turned Asian American women into collateral damage.

April Moh, chief marketing officer for a financial technology company and board member of Stand with Asian Americans, told the Associated Press[^1] that women in technology had reported strange looks and questions about laptop security while visiting China. One woman was jokingly asked, “So, are you a spy?”

It was supposed to be a joke.

That is how prejudice hides when it is useful: inside laughter, where everyone can preserve deniability afterward. The joke made the woman carrying it responsible for the fear. Her work, her judgment, her access, her professional competence—all of it became evidence for the performance. Moh did not pretend otherwise. “It’s not funny to her or me.”

The joke was the story’s destination. The allegation was only the road.

After [Jensen Huang resigned as Nvidia CEO following reports of an extramarital affair](/articles/2026-11-12-jensen-huang-resigns-as-nvidia-ceo-after-affair-reports/), the scandal did not end with the people involved. Alice Huang, the Chinese businesswoman at its center, was not related to Jensen Huang. According to the Associated Press, the Justice Department had not found sufficient evidence that she was a spy, even as [multiple Justice Department investigations involving Huang](/articles/2026-11-17-justice-department-leads-multiple-investigations-involving-alice-huang/) continued. President Donald Trump separately told reporters, “I don’t know if that’s been determined yet.”

No. The sentence did not make the allegation true. It suspended the question of proof and left the image attached to the woman’s face.

That does not excuse Jensen Huang’s reported affair. Accountability for his conduct does not require racialized retaliation against Alice Huang. A powerful man can be held responsible for what he did without making an unrelated Asian woman a foreign agent.

The evidence stops where the evidence stops.

The stereotype does not.

Cui bono? Who benefits when Asian women are made to serve simultaneously as sexual objects, suspected agents, and obedient technicians rather than as full subjects of their own lives?

The answer is not one villain sitting in one room. The arrangement does not require a conspiracy. It requires several functions, each performed by a different institution.

Political officials can use a ready-made frame for suspicion and control. The allegation arrives attached to a Chinese surname, and the old machinery of national security supplies the machinery of legitimacy. For employers, the stereotype supplies a structural advantage: Asian workers can be praised as dependable without being trusted to command. Online platforms and memes can profit from attention. Private individuals can turn inherited contempt into a joke that can be denied when the joke lands.

The benefits are distributed. The injury is concentrated.

The workers Moh described did not need to understand geopolitics or follow the case. They had been handed the translation: Asian woman means possible spy. Once that translation was available, ordinary suspicion acquired a formal disguise. The laptop became suspicious because its owner was Asian. The joke became a screening device.

The stereotype also gives hiring and promotion decision-makers a ready-made rationale for denying command without announcing racial exclusion. “Fit,” “culture,” and “judgment” can do the exclusionary work. The institution does not have to name the mechanism because the stereotype has already supplied its vocabulary.

Rachel Lee, president and general counsel of Stand with Asian Americans, said many of the organization’s clients were Asian women who had proved their competence yet were still accused of being fraudulent or deceitful. Her formulation was exact: Asian Americans are made into “perpetual foreigners, untrustworthy, disloyal.”

Perpetual foreigners cannot be given authority without an expiration date. Their competence is welcome so long as it does not disturb the hierarchy. Their reliability is valuable so long as it does not become ambition. Their leadership must be proven, and then disproven by the stereotype that made the proof necessary.

That is not recognition. It is recognition with a locked door.

No principal creator or coordinator of the meme wave is identified in the record. The documented chain establishes circulation, not a mastermind; naming one would outrun the evidence. What the evidence does establish is the effect: a contested allegation became material anyone could repeat without defending an accusation.

If we stop at “social media spread a bad idea,” we mistake the effect for the cause.

The cause is older and more useful. The meme did not invent the Asian woman as spy. It attached a new celebrity face to an old operating system.

Congress built the legal grammar of that foreignness. The Page Act of 1875 controlled immigration from what the statute called “any Oriental country” and included a specific prohibition on women coming for prostitution. The Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882 then barred Chinese laborers from entering the United States. Congress made exclusion law before Hollywood learned how to perform the seductress-spy script.

May-lee Chai, a creative writing professor at San Francisco State University, traced the next link into early twentieth-century cinema, where the Asian woman became an archetype of the seductress, the spy, and the “dragon lady.” Those images were not born in last month’s news feed. They were available as ready-made roles, complete with costumes and meanings.

Misa Chien, founder of The Authentic Asian, a network for Asian American women leaders, knew the role from the inside. Twenty years ago, a building-security advertisement in Hong Kong cast her as a spy and instructed her to wear three bras to resemble Angelina Jolie’s figure. Chien later wrote that the advertisement showed how readily the media placed Asian women in roles that made them objects of suspicion and spectacle.

Now the role traveled with a real name attached. The advertisement required exaggeration; the news cycle required only a rumor. One social-media collage joined CEOs with their Asian female partners. Chai called it “disgusting.” Its accusation was subtler than saying every wife was a traitor: it made the wives appear interchangeable, ornamental, foreign, and therefore capable of serving a hidden foreign cause.

The trope did not need to choose between degradation and suspicion. It could do both at once. Make the woman sexual, then make her secretive. Make her the trophy, then make her the traitor. Make her subordinate, then use that subordination as proof that she belongs elsewhere.

The government did not stop building the apparatus when the culture moved from Hollywood to the immigration office and then to the workplace. The first Trump administration created the China Initiative, a plan to profile Chinese professors as part of espionage investigations. Critics said the program contributed to the 2024 suicide of Jane Ying Wu, a 60-year-old Northwestern University neuroscientist who had lived in the United States for forty years, came under federal investigation in 2019, and was never charged with anything.

The Associated Press presents that causal claim as a criticism, not an adjudicated finding. What the documented sequence establishes is that federal scrutiny entered the life of a woman the government never charged. Chai’s verdict was the verdict the record demands: “This type of pernicious stereotype can actually destroy lives.”

Over the same period, more than two dozen states proposed or enacted “alien land laws” designed to prevent Chinese nationals from buying property. The number of international students enrolling in American universities also fell.

Those institutions made ethnicity operational as a substitute for individualized evidence. That was the institutional benefit: power without proof, suspicion without investigation, and a stereotype furnished by law.

Name the authors. Congress wrote the Page Act and the Chinese Exclusion Act. The first Trump administration created the China Initiative. State legislatures wrote the alien-land statutes. Social media platforms carried the memes. Colleagues turned them into suspicion at work.

“The system” is not an alibi. Every link has an author.

The Asian American Foundation’s annual Social Tracking of Asian Americans in the United States Index found that at least half of U.S. adults characterized Asian Americans in the workplace as “respectful” or “reliable,” while only one in ten viewed them as “assertive.” That survey measured perceptions of Asian American workers generally, not Asian women specifically.

Those words sound complimentary until they are placed inside an organizational chart. Read there, “respectful” becomes code for unlikely to cause trouble; “reliable,” unlikely to disrupt operations; and a reputation for not being assertive makes command look like a breach of character rather than an aspiration for which evidence is required.

Respect without authority is not respect. It is a job description with prettier nouns. Reliability without decision-making power is not trust. It is a promise that the institution can use you without ever preparing to obey you.

That distinction separates a valued employee from a trusted decision-maker. The first proves competence. The second threatens the arrangement that assigned competence to the lower rank.

Chien said the leading theme in onboarding interviews for new members of The Authentic Asian during the previous week was, “I’m mistaken for the secretary, I don’t look like the typical face of leadership.”

The employer sees the qualification. It simply refuses to read leadership into it.

The survey establishes a preexisting workplace stereotype. It does not prove that the survey caused the event-specific stares, security questions, or jokes. What the reported accounts establish is that people were subjected to those harms after the allegation arrived. The stereotype helped make the allegation legible once it was here; it did not create every prejudice that followed.

The documented history shows that this culture can turn stereotype into violence. It does not turn the stereotype into evidence. History explains why the spy allegation found a prepared ear. It does not establish the allegation.

King’s late work refused to treat racism as a collection of private attitudes floating above the material order. In “Beyond Vietnam: A Time to Break Silence,” delivered at Riverside Church on April 4, 1967, he located racism inside a larger structure of power and interest. The narrower application here is exact: the same labor order that rewards Asian workers for reliability can preserve the right to command against them.

That is one hierarchy, not two problems. A company can celebrate Asian technical excellence while treating that excellence as most useful below command. It needs reliable expertise, careful compliance, and workers who do not interrupt. It need not place them in the room where direction is set.

King’s *Where Do We Go from Here?* (1967) likewise refused the confusion between formal inclusion and structural transformation. Promoting a worker can remove the most obvious insult while preserving the architecture underneath. Inclusion without decision rights is a more sophisticated form of segregation.

His April 16, 1963, letter from Birmingham distinguished the mere absence of conflict from the actual presence of justice. A room can be quiet because nobody challenges the stereotype, and that quiet can still be injustice. Positive justice is not Asian women being liked. It is their authority being ordinary.

Fairness also requires us to keep the separate questions separate. Jensen Huang’s conduct is one question. Whether Alice Huang was or was not a spy is another. Neither question gives publishers, politicians, employers, or coworkers permission to turn one person into a racial exhibit. The same evidentiary rule applies regardless of a person’s race, gender, wealth, or office. An allegation is not evidence of collective suspicion. A joke is not harmless because its author calls it one.

Rachel Lee said people should not have to exhaust the legal system before receiving assistance with workplace discrimination or harassment. She is right. By the time a discrimination case has been proved, the worker has already absorbed the stares, the questions, the jokes, and the suspicion. Prevention cannot begin after the lawyer’s letter.

A manager who hears “So, are you a spy?” does not need a damages verdict before treating it as harm. Employers must interrupt the joke rather than ask the targeted woman to prove she is joking along. Employers should treat this as governance, not sensitivity training: identify where the stereotype governs hiring, promotion, client assignment, security clearance, and leadership; provide reporting and support without requiring proof; and stop retaliation against people who report the harm.

Workplace training cannot consist of telling women to make more room for themselves while the room reserves the appearance of leadership for someone else. Public officials must keep public language no stronger than the evidence. Platforms must refuse the amplification. State legislatures should repeal laws that turn nationality into a property defect. Communities must teach the history rather than treating each incident as an isolated misunderstanding. Citizens must refuse the easy laugh.

The burden belongs to the institutions with authority, not to the woman made to carry the stereotype.

King’s Beloved Community, as he developed it in *Where Do We Go from Here?*, is not a mood at the end of a speech. It is a common world in which no person is made a permanent foreigner and no one’s authority depends on keeping somebody else quiet.

No woman should have to prove she is not a spy before her work is believed, her clients trust her, or her leadership is taken seriously. That is a standard, not a favor.

The spy trope does not make the country safer. It makes Asian women less safe when they exercise authority, and it gives public institutions a cheaper excuse for their own prejudice.

The work is specific. Employers interrupt the joke. Communities teach the history. Platforms refuse the amplification. Officials wait for evidence. Citizens refuse the easy laugh. We build institutions in which competence is recognized as competence, authority is distributed without a racial reservation system, and citizenship is not performance for the perpetually foreign.

No more secretaries by default. No more spies by stereotype. No more authority denied by making another person foreign.

King’s long moral arc, worked out across *Letter from Birmingham Jail* and *Where Do We Go from Here?*, bends toward justice only when people push it. This is the hour to push.

The moral arc bends only when specific people, with specific authority, refuse to carry the old lie forward.

End the stereotype. Stop the joke. Break the chain.

Malcolm Little King
