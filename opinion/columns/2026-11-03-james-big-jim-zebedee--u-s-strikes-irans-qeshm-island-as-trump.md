The Trump administration is striking Iran in secret while saying the war is over.

The decision is concentrated in offices; the danger is distributed among people who had no hand in making it. The all-volunteer force changed the gate from the draft board to the recruiting office. It did not abolish the old class arithmetic. Families with money can steer children away from military service; communities with fewer choices send more young people through the door. A senator’s child, a financier’s child, and a senior official’s child have options that a factory worker’s child may never have. None of that makes honorable service dishonorable. It makes those who control the war responsible for who is made to carry it.

At Zebedee Auto, I can tell a customer that a truck is finished only when the work has been checked from the road back to the frame. You do not certify a machine because a switch is off today. You look at what is fueled, what is connected, and what will move when somebody starts it. The same check applies here.

On Monday, President Trump said the United States had not been fighting Iran for months. “We had a war with Iran. We won.” Asked whether he was considering a strike after Tuesday’s midterms, he said no, although administration officials said he was still weighing his options. That denial came after Trump had already [promised not to attack Iran before the midterm elections](/articles/2026-10-08-trump-says-us-will-not-attack-iran-before-midterm-elections/).

U.S. officials said the U.S. military struck Iran’s Qeshm Island the same day. By their account, it was another round in a campaign of secret, small-scale attacks. Earlier MSI reporting [tied the limited Qeshm strikes](/articles/2026-11-03-us-officials-say-limited-qeshm-strikes-responded-to-irgc-fire-on-us-aircraft/) to fire by Iran’s Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps, or IRGC, on U.S. aircraft. Unlike earlier in the war, the United States did not publicly announce the operation. One official said the administration was keeping the campaign quiet so it would not signal an intention to resume major combat operations.

The chain of command leaves physical records even when the explanation stays quiet. Adm. Brad Cooper, chief of U.S. Central Command, the regional headquarters commanding American forces in the Middle East, briefed Trump last week. CENTCOM moved more missile defenses and munitions into the region and laid groundwork for broader strikes. The United States told Gulf nations to prepare for a short and intense operation shortly after the election. Senior administration officials presented Trump with options that included tighter sanctions and a blockade on Iranian trade.

Then came the strike on Qeshm. The Defense Department referred questions to CENTCOM, which did not immediately respond. Explosions around the island were reported on social media. Qeshm had already been targeted in earlier rounds of fighting, and officials had discussed seizing it.

Some officials described Monday’s attack as defensive, intended to prevent Iran from attacking commercial ships in the Strait of Hormuz. That concern has force. Merchant shipping is not an abstraction. A ship is a workplace, cargo is somebody’s business, and a threatened strait reaches merchant sailors, port crews, truck drivers, warehouse workers, and the families waiting for their wages. If insurers or shipowners reroute around risk, ships burn more fuel and spend more time at sea. Delays at the port move into freight prices, and freight prices move into the price of necessities.

Qeshm makes the civilian danger concrete. The island sits at the mouth of the Strait of Hormuz and contains a large underground missile site, a naval base, and a major desalination plant, which turns seawater into fresh water. People on the island live with those military installations and depend on the water plant. A strike nearby could interrupt an essential service even if the plant itself was not the target.

No list of the installations hit has been published. No damage assessment or casualty count has been provided. No explanation has been given for whether civilians were warned. The absence of those facts does not make the danger disappear. It leaves the people near Qeshm and the merchant crews in the strait without the information they would need to judge their own risk.

From where I sat in the gunner’s seat in a Third Infantry Division Abrams crew in Iraq in 2003, a secret order still had a driver, a route, and a destination. The men up the chain could withhold their reasons. The crew could not pretend the ammunition was harmless because the public account had changed. Monday’s order has the same physical shape: strike Qeshm, move defenses and munitions, warn Gulf partners, and keep the campaign quiet.

The costs do not stop at the island. Military families absorb deployment orders, strained household budgets, separated marriages, and the risk of injury or death. They are told to make plans around uncertainty while the public is told the fighting has stopped. Port workers and merchant sailors face altered schedules, longer voyages, harder conditions, and the loss of paid working time. Public money follows the military orders as well. At home, the war enters the neighborhood through fuel prices, delayed goods, base deployment orders, and taxes.

Some decision-makers share the risk. That does not change where the system places most of it. The people making the war can preserve diplomatic flexibility, keep the option of larger attacks, and avoid the accounting that comes with public authorization. The people below carry the danger and the bill without choosing the policy that put them there.

Eisenhower, in his farewell address of January 17, 1961, paragraph 24, warned: “In the councils of government, we must guard against the acquisition of unwarranted influence, whether sought or unsought, by the military-industrial complex.” His next sentence said only “an alert and knowledgeable citizenry” could make the huge military machinery of defense answer to the country’s peaceful aims. He was describing machinery that can acquire dangerous influence without anybody having to purchase it. Here the machinery is moving. The citizenry has been told there is nothing to watch because the President says the fighting stopped.

Officials still have not said which installations were hit, what damage was done, how many people were killed or wounded, or what conditions would end the campaign. Calling an operation “defensive” states its claimed purpose. It does not answer the questions that purpose should generate. Iran’s Foreign Ministry spokesman Esmail Baghaei said the “ball is in the U.S. court” on any prospective deal, while Supreme Leader Mojtaba Khamenei and other Iranian officials warned of retaliation within and beyond the Middle East if the United States reignited the conflict. That warning is not permission for further American strikes. It is another reason for the American public to know what the orders are and what they are meant to accomplish.

Congress cannot authorize what the executive branch refuses to disclose. If this campaign is to continue, the President should state the installations targeted, the domestic and international legal authority for the strikes, what would count as victory, and the expected harm to civilians, then seek congressional authorization for the continued use of force. Without that disclosure and vote, the strikes should stop.

At Zebedee Auto, a truck with its engine running and its driveline connected is not a parked truck. Monday’s machine was running. CENTCOM had moved defenses and munitions, Gulf governments had been warned, Qeshm had been struck, and sanctions and blockade remained on the President’s desk. The Trump administration has concentrated the decision at the top and spread the danger and the bill across everyone else. The machinery is running. The country is not being told.
