Australia's coal industry ships the coal overseas and calls the smoke someone else's problem. This week Australia's High Court ruled 3-2 that the smoke is part of the project.

The diesel pump at the co-op in Friendship is where this touches my ground. I buy my fuel there. Daniel Yergin wrote the whole arc of it in *The Prize*, and the short version for a man at a bench in Adams County is that the diesel I buy at the co-op in Friendship, Wisconsin is wired by pipeline and tanker to a fight that has been going on since Ibn Saud. That fight just changed maps again. It changed in a courtroom in New South Wales, and it changed the way things change in a county like mine — not by argument, but by who is required to count what.

The court ruled on the Mount Pleasant coalmine at Muswellbrook, in the Hunter Valley. MACH Energy had appealed a 2025 New South Wales Court of Appeal decision that overturned the company's proposed expansion; that lower court had already found the planning commission was required to consider the impacts of all emissions associated with the project on the local environment. The Land and Environment Court had ruled for MACH before that. The High Court dismissed the appeal. The majority found that the NSW Independent Planning Commission was required — and failed — to consider conditions to minimize the mine's scope 3 emissions: the greenhouse gases produced when the exported coal is burned overseas. The company won at the trial level, lost at the appeal, and lost at the top.

The expansion was 21 million tonnes a year, running to 2048, doubling the mine's output — enough to put well over four hundred million tonnes of additional coal on ships, with 98% of the project's projected emissions classified as scope 3.

The commission was required to consider the conditions. It didn't. That failure is now a holding.

The industry's answer to this has been the same sentence for years: the emissions from burning exported coal are someone else's problem, in someone else's sky, in someone else's atmosphere. The scope 3 category was built for that sentence. It takes the true part — the coal is burned somewhere else — and stops the argument exactly where continuing it would implicate the person selling the coal. It is not the same move the tobacco industry ran. A tobacco company's own memo said, "Doubt is our product," and Naomi Oreskes and Erik Conway documented how that architecture got reused — the names are in the documents. The coal exporter's version is doubt about the arithmetic, and the arithmetic is where the money is. Either way, the argument halts at the link where the beneficiary sits.

The court's majority did not weigh the climate merits of coal. It applied the planning statute to the project. That is a narrower holding than a full climate ruling, and it is therefore more durable: it does not depend on the political composition of the bench or the next government's appetite. This was the first time Australia's highest court had been asked to rule on climate change grounds, and the answer came back in the language of planning law. It is not the world's climate negotiator. It is a planning body applying planning law, and it ruled that you cannot greenlight a 21-million-tonne-a-year expansion running through 2048 without putting real conditions on the table for the emissions that happen overseas. The ruling binds the NSW Independent Planning Commission, and it will bind every other planning body asked to weigh a fossil fuel project in the state.

The frame already circulating is that Australia's High Court handed a community group a veto over a coalmine — a precedent that could redraw the rules for fossil fuel projects across the state, gatekeepers now holding a judicial mandate to decide whether a project should exist at all. That frame is wrong, and the way it is wrong is worse for the industry than the frame is good for it. A veto is a decision; this is a requirement to consider. A veto can be reversed by the next government; this is a reading of a planning statute by the highest court in the country. A veto stops a project; this stops a project from being approved on a bill that only counts part of itself. Australia's High Court has gone further than the veto frame allows — it has put the downstream emissions inside the project's scope, at the planning gate. For mining companies, this turns the planning stage into the point where the arithmetic has to be honest, and that is exactly the arithmetic the coal trade has spent years keeping off the table.

Wendy Wales, a retired science teacher and president of the Denman Aberdeen Muswellbrook Scone Healthy Environment Group — Dams Heg — cast the decision in sweeping terms. "Today the high court has said what we have always known: we cannot dig up coal, ship it overseas, watch it drive climate change, and then pretend the consequences have nothing to do with us and won't be felt by us," she said. "The droughts, the bushfires, the floods – are directly connected to greenhouse gases, and massive fossil fuel projects like Mt Pleasant Optimisation simply 'add fuel to the fire'." She called on lawmakers to turn their "full attention" to addressing climate change. She and her partner Tony Longeran pursued this through the Australian courts for more than four years. That sustained pressure, not a single ruling, is what shifted the legal terrain — and it is the model other regional communities can now replicate.

Muswellbrook is not the villain in this story, and the mine is not going to close. The court did not shut the existing operation. The expansion would have anchored jobs, royalties, and local supply chains through 2048, and it lands as another blow on a region whose economy has long been tied to coal — and the people who work in those mines are not the ones who wrote the accounting rules. My county knows the other end of this. Adams County is the county the extractors passed over. The Wisconsin Geological and Natural History Survey will tell you why: our sand is too impure and too angular to be worth hauling. So the frac-sand wave that ran through Trempealeau, Barron and Chippewa stopped at the county line, and the sand barrens out here became a golf resort instead of a mine. That is luck, not virtue. I have driven past that golf course enough times to know exactly what it is: the extractors' arithmetic, run on different geology. Muswellbrook's arithmetic ran on coal that was worth hauling. The bill for burning it went to somebody else's sky, and this week a court ruled that under New South Wales planning law those emissions are part of the project.

The pattern on this side of the Pacific runs the same way. Federal appeals courts have rejected a Trump administration order to keep a Michigan coal plant open, and rejected a Trump EPA bid to abandon a soot pollution rule. On both sides of the Pacific the trajectory now runs through judges rather than legislatures. That should not be mistaken for the climate side of the argument winning, and here is why: courts are not the climate movement's instrument of choice. They are the fossil fuel industry's last viable instrument of expansion, and they are steadily being closed off. One jurisdiction at a time, the permitting question — can this project go forward under existing law? — keeps coming back no.

A government can repeal targets. It can repeal safeguards. It can gut the climate-adjacent machinery of government. The Taylor government in Australia has pledged to roll back the country's climate and energy policies, and that pledge is real politics in a country that runs one of the world's largest coal-export trades. What it cannot do is retroactively rewrite the High Court's reading of what the Independent Planning Commission is required to consider when a fossil fuel project comes before it. The ruling and the political project collide, and the ruling wins on the legal terrain where the industry's expansion plans actually have to be approved. The implication is not national. It is global. New South Wales sits at the heart of one of the world's largest coal-export trades, and the Hunter Valley is the backbone of that trade within Australia. Any Australian exporter that wants to argue — in a capital markets disclosure, a bank ESG policy, or an offtake contract — that its scope 3 emissions are someone else's problem now has an Australian High Court ruling telling it otherwise.

Capital that was still betting on Australian coal expansion as a growth thesis is now allocating to stranded-asset risk. Capital that was waiting for a binding legal signal that the scope 3 dodge is closed in one of the world's largest coal-exporting jurisdictions has just received one. The global coal trade has run out of legal room.

This is what the transition looks like in practice. It is not a slogan and it is not a target. It is a planning tribunal applying a planning statute, and concluding that you cannot ship well over four hundred million tonnes of coal overseas without treating the resulting emissions as part of the project. The Hunter Valley community that fought this case for four years did not win a climate policy. They won a planning ruling. It is more useful than a policy because policies can be repealed. Planning statutes get read against the project, and the project cannot lie about its scope 3 share.

What real rural energy policy looks like from where I stand is the count. Every project asked to show the whole bill before it gets a permit — the coal that gets burned, the smoke that lands in somebody else's sky, the nitrate that comes out of somebody's well on the south side of my county — and a planning body that reads the statute instead of reading the press release. Wendell Berry has spent decades writing about the extractive mind, the habit of treating the land, the animals, and the people as inputs nobody has to account for. The scope 3 dodge is that mind at the invoice level. You do not have to argue with the extractor about whether extraction is happening; you make him write down what it costs. Mike and Quinn will inherit either the count or the bill. I would rather they inherit the count.

Aldo Leopold, who farmed a sand county next to mine, wrote in "Thinking Like a Mountain" that conservation is a state of harmony between men and land. You cannot have harmony with a bill you never received. The energy transition does not need every country to legislate the phase-out. It needs one major exporting jurisdiction to make the expansion math not work, and then for every bank, insurer, and offtaker to read the math correctly. The High Court has just done the math, in a 3-2 split, with the majority opinion written in the language of planning law. It will outlast the political weather. It will outlast the repealers. The scope 3 escape hatch is closed.
