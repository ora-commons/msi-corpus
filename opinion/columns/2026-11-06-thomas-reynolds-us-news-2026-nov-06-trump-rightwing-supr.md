Clarence Thomas and Samuel Alito are holding two Supreme Court seats for Donald Trump. Thomas, 78, the oldest justice on the Court, has announced no plan to leave. Alito, 76, denied the retirement rumors himself, and he delivered the denial to Fox News on Thursday — two days after an election took both chambers of Congress from the coalition that assembled this Court — saying he intends to serve through the remainder of the term regardless. Four justices are in their seventies, and six weeks remain of an outgoing Republican Senate that could, in theory, confirm any replacement the President names before the new Congress is seated. The two seats the retirement watch has settled on are the Court's two oldest. Every operator in Washington read that arithmetic the way the machine taught them to read it: as a portfolio question.

The occasion is Chuck Schumer's pledge. The incoming Senate majority leader told the Associated Press that Democrats will confirm no "crappy nominees" to the Supreme Court, the lower courts, or the cabinet — [a pledge already on the record in these pages](/articles/2026-11-05-schumer-vows-democrats-will-not-confirm-a-right-winger-to-the-supreme-court/) — and that "we're not going to allow a rightwinger on the court." Democrats won 54 Senate seats this month and lead in the two uncalled races that would make it 56; they flipped more than 40 House seats and hold at least 253. Trump has [signaled the vetoes already](/articles/2026-11-04-trump-pairs-conciliatory-remarks-with-veto-hat-as-democrats-face-governing-test/). The confirmation calendar is the one lever Democrats hold that a veto pen cannot reach — a confirmation is a Senate vote, not a bill — and the old majority's remaining window is long enough, under the simple-majority rule the Senate adopted for Supreme Court nominees in 2017, to seat a successor if a seat opens.

The strongest case for everyone involved is real. Nothing in the constitutional text requires a justice to resign because an election went badly; Article III commissions justices "during good Behaviour" — life tenure — and the seat belongs to the justice until death or resignation. A majority leader who declines to act on a president's nominees is exercising ordinary, contested, constitutional craft, and which blockade practice prevails is genuinely unsettled — both parties have run one. A justice who denies published rumors of his own retirement has done nothing the ethics canons govern; the Code of Conduct adopted for the Supreme Court in 2023 carries no enforcement mechanism, and no canon tells a justice where he may give an interview. Ruth Bader Ginsburg declined to step aside in 2016 with a Democratic president positioned to name her successor and said publicly in 2018 that she planned to serve at least five more years; the seat ultimately went to Amy Coney Barrett four years after the first decision. Retirement timing has been political for as long as confirmations have been political. On this account, a justice who stays is simply refusing to treat his seat as a prize in somebody else's game. Every actor this week is working recognizable tools.

The defense holds until the record of how these seats were filled enters. In 2016, a Senate majority held Merrick Garland's nomination without a hearing until it expired. In 2020, the same majority confirmed a justice eight days before a presidential election. Nothing but the calendar separates the two acts, and the calendar belonged to whichever party held the gavel. That sequence — not any opinion, not any ruling — is the moment confirmation at the Supreme Court became pure power, and power made pure gets managed. The majority took its present shape across three vacancies in a single presidency — Neil Gorsuch, Brett Kavanaugh, and Amy Coney Barrett — each seated through a selection operation the scholarship on the movement that built it has documented for years: movement vetting, a coordinated pipeline, and a litigation apparatus feeding the docket behind it. Amanda Hollis-Brusky's work on the Federalist Society's judicial infrastructure is the standard account. The result is a six-justice supermajority whose record includes batting away many — though not all — of the challenges to the administration's second-term policies. A seat in that arrangement is not a vacancy to be filled. It is an asset position to be held, and its holder is expected to manage it the way a director manages a board seat.
