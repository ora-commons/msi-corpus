The party of Main Street wants power without responsibility, and the latest signature on that bargain is due before Christmas. In Stephen Moore’s Fox News opinion column, [Nine million jobs are in limbo, and Congress can fix it before Christmas](https://www.foxnews.com/opinion/nine-million-jobs-limbo-congress-fix-christmas), Moore argues that about 832,000 franchise small businesses support roughly nine million jobs, but federal joint-employer rules have changed four times since 2015, leaving owners unable to plan; he urges Congress to pass the American Franchise Act, H.R. 5267/S. 3525, which would make a franchisor a joint employer only if it both possesses and exercises substantial, direct and immediate control over wages, hiring, scheduling, and other essential terms of employment, while brand standards, training, and safety qualifications would not, by themselves, suffice. Moore wants certainty for the local employer. What he has mistaken is the location of the payroll for the location of power.

His strongest point is mechanical, and it deserves to be granted before it is dismantled. A man who borrows against his house to sign a ten-year franchise agreement cannot plan his family’s life around a legal definition that changes with every labor board. Moore’s chronology begins with the Obama labor board’s 2015 Browning-Ferris decision, which he says allowed liability based on indirect or even unused control; a 2020 rule restored a narrower standard; and a 2023 Biden-board rule went further, declaring that merely reserving the right to control was enough, before a federal court struck it down. Another case could move the line again within months. “No small-business owner can plan a five-year expansion around a rule that changes every four.” Four reversals in ten years is four too many. Uncertainty is a tax the powerful do not pay and the small holder always does.

The mechanical point is sound. If a training program, an HR template, or a workplace-safety manual can become evidence that headquarters is the employer, the chain can respond by withdrawing that support and leaving the local owner alone. Moore cites an International Franchise Association and U.S. Chamber of Commerce study estimating that the expanded standard costs the economy $33.3 billion a year, produces as many as 376,000 fewer job opportunities, and has driven a 93 percent increase in lawsuits against franchise businesses. Ninety-two percent of the brands and owners surveyed said the rule had led them to provide less support. A small employer should not be handed years of litigation for asking a national office for a safety manual.

The rest of Moore’s case is concrete, too. His paper says one in eight American businesses with at least one employee is a franchise, nearly two-thirds of local franchise owners are first-time business owners, and franchising is projected to create more than 150,000 new jobs this year. The American Franchise Act was introduced by seven Republicans and seven Democrats, has 158 House cosponsors, cleared the House Education and Workforce Committee in July, and has White House backing. Rep. Kevin Hern, who owned and operated McDonald’s restaurants before entering Congress, understands the payroll from the inside. Moore says the bill costs the Treasury no dime and helps owners keep prices down. Franchise owners have already spent ten years in limbo. Those facts establish scale, uncertainty, and a constituency. They do not establish which institution deserves the legal liability.

His error is larger: he mistakes a local payroll for local control.

The local franchisee may put savings on the line, sign the lease, make the payroll, and live in the town served by the business. Moore counts that arrangement as local ownership because the money and workers are local. But his own account says the national company can withdraw the training, compliance help, and brand support that make the arrangement possible. A business that must ask headquarters whether it will receive help is not sovereign. It is a branch office with a local sign.

It is tenancy wearing the paperwork of ownership. In many franchise arrangements, the local operator does not own the name but pays a royalty for permission to use it; supplies, prices, scheduling software, performance targets, and renewal terms may all be imposed from above. Moore’s bill can tell us who signs the payroll. It cannot tell us who governs the work. If the chain sets the conditions and the local operator bears the liability, local control becomes little more than liability allocation.

The franchise owners may be honest and capable, and they have built businesses in towns where the rentier would not bother to stop. But Chesterton’s warning was not too much capitalism but too few capitalists. Moore counts 832,000 local operators, while the control of the brand and the system above them remains concentrated among far fewer owners. Belloc warned of security purchased by surrendering independence. Moore’s bill may give the franchisee legal certainty while fastening his dependence more securely.

I traded agricultural futures, and the rule at the desk was simple: the party that held the position answered for the outcome. You could write the terms of the contract; you could not then declare yourself a bystander to its consequences. No clearinghouse on earth accepted, “I set the strike; the loss belongs to the other fellow.” That was not red tape. It was the difference between a market and a rigged one.

Franchising applies that separation to labor. A contract can name the crop while the party that owns the land, does the work, and bears the weather has little voice in the price. A franchise can name the employer while the national brand owns the system under which the work must be done. We should not mistake a claim on power for power itself.

The distinction between possessing a right and exercising it matters. A reserved right is not automatically the exercise of control, and no serious argument should pretend otherwise. But control can arrive wearing exempted uniforms. The brand does not hire anyone; the standard hires. The brand does not schedule anyone; the software schedules. The brand does not touch the timesheet; the template signs it. Governing by standard does not rise to governing by hand, and Moore’s proposed statute is written to keep the two formally separate.

At the counter, the local owner may know every worker by name while the clock-in rules, training manual, and legal machinery arrive from headquarters whose executives the owner cannot vote out. Private franchise rights are legitimate, but no property claim gives a chain the right to use a local worker as a shield against decisions made above that worker’s head. Human dignity stands above the franchise agreement. Duty must follow control. When a national brand exercises substantial authority over a person’s employment, it cannot hide behind the narrower legal identity of the person who signs the payroll.

The conservative tradition has a word for where authority belongs. Pius XI called it “a grave evil” to assign to a higher association what a lesser one can do; subsidiarity means that power and responsibility remain close enough for the people who bear the consequences to have a voice in them. Moore’s bill arranges the mirror image: the higher association keeps whatever authority it can hold without triggering the test, while the lesser association keeps the answering.

That is why I distrust Moore’s Main Street when he makes it a metaphor. I would invite him to bring his certainty argument to my county sometime. Adams was built as a Chicago & North Western division point; dieselization, crew cuts, and the abandonment of sections thinned the work. The last *Twin Cities 400* ran there on July 23, 1963. Union Pacific absorbed the C&NW in 1995. The regional paper-mill work came apart too: the Wisconsin Rapids mill, which had been operating since 1904, idled in 2020, taking roughly a thousand jobs with it. Wisconsin’s dairy farms fell from 16,886 in 2002 to 6,216 in 2022, taking farm families, schoolchildren, and names from church councils and school boards with them. The Catholic parish in my own county began in 1884. That history does not settle the franchise question. It settles the word.

Local means power near the work, not merely a payroll in the county.

The evidence behind Moore’s case also comes from the interested parties pressing for franchise relief. The International Franchise Association and the Chamber of Commerce may possess the best available account of regulatory uncertainty, but their figures establish the cost of uncertainty, not the moral correctness of permanent insulation for the chains. The old conservative warning is not to surrender the common good to the loudest lobby merely because the lobby furnished the study to match its brief.

The clock matters less than Moore says. Deadlines are useful for votes. Permanent rules are built from habits, enforcement, and the interests that survive after the hearing room empties. If the rule makes the national chain safer whenever the local owner can be named employer, it removes the volatility Moore fears while leaving the underlying dependence untouched.

“Clarity is the cheapest pro-growth policy Congress can pass,” Moore writes. I would ask whose whiplash is being clarified. His proposal does not restore neutrality; it fixes the pendulum at the end favorable to franchisors. Clarity about who signs the payroll does not create independence at the counter.

Nor is the answer another distant bureaucracy tracing every shift, schedule, and training decision in every outlet. A labor board must be able to police a national corporation; no remote office, however well intentioned, can know the practical bargain made in a franchise restaurant at closing time. Concentrated capital and concentrated state power are the same disease in different coats. We want power closest to the work, under institutions capable of correction.

Where an outlet can be bought locally, an employee ownership trust can put some of the residual claim and the vote with the people who work there; a one-member, one-vote cooperative can go further. The franchisor may retain the brand and genuine safety standards. It may not retain control over the conditions of work while sheltering behind the legal identity of the person who signs the payroll.

Where a national chain will not permit local ownership, workers and franchise owners need counterpower rather than separate liability pockets: collective bargaining, joint negotiation over the schedules and rules the brand imposes, and sectoral institutions where one outlet is too small to stand alone. A local credit union can keep finance within the community. Purchasing cooperatives can keep more of the money spent on the ordinary necessities of Main Street in Main Street.

A retail cooperative is the franchise’s honest cousin, but with the ownership reversed. The independent hardwareman or grocer who cannot out-buy the chain joins with other owners, obtains purchasing scale, training, and a national brand, and receives a vote. In the franchise, the brand owns the name and rents it to you. In the cooperative, the owners own the brand.

The cooperative answer is not sentiment. The Rochdale Pioneers wrote one member, one vote in 1844. The Capper-Volstead Act of 1922 gave farmers limited legal room to bargain together against concentrated buyers without treating their cooperation as a cartel. The Adams-Columbia Electric Cooperative, headquartered up the street from me in Friendship, is the largest rural electric cooperative in Wisconsin, serving about 31,560 member-owners across twelve counties under a member-elected board. Land O’Lakes has carried the same logic since 1921. Organic Valley, in La Farge, joins more than 1,600 organic family farms. Credit unions run it on money; mutuals run it on risk.

The co-op I manage is harder here because it cannot promise a farmer a price the market has not paid, because members can disagree about capital and margins, and because nobody distant can absorb a bad year for them. Yet because the members own the institution and elect its board, there is no absentee shareholder behind whom responsibility can hide.

It asks for citizenship—the annual meeting, the board seat, the vote, the patience—where the franchise sells the costume of ownership without its duties. Co-ops decay too, when members stop coming. But a co-op’s decay shows up at the meeting, where it can be voted upon; a franchise’s decay is nobody’s to vote on at all.

The answer is not a permanent shield for the chain. It is power made answerable to the people whose lives the chain governs: the worker cooperative, the employee trust, the local credit union, the purchasing cooperative, the independent Main Street store, the parish whose bell calls people who still have reasons to answer one another. At the counter of a business that owns itself, in a town where the stores that are left belong to people who will be buried in the churchyard up the hill, leave the town its life. That was the whole of the promise, and it is the only part I am still waiting on.
