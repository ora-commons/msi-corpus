Cuba's grid isn't dying of an embargo. It is dying because the money that should have gone to the wires went to the hotels — and no embargo wrote that budget. Mary Anastasia O'Grady makes most of this case herself in the Wall Street Journal, in [Why the Lights Go Dark in Cuba](https://www.wsj.com/opinion/why-the-lights-go-dark-in-cuba-bee46dce?mod=rss_opinion). The blackouts, she argues, are the death rattle of a utility no management can save: the thermoelectric plants that supply 60 percent of Cuba's generating capacity burn Cuban crude no American sanction can touch, Venezuela's oil patronage has run out, and from 2020 through 2024 the government spent more than three times as much on luxury hotels, restaurants and real estate as it did on electric, water and gas. Her prescription is to keep tightening sanctions until the regime falls, then invite the investors in. The diagnosis is beyond argument. The prescription has a billing problem, and the diagnosis stops one door short of the room where the answer lives.

Let me concede everything worth conceding first, quickly, because pretending otherwise is how people who should know better embarrass themselves on this subject. The system the Castros built — one party, one newspaper, one employer, for the better part of seven decades — is a catastrophe. That verdict is in, and I'm not relitigating it. Her numbers hold. Fuel shortages reach only about 13 percent of generating capacity, and that is because a third of the diesel plants are already inoperable — upkeep nobody paid for, money that never came. A grid that dies before the fuel even arrives is not an embargo statistic. It's a budget. More than 800 political prisoners are rotting in Cuban dungeons, and the goon squads always have gasoline. I have no argument with any of it, and none whatsoever for anyone still romanticizing that island.

But she stops one line short of her own ledger. Money does not evaporate. A country that spends three to one on hotels over power has not failed to plan — it has planned. Somebody chose the resort. Somebody chose the lobby. Somebody let the diesel plants sit until a third of them rusted. Even the fuel that gets past the embargo gets captured: it ships to the "private" importers Washington's exemptions allow, and the investigation her own column cites found shipping records showing it redirected to regime companies. That is not a maintenance problem. That is a decision with a beneficiary, and the beneficiary is not the person in the dark.

Now watch the pivot midway through her own piece. For three paragraphs the sanctions are too leaky to darken a bulb — exemptions Havana games, fuel slipping through to front companies. Then the same sanctions become "part of its strategy to starve the beast." Both claims cannot stand. If sanctions can't light a bulb, they can't starve a government that fuels its police and lights its tourist quarter. A sanction that binds only where ordinary Cubans live — the diesel that moves their food, the fuel that runs the small workshops the regime hasn't swallowed — is not pressure on the leadership. It is pressure on the leadership's hostages. The regime sets the rationing order: security first, hotels second, kitchens last. Every pinch passes down until it lands where it always lands — the Havana neighborhood where the blogger Yoani Sánchez reported the power out for forty hours straight. Misrule broke that grid. The starvation plan lengthens the dark. A strategy is either pressure on the palace or pressure on the people. This piece's own receipts say which. Say plainly who eats the cost, or retire the strategy.

There is also the passage where the coming images of dying babies in blacked-out hospitals get filed under regime propaganda, pending broadcast. I usually find this writer's genre entertaining. Not there. The babies will be real. So will the hospital. A strategy that starves a government through its citizens has no mechanism for skipping the citizens, and the leadership has spent seven decades demonstrating that it does not skip its own meals. You cannot ration darkness as leverage and then object when the dark gets photographed. The woman in that apartment is not a camera angle. She is the customer. She has no bill to dispute, no regulator to call, no union to join, and no vote. If the column's whole point is that she deserves better management, she deserves better than being used as a rebuttal.

So what is the offer? Investor certainty. New rules. Change at the top. Walk me through it slowly: which part of that decides who gets the capital? Rules are written by owners, and a family that can redirect fuel to its own patrols can rewrite an investment law the morning after it signs one. Take her phrase literally. Certainty that the beachfront is for sale. Certainty that the labor is cheap and frightened. Certainty that the hotels the regime built with the boiler money change hands on favorable terms — possibly to the same sort of owners, in better suits.

Her Soviet analogy — party insiders getting rich as the experiment disintegrated — is the best sentence in the column, and it argues against her. The insiders don't disappear in that ending. They get partners. The people who sat through the blackouts get jobs at the hotels. That is not what reform looks like when it fails. That is what reform looks like when ownership hasn't changed. "Money, Money, Money" is a catchy song. "It's a rich man's world" is a lousy constitution for a rebuilding country. Anyway.

Now take the same windfall and change the owners. Norway struck oil and put the money into a fund worth more than two trillion dollars that belongs to the future. Texas struck roughly the same commodity and produced a boom, a bust, and a bumper sticker. Cuba got Venezuelan oil and got resorts. Three windfalls, three ownership decisions, three ledgers.

America has answered this question at least three times without anyone calling it radical. My grandfather helped run a rural electric cooperative in western Minnesota. In the 1930s the investor-owned utilities wouldn't string wire out to his county — not enough customers per mile to please the shareholders — so the county strung its own, under the New Deal's rural electrification program, as a utility owned by the people it lit. The math didn't close, so the people who needed the power bought it themselves. About 900 of those cooperatives are still running. They serve 42 million Americans across 56 percent of the country's landmass. You probably cash your check at a credit union: 145 million members, same idea. North Dakota has run a state-owned bank profitably since 1919, and nobody ever called Bismarck the Kremlin. Those aren't utopias. They're plumbing, and some of the pipes leak.

Not the gulag. Not the strip-mine. The third item on the menu. We already do this. We just won't say the word.

Here is the hard part, and it applies to us as much as to Havana: every one of those works because the members can fire the manager. They can organize, complain, audit, vote, leave. That right is exactly what a one-party state forbids. A co-op is neighbors meeting, voting and firing their own board without fear — and that takes the civil space the Castros spent seven decades demolishing. Which is why the prisoners are not a sidebar to the economic story. They are the reason there is no ownership answer in Cuba yet. You cannot hand the people in the dark a share of the grid while jail is the price of asking for one.

Ownership can't be airlifted in with the investment bankers, and it can't be sanctioned into existence from Washington. It gets built by Cubans, slowly, one working institution at a time, starting wherever two people can still meet without an informant taking notes. Build that first. Then the turbines.

Washington cannot light Cuba. Havana will not. The people in the dark will — the day the wires belong to them. The economy is a set of choices, not the weather. Havana chose the hotels and got the blackout. Sanctions chose the timing and got their leverage. Both are choices — and only one of them gets changed by people who are allowed to speak. Someday soon somebody on that island will choose the other way, and the only question worth asking about that day is whose name goes on the deeds. It should be the names of the people who sat through the dark.
