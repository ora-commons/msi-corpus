Oregon Housing and Community Services hid affordable-housing costs from the public for 30 years.

The 1997 exemption was not an accidental loophole. OHCS persuaded lawmakers to create it, and the agency has administered the arrangement ever since. The result is a black box around the public money that builds affordable homes: taxpayers can see the aggregate spending, but not the project-level financials that explain what each apartment costs, who was paid, and where the money went.

Now the black box is finally under review.

A reporting series this summer showed that Oregon has awarded roughly $1.4 billion in affordable-housing subsidies since 2021, while the cost of developing a low-income apartment has climbed to about $540,000. Another $850 million in projects is waiting in the pipeline. The reporting described the per-unit cost as nearly doubled.

Those numbers are not themselves protected by the exemption. The public can see the totals. What it cannot see are the project-by-project breakdowns: acquisition prices, construction costs, contractor markups, developer fees, financing charges, and other line items that would show how a $540,000 apartment becomes a $540,000 apartment.

That is not transparency. It is a public investment with the receipts withheld.

Rep. Pam Marsh, chair of the House Committee on Housing and Homelessness, said she “pretty much immediately” looked up the exemption after reading the coverage and now plans to introduce repeal legislation. State Sen. Khanh Pham, chair of the Senate Committee on Housing and Development, wants the Legislature to address it in 2027. Oregon’s Sunshine Committee, which reviews public-records exemptions, is examining the housing-financials carve-out at the request of its co-chair, Charlie Fisher.

Three decades of secrecy ended because someone finally asked the obvious question.

The old defense is that disclosure would harm the industry’s ability to negotiate. Project-level costs, the argument goes, are competitively sensitive. A developer’s bids could be used by competitors to undercut future projects. Line-item disclosure could mean longer timelines, higher contingency reserves, and fewer bidders willing to compete for federal Low-Income Housing Tax Credits, state bonds, or local inclusionary-zoning opportunities.

That concern is real. It is also no longer enough.

Officials in Washington and California, where state spending on subsidized housing is public, say transparency has not interfered with low-income housing production. Their experience does not prove that every line item should be posted without redaction. It does prove that “the industry cannot operate under public scrutiny” is not an economic law.

And Housing Oregon has now helped destroy the case for secrecy from inside the industry.

The trade group represents low-income housing developers, lenders, and related businesses. Its spokesperson, Kevin Cronin, said the questions raised by the reporting “deserve serious attention” and endorsed repeal. He framed repeal as serving “the public’s interest in ensuring that public investments in housing are used effectively, responsibly, and with visibility.” He also conceded that any new transparency regime must “still protect genuinely proprietary or competitively sensitive information.”

That last sentence is the whole policy argument. Protect genuinely proprietary information. Stop protecting an entire financial system from public examination.

When the industry’s registered lobbying group stops defending its own shield, the shield is not a shield anymore. It is institutional habit.

OHCS has declined to take a position on repeal, citing the standard practice that state agencies do not weigh in on pending bills. Its executive director, Andrea Bell, said in July that she is “committed to transparency” while “following the law as it stands today.” A spokesperson said the agency appreciates the opportunity to review legislation introduced in 2027.

That is the polite shape of an institution protecting its own past.

The political trigger matters because it reveals how little scrutiny the exemption had received. Marsh and Pham moved after the reporting. The Sunshine Committee began examining the exemption after the reporting. None of them has identified a specific abuse that the exemption prevented, a specific harm disclosure caused, or a specific dollar amount that would be lost under transparency. The state maintained the black box for decades, then treated the first serious public inspection as an emergency.

This is the “study shows” version of governance: the report arrives, the headline supplies the crisis, and officials rush to legislate before anyone has built the record.

But the answer cannot be to preserve secrecy simply because repeal began reactively. The answer is to repeal the exemption and replace it with a disclosure system that is actually designed for public accountability.

Marsh has said she wants project financials posted online as part of the public materials reviewed before the Housing Stability Council awards subsidies. That is a clean design: the same documents, at the same decision point, on the public side of the table. Redactions can protect genuinely proprietary bids and personal information. Aggregated data can protect competition without protecting unexplained costs. Public registries can show what is being built, what it costs, which affordability restrictions are expiring, and where the next dollar should go.

Pennsylvania has just [stood up a database to track expiring affordable-housing units](/articles/2026-08-26-pennsylvania-creates-database-to-track-expiring-affordable-housing-units/). That is what governing with information looks like: not dumping every private line item onto the internet, but building a public record capable of tracking public assets over time.

Oregon should study that model instead of defending a 1997 secrecy regime. It should also notice the direction of travel elsewhere. In federal environmental policy, [the EPA is moving to make air-pollution disclosure voluntary for datacenter permits](/articles/2026-08-25-epa-proposes-making-air-pollution-disclosure-voluntary-for-datacenter-permissions/). Different subject, same instinct: turn a public obligation into a private discretion, then call the absence of information a technical necessity.

The cost of an apartment is not just an accounting number. It determines how many apartments can be built, how many families remain on waiting lists, and whether a public subsidy creates a home or merely passes through an opaque development process. The kitchen-table version of $540,000 is not an abstract scandal. It is the difference between a family getting a place to live and another year of moving the same numbers between the same two columns.

Trust is not a feeling. It is a public document.

Oregon should repeal the exemption, disclose the financials, protect only the information that is genuinely proprietary, and build a registry that lets the public follow the money after the ribbon-cutting. The state spent three decades making affordable-housing costs invisible. It does not get to call visibility the threat.
