The multi-use hall at the University of Maryland Baltimore County was quiet on a fall-break Friday afternoon — the quiet that settles over a commuter campus with nobody on it — and then it wasn't. Muslim students roll out prayer mats. Catholic students run a rosary service and worship music. Lisa Huberman, social action engagement associate at UMBC Hillel, walks in as the last of the Friday prayers concludes. "It's very much like a delicate dance," she said. "Even just basic functions of existing, even in a mundane way, we're always negotiating. But it also means there is an opportunity to encounter difference in close proximity at UMBC."

That negotiation is the education. Everything else is decoration.

The decision behind it belongs to the university, and Matt Hoffman, director of UMBC's Center for Religion, Spirituality and Pluralism, states it plainly: the university has no formal chapel. What UMBC built instead is one multi-use hall, run through the Center, and an arrangement in which religious communities hand the room to one another inside a single evening — mats and gender dividers down for Muslim prayer, struck for the Catholic rosary, struck again for Shabbat dinner and prayer services. Nobody gets a permanent address. Each community's access to its own worship space is a scheduling question, answered by whoever administers the hall that week, and the students pay for it every Friday in setup and strike: a sanctuary packed into a backpack, laid out, prayed over, and cleared before the next group walks in. That cost does not land evenly. It falls on the students whose belonging on campus is already conditional — the commuters, and the ones who have the most to lose by being seen.

Scarce space is a teacher that abundant space never manages to be. Build a dozen chapels and you can schedule coexistence out of the week entirely — each community worshipped on its own acre, congratulating itself on tolerance from a comfortable distance. UMBC's shortage forecloses that option, and the constraint turns out to be worth more than the facility would have been. Proximity was handed to these students as a condition rather than offered to them as an elective.

The negotiation is not hypothetical. Faculty describe UMBC as one of the country's "most religiously diverse campuses," with a student culture spanning multiple branches of Christianity and Islam alongside Jewish, Sikh and Dharmic groups, and student groups that have recently hosted a Muslim women's fellowship, a Sikh festival, a queer Orthodox Jewish gathering and a South Asian Christian organization. Hoffman's question for the whole apparatus is short: "What do we do with the diversity?"

Picturing Pluralism is one answer. A semester-long project pairing personal photography with structured dialogue across religious, spiritual and cultural differences, it brought about 30 students and 10 faculty and staff to a two-day retreat over fall break, where tables of five and six showed each other pictures of their own lives and listened for four minutes at a stretch before speaking, responding to prompts about belief, belonging and the barriers to belonging on campus. The result becomes a public exhibition opening Dec. 2 at UMBC's Albin O. Kuhn Library and Gallery.

Which is why the real content of the retreat was never really the photographs. Keven Guruswamy, a senior psychology major and Seventh-day Adventist raised between Hindus and Christians, brought a shelf of Bibles — some in Tamil, some in English, some purchased, some gifted, including one from his late grandmother. Onkarpreet Kaur, a second-year microbiology student, showed the kara she has worn for six years, the circular steel bracelet that is one of the five articles of faith in Sikhi: a circle standing for continuity, but also for the distance of diaspora from Sikhi's historic center in Punjab, India. She kept it partly because she didn't know where to buy another near campus. "It just reminds me of my values," she said. "It helps me get to the right path." Fatima Hafeez, a second-year political science student, showed St. Peter's Basilica and heard her own Hajj and Umrah in the devotion of Catholic pilgrims she admired. "It's really easy to group people together," she said. Listening to personal stories, she added, is a reminder that "everyone is a human with their own individual experience." A Palestinian-American student showed a self-portrait and said she had spent years feeling too Palestinian to be American and too American to be Palestinian. Velma Funebe, a senior public health major who sometimes posts written reflections about religion on LinkedIn, wondered whether a photograph could say more: "I think people can take so much from just a picture." And Duncan Winger, agnostic, raised in an atheist family, heard that someone on his daily campus walk was praying and came away saying it "opens up empathy" — and that it freed him from feeling he had to master a faith before engaging it: "I can still ask and be curious."

None of that is tolerance. Tolerance is the word you use when you have arranged never to be inconvenienced by the person you're tolerating. What Guruswamy described — the move from "cultural competence" to "cultural humility," the chance to "be humble with one another" — is something older and less flattering: the admission that you will not get to master another person's faith as a subject, that you can stay curious without ever completing your understanding. Competence promises you a certificate. Humility only promises you a seat at the table, and only if you show up while the room is still being negotiated.

The timing matters, because the room was being negotiated under pressure. Days before the retreat, students held vigils both to remember the victims of the Oct. 7, 2023 Hamas attack on Israel and to protest the ensuing war in Gaza. Organizers said they were sensitive to those tensions and braced for possible conflict rather than importing a pressure-free zone from a better decade. And as the Trump administration's mass deportation agenda swept across American cities, some students who were not born in the United States were cautious about being interviewed or photographed at all; some images and explanations touched on immigration status and fear. That chill traces to federal enforcement policy, not to anything UMBC did to those students — the university was the institution asking them to be visible. A project about visibility, in other words, had participants who reasonably did not want to be visible, which is a second sentence written in the margin of every claim a campus makes about belonging.

On the second day, the personal stories became a collective one. Participants chose the photographs they considered essential, asked what was missing, and wrote the captions together. Abdul Samad Amjad, a Photovoice facilitator and scholar of community-based participatory research, born in Pakistan and living in Ohio, said the method gives participants a role in defining the problems and the possible solutions. "There are different stories being told," he said. "But at the end, what I'd realize, there is always this story of us, that collective story. Our shared humanity gets represented there."

The facilitators were careful not to oversell it. Joshua Polanski, Interfaith Photovoice's director of engagement, said the model has reached multicultural communities from Michigan to Oman, Pakistan and Texas. Roman R. Williams, the organization's founder and a visual sociologist, is right to resist the overclaim: the goal is to take someone "maybe even barely an acquaintance" and shift that relationship into shared knowledge, and photographs cannot resolve every division people carry into a room. Building relationships takes longer than a weekend, and nobody should treat a December opening as proof that photography dissolves polarization. "It's a theory of change, but we see it happen," he said. "Sometimes in very small ways." Small is fine. The same instinct runs through a [traveling show on Muslim giving that opened in Minneapolis this spring to challenge stereotypes](/articles/2026-04-08-traveling-exhibit-opens-in-minneapolis-to-challenge-stereotypes-about-muslim-giv/) — objects and captions doing the work of exhortation — and through Picturing Pluralism both: pictures move people where position papers do not, and a shortage of square footage plus a rule that you listen for four minutes before you speak is a curriculum whether anybody designed it or not. Institutions keep buying the opposite arrangement — new chapels, funded dialogue series, awareness weeks, guest speakers — and then wonder why proximity never arrives.

Onkarpreet Kaur arrived at UMBC unsure how people with different beliefs could live together, and some of those people are now among her closest friends. "We are living in the same space with different beliefs," she said. "We coexist but we are not blending with each other. We have our own identity, and it is so beautiful." That is the chapel they never built — not in stone, but in the habit of making room, one Friday at a time, for people who were never going to be given a room of their own.
