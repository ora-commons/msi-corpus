Trump and Musk killed 700,000 people and called it efficiency.

That is the count Senator Chris Van Hollen is carrying into his challenge to Chuck Schumer for Democratic leader, and it is the count the State Department met with an accusation of manipulation. Credible analyses, the kind NPR's own reporting points to, put more than 700,000 people dead worldwide between January 2025 and January 2026 from the cutting of USAID, and Van Hollen put the figure on the record this week with the projection beside it: if the trend holds, the coming years run to millions. A clinic that closes is a vaccination that never happens. A surveillance system that stops is an outbreak with a head start. That is the arithmetic of what was done, and the arithmetic is not in dispute except by the people who did it. The department's statement, when it finally came, called those studies "rooted in outdated thinking, faulty research and manipulation." On one side, a count of the dead. On the other, an accusation that the counting was manipulated. Nothing in between.

The scene where it got done is worth setting in full. February 2025, the White House: Trump at his desk, Musk standing behind him, a reporter asking what the cuts to the Ebola program meant, and Musk's answer amounting to "Whoops, yeah, we missed that." He said it had been restored. It was not restored adequately, and there is good evidence the Ebola outbreak now running in the Democratic Republic of Congo could have been better contained if the United States had not taken a chainsaw to public health. When a customer pays up front for a job, I owe him the job. Congress paid for the clinics and the vaccination campaigns and the outbreak watchers, and the money was taken and the work was not done. Deaths anybody could have foreseen from reading the budget got booked as a rounding error.

Here is the machinery. Over $500 million for global health was rescinded, and the majority that held Congress at the time waved it through. Roughly $1.5 billion appropriated years ago for global public health is now, on Van Hollen's reading, being spent not on public health but on winding USAID down. What Congress writes into an appropriations bill to tell the executive branch what the money is for is called report language. It is guidance, not statute, and this administration treats it as a suggestion and skips it. Van Hollen's explanation for why is the plainest line in the interview: the more they let them get away with it, the more they do it.

There is a second thread in the record. Van Hollen has authored statements condemning the tying of health aid to mineral deals, which is what it sounds like: public-health money held out for access to somebody else's resources. That does not pencil out as aid. It pencils out as a bill of sale, with the sick carrying the cost.

He does not want the old USAID back. Nothing the Trump administration did, he says, is worth preserving, and he is not arguing for a return to the way the agency was. He wants a plan for how the country performs that role, written into law. He already sits as ranking member on the Senate Foreign Relations subcommittee that oversees the State Department and what is left of USAID, and he led the protests outside the Capitol when the agency was dissolved. He has shown the posture already. One of the great benefits of the job, he says, is that you actually have a gavel and can call a public hearing. Priority one is an analysis of all the harm, run in the open with the count on the record. He told Secretary of State Marco Rubio at a hearing last year what the cuts were: "People died because of those actions, mothers, fathers and children." He has been plainer since. This has been a lawless president, and he is still in the White House.

Rubio's department answered NPR in writing. The answer leads with the claim that the administration has led "critical reforms to make U.S. foreign assistance more efficient, more accountable and aligned with U.S. national interests," and that claim sits against a foreign record this publication has been tracking, most recently in a [risk roundup led by Witkoff's remarks about Putin](/articles/2026-10-22-php-security-issue-and-witkoff-s-putin-remarks-lead-a-risk-roundup/). It says every dollar was obligated before September 30. Obligated, in the budget sense, means legally committed on paper, and a purchase order is not a vaccination. It says the United States answered the Ebola outbreak faster than any prior outbreak and remains its largest contributor, with more than $780 million in direct assistance and $350 million more in humanitarian aid; a fast response to an outbreak its own cuts slowed is the receipt for the delay, not a defense of it. NPR asked for a response before deadline and got none in time. The statement arrived later, and on the 700,000 it calls the studies counting the dead manipulation. Calling the count manipulation is not a rebuttal of the count. It is what a man says when the ledger is accurate and he has no answer for it.

The constitutional posture underneath all of it is simple enough for a shop floor. Article I gives Congress the purse, and money appropriated by law is not an allowance the executive may hold back, redirect, or spend against its written purpose. What the posture requires is what Van Hollen proposes: spending directions written into binding statute instead of guidance, enforcement in the courts when an administration diverts the funds, and oversight gavels actually used. A republic that lets its executive treat appropriations as decoration has already decided the Constitution's oldest check on war and waste does not apply, and the ledger shows what that decision costs.

Seven hundred thousand people. Mothers, fathers, children, in the words the senator put to the Secretary of State. The man who swung the chainsaw answered whoops. The department holding the wreckage calls the count manipulation. Congress is writing its next appropriations, and the question that matters is whether the next line item is a law or a suggestion.
