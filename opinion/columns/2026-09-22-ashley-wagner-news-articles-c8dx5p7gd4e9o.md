The government replaced the safety net with your parents' bank account. Universal Credit pays £360 a month. Rent costs £460. That is the arithmetic of the British welfare state for a 23-year-old carer in Sunderland — a hundred pounds a month short of shelter before she eats. Spiral that for twelve months and you get £4,000 in arrears, which is exactly what another 23-year-old in Cambridgeshire accumulated when she lost her job and her parents could not help. In American terms, a young person on SNAP gets $234 a month against average metro rent of $1,400. The currency changes. The system does not. Three women profiled by the BBC this week are evidence that the replacement is complete — and the majority of test-takers were never given the materials.

Eleanor Bell is 23. First-class degree from the University of Central Lancashire. Applied for more jobs than she can count. Taken into care at 10 — her family did not survive into her adulthood in any form that could write a cheque. She lives with her boyfriend outside Penistone because that is what is available when there is no parental home to fall back on. The UK government's own figures say 39% of care leavers aged 19 to 21 are not in education, employment or training, against 13% of young people overall. The American version is worse: roughly 20% of youth who age out of foster care become homeless within three years, and only 6% earn a college degree by 26. Eleanor has the degree. She does not have the parent. She is in the 61%, and she got there alone. That is not a buoyancy-aid deficit. That is a credential.

Sam Barrett is 24, in Sunderland. She quit full-time work to care for her mum and studies health and social care part-time. Carers Allowance and Universal Credit do not cover her main expenses. "The government aren't doing anything to help you, they're basically saying just get into debt," she told the BBC. The American translation: there is no federal Carers Allowance. A young American caring full-time for an adult parent receives nothing from the federal government for doing so — the backstop is Medicaid if the care recipient qualifies, and nothing else. Sam is angry at the right target. That is citizenship, not victimhood.

Ezgi Polat is 23, from Ramsey in Cambridgeshire. Lost her job, parents could not help, fell £4,000 into arrears — £360 in Universal Credit against £460 in rent, with her local bus service cancelled and her benefits cut for missing the job centre she could no longer reach. The council eventually wiped her rent arrears. She negotiated repayment plans with her utility company. She is now working in a warehouse. In the United States, a young woman in the same position applies for emergency rental assistance — when funds exist, which is not guaranteed — and joins a Section 8 waitlist that stretches five years in Philadelphia and longer in most metro areas. Ezgi's safety valves fired. Most American equivalents would not.

The divide the BBC identifies is real, but the framing is wrong. This is not a divide between young people who are struggling and young people who are fine. It is a divide between young people who are forced to be competent and young people who are enabled to be dependent. The cushioned spend their twenties treating parental support as a baseline and discover arrears the moment the transfer pauses. The un-cushioned spend their twenties learning what arrears look like and how to negotiate them. The inheritance ladder is treated as normal. The absence of it is treated as a problem. The absence of it is the better education.

The TikTok inheritance joke — "How did you afford your house?" "My grandmother died" — is not a joke. It is a documentary record of the same arithmetic. Every millennial who posts it is saying what these three women already know without needing a meme: the transfer is the system. Swift named it — "You're On Your Own, Kid" — and that is not a lyric. It is the government's actual policy.

The government has put £2.5 billion into apprenticeships, job grants and training and passed the Renters' Rights Act to give tenants more protection from excessive rent increases. Luke Young of Citizens Advice says the divide between the supported and unsupported is widening. He is right. The divide is widening because the supported are being supported into a market they cannot afford without support. The Milburn apprenticeship review — [Milburn studies Dublin apprenticeships as UK youth Neet rate climbs](/articles/2026-08-07-milburn-studies-dublin-apprenticeships-as-uk-youth-neet-rate-climbs/) — makes the same point from the supply side: an apprenticeship route is the most credible counter to a 39% care-leaver NEET figure, and the £2.5bn is the test of whether ministers mean it. On the demand side, the renter-owner wealth gap is at its highest since 1989 — [Renter-owner wealth gap at highest level since 1989, analysis finds](/articles/2026-07-30-renter-owner-wealth-gap-at-highest-level-since-1989-analysis-finds/) — and a generation priced out of ownership is exactly the generation for whom a warehouse wage and a repayment plan is the floor, not the fall.

The policy that would actually close the gap is not complicated: a care allowance that covers the cost of caring, housing construction that brings rent within reach of a working wage, and an education system that does not require a parental chequebook to function. Most European countries with a functioning welfare state already have at least two of the three. The question is not whether the policy exists. The question is whether the government intends to fund it for people who do not have parents who can.

Read the three women as the floor, not the exception. They are not treading water. They are the only ones swimming.
