The river was a creaturely common good — the earth was given for all — and they sold it to the debt traders and called it a turnaround. The Commons Environment, Food and Rural Affairs Committee, in a [report published Friday](https://www.theguardian.com/environment/2026/sep/18/mps-urge-ministers-thames-water-talks-hedge-funds-special-administration-regime), has told ministers to break off talks with a US-led consortium of hedge funds and distressed-debt investors and to pass emergency legislation to take Thames Water into public control. The committee named Elliott Investment Management and its consortium London & Valley Water, arguing that these are not water engineers but debt traders who have kept Thames in limbo while raking in millions in interest and fees, seeking relief from environmental fines of up to £1bn.

Let me grant the consortium its strongest point. Under the privatized framework as it stands, distressed-debt buyers may be the only source of patient capital at scale. No public body has the balance sheet to recapitalize a £20bn-debt utility; no private buyer with public-service credentials has stepped forward. The London & Valley Water consortium says its enhanced proposal will write off billions in debt and provide £10bn of new capital, with no cost to taxpayers; all fines will be paid, all profits reinvested, no dividends taken until turnaround. In a world where the state has retreated from infrastructure investment, the creditor class is the only actor with the capital to keep the water flowing. That is the honest point.

But the indictment is precisely that fact — that the only rescuer is the hedge fund. The very framework has been designed to force the public to bargain with vultures. Water is a creaturely common good. The earth was given for all. The social mortgage on creation means no one owns the rain. The rentier class has violated this by treating a public necessity — the water that 16 million people depend on — as an abstract revenue-generating asset, something to be bought at pennies on the pound, flipped, and stripped for fees.

This is the same machinery I have watched dissolve the county I came home to. The financialization of the real, the severing of a utility from its place, the treating of a community's livelihood as a disposable abstraction owned by people who will never set foot there. The hedge fund that holds the debt does not see the river. It sees a revenue stream. It does not see the woman who turns on the tap. It sees a cash flow. The rivers are polluted, the pavements are broken, the bills are rising, the customers have lost faith — and the consortium drags out negotiations while raking in millions in interest and fees. This is the violation: the severing of a public necessity from the people it serves.

Catholic social teaching says this plainly. Leo XIII, in *Rerum Novarum*, affirmed that property is real and good but answers to the common good. Pius XI, in *Quadragesimo Anno* §79, named the principle of subsidiarity: higher bodies should not absorb what smaller ones can do. The lower body — the community, the locality — is where water provision belongs, and when the higher body — the hedge fund consortium — has captured the utility, the principle demands that the community reclaim it. The universal destination of goods, the earth given for all, is the theological root: no one owns the river. It is given for all.

Prime Minister Andy Burnham, a steward who has been too slow to confront this, is not the villain of this story. He is a fellow steward who has failed his own people. The communitarian voice grieves with him, not against him. The failure is not his alone — it is the failure of a framework that has been built to ensure that the only available savior is the creditor class. The Efra committee's demand is the right one: emergency legislation, public control, a withdrawal from the hedge fund negotiation. But the deeper demand is the one that Catholic social teaching makes: water is a common good, and the proper governance of water is by the people who depend on it, not by strangers who will never set foot there.

The concrete counter-model is a water commons trust — a regional commons authority, governed by elected local representatives, operating under the subsidiarity principle. Not a centralized state monopoly, which concentrates power in a different coat. But a distributed governance: a mutual, modeled on the cooperative tradition — the Rochdale Principles, the credit union, the member-owned utility. A water mutual, with governance by elected local representatives, where the members are the people whose taps depend on the river. The Thames Water saga is a parable: the same machinery of abstraction, the financialization of the real, the severing of a utility from its place. The river belongs to all. It was given for all. And the debt traders do not own it. The water commons trust, governed by the people it serves, is the answer.
