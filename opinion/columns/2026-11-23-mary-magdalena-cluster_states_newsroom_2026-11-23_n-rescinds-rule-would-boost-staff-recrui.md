Last week, the Administration for Children and Families, under the Trump administration, rescinded the mandatory wage-and-benefits increase for Head Start teachers adopted in the 2024 Head Start Program Performance Standards. The rule set four standards for staff wages and required implementation by Aug. 1, 2031. Its Federal Register notice said, “For decades, the Head Start programs have been subsidized by low paid workers committed to the mission; now is the time to enact clear Federal requirements for staff compensation.” The notice said higher compensation would help retain high-quality staff and warned that failing to improve it would disrupt relationships between teachers and families and negatively affect children.

ACF said the mandate was “not in line with the plain language of the Head Start Act” and “costly and overly prescriptive to Head Start programs and staff.” It estimated the rescission would save more than $2 million in future costs. Without more congressionally approved spending, it said, higher wages and benefits would divert money from classroom capacity and cause enrollment loss. It rejected “a one size fits all approach to staff compensation.” Its answer was “maximum local flexibility.” The move was the administration’s [cancellation of the Head Start teacher pay rule](/articles/2026-11-14-trump-administration-cancels-head-start-teacher-pay-rule/). Earlier MSI reporting had documented the [end of an expected $10,000 raise for most Head Start teachers](/articles/2026-11-17-federal-rule-ends-expected-10-000-raise-for-most-head-start-teachers/).

The standards were prospective. The rescission blocked the future compensation requirements. It did not repair the low pay that had made them necessary. Head Start serves eligible low-income families with children ages zero through five and expectant parents. New Hampshire has 33 Head Start programs, managed and paid for by five regional Community Action Partnership organizations. Required teacher-to-child ratios exist for safety and developmental reasons. When too few teachers are employed, a classroom cannot fill while maintaining the required ratio. Based on yearly enrollment and required ratios, at least 109 teachers would have been needed to staff New Hampshire’s Head Start programs in 2025, down from at least 201 in 2011. Programs may still adopt the 2024 standards voluntarily. A separate pending rule would overhaul Head Start curriculum, safety, ratios, and administrative requirements.
