They have made the ballot a substitute for deliberation, turning Colorado and Washington into a testing ground for a national campaign built on preference rather than the common good. The [Associated Press reports](https://apnews.com/article/transgender-sports-ballot-question-washington-colorado-7c11cbd85a5c6da201ef2f12324d38d2) that activists have gone around elected officials who rejected bans on transgender women and girls from women’s sports, putting proposed bans before voters for the first time. Supporters hope passage will open the way to similar campaigns elsewhere.

That deserves two answers, not one. A ballot is not theft. When representatives will not protect a rule their constituents want, the initiative is a legitimate check upon them. But the possession of a ballot does not make every question plebiscitary, and a majority preference does not automatically become the common good. The question here is not whether voters may speak. It is whether their vote, without the deliberation that belongs to the institutions nearest their lives, deserves to settle the meaning of sex, fairness, safety, and human dignity.

The strongest case for the campaigns begins with a genuine grievance. Colorado and Washington are reliably Democratic states, yet elected officials in both rejected these bans. Voters may reasonably conclude that the officials protecting women’s sports were too responsive to organized interests, too fearful of controversy, or unwilling to defend a distinction that matters to athletes. In some sports, sex is not a decorative label. It bears upon physical contact, safety, fairness, and the conditions under which women and girls are permitted to compete. Those are real interests, not phantoms invented by conservative grievance.

The interests of transgender women and girls are equally real. Their legitimate claim is not to be reduced to a contested device in somebody else’s campaign, compelled to disclose intimate medical facts, publicly singled out, or treated as a trick rather than a person. Their sex, their bodies, their histories, and their claims upon competitive sport cannot be adjudicated by a rally or reduced to a poll of strangers. Whatever conclusion one reaches about participation in particular sports, it must leave the dignity of the persons affected intact.

And the judgment of elected officials cannot be dismissed merely because it is unpopular with the people who organized the campaign. A legislature is not an oracle. It can fail the people it represents, and a ballot initiative exists precisely to correct such failure. But the [Associated Press account](https://apnews.com/article/transgender-sports-ballot-question-washington-colorado-7c11cbd85a5c6da201ef2f12324d38d2) does not tell us what those legislatures heard, what evidence they considered, or why they rejected the bans. That silence does not prove them right. It does mean their judgment is a judgment rather than a nullity, and their rejection deserves a presumption rather than the instant delegitimization that follows every successful campaign against an elected body.

The specific betrayal is visible in the campaign’s declared ambition. These activists are not merely asking Colorado and Washington to revisit a question their legislatures considered. They are seeking a ballot victory that will establish a usable model for other states. Chris Melody Fields Figueredo, executive director of the Ballot Initiative Strategy Center, which supports LGBTQ+ rights, called what is happening in 2026 “a testing ground for the future.” Whether warning or description, the words identify the strategy: first establish the precedent here, then carry it elsewhere.

That is national ballot-template activism, and it is the enemy of subsidiarity. The bodies nearest the competition—school boards, athletic associations, families, coaches, and athletes—are the bodies most likely to understand the peculiar circumstances of a particular league, age group, sport, and level of competition. A statewide campaign is not always wrong to intervene; sometimes legislatures will refuse the protection a community needs. But when the campaign’s purpose is to convert one state’s decision into a template for the country, the institutions nearest the consequences are made disposable. Local judgment becomes merely the preliminary round before the national machinery proceeds.

The polling reported in the source cannot cure that defect. Polling can establish that Americans generally favor the bans. It cannot tell us what Colorado and Washington will decide, and it cannot tell us whether a preference has become sufficiently just to settle the claims of women, girls, and transgender women and girls upon one another. A poll measures sentiment. It does not hold a hearing. To say that the public supports a rule is not to show that the rule respects the persons affected by it.

The Catholic social tradition has always held that a community is more than a market for preferences. Persons are not units to be counted; they are ends whose dignity cannot be surrendered by majority vote. That principle does not exempt any side from scrutiny. It asks what arrangement protects the vulnerable without making one group disappear and what preserves the common good without stripping the community of local knowledge. Solidarity does not mean pretending there is no dispute. It means neither side may use the dispute to declare the other side less human.

Subsidiarity supplies the institutional discipline. In *Quadragesimo Anno*, Pius XI condemned the grave evil of assigning to a higher power what lesser organizations can do. Applied here, it does not mean that legislatures are sacred or that local boards are infallible. It means the burden of displacement must be met: if a state legislature or athletic association can hear the affected parties, consider the evidence, and make a reasoned rule, then a statewide campaign should not bypass that work merely because the route to victory is politically easier. An appeal to the people is not a substitute for judgment; it is another way of asking who bears responsibility for the judgment.

The counter-model can be called the local athletics covenant. State law would establish a floor for safety, equal protection, and institutional accountability. Each school district and athletic association would then hold an open hearing in which women athletes, transgender women and girls, coaches, parents, physicians, and civil-rights counsel could put their claims before the body that would govern participation. Eligibility rules would be written and independently reviewable; medical privacy would not become a public spectacle; reasons would be published; and affected persons would have a route of appeal. National organizations might offer model standards. They should offer assistance, not command a uniform campaign from the ballot box.

This is cooperative logic applied without romanticism: those who do the work and bear the consequences must have a voice in governing the enterprise. It is harder here because disagreement is real, because sex is genuinely difficult, and because neither women and girls nor transgender women and girls can be reduced to a rhetorical instrument. Difficulty is an argument for better deliberation, not for fewer voices.

Let the people hold their elected institutions accountable. Let them replace lawmakers who betray their trust. But let them do so after the institutions nearest the problem have done the work they were formed to do, and without turning one state’s ballot into the engine of a national template.

The common good is not a larger count. It is a rule heard in the presence of those who must live by it. Leave the work where the lives are.
