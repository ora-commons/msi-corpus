You paid for the condenser coil humming behind your house. You think that makes you the customer. You're not. You're the reason the line exists. The customer is me. The line is Elspeth.

Elspeth brazes condenser coils on a line I own outside McAllen, Texas. The brazing furnace runs 1,100 degrees. The shop around it runs 112. She feeds a coil, runs the silver solder down the joint, and does it again. 62 coils an hour, 8 hours a shift, 5 shifts a week, 4 years. Every joint in the unit cooling the room where your daughter sleeps went under her flame. I pay her $14.90 an hour.

The Lord gave her the steady hands. I gave her the shift.

The local-exhaust arm that would pull the flux fume off her face before she breathes it costs $2,400 a station. I run 34 stations and have bought none of them. The evaporative cooler that would drop her floor from 112 to 95 costs $27,000 installed — $0.03 a coil over the life of the line. I have not bought it. The cooler is bolted to the wall of my office. It cools a room I occupy maybe two rounds a shift. I own the line. I do not cool it. The Lord made July. I made the line that sits in it.

Now watch what the regulation handed me.

[Ben Lieberman wrote in the *National Review* this week](https://www.nationalreview.com/2026/10/the-affordability-crisis-hits-air-conditioning/) that the affordability crisis has reached air conditioning, and he brings the figures with him — the $20,000 installed system in Florida, up from $15,000 in 2024, and he credits the EPA's refrigerant rule under the AIM Act, a law signed in December 2020 with almost no debate. He is right about the sticker. He has written carefully about who pays it. I want to talk about who was paid, because the answer is me.

Here is the principle the case was only an example of: a regulation inspects the machine. It does not inspect the air above the bench. The AIM Act's phase-down schedule forced a transition to a new refrigerant — R-454B, replacing R-410A — and the new refrigerant demands a different brazing alloy. Different alloy, different fume. The rule specifies what the finished joint must withstand under pressure. It says nothing about what leaves the joint while it is being made. The alloy changed in February. I put it on Elspeth's station in February. The exhaust arm stayed in the catalogue.

Read the next sentence twice. A $2,400 exhaust arm amortized over a station's output comes to 1 cents a coil. The new alloy runs me $0.038 a coil more than the old one, and I pass every fraction of it to the buyer without a murmur, because your contractor already had the $20,000 on the estimate and one more line on the invoice looks like arithmetic. So the increase was yours. The saving was mine. None of it came back to the bench.

> "Hath not the potter power over the clay, of the same lump to make one vessel unto honour, and another unto dishonour?" — Romans 9:21, King James Version

He was writing about clay. I am writing about copper. The principle survives the material. Some vessels get the cooling. Some vessels make the cooling. The clay does not choose which one it is.

Odell works the recovery area past the divider, breaking down the condenser units people scrap when the $20,000 quote arrives and they decide to get one more summer out of what they have. He recovers the refrigerant and sells it back at $46 a pound, and he breathes whatever the exhaust hood does not catch. He asked, in his second interview, what cartridges the plant stocks. I told him the plant stocks what the manufacturer's sheet recommends for the alloy, which is true. The sheet was written for the joint. Not for the room. That is the answer a man gives when he has trained himself to answer the question that was asked.

The AIM Act sets a phase-down schedule that tightens on its own timetable, and it lets any future administration add to it, and one did. You have been told this is a fight about the climate. It is not. It is a fight about timing, and I have watched it from the side that collects. Regulation was, in my experience, the long expensive interruption in a very old trade. The interruption is over. The trade has kept everything the interruption handed it — the higher price, the fuller order book, and one change order per quarter that I do not have to explain to anyone, because no one on the state's compliance form asks the question the change order answers.

I am told the newer refrigerant costs more per pound, and that some households will run what they have one more August rather than pay the new number. I have not asked what those rooms are like in the third week of a Texas summer. The demand moves somewhere. The summers are longer than they were. That is somebody's column and not mine.

I am told the industry will see a shift toward more efficient equipment. I have not asked what that shift does to the woman running the old line in the room beside the furnace. The compliance certificate on my new run is signed. It covers the coil's pressure rating, the alloy composition, the joint integrity. It says nothing about the air Elspeth breathes while making it. Not because anyone hid anything. Because nobody asks.

Elspeth's station runs Monday. Odell's hood runs Monday. Both are on the schedule I set, and the schedule does not include the exhaust arms, and I have not decided whether to buy them, and the quarter closes on the 30th, and the price of deciding is already written on your invoice.

I cool your house. I don't cool hers. The difference is mine to keep.
