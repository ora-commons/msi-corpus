Somewhere tonight a man who has never seen this county will call a widow in Friendship, tell her to keep the conversation between the two of them—and she may, because everyone she might have told is gone. This is what the movement that calls itself conservative has left us: a market for every confidence trick and a stranger in every kitchen. An [Associated Press advisory](https://apnews.com/article/global-scams-sextortion-tips-ccf4d344c429de5b76b7561b0217fc8c) published this week reports that polling finds nearly every American adult says they have been targeted by scammers, that research shows just about anyone can fall victim, and that most victims never report the crime while few recover their money. Cyberscams have become a hugely profitable global criminal industry, and its tactics are improving faster than our ability to recognize them. For financial sextortion—blackmail through intimate images, especially against teenage boys—the Associated Press says do not pay, stop replying, preserve the images and messages, report them, and seek help. At the end, beside those instructions for a teenager threatened with exposure, the guide gives the national crisis lifeline: [988](https://988lifeline.org). That is not a footnote. It is the wire service’s plain acknowledgment of how far this industry can carry the young.

Give the guide its due. It refuses two cheap substitutes: the sermon that victims should have known better and the spectacle that treats every scam as proof the internet has made us all helpless. The offender is the criminal, not the frightened person who answered. Hang up. Call back through a number you found yourself. Search the message before trusting it. Stop, document, report. Sound, unglamorous advice. The market that built the telephone built marvels; tools in their place, the trouble was never the tool. Common sense, said plainly, is sometimes the most radical thing left to say.

But notice whom the advice assumes. Stop communicating, collect the records, call the bank or cryptocurrency exchange, call the platform, call law enforcement, or call the trusted family member or friend. The person in crisis is expected to assemble the enforcement system out of several calls. Main Street Independent has already reported both [record $15.9 billion in scam losses last year](/articles/2026-09-02-americans-reported-record-15-9-billion-in-scam-losses-last-year-joint-investigat/) and a [congressional report finding a fragmented U.S. response to overseas scams](/articles/2026-10-27-congressional-report-finds-fragmented-u-s-response-to-overseas-scams/). On the Chicago desk I watched money move through markets in heartbeats; a county sheriff’s phone call did not move at that speed. The money crosses borders easily. The shame remains local. The enforcement machinery is scattered among offices that rarely speak to one another before the money is gone.

No wonder the fraud thrives.

A community is not a mood of neighborliness. It is a set of institutions mediating between the person and the market: family, parish, school, bank, credit union, cooperative, local paper. These are where trust is embodied, where a counterfeit check is noticed, where an elderly member’s altered voice draws a second call, where a teenager can ask for help without first deciding shame has made the request impossible. Dissolve those bodies and you do not produce a liberated individual. You produce a target.

The parish in this county traces back to a frame church raised in 1884; the rural parishes have been closing and consolidating for years. The VFW hall where I used to sit with this county’s older veterans emptied as the men who built it died. The Adams-Friendship Times-Reporter is thinner every year. Friendship’s first bank opened in 1910 and failed in the Depression; its old counter is a museum now, and with the counter went the teller who knew your signature and could say wait.

For generations, the county’s first fraud detector was not an algorithm. It was the neighbor who knew the family—who knew what the grandson sounded like, knew he never called about bail. A stranger’s voice can impersonate a grandson today because the economy dissolved the layer of people who could vouch for a voice. That dispersed, local knowledge, the kind no headquarters holds and no app sells, was an old fraud filter, and we sold it off with everything else. Then we sold the widow Caller ID as the replacement.

Oakeshott called the conservative temper a preference for “the familiar to the unknown, the actual to the possible… present laughter to utopian bliss.” The thief’s inventory is the unknown dressed as the familiar, marketed to people whose familiar has been repossessed.

For two generations, the movement that campaigns on family, faith, and local control has presided over the dissolution of the very institutions that might have caught the call at the kitchen table. Conserve what, exactly?

You call yourselves the party of family. You dissolved the circle that stood between the widow and the stranger, then called the dissolution freedom. Every safeguard became an infringement; every rule, tyranny; every untraceable wallet, liberty. That is not conservatism. It is the abandonment of prescription, prudence, and the common good, dressed in the vocabulary of freedom.

The machinery was not accidental. I traded agricultural futures in Chicago—paper claims on corn grown by people very much like my neighbors—so I know what abstraction at one remove looks like. This is abstraction all the way down: wallets on wallets, wealth with no county attached, moving past any teller who might have said wait and past any sheriff who might have asked questions, into servers where no subpoena lands comfortably. The scam industry is parasitism in its purest form. The functionless investor carried to his criminal conclusion: the functionless thief, taking without making, living on other people’s trust the way a tick lives on blood.

And the record now shows what that doctrine bought. Americans reported a record $15.9 billion in scam losses last year. The federal response to the overseas industry remains fragmented. The answer offered to the widow is a website she is expected to find after the money is gone. This is the movement that put a casino in every pocket and called it liberty. The stranger in the kitchen belongs to the same product line.

Consider what the industry does to boys. A teenager alone with a screen—alone because the lodge, the troop, and the parish hall have thinned around him—is threatened with exposure. Shame does the blackmailer’s second work: a frightened boy may hide the threat, delete the images, and tell no one because disclosure feels more dangerous than compliance. The blackmailer counts on precisely that isolation.

The guide’s counsel—do not pay, stop responding, preserve the evidence, report it—exists to break it. But a boy who belongs to two or three institutions that know him has somewhere to carry the shame. A boy who belongs to none has only the screen. Nor is this betrayal one movement’s work alone. The modern ideal of the unencumbered, self-directed individual, the self as its own project owing nothing it did not choose, has dissolved the same obligations from the other end. “Tell no one” is the autobiography of that ideal. A person constituted as a bundle of private preferences, answerable to nobody he did not contract with, is exactly the person the blackmailer’s script was written for.

What gets built, then, is not a bureau. The FBI’s portal, banks, exchanges, platforms, NCMEC, and law enforcement can do what only they can do: remove images, preserve evidence, trace criminal payments, freeze transfers, and pursue the networks across borders. But interrupting a wire is not the same as knowing the family. A national reporting portal is useful. It is not a community.

What gets built is the layer between the person and the stranger—the layer we spent forty years selling off—together with the unglamorous practices that make it real: a trusted contact named on the account, a callback to a number the family chose before any money moves, a standing call on the elder who lives alone. A credit union where the teller can say wait because the depositor is a member and not a fee event—and can reach the daughter whose name sits on the signature card. A mutual insurer owned by the people it insures. The parish phone tree. The card party that refuses to die.

In Friendship, the Adams-Columbia Electric Cooperative serves about 31,560 member-owners across twelve counties under a member-elected seven-person board. A member can call the number printed on the bill and reach an institution whose governance is not hidden in a fund prospectus. No one should pretend that makes it scam-proof; phone and video calls can be faked. But it shows the form of an answer: a local body with known offices, known people, and known responsibilities. The scammer does not merely encounter a consumer. He encounters a member who can ask a neighbor, check a bill, and reach the institution by a road she can drive.

That is subsidiarity in plain language. Let the competent smaller body do what it can, while larger systems do what only they can do. A shop on Main Street can question a counterfeit check. A parish can notice isolation. A school can teach a child to call before sending money. A local bank or credit union can catch a transfer. The cooperative can keep one telephone number members know and publish a bulletin saying when a message is ours and when it is not. Schools can name an adult, rehearse what a child should do when threatened with intimate images, and make reporting an ordinary act rather than a shameful emergency. Parishes and families can know where to call. Local papers can publish confirmed warnings instead of copied outrage. The National Center for Missing & Exploited Children, Project Arachnid, IC3, and the Federal Trade Commission can help remove images and pursue the offenders. A child should not have to become the investigator of a global criminal industry.

Some defenders of a purely managerial answer say to train every household to become a better fraud detector and require platforms to catch whatever slips past. Teaching is necessary. But if institutions can hide behind “user responsibility” whenever a frightened person is harmed, that is not personal responsibility. It is abdication with a lesson plan. A community cannot leave all local judgment to the person most frightened and least able to compare notes.

Nor can we cure the fraud economy with one more bureaucracy. We should distrust the platforms and intermediaries that monetize scam traffic, the robocall farms that supply the calls, and the networks that move criminal proceeds before local institutions can compare notes. We should distrust equally a state bureaucracy that imagines one command can make the danger handled. We need no Ministry of Scam Prevention, another institution whose name promises safety while its distance makes the remedy abstract.

The cooperative tradition already knows what the managerial answer forgets. The rural electric co-ops began wiring this country when only about one farm in ten had central-station electricity in the mid-1930s; neither the market nor government alone would take the job. The seventh modern cooperative principle is concern for the community. It sounds like sentiment until you read it as security policy: a county where every elder belongs to two or three bodies that call on a Tuesday is a county the thieves cannot work.

At our co-op counter one January morning in 2024, a member set her phone beside the bill and asked whether a message warning of a delayed payment had come from us. It had not. She had driven in from the farm because there was no one else to ask.

In Adams County, the counter-model would be embarrassingly ordinary. It is harder here because trust must exist before the emergency, and even a cooperative’s member bulletin can be spoofed while warning against spoofing. Roughly a third of this county is past sixty-five. The 2020 count found nearly half our housing units vacant, overwhelmingly seasonal second homes on the lakes. The 2017 figures put labor-force participation at the lowest in Wisconsin, and in the city of Adams better than one person in four lives below the poverty line. The county is classified as a high-recreation retirement destination: elders living alone in a place that swells all summer and thins when the season turns. That is precisely the population this industry prefers.

The thieves will keep dialing. The tactics will improve. The wire service will publish another guide next year, as good and as insufficient as this one. The whole work is contained in the seventh instruction—check with someone you trust—and the work is to rebuild a county where that sentence has someone to land on. There is no shortcut around it. It proceeds one known number, one rehearsed classroom, one verified notice in the local paper, one frightened teenager reaching an adult who will act at once. That is not triumph. It is maintenance—the old, unglamorous kind that keeps a community answerable to itself.

The answer is not a law passed from one central office. It is a Main Street where a farmer can call a co-op he owns, a bank can ring a person back before sending away the money, a school can reach a frightened child, and no stranger owns the last word.

Let them dial into a place that answers.
