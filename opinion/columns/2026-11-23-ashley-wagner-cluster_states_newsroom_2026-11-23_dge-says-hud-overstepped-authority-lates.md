*Congress allowed extra criteria, not permission to nullify mandatory housing factors.*

Scott Turner and HUD are stealing $1.3 billion from permanent housing programs.

On June 1, HUD tried to set aside $1.3 billion of its roughly $4 billion Continuum of Care program for new projects, favor shorter-term housing and supportive services, and impose new conditions on grant applicants. Divide $1.3 billion by $4 billion: roughly 32.5 cents of every dollar in the competition. U.S. District Judge Mary S. McElroy struck down the set-aside, finding HUD and Turner had exceeded their authority and diverted money away from existing programs and permanent housing toward shorter-term solutions. Her ruling ran 59 pages. “Although Congress delegated to the secretary the power to create additional criteria for awarding funds, this residual clause cannot be read as allowing HUD to nullify the other congressionally established mandatory factors,” McElroy wrote.

A judge Donald Trump appointed during his first term ruled against his housing secretary. This is not a partisan hunch. It is a 59-page order.

Congress allowed HUD to add award criteria. It did not allow Turner to nullify the mandatory factors. That distinction is not technical: once the secretary can nullify them, the funding notice, not Congress, governs the money. McElroy had already invalidated HUD’s November and December 2025 notices in June and blocked the June 1 directive in August after another violation. This was not one stray sentence. It was a repeated attempt to redirect billions.

At my kitchen table, I keep a spreadsheet because the math is the math. David and I bring home $8,800 a month after taxes. Childcare for both children takes $2,400—more than 27 percent of that income—leaving $6,400 before the 7 percent mortgage, utilities, food, and health care. My parents paid off their Lansdale house in 2007 on my father’s postal salary; that margin is gone.

I name the advantages because the analysis depends on them. David’s grandmother’s estate helped us buy the Fishtown rowhouse, two salaries arrive, and our household is white and middle class, not a representative sample of homelessness. This is my household’s record, not evidence that every household has the same margin. It shows how little room remains for a housing emergency.

The Harvard Joint Center for Housing Studies found in 2022 that 22.4 million renter households—half of all American renters—spent more than 30 percent of their income on housing. Among renters earning $45,000 to $75,000, the cost-burdened share reached 41 percent, up 5.4 points from 2019. For cost-burdened renters earning under $30,000, the study found roughly $310 a month left for everything other than housing and utilities.

That is not an abstraction from another century. This is the housing market into which HUD’s grant rules would send families. Below those households is the intake line at a Continuum of Care provider—the families already inside homelessness, for whom the grants finance providers offering a lease first and help attached. The fight is over whether that line remains standing when a job breaks or a lease disappears. Permanent housing cannot be treated as a discretionary preference while Washington decides which providers remain eligible.

Who gets the money? The applicants insulated from the old competition by the set-aside. Who carries the cost? Existing permanent-housing programs, then the people who depend on them.

HUD threatened penalties for programs using harm-reduction strategies and tied eligibility to local law enforcement’s campaign against encampments, diversity and inclusion policies, and immigration-related restrictions. The local provider with a record of housing people now has to win its way back into an application process HUD redesigned. The rule did not merely place new projects in contention. It changed the terms of competition: new projects over demonstrated performance, federal approval over local priorities, federal conditions over local judgment.

McElroy found no evidence contradicting the plaintiffs. “Defendants offer no evidence contradicting Plaintiffs’ showing that the Set-Aside distorted the application process,” she wrote. The burden was theirs. They brought a mission statement.

The ideological agenda Turner points at has names on it: 21 states and the District of Columbia; Santa Clara and King counties; San Francisco, Boston, Cambridge, Nashville, and Tucson; the National Alliance to End Homelessness and the National Low Income Housing Coalition; and Providence providers Crossroads Rhode Island and Youth Pride Inc. Rhode Island Attorney General Peter Neronha said the changes could have put “thousands of Americans at risk of losing their homes.”

This is the same department that just [severed Los Angeles’s homelessness agency from its federal funding and demanded $150 million back](/articles/2026-11-17-hud-deal-severs-lahsa-from-federal-funding-and-requires-150-million-return/), and it now faces a fresh [states’ lawsuit over a housing-aid rule affecting mixed-status families](/articles/2026-11-23-states-sue-hud-over-housing-aid-rule-affecting-mixed-status-families/). The pattern is the same: decide who deserves a door, then make deserving harder.

Read diagnostically rather than aspirationally, Taylor Swift wrote the mission statement of American care infrastructure, and it is five words long: “You’re On Your Own, Kid.” The song’s bridge makes the net lateral—the people who show up. HUD’s application turns that handoff into paperwork: local programs must fit federal preferences before communities can compare whether they actually house people. On Friday, the lateral net showed up at the scale of states, counties, cities, providers, and a judge. That is what the set-aside changed. Not the amount of money in the room; who was allowed to compete for it.

There is a true half in Turner’s argument, and I will concede it straight: addiction is real, mental illness is real, treatment saves lives, and nobody running a homelessness-services agency disputes any of that. What the argument suppresses is who pays when the condition becomes the toll booth. Stability is the platform treatment stands on; take the platform out from under a family and you have not replaced anything with anything. You have moved the cost onto the household.

Annie Lowrey has the frame for this: “Poverty in the United States is a choice.” Not weather. Decisions by named actors at named moments. McElroy’s record asked HUD to account for its choices. It offered no evidence contradicting the plaintiffs’ showing.

Anne Helen Petersen’s *Can’t Even* is useful because it refuses the private-failure story: burnout is a structural condition, not a personal defect. HUD’s rule has the same architecture. It reroutes the housing money, relocates funding judgment to Washington, and scores any provider that cannot produce the approved paperwork as a bad actor. The people running local programs are not the villains. They are being used as the mechanism for a policy their communities did not choose. This is central control with a grant application attached.

I know the screen “Treatment First” builds because I grew up downstream from the alternative. At St. Stanislaus in Lansdale, the corporal works of mercy were operational: feed the hungry, house the stranger, visit the sick. When a family in the pew lost a job, the Saint Vincent de Paul conference quietly arranged rent or utility assistance. There was no asterisk and no sobriety checkbox in front of the obligation. That was not sentiment; it was a list of duties the parish could not hand back to the market.

Dorothy Day put the scale plainly: “The Gospel takes away our right forever, to discriminate between the deserving and the undeserving poor.” Condition-first funding turns the same distinction into a grant screen—sorting who has earned a door from who still has to. McElroy has now rejected three attempts by HUD to redirect the competition outside the law Congress wrote.

She declined a permanent injunction barring similar changes, and Turner has reserved an appeal. The remedy is real but bounded: applicants must be allowed to amend and resubmit by December 1, and the award deadline moves to December 31. That limits the remedy; it does not erase the wrong the court identified. The pro-family policy is not a mystery, and it does not require inventing anything: Congress should fund permanent housing and supportive services. HUD should award grants by local need and demonstrated outcomes, keep treatment attached to housing as a service rather than a toll, and score no application on whether its local policy pleases Washington. Make the December 31 awards on the merits the judge restored.

The families underneath the housing column are the ones who pay. At my kitchen table, the spreadsheet still has two columns: what comes in and what must be paid. HUD’s design adds a third marked ideology. I am done letting HUD steal the difference.
