The proposed Union Pacific–Norfolk Southern merger would not merely make shipping cheaper. It would make walking away harder for the customers who need it most. It is not a shipping-cost story. It is a power-and-safety story dressed up as logistics. The power to set rail prices would sit in fewer hands. That is the fight now before the Surface Transportation Board, in [News From The States’ reporting on the merger](https://www.newsfromthestates.com/article/union-pacific-says-merger-would-make-shipping-cheaper-farmers-labor-and-shippers-are), and the people waiting for the answer are not abstractions. Start with a grain elevator whose only railroad sets its price, a Gulf Coast chemical plant whose tank cars cannot be moved by an ordinary truck, and the maintenance crew welding rail back into place after eight inches of rain.

Six major railroads would become five. Union Pacific would become the largest in the country and the first to serve both coasts. For a captive shipper, geography does not change, but bargaining position does. “If a grain elevator can only ship on one railroad now, and they set the pricing, that’s going to affect all of the farmers that get grain to that elevator, and so now those farmers may have to eat more costs,” said Ash Anderson, education director for the Brotherhood of Maintenance Way Employees. The American Chemistry Council’s Jeff Sloan sees the same trap for Gulf Coast plants: “Essentially, if you’re not afraid of losing your customer, you treat the customer differently.” Bob Granatelli, a transportation consultant who has been in the business since the 1970s, put the consequence plainly: “This is frightening for a captive shipper. It will make captivity even stronger.”

Paul Bellows, vice chairman of the Brotherhood’s Unified System Division, worries that this merger could start another round of consolidation and calls the order it would create “basically a duopoly.”

Union Pacific has one true point. Interchanges waste time. The American rail map splits at the Mississippi, with Chicago, Kansas City, and St. Louis serving as major transfer points. A load that changes railroads waits, moves twice, and pays for another handoff. Union Pacific says the first single-service transcontinental railroad would eliminate those transfers. Its merger application projects 2.1 million fewer truckloads on the road each year. Norfolk Southern executive Mike McClellan says trucks will remain the price ceiling. Fine. That is honest. Concede it and move on, because it is not the question.

The question is who keeps the savings. What happens after the handoff disappears?

That is where the pitch stops being about logistics. Every interchange has a second face: competition. Often, another railroad stood ready to take the freight. The right to walk away was doing the work that a second set of rails cannot. It does not add track; it deletes the exit.

I’m not against railroads. I’m against extraction. Interchanges can waste money. Consolidation can remove some waste. But the danger is not merely that a merged railroad may charge more. It is that the customer may lose the practical choice to leave, while thinner staffing and longer trains make maintenance a thinner margin. Those are different things. The pitch calls them one.

Railroads are sometimes natural monopolies because building a parallel network costs far more than adding a train. Nobody wants three competing rail grids across Nebraska, any more than three sets of water pipe under the same street. But the pipes were never the whole story. A natural monopoly is a reason to regulate price and service, not a reason to approve consolidation. “The typical things that happen as monopoly power rises in a market are increases in price or reductions in service or product quality,” said Elena Prager, an assistant professor at the University of Rochester’s Simon Business School. In general: higher prices, lower quantities, worse quality.

The network is natural. The monopoly needs a leash.

McClellan’s truck-price ceiling is real only where a truck can take the freight. A Gulf Coast chemical plant may have no practical truck substitute for tank cars or hopper cars. The farmer at the grain elevator may have no practical truck substitute at all. A ceiling is still a ceiling when the party setting the rent beneath it would span the country.

Then the pricing-power argument acquired an uncomfortable receipt. At a March meeting of the Southwest Association of Rail Shippers, Union Pacific CEO Jim Vena said, “I’ll probably increase everybody’s rates that said they didn’t want a seamless railroad by 3 or 4% just to teach you a lesson.” A federal judge ordered the recording released in late October. Union Pacific said its customer’s merger position would not determine its rates or service, and Vena [apologized](https://www.msi.news/articles/2026-11-20-union-pacific-ceo-apologizes-for-rate-remark-amid-norfolk-southern-merger/). A video is not a merger order. It is still a receipt of how the CEO imagined pricing power. Anyway. A seamless railroad. Seamless, apparently, in the sense that nothing stands between the customer and the price. Walk me through that arithmetic: if one railroad can cross the whole country while the customer’s practical alternative disappears, who sets the price?

The second cost is measured in wear. Nate Lee, a BNSF rail worker and local chairman for the Brotherhood of Maintenance Way Employees, spent October chasing washouts after eight inches of rain in southeast Nebraska. His crew worked overtime. They welded rail back into place and leveled the ballast beneath it. Workers fear the merger could reduce the maintenance workforce as investors press for cost cuts. Meanwhile, rail companies have used longer trains to reduce labor costs and move freight faster. A train once half a mile long can now stretch one and a half miles, or two. It takes longer to stop. It loads the track harder. It can cut off road access at a crossing.

No one has shown that the merger would cause another East Palestine. We do not need that fiction. In 2023, a Norfolk Southern train carrying hazardous materials derailed near East Palestine, Ohio. The cargo caught fire and sent a toxic cloud over the town, forcing evacuations. A lawsuit alleges the fumes contributed to multiple deaths; the case remains unadjudicated. If a thinner maintenance workforce, longer trains, and heavier workloads leave fewer people able to catch what is missed, the margin is thinner. Lee’s point is arithmetic: “You can only run a person ragged for so long before it starts to wear on them.” And when people get tired, “Things that shouldn’t be missed may get missed.”

Union Pacific calls its proposal “Jobs For Life.” The International Association of Sheet Metal, Air, Rail and Transportation Workers Union negotiated the agreement and supports the merger. The maintenance workers’ union does not trust it. A lifetime is a long contract to take on faith from a company whose chief executive priced a grudge at 3 to 4 percent. Should workers have to treat the employer’s promise as their safety plan? Minimum safety cannot be bargained down. Nor can the awkward transition in which Norfolk Southern employees must adapt to Union Pacific’s safety practices be waved away as free efficiency.

Local government cannot repair the arithmetic with an ordinance. Existing legal precedent leaves rail rates and service largely to the federal government. A city council can complain about a blocked crossing. It cannot rewrite the rate schedule or call the dispatch office to account. Clark Ballew, communications director for the maintenance workers’ union, put the mayor’s predicament plainly: if you are a politician, “there isn’t much you can do to help.” Every grievance in this story about price and service routes to one economic address: the railroad that sets the terms. The Surface Transportation Board is the serious check on that address.

The board is independent, and its members are sworn in by the Senate. One seat is vacant after the firing of Robert Primus, the only member who voted against the 2023 merger of Canadian Pacific and Kansas City Southern.

This is not a rescue of two failing railroads. The industry has been shrinking since deregulation, when roughly 40 major freight railroads consolidated into six. This deal would make it five. The $85 billion transaction is between companies that just posted [higher profits](https://www.msi.news/articles/2026-10-23-union-pacific-norfolk-southern-and-csx-post-higher-profits-amid-rail-shift/). Railway Age editor William C. Vantuono thinks Wall Street’s excitement tracked the hope that regulations might be scuttled. He also thinks the board’s members are ethical. Fine. Let the board earn that description in public.

In October 2025, months after the merger was announced, Union Pacific donated $2.5 million toward construction of the White House ballroom. Vena denied that the gift was meant to persuade President Trump to back the merger. No document shows it bought a ruling. The receipt shows access. The railroad can afford gestures. Sens. Tammy Baldwin and Roger Marshall have both asked the board to scrutinize the plan. The fact that two such different political constituencies want the same answer is not proof of a conspiracy. It is a sign the captivity arithmetic has become too large to ignore.

Public comments closed November 18. The board will hear from other federal agencies through the winter, with more hearings planned for the spring. So what do we build?

The immediate build belongs to the board. It can make a yes conditional. First, captive shippers need enforceable rate protections, written as a schedule with a clock on it, not a press release. Second, the major interchanges need protected open access: competing railroads must be able to use transfer yards and connecting track on published, nondiscriminatory terms. Third, staffing, inspection, fatigue, and maintenance standards need measurement and audit attached. Minimum staffing, inspection, and safety standards must not disappear because shareholders like longer trains. “Jobs For Life” should become an audited fact, not a slogan.

If Union Pacific believes its own arithmetic—if a seamless railroad genuinely saves enough that everyone wins—it should race to sign binding rate and service protections. A company that promises cheaper freight and refuses binding price commitments has told you which line the savings are booked under.

The burden belongs to the merger. If Union Pacific wants one coast-to-coast railroad, it must show that consolidation leaves service better, rates fairer, and maintenance work safer. If it cannot, the board should say no. If it can, the promises must arrive as enforceable conditions, not corporate poetry.

The pitch is elegant: one railroad, fewer handoffs, cheaper freight. The arithmetic is less elegant. Fewer owners can mean fewer choices. Longer trains can mean less inspection. A captive shipper gets a larger invoice while the maintenance crew comes up a body short. We know how to build the next thing.

The second build belongs to customers: ownership. Not a government monopoly. Not a sentimental return to six separate carriers. A counterweight at the choke points.

Public ownership is not a foreign fantasy. Amtrak already owns most of the Northeast Corridor. A public freight option need not carry every train. It could own strategic trackage and interchange yards, lease them on open terms, and give smaller carriers or new entrants a route the largest company cannot revoke. The hard part is that freight rail has different asset, safety, and labor economics from passenger rail. We would be building new machinery, not pointing at an existing machine and calling it proof. But the alternative to building machinery is granting private firms the power to decide who may enter and what everyone pays.

History offers more than one American answer. When states began regulating grain elevators, *Munn v. Illinois* made clear that a private warehouse was not a private kingdom. Congress created the Interstate Commerce Commission in 1887 as a referee for railroad rates. When investor-owned utilities priced whole counties out of electricity, the Rural Electrification Act of 1936 financed member-owned cooperatives. About 900 of them now serve 42 million Americans across more than half the nation’s landmass. My grandfather sat on the board of one. Nobody at that table thought they were building socialism. They thought they were keeping the lights on.

A grain elevator or Gulf Coast plant can run the same arithmetic: organize with other captive customers, buy or build the terminal or short line that ties it to the network, and finance it with patient cooperative and public capital. Conrail is the other American receipt. When the Northeast’s private freight railroads collapsed half a century ago, government stitched the wreckage into a public railroad. Conrail turned a profit under public ownership before it was privatized. Consolidation by itself is not the villain. The question was never whether railroads merge. It is who holds the pricing power when they do, and whether anyone can overrule them.

The hard part is plain. A customer-owned terminal or short line takes years and capital the market will not lend. Cooperative finance has to be built, not summoned. The coalition of unions, farmers, chemical plants, and shippers is already the customer side, organized. That is not a weakness to lament. It is an asset to build with.

The third build belongs to the people on the track. A union is a thousand workers negotiating one price together. In rail, collective bargaining must cover staffing, fatigue, work rules, and wages across the industry. Minimum safety cannot be bargained down, but everything else can be negotiated around a common floor.

Denmark does not make rail workers braver through national character. It organizes employers and workers into sector-wide bargaining, with the state at the table. The price of work is not set one frightened worker at a time. Remind me what the obstacle is again: a shortage of workers willing to organize? We have right-to-work laws, fragmented employer organizations, and rules that let nonmembers benefit from union contracts without paying for them. We do not lack unions as an idea. We lack the machinery that makes bargaining compulsory at the sector level, though wage boards can make it compulsory in narrow slices.

That machinery took the Nordic countries a century to build. It is harder here because federalism divides authority, political parties are weak, and employers can decline to bargain as a bloc. We cannot copy Denmark whole. We can start with institutions already here: merger conditions from the Surface Transportation Board, safety floors from federal regulators, public ownership where the public has a comparative advantage, and industry bargaining in the rail sector.

The original transcontinental railroad began with the Pacific Railway Act. It was built on federal land grants and government bonds. This one costs $85 billion and closes with a signature. Sign it with strings—binding rates for the captive, protected interchanges for everyone else, safe staffing for the crews—or do not sign it at all. The economy is a set of choices, not the weather. The rails already run through every town that pays the costs. The board is choosing. Build the counterweight.
