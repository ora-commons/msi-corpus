Elon Musk and Silicon Valley executives are shielding Huang from export scrutiny. The open letter they signed asks Nvidia's board to "bring Jensen back while an independent investigation establishes the facts," and it has collected more than 158,000 signatures — Tim Sweeney of Epic Games, Garry Tan of Y Combinator, Travis Kalanick, and more than 7,500 people who identified themselves as Nvidia employees, about 18 per cent of the workforce. Kalanick stated the case in its cleanest form: Huang had "erred, yes – to err is to be human – but I do not believe anything he has done rises to the bar of separating him from his company."

It is true that the private conduct is the weakest reason anyone has offered for removing a chief executive, and Kalanick is right that a founder's personal life is the kind of thing boards have always been left to weigh for themselves. It is true as well that an independent investigation is better than no investigation, and that a company whose chief executive has just left has a legitimate interest in continuity. The trouble is that the letter does not ask the board to weigh the personal life at all. It asks the board to act before the facts are established — and it does not say which facts. The internal investigation Scott Bessent asked about on Sunday is into Huang's behaviour: at least two extramarital affairs, one with a subordinate, one with a woman accused of spying for China. The questions now arriving from Washington are about silicon.

Bessent's question is narrower than the letter's and considerably harder. Not whether Nvidia's founder is a good man, but whether the company knowingly allowed its most advanced chips to reach China, and whether it "had a culture of compliance around the US export restrictions" — the demand for answers within ten days we [covered here](/articles/2026-11-15-bessent-urges-nvidia-to-hire-outside-ceo-and-answer-export-questions/). A culture is not a control. A culture is a story people tell; a control is a rule enforced, an exception recorded, and a result reviewed. The control itself is not mysterious. Sales to China of the most cutting-edge AI chips are forbidden; the line is drawn at chip performance; in late 2025 the administration authorised sales of the H200 class and, in Bessent's words, "Nothing above the H200s can be sold to the Chinese." A control drawn as a performance threshold is auditable the way a specification is auditable: you compare what shipped against what the licence permitted — chip class, end user, destination — and the paperwork either exists or it does not. No amount of affection for a founder determines which of those is the case.

Here the letter and the record are answering different questions. The letter's evidence is affection — signatures, an executive roster, a line about human error. The compliance record is classifications, licences and shipping manifests, none of which a petition can supply. Bessent said on Sunday that the export question mattered more than the affair. On that he is simply right. Members of Congress from both parties have demanded that Huang and Nvidia appear to explain whether US national security was put at risk; Huang and other officials have agreed to testify, and no date has been set.

Cory Doctorow's four forces — competition, regulation, self-help, labour — are a useful way to sort what this letter is. It is not competition: signatures do not create an alternative source of supply or a countervailing buyer. It is not self-help: a petition creates no independent route into Nvidia's shipping records. It is a play on the fourth force, the only one of the four that operates inside a company at the moment a decision gets made — 7,500 names of people who work there, offered on behalf of the man who ran it. Doctorow's argument has always been that labour counts because it sits at the point of decision. What the petition demonstrates is that the seat is available in that direction too.

Frank Pasquale's account of opaque institutions is useful on the other side of the ledger: opacity is not independence. Independence is not a title attached to an investigation; it is something the public can test through the investigator's mandate, the evidence considered, conflicts disclosed, the timetable, and the treatment of dissent. The letter asks for the sequence in reverse: appoint first, investigate later, and let the findings bless a decision already made. A board cannot outsource governance to a signature count, because a petition proves reach, not independence.

The war room is the part of this that has nothing to do with Huang's character. An ally of Huang told the *Financial Times* that they were putting together a "war room" of Silicon Valley executives to lobby for his return and would try to garner support from the White House, with Huang's backing; the same source offered the assessment that "Everyone that matters in tech outside the frontier labs loves Jensen." Read the signature list beside that and it maps a dependency network rather than a fan club: a games publisher, a startup incubator, investors — two major shareholders, Brad Gerstner and Gavin Baker, have already urged the board to keep "all options" open — and the company's own workforce. Lina Khan's argument in *Amazon's Antitrust Paradox* is that a platform's power shows up first in what it can demand of the businesses that depend on it. Read that way, the signatories are not admirers; they are the dependent side of an arrangement — Nvidia financing datacentres and startups, the whole speculative edifice critics say the company fuelled — declaring itself in writing. A war room heading for the White House is that dependency converted into political weight.

The 7,500 employee signatories should not be dismissed. They know things about morale, strategy, and internal authority that outside observers do not, and to people who work at the place the alternatives under discussion — an outside hire, a "dramatic shake-up" — are not an abstraction. A petition of 7,500 names is still a fact about hold, and hold is the currency of a fight like this. In the shop I grew up in, a petition would have been read as evidence of exactly one thing: how much of the workforce the owner could move. What the names establish is that Nvidia's workforce is willing to be counted on its founder's behalf. What they do not establish is anything about what shipped. Their petition cannot substitute for an independent report; it turns a mass expression of preference into a false finding. Those asking to be investigated cannot be left to grade the investigation.

The indispensability argument deserves its own answer. A 33-year founder can create an organisation that depends so heavily on one man's judgment and tacit knowledge that ordinary succession breaks it. If Huang is the only person capable of exercising the office, the board failed at governance before the board needed to discuss his conduct. If others can exercise it, then 158,000 signatures cannot discharge the national-security question. The claim of indispensability is not a category error; it is a warning that the institution has failed to distribute authority.

To be fair to the signatories, none of this means the board should treat every disagreement with Huang as evidence against him. He opposed constraints on the AI race and disagreed with Anthropic's Dario Amodei; those are industrial-policy positions, not proof that he broke an export rule. Bundling every disagreement into a national-security case would be intellectually dishonest — as dishonest as the opposite shortcut, that a permissive chip policy proves personal fitness. The export questions stand on classifications and manifests regardless of who wins the argument about the pace of the AI race.

The remedy is mostly a matter of sequence, and sequence is the one thing the letter gets backwards. The export answers should precede the board's decision rather than follow it: the licence classifications and shipping records should be the first documents in front of the board, and the petition the last. Congress should put a date on the testimony Huang and officials have already agreed to give, since an agreed appearance without a date is a calendar with nothing attached to it. The rule worth writing down, whichever way the board votes, is the standing one: an independent export-control compliance review with access to classification, licensing, and shipping records, its findings delivered to both the board and Congress before any reinstatement decision, and no chief executive reinstated while a federal export question about his company is still open. The sequence our own pages recorded — [Huang resigning after the board pushed him out](/articles/2026-11-13-jensen-huang-resigns-from-nvidia-after-board-ousts-him/) within days of the board's move — shows how fast this company acts when it wants to. The petition asks it to act fast in the other direction, on the one question where slowness is the whole point.

No date has been set for the testimony. Bessent's ten days started on Sunday, which makes his deadline the only clock in this story anyone has actually started. The letter offers signatures. The export regime reads manifests.
