Trump’s Justice Department is trying to abolish reparations by blocking local programs.

Joe Martin is 46 and a Delta flight attendant. He is a recipient of Evanston, Illinois’s municipal reparations program, which offers a $25,000 housing grant to Black residents whose families were harmed by discriminatory housing policies. Judicial Watch brought the case against Evanston. A federal judge has permitted the Justice Department to [intervene with its own challenge](/articles/2026-09-14-justice-department-joins-lawsuit-challenging-evanston-reparations-program/), alleging that the program violates the Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment and the Fair Housing Act.

Not a fortune. Not a luxury. A housing grant substantial enough to repair some damage and small enough for the federal government to dismiss as theft.

That is the administration’s strategy in one line: make the repair unconstitutional.

Here is the kitchen-table translation. A $25,000 housing grant and a $10,000 scholarship are not fortunes. They are targeted amounts that can interrupt two concrete forms of exclusion in the present: housing and education. The grant gives a Black resident harmed by discriminatory housing policy a foothold; the scholarship gives a descendant of an enslaved person money to pursue a degree. Neither amount pretends to erase the history. Both make repair tangible now.

The point is not to inflate modest money into a grand verdict. It is to see where the money lands. One recipient can put $25,000 toward a home affected by discriminatory housing. Another can put $10,000 toward a degree. Those are different bills, but the same principle: money transferred to descendants of people the country injured interrupts the conversion of inherited harm into present obligation.

Put housing and education on the same page, and the forced choices become legible. When a family can direct scarce cash toward housing or education, one bill can crowd out the other. The grant and the scholarship interrupt that chain. They do not solve the housing market or the history. They keep one bill from forcing the next.

Marcus Anthony Hunter says the money is not a lot of money. That makes the administration’s affordability argument true and evasive. A modest payment cannot undo chattel slavery. It is also a modest payment against centuries of dispossession and decades of exclusion. The government is using the size of the repair as proof that the repair should not exist.

Local governments cannot carry a national debt alone, and they should not be left to do it while Washington writes no check. N’nanba, Robin Rue Simmons, spent years asking what repair would actually look like. She helped create Evanston’s $25,000 housing grant, the nation’s first municipal reparations program, and is now spreading the model to more than 100 communities. Her answer has been the same: federal redress remains the collective north star. The administration has declined to provide it. Now it is trying to foreclose local action.

For the plaintiffs, the constitutional objection is concrete: Evanston selects recipients by race without identifying the specific harm each grant is meant to repair. The Justice Department says race-based government benefit programs are unconstitutional and often violate federal civil-rights law. Equality law can require scrutiny, individualization and proof. What it cannot do by itself is erase the history the remedy answers.

The sleight of hand is treating descendants of enslaved people as a racial group receiving a preference while leaving the racial system that dispossessed them outside the argument. Equality becomes the right to ignore the injury that produced the inequality.

The history is not subtle.

For more than three and a half centuries, the trans-Atlantic slave trade forced an estimated 12.5 million Africans onto ships bound for the Americas. An estimated 1.8 million did not survive the journey. Roughly 388,000 enslaved people were taken to what is now the United States, where they and their children were treated as property until abolition in 1865. Jim Crow segregation, discrimination and systemic racism further depressed Black wealth-building, leaving wealth still largely concentrated among white families.

The unfulfilled promise of 40 acres and a mule was not the only debt. America owed freedom, land and a meaningful share of the fruits of Black labor. Reparations are the argument that repairing that debt is preferable to pretending it never existed.

William Darity, who wrote a book on reparations with A. Kirsten Mullen, described the political change with precision: “I don’t think there’s been any president who’s been supportive of comprehensive Black reparations — for a long time it was just taboo to even speak the word. Now, Trump will speak the word, but with hostility.”

It is recognition stripped of responsibility.

Trump told his supporters in September that “They want to steal your money for reparations.” He has also said, “Our country doesn’t have that kind of money.” But whose money built the country? Whose labor was counted as property? Whose children inherited the restrictions, and whose grandchildren inherited the gaps?

Look at what “stealing” looks like on the ground: a $25,000 housing grant, a $10,000 scholarship, annual payments from a $2.8 million endowment. These are not fortunes. They are repairs, and they should come from the government responsible for the country that committed the injury.

Even the world has already taken the measure of that crime. In March, the U.N. General Assembly issued a resolution declaring the trafficking of millions of enslaved Africans “the gravest crime against humanity” and calling for reparations. More than 120 countries supported it. The United States cast one of only three votes against, alongside Argentina and Israel. Fifty-two countries abstained, including England and 27 members of the European Union.

The United States voted no on a question its own history had already answered.

As the country marks its 250th anniversary, [communities and historians are broadening the account of the nation’s founding](/articles/2026-07-03-as-us-marks-250-years-communities-and-historians-broaden-accounts-of-the-nation/). The administration’s campaign against race-based initiatives and Black history is narrowing the public account until there is nothing in the past that requires an answer. The anniversary becomes an occasion for celebrating freedom while making responsibility for the violence that secured it disappear.

That pressure does not stop at Evanston. Simmons is spreading the model to more than 100 communities, and the Justice Department sent warning letters last year to leaders in Asheville and Buncombe County, North Carolina, as they considered programs of their own. The federal government wants local communities to carry a national debt and then threatens them for trying.

Across higher education, new reparations efforts have stalled. Kirt von Daacke, a University of Virginia historian who leads a consortium of colleges studying slavery, says there has been no sign of new scholarship programs since Trump returned to office because institutions do not want to threaten federal research dollars.

At Harvard, days after Trump returned to the White House in January 2025 and signed an anti-DEI order aimed at universities, Harvard fired the entire team that had identified nearly 500 living descendants of people enslaved by the university’s past leaders. Research by American Ancestors continued. Harvard still has not provided redress.

Georgetown University continued considering descendants of enslaved people sold by its Jesuit priests to Louisiana slaveholders. Juan Woolfolk, 36, received a $10,000 scholarship indirectly through a foundation for descendants and is using it to pursue museum studies at Southern University at New Orleans. He calls it a “tremendous opportunity,” especially as the nation’s presentation of its past comes under attack.

Some Virginia universities have quietly taken steps to provide scholarships to descendants, as required under a 2021 state law. The Virginia Theological Seminary makes annual cash payments, with no strings attached, to descendants whose family members labored on its campus during slavery and Jim Crow eras. Its endowment has grown from $1.7 million to $2.8 million. Payments vary according to the closest family lineage and continue in perpetuity.

The seminary’s director of reparations, Ebonee Davis-Hayes, put the point beyond arithmetic: “The moral repair, the healing aspect is as great as the monetary one.”

Marcus Anthony Hunter, a sociologist at UCLA, said reparations are about atoning for the past crime of chattel slavery, even when programs take the form of housing grants or college scholarships rather than direct cash payments. The money is modest. The recognition, acknowledgment, responsibility and remedy are the point.

The people receiving that repair are not abstractions.

Joe Martin was pleasantly surprised by the grant. He has seen how American history has ignored the contributions of Black Americans and the terror they endured. Now the administration is trying to stop the program he benefits from.

“We all know the history, 40 acres and a mule didn’t go down the way it was supposed to,” Martin said. So someone establishes a legitimate program, and “the government’s like, naw.”

Read as an economic document, Taylor Swift’s “You’re On Your Own, Kid” names the retreat the administration is building: the national government recedes, and local institutions and families absorb the consequences. Evanston, Georgetown, the Virginia universities and the Virginia Theological Seminary are refusing that assigned aloneness. They are building the common infrastructure the country refuses to fund.

The repair will not be complete, and no grant can undo chattel slavery. But a country can name the crime, accept responsibility and make a remedy. The Justice Department has joined Judicial Watch’s case against one program it could have left alone. It has called modest repair theft. It has made institutional survival depend upon silence. A government can block a grant without erasing the debt. It cannot make the history disappear by demanding that everyone else stop looking at it.

At my kitchen table, an unpaid bill does not become history because the envelope is closed. It becomes the reason the next month is tighter. The repair is still small. The refusal is not.
