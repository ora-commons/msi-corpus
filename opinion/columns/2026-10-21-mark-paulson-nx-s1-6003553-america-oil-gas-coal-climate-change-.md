The Trump administration is abandoning Adams County families to fossil-fuel heat.

The place to see federal energy policy become a household expense is the Adams-Columbia Electric Cooperative. Headquartered in Friendship, ACEC buys wholesale power through Dairyland Power Cooperative and delivers it to homes, farms, dairies, and businesses across Adams County. My own house and one-man shop sit on that system. The co-op is member-owned. Washington sets the fuel and tax rules. ACEC members still pay for the power.

The cooperative model came out of a deliberate federal choice. The Rural Electrification Act of 1936 put long-term loans behind member-owned electric utilities when rural service did not look profitable enough for private companies. Fewer than 11 percent of farms had electricity then; by 1950, about 80 percent did. The public carried the capital because the country decided rural homes were not too poor to wire. The members owned what stood on the line.

The new United Nations report gives that local machinery a harder edge. In 2025, global greenhouse-gas emissions were essentially unchanged while American emissions rose sharply. The United States was the only major economy where pollution increased dramatically. The report attributed the rise to growing electricity demand and Trump administration policies: coal plants kept open longer than planned, vehicle fuel-efficiency standards rolled back, and tax credits for solar and wind ended.

For ACEC, none of that is a chart on a distant computer screen. Rising demand means Dairyland must secure more generation, capacity, and transmission. The federal choices change the economics surrounding what ACEC buys. Keeping coal plants alive can add fixed costs. Gas can supply dispatchable capacity, but it carries fuel-price risk. Ending clean-energy tax credits raises the hurdle for new solar and wind. Rolling back vehicle efficiency does not touch the co-op’s meter directly, but it raises what families and farmers pay at the pump.

The chain from Washington to a kitchen table is short. Federal policy changes demand and the cost of supply. Dairyland’s board decides which generation to buy and what infrastructure to build. ACEC then decides what it pays for wholesale power, how it maintains its own lines, and how much it must collect from members. The co-op cannot pretend it has three harmless choices: raise rates, defer needed investment, or take more exposure in the wholesale market. A rate increase lands across ACEC’s bills. Deferred investment becomes a reliability problem. More exposure to a fuel market becomes a price problem later.

That last part matters on the farm. Milking cannot wait for a cheaper hour. Milk has to stay cold. Barns need ventilation. Manure pumps have to run. During a hot week, air conditioners and well pumps add to peak demand, and a failed line or transformer puts milk, livestock, and stored food at risk.

If the present energy path continues, ACEC households will pay more for power supply, delivery, and shared infrastructure when the system must buy or build fossil-backed capacity. Dairy farmers will carry that increase in the cost of cooling, pumping, processing, and keeping barns alive. Motorists will pay more at the pump as weaker fuel-efficiency rules make new vehicles use more fuel. And after heat or flood damage, Adams County taxpayers will carry more of the response through emergency management, road work, public health, and Adams County Memorial Hospital.

That is not saying every rate increase or every storm is the fault of one Washington order. Electricity is a real business, and storm cleanup is expensive. The point is the direction of the decision and who receives the bill.

Main Street Independent previously documented [the U.S. gas-fired power buildout outpacing China amid the AI data-center buildout](/articles/2026-08-25-us-gas-fired-power-buildout-outpaces-china-amid-ai-datacenter-buildout/). That national direction reaches ACEC through the regional grid and wholesale market. Large new loads compete for generation and transmission. If gas supplies the new capacity, ACEC members take its fuel, operating, and infrastructure costs. If clean supply is crowded out or delayed, rural members are left financing a more fuel-exposed system.

The United States also tied Russia for the highest per-capita greenhouse-gas emissions among G20 countries. China emitted more in total, but its population is more than four times the size of the American population. ACEC has no answer to that arithmetic. A co-op cannot solve the climate crisis by itself, but its members can see what their own power purchases add to the total. The American outlier is not some foreign abstraction. It is a country choosing coal, gas, and oil while telling its own electric members to carry more of the bill.

Nor can ACEC wait for a better global bargain. About two-thirds of countries have submitted current emissions plans under the Paris agreement. If every one of those countries keeps every promise, the plans would cut global emissions by about 15 percent over the next decade. The United Nations report says a cut of roughly 25 percent is needed to keep warming below 3.6 degrees Fahrenheit.

Those percentages become local procurement choices, efficiency investments, building rules, grid plans, and rate decisions. If governments deliver only the promised 15 percent, ACEC members will still live under the higher fuel costs and infrastructure burden that follow. A utility cannot schedule its capital plan for the day international politics improves.

The gap is no longer a warning that can be filed away. Main Street Independent previously reported that [a United Nations assessment had acknowledged Earth would exceed the Paris climate threshold](/articles/2026-09-02-un-report-acknowledges-earth-will-exceed-paris-climate-threshold/). The new report says the present course points toward as much as 5 degrees Fahrenheit of warming this century, with catastrophic consequences including mass sea-level rise and the mass extinction of plants and animals.

For Adams County, 5 degrees is not a line in a diplomatic communiqué. It means higher peak electricity demand, more heat-related illness, flood response on county roads, added pressure on bridges, culverts, and public water systems, and more work for Adams County Emergency Management and the county hospital. These are public costs before they become private bills.

The Environmental Protection Agency now says greenhouse gases do not threaten human health or welfare. That position does not govern the weather. It only lowers the standard public officials use when they decide how much heat, flood recovery, and grid hardening a county must handle.

Wendell Berry’s warning in “The Unsettling of America” was that communities lose themselves when decisions and returns leave while the costs stay. ACEC was built against that: local members own the system. The federal retreat can turn that local institution into a collection point for distant fuel choices.

ACEC and Dairyland still have room to act. ACEC can buy efficiency and dependable lower-carbon supply, support community solar and storage, and put generation choices and rate impacts in front of members before purchases become bills. Wisconsin regulators can require new plant and transmission costs to be assigned plainly rather than buried in rates. The question is not whether every federal dollar is good or bad. It is who owns the wire, who sets the price, and who has to live with the result.

Washington can stay out of Turkey. It cannot keep the bill out of Adams County.

The leaders meeting there will discuss clean-energy investment and who pays for climate damage. America will be absent. If the present path continues, ACEC households will pay the power bill, farms will pay for reliable electricity and fuel, and county taxpayers will pay for the damage. The furnace does not recognize a political exemption. It sends the bill home anyway.
