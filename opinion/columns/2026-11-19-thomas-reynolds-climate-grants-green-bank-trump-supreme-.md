The Trump administration is stealing sixteen billion dollars of public money.

The administration’s strongest case is jurisdictional, not substantive. The Tucker Act, 28 U.S.C. § 1491, sends contract claims against the United States to the Court of Federal Claims, a specialized court that can award money but cannot order EPA to resume a grant program or administer its climate portfolio. If the awards are contracts, EPA has a plausible claim that the case belongs there.

EPA also invokes sovereign immunity—the government’s protection from suit—and says the nonprofits filed in the wrong court. Climate United Fund and the other operators answer that this is not an action to enforce a contract. Their lawsuit attacks EPA’s cancellation of awards made under a program Congress created and funded. At its strongest, the government’s position says Congress repealed the money not already obligated, the remaining disputes are contract claims, and the Tucker Act governs. Most of the filing is contestable but recognizable craft. Two load-bearing pieces are not.

The first is what the forum argument actually does. The government tells the Court this is a contract dispute belonging in the Court of Federal Claims. In the same litigation, EPA maintains that it is immune from legal and constitutional claims altogether. Both positions are the government’s. The nonprofits demand access to the awarded funds. Whether a money judgment can substitute for an order releasing them is the legal question the petition presents. What the filing assembles is a courthouse of the government’s choosing, a remedy it describes as money, and an immunity claim held in reserve against every other route. That is not the Tucker Act doing its ordinary work. It is forum selection recruited into the taking.

The factual record begins with the money. The 2022 Inflation Reduction Act created the $27 billion Greenhouse Gas Reduction Fund, the climate program commonly called the “green bank.” The Biden administration awarded $16 billion to Climate United Fund and other nonprofit operators selected to run it. EPA canceled the grants soon after Donald Trump’s second term began in January 2025. Administrator Lee Zeldin accused the groups of mismanagement and potential fraud. Last year, he told Fox News that he suspected the green bank “was a clear-cut case of waste and abuse.”

On fraud, the record is fatal to the government’s stated reason. U.S. District Judge Tanya Chutkan said that when the government asked for evidence of fraud, EPA provided none and shifted its position to general concerns about the program’s level of oversight. She said the government could not terminate the contracts in violation of law and that the nonprofits should have access to some of their frozen money, held in an account at Citibank.

The government had a stated fraud case. It failed to substantiate it. Then it changed the subject.

In August, a divided federal appeals court in Washington sided with the nonprofits, said the administration improperly terminated the grants, and paused disbursement to give the Supreme Court time to consider review. The petition now asks the Court to uphold the cancellations.

That forum argument has teeth. The Court has long routed contract claims against the United States to the Court of Federal Claims because splitting them between that court and ordinary federal courts would produce competing judgments and duplicated proceedings. It reaffirmed that rule in *United States v. Navajo Nation*, 542 U.S. 200 (2004). If these grants are contracts, EPA has a plausible claim that the Court of Federal Claims must hear them. A court cannot cure that error by hearing the contract claim in the wrong place.

EPA’s jurisdictional turn does not repair the reasoning. If the cancellations are agency action, the Administrative Procedure Act requires a reviewing court to set aside action that is “arbitrary, capricious, an abuse of discretion, or otherwise not in accordance with law.” 5 U.S.C. § 706(2)(A). A cancellation theory announced after the alleged fraud produced no evidence cannot replace a reasoned explanation tied to the grants and the statute. An objection over the proper court cannot do EPA’s merits work for it.

Congress’s rescission—the repeal—of part of the green-bank statute and the money not already obligated is the government’s best answer based on the law’s text. It narrows what the program can spend. It does not establish that EPA may cancel awards already made. And it does not answer the question that matters to the recipients: why may the agency destroy an award it made under the program? The government’s position asks the Court to accept its characterization because its original accusation failed.

This is not one grant docket. The $16 billion was authorized to finance clean-energy projects and accelerate the transition away from fossil fuels. EPA calls the dispute the largest grant-cancellation case ever. The administration’s filing says a favorable Supreme Court outcome could help it defeat other grant lawsuits. The pattern is visible elsewhere. Administrator Zeldin [called the climate rules a “war on coal” at the G20](https://mainstreetindependent.com/articles/2026-09-14-zeldin-calls-climate-rules-war-on-coal-at-g20-as-epa-ends-power-plant-limits/) while [EPA moved to repeal every greenhouse-gas standard for power plants](https://mainstreetindependent.com/articles/2026-09-14-epa-moves-to-repeal-all-greenhouse-gas-standards-for-power-plants/). A [21-state coalition has sued](https://mainstreetindependent.com/articles/2026-10-01-new-york-led-coalition-of-21-states-sues-epa-over-power-plant-rule-repeal/) over the repeal. The agency is closing the climate framework from two directions—repealing the rule and canceling the money.

The legal structure survives substitution. Exchange the green bank for the canceled National Institutes of Health grants, the teacher-training grants, or any appropriation a later administration finds inconvenient, and the load-bearing analysis remains: an executive branch cancels awards, asserts jurisdiction over the resulting claims, invokes immunity against the alternative theories, and asks the Supreme Court to make that arrangement harder for the next plaintiff to defeat. This is not about the atmosphere. It is about the treasury. An agency that spends the year attacking climate rules while canceling the money is not policing bookkeeping. It is running a cancellations program, and it wants the Supreme Court to hand that program a precedent.

The Court has not said whether it will take the case. Certiorari—the Court’s discretionary gate on review—has produced one visible pattern already: the justices recently ruled for the administration in separate disputes involving canceled NIH grants and teacher-training grants. EPA is steering this $16 billion dispute onto the same track. Its filing makes the broader purpose explicit. A win could help defeat other grant lawsuits.

Seven years on this beat, I have not before covered an administration asking the Supreme Court to certify that arrangement. A rule letting an executive keep awarded program money on policy grounds is not a rule for one administration. It binds whoever inherits the pen, which is why the same standard applies now, without a partisan discount.

The Court’s recent record does not supply the shortcut. In *Collins v. Yellen*, 594 U.S. 220 (2021), the Court invalidated Congress’s removal protection around the Federal Housing Finance Agency—the limits on the President’s power to fire its director—but left the agency’s financial arrangement intact because the plaintiffs lacked a cause of action, a legally recognized claim permitting the requested relief. *Collins* is not authority for keeping awarded grant money in the executive’s hands. It records the Court separating a structural ruling from the money: one question answered, the other left open for lack of a claim that carried it. The petition asks for the inverse—the money kept, and the relief question routed to the forum the government chose for it.

The money was authorized to finance clean-energy projects and accelerate the transition away from fossil fuels—a purpose Congress wrote into statute. The government’s forum argument treats program and purpose as irrelevant. Bob Wagman, president of the Court of Federal Claims Bar Association, called the administration’s broad use of contract cancellations to enact policy “a novel approach.” He said, “I would hate to think that the only programs that could be supported have a four-year life,” mentioning cancer research as an example of work that cannot responsibly belong to one administration. “It is just not good for the country.”

The Court should decide the proper forum without deciding EPA’s case for it. If the claims are contract claims, the Court of Federal Claims must adjudicate them. If the claims are statutory or constitutional claims outside the Tucker Act, they belong in ordinary federal courts, where EPA must still confront its sovereign-immunity defense. In either forum, EPA must prove that the Inflation Reduction Act, the terms of the awards, and the later rescission authorize the cancellations. Jurisdiction allocates power. If it validates cancellations without proof, it becomes the instrument of grant cancellation rather than its boundary.

If the Court declines review, the nonprofits can regain access to their funds. If it grants review and upholds the cancellations, the ruling would make grant termination the fastest presidential tool for erasing a congressionally funded program. The disputed money sits frozen in a Citibank account while the green bank waits behind a petition to bless the taking.

Congress wrote the appropriation. The executive awarded it. Two courts called the termination unlawful. The administration’s answer is a forum of its own choosing, a money remedy, and sovereign immunity behind it. This is not a dispute about the right courthouse. It is theft with an immunity claim attached, and the Court is asked to bless it.
