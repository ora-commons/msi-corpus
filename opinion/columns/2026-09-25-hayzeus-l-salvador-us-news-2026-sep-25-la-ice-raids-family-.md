ICE tore a father from his children and called the cruelty enforcement.

For six months, every night at bedtime in a house in Watts, a seven-year-old boy named after his father asked his mother the same question in the dark: "Why did they take my dad away?"

His mother held him and gave him the only honest answer she had, which was not much of an answer at all.

On the morning of January 27, 2026, the father, 41, left home to pump gas. His partner handed him two burritos and a coffee and told him to eat. By afternoon, Immigration and Customs Enforcement had taken him into custody in downtown Los Angeles. He had lived in the United States for more than 20 years. He had raised four children in Watts. He worked washing cars.

The family had talked about sharing locations in case a raid came. They had never rehearsed what to do if it actually did.

He was one of more than 1,400 people detained in the Los Angeles area that January, according to the Deportation Data Project. He was one of at least 373 car-wash workers detained between June 2025 and January 2026, according to data compiled by the Clean Carwash Worker Center.

Those numbers do not erase the man. They show the machinery.

A workplace became a trap. A father became a case number. A child became the keeper of a question no seven-year-old should have to carry into sleep.

The car-wash industry has long depended on undocumented labor, low wages, dangerous conditions, and employers willing to evade tax and labor law. The people who built that arrangement are not the children who wait at the empty chair. The people who profit from precarious labor are not the mothers calculating whether groceries or legal fees come first.

This is where our own complicity begins. We have washed our cars, eaten our food, and accepted cheap services while treating the workers who provide them as disposable. The system did not appear from nowhere. We built a country that wanted the labor and feared the laborer, then called the resulting human being illegal.

The Torah does not permit that word to become a moral eraser. "You shall not wrong or oppress a resident alien, for you were aliens in the land of Egypt." Leviticus goes further: "The alien who resides with you shall be to you as the citizen among you; you shall love the alien as yourself."

Jesus made the test more severe. "I was a stranger and you welcomed me." Then he gave the warning: "I was a stranger and you did not welcome me."

The question is not whether a government may administer an immigration system. It may. The question is what kind of system we have chosen to administer, whom it treats as expendable, and what we call the damage after we have caused it.

Inside the detention facilities—first Adelanto, then California City in Kern County—the father refused to fold. He got a job in the detention-center kitchen because he did not want to drown in depression. Any money he made, he told his partner, would buy soups and snacks so she would not have to put money on his commissary account.

This is who he had been at home. This is who he kept being.

The family carried the separation in different bodies. The seven-year-old tried to be a tough little kid. The toddler turned toward the empty space beside his mother and whispered, "Me hace falta mi papá." The seventeen-year-old stepson, who has autism and relied on his father for bathing and school meetings, needed therapy. The children's mother learned new ways to walk them through the feelings that arrived at bedtime, on the school bus, and at the empty chair during dinner.

The eldest wrote the immigration judge a letter about how much he missed his father and how badly he wanted to see him in real life, not through a phone or a tablet.

A 2020 study in Pima County, Arizona, found that an immigration arrest costs the average household more than $24,000 in lost income, legal fees, seized belongings, and other expenses. Those are the spreadsheet costs.

The real cost is the boy asking the same question into the dark for half a year.

A University of California, Riverside School of Medicine report led by Dr. Lisa Fortuna described the enforcement actions as a "public health emergency" for children. Dr. Arthur Evans Jr., chief executive of the American Psychological Association, has said that detention, deportation, and the constant threat of both produce chronic stress that increases anxiety, depression, trauma-related symptoms, and long-term health risks. The Children's Equity Project at Arizona State University has described separation as a source of toxic stress.

These authorities do not need to replace my judgment. They confirm it. Separating a child from a parent is cruel. Making that separation ordinary is a social sin.

I know the Catholic Church has no clean hands here. American Catholics have defended "law and order" when it meant disorder for the poor. We have accepted the comfort of national borders while asking migrants to bear the costs of the labor and consumption that sustain our comfort. The church has preached welcome and sometimes delivered suspicion. We have all known the long loneliness. We have not always built the community that could end it.

Óscar Romero did not heal injustice lightly. "In the name of God," he said, "in the name of this suffering people," he begged, pleaded, and ordered: "Stop the repression!"

The order belongs here too.

Stop tearing fathers from children and calling the wound administrative. Stop treating detention as a neutral waiting room when the waiting enters a child's nervous system, a mother's bills, a teenager's therapy, and a toddler's sleep. Stop making "due process" carry the moral weight of a process that begins by placing a family in crisis.

On August 4, 2026, six months after his detention, the father walked out. His children waited for him at Union Station in Los Angeles. The Immigrant Defenders Law Center, which helped with the family's case, captured the reunion.

His partner rounded the corner with her sons and said, "Who's that, papa?"

The boy did not answer in words. He ran.

The family that had been a great team before January 27 became a great team again. That is not a small thing. It is not a poster. It is not a speech. It is a child putting his arms around his father after the state took six months of their life.

Across the country, families separated by the same machinery are clawing their way back to one another: a [Maryland teenager crossing a graduation stage](/articles/2026-06-06-maryland-teen-graduates-high-school-after-ice-deported-his-father/) without his father in the audience; a [Montana mechanic walking out of a county jail](/articles/2026-05-16-montana-mechanic-walks-free-after-more-than-100-days-in-jail-on-immigration-char/) after more than 100 days inside; a [Los Angeles city carrying scars and reaching for resilience](/articles/2026-06-14-one-year-after-la-ice-raids-a-city-carries-scars-and-reaches-for-resilience/).

One family at a time. One release at a time. One bedtime question answered at a time.

The father's case is not finished. The GoFundMe for attorney fees remains open. The children are not healed because a door opened at Union Station. The law must provide more than reunion. It must provide safety, status, repair, and a system that does not manufacture the wound and then congratulate itself when the family survives it.

The Catholic vision is not a nation without law. It is a nation whose law recognizes the human person before it recognizes the category. It is a nation where the stranger is not prey, where work does not require invisibility, and where children do not have to become tough enough to survive the removal of their parents.

"The Gospel takes away our right forever," Dorothy Day wrote, "to discriminate between the deserving and the undeserving poor."

The father did not need to become deserving before his children deserved him.

He was already a person. They were already a family.

And the door, when it finally opened, should never have had to be closed.
