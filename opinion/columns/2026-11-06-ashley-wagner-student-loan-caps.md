Donald Trump is selling the next generation of doctors, nurses, and lawyers to Sallie Mae. The loan caps in his Big Beautiful Bill took effect this fall, and the students they squeeze did not stop needing tuition. They went to the private market. Same policy, two ledgers: the financial aid office calls it a crisis, and the originations desk calls it a record quarter. SoFi reported $2.9 billion in student loan originations for the third quarter, nearly double a year earlier. Sallie Mae, which already controls more than half of the private student loan market, reported graduate volume up 119 percent year-over-year. The federal guarantee of access to the professions did not shrink. It changed hands, and the handoff was already on record by [late October](/articles/2026-10-22-federal-loan-caps-expand-private-student-lending/), before the first capped class finished a semester — MSI has been on this since [the limits were set to take effect July 1](/articles/2026-06-28-new-graduate-loan-limits-take-effect-july-1-as-economists-debate-tuition-impact/), and the origination numbers are the debate settling itself.

Here is what the bill does at the level of a tuition bill. Professional-degree programs — medicine, law, dentistry — are capped at $200,000 in federal borrowing. Other graduate programs are capped at $100,000. New borrowers face a lifetime cap of $257,500, and for the first time the law limits what parents can borrow on top of that. Before this fall, a graduate student could borrow the full cost of the degree from the federal program, on federal terms. Jordan Matsudaira, director of American University's Postsecondary Education & Economics Research Center, estimates that once the caps fully phase in, more than half a million borrowers will need an extra $20,000 a year from somewhere other than the federal government, concentrated in medicine, law and dentistry and in the high-cost states where the training happens. The gap between the old limit and the new one did not disappear. Gaps don't. It moved — onto SoFi's books, onto Sallie Mae's, onto South Carolina Student Loan's.

The kitchen-table version of $20,000 a year: it is about seventy percent of what we pay for childcare for two kids. $2,400 a month, $28,800 a year, the number that decides every month whether our spreadsheet closes. Multiply it across a multi-year program and you get $40,000, $60,000, $80,000 of training debt that used to sit on protected terms and now sits on a lender's underwriting — priced at the exact moment a new graduate's salary is smallest relative to what she owes. The federal deal had income-driven repayment that bent to income, the possibility of forgiveness after ten years in public service and, for many borrowers, lower interest rates. The private deal has credit checks, a cosigner, and none of the above — an underwriting model.

That is the actual change. It is not the price of school. It is who decides who gets to sit in the seat. Under the federal program, access was a matter of statute: borrow up to cost, repay by income. Under the new arrangement, access is a matter of credit. Chloe Frabotta, a 25-year-old physician assistant student in New York, borrowed about $6,000 in federal loans for her first trimester and covered the rest privately, with her mother as cosigner. "At least in my circles a lot of people were like, 'I guess that's what I'm going to have to do, and I'm going to try to make it work,'" she said. The shrug is the policy. The "try to make it work" is what the policy leaves to the borrower — and it is the sound of the policy working: for the lender, on the student. Steven Freedman, associate dean of admissions at the University of Kansas School of Law — which responded to the caps by getting into the lending business itself, eight loans approved so far, more in process — put it without decoration: "Now, frankly, private banks get to choose who goes to law school." Dr. Robert Harrington, dean of Weill Cornell Medicine and himself a first-generation college graduate, said what every admissions office now knows: "What we don't want to see is our programs just becoming the purview of people whose family can afford to pay that bill."

Anne Helen Petersen spent a whole book — *Can't Even* — on how this cohort and the one before it were trained to understand themselves as human capital: walking résumés, investments compoundable by effort. That cohort taught this one the method, and the caps weaponize the training: these students are doing exactly the math they were taught to do, and the math now returns one answer — go ahead, if your family can float you. The caps finish the thought. An investment requires a sponsor, and the sponsor is now a formal underwriting requirement with your mother's name on it. A cosigner is a parent with good credit. Savings are parents who could help. I'll flag what that means in my own house: our down payment came out of David's grandmother's estate, and my parents handled my financial aid paperwork because they knew how — I have since learned how much of that season's terror is simply not knowing what the forms are asking, and first-generation students carry that alone. Frabotta's cosigner is the same inheritance, converted from a family advantage into an eligibility criterion. The students without one are not a rounding error. They are the point at which the professions start narrowing.

The administration's case is that uncapped federal lending inflated tuition and the caps will force schools to face their own prices. There is a true half in there. Graduate PLUS-era borrowing did coincide with a tuition spiral, and borrowing more than a degree can repay is a real default risk. The American Enterprise Institute counts at least 40 schools that have cut the cost of some programs since the caps took effect. But a cap sets a ceiling on the borrower, not on the sticker — nothing in the bill compels a school to cut a single dollar, and those forty cuts were voluntary, which means the next one is too. Forty programs is a rounding error against the size of American higher education; it is a news cycle, not a price system. A ceiling on the student does not make an unaffordable program affordable — it just changes who is allowed to attempt it, and on whose credit.

Now restore the variable the argument leaves out: follow the volumes. If cost discipline were the mechanism, the observable would be falling tuition across higher education. The observable is lender origination. SoFi's originations nearly doubled. Sallie Mae's graduate book is up 119 percent. South Carolina Student Loan reports graduate borrowing up 400 percent this year. A law school went into the lending business. The $20,000-a-year gap does not return to students as savings. It routes to lenders, at rates and terms the federal program existed to prevent, and the students get less protection for more money. The lender ELFI's general manager says the number of students who truly cannot find an option is "a lot smaller than many initially feared." Found an option is doing a lot of work in that sentence. The question was never whether a lender exists. It is what the option costs, what it protects, and who it excludes. Annie Lowrey's argument about American poverty — that a country this rich has poverty because it chooses to — transfers here with the nouns swapped: the issue is not that the country cannot finance its future nurses and lawyers on public terms. It is that it has chosen not to. If the caps are about cost discipline, their authors owe us the mechanism by which the savings reach students. On this record, the savings land at Sallie Mae.

Here is the part the "excessive borrowing" argument leaves out: the same bill that installed the caps also dismantled the repayment system that made federal debt survivable in the first place. The SAVE plan — the income-driven plan millions enrolled in — was struck down in court and then terminated by statute, and borrowers report their required payments tripling as they're pushed into the law's replacements. The door narrows while the net under the tightrope is cut down. Follow the cost and it lands where costs in this economy reliably land: on the student, and on the family underneath her. Follow the volume and it lands on an earnings call, where Sallie Mae's CEO told investors the chaotic rollout had stretched peak lending season into October. The line item that doesn't show up in the press release is who's paying for the volume. A tuition squeeze that reads as good news on a lender's earnings call is working exactly as designed. The design just wasn't drawn for the student.

The execution tells you how much discipline was ever the point. The legal fight over which programs count as "professional" — and therefore qualify for the higher limits — ran until days before the caps took effect. A federal judge had to block the administration's rule that would have held nursing and physical therapy students to the lower limits, and the rescue arrived so late that schools delayed aid packages and the lending season stretched into October. When Frabotta's program was finally designated professional — making her eligible for up to $50,000 a year in federal loans — she deliberately borrowed only a fraction of it, because she doesn't trust what the terms will look like by graduation. That is not budgeting. That is threat assessment. When the stable option is the one students are afraid to use, instability itself is the tax.

None of this is new, and the record is the indictment. The last time the federal door narrowed, the private student loan market boomed through the 1990s and early 2000s under an earlier generation of loan limits, and it collapsed only when Graduate PLUS swung the door back open and extended federal borrowing to the full cost of attendance. Critics blame that expansion for the default rates that followed — students borrowing past what they could ever repay. Take the criticism at full strength and it still convicts the caps' authors: debts were outgrowing wages even on protected terms, which is an indictment of a shredded repayment floor, not of a public door — and repayment protection is exactly what the same bill tore out. Whoever holds the door holds the professions. Before that, the arrangement was more explicit still: for years Washington guaranteed federal loans and paid private banks to deliver them — a subsidy scheme the Congressional Budget Office scored in the tens of billions before Congress ended it in 2010. Pell Grants covered roughly 80 percent of the cost of a four-year public university at their mid-1970s peak. Today they cover roughly a quarter. I grew up inside the version that worked — my father's postal supervisor income carried three kids through Catholic high school in Lansdale, and the mortgage was paid off in 2007 — and that arithmetic sat on top of a public stack my cohort inherits in name only. Each round, the public guarantee narrows, the private channel grows, and the collateral quietly becomes the family balance sheet.

Heather McGhee traced the signature move: this country built the public goods, then drained the pools rather than share them. Higher education ran the same play on its own clock — open the gates first, then drain the fund — state appropriations for public universities giving way to tuition dollars, year after year. The caps are the same move at the graduate level: close the public option, capitalize the difference into private lenders' books. The beneficiaries report quarterly. The costs have faces, and this story puts both on the same page. I keep coming back to my mother. She trained as a nurse and spent her career as the nurse at a Catholic school in Lansdale — a job a working-class family could point a daughter toward, on one postal salary, under a tuition math that used to work. The road she took into the white coat is now priced with a cosigner's signature. I sat at my own kitchen table at eleven at night in the spring of 2022 and watched four versions of the same household budget refuse to balance; the column that used to read "what the government lends you on protected terms" now reads, for half a million people, "what your mother can cosign." The math is the math.

The door is already closing on specific people. Madison Jessen, a first-year physician assistant student at Weill Cornell, paid her first semester largely out of savings and says several younger friends who had planned on PA school pivoted when the caps landed. We were documenting that deterrence in [June](/articles/2026-06-25-new-federal-loan-caps-deter-would-be-physician-assistants-groups-say/), before the first capped student enrolled. This is how a profession gets rationed without anyone announcing it: not by an exam, not by a quota — by an underwriting file. The nurse who was not hired, the lawyer who did not enroll, and no press release for either.

Taylor Swift titled the policy summary of American care infrastructure four years ago: "You're On Your Own, Kid." The bill has written the title into lending policy. It was a diagnosis when she wrote it. It is a loan disclosure now. The song's only safety net is the lateral one — friendship bracelets, the person who shows up — and the private loan is that net rendered as a credit file, your mother's good standing standing in for the state's. The students in this story are living the title — rationing their own eligibility out of distrust, emptying savings accounts, walking away from nursing and PA school — and the only safety net left is the friends in the group text trading lender names like friendship bracelets. The lateral net is still being woven by whoever is nearest: Lewis & Clark's graduate school of education and counseling began offering a need scholarship this fall for students the caps squeezed, and Mount Saint Vincent in the Bronx boosted aid even as more of its first-year students leaned on private loans. The net still gets built — the state has just quit the job.

There is a pro-family version of this, and the policy shops on both sides sketched it years ago: keep the federal guarantee up to the full cost of a degree; aim discipline at interest rates and sticker prices — or tie caps to program outcomes, so schools charging six figures answer for their graduates' defaults — instead of at access; protect income-driven repayment as the floor under every federal borrower, not a bargaining chip struck down and swapped every eighteen months; restore Pell toward the coverage it actually had; and fund the paid leave and childcare support that has been sitting scored and ready while Congress walks past it. The bill that passed does the opposite of all four. That agenda keeps losing to the version with a revenue stream. So the person who draws your blood in 2035, the one who argues your case in court — each will be selected by an underwriting model before any committee sees the file. The distance between the doctor we needed and the doctor we get is a parent's signature.

I closed my spreadsheet that night in 2022 and started writing. The students in this story are closing theirs and signing wherever a signature will hold. That is the sale, completed one cosigner at a time: the public road into the middle-class professions, taken from the public and resold to whoever's family can cover the entry fee.
