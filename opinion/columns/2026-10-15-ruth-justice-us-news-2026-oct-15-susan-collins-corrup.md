The senior senator from Maine, a Republican up for re-election in November, stood before reporters this week and offered four words meant to make the story go away: "completely false," she said, of allegations that she traded federal contracts for campaign cash.

It is the response of a woman who has been caught.

The receipts are not in dispute. Martin Kao — former CEO of the defense contractor Navatek, now serving 87 months for fraud and campaign finance crimes — told FBI agents and federal prosecutors that his company bankrolled politicians to secure millions in federal funding, and that Susan Collins was his most important patron. Kao said the head of Collins's super PAC asked him for $500,000 in 2019 and that he routed $150,000 through a shell company. That same year the Senate allocated at least $10 million based on Navatek proposals, after Collins's office promised to help secure $32 million in naval contracts — a figure never awarded. And when Kao presented the shell-company plan, Collins's super PAC chair, Scott Reed, replied two words: "Very smart." Then he asked for Kao's phone number so the senator could thank him.

Collins has an answer for every line of it, and she has been delivering it calmly for years. The University of Maine, not a contractor, asked her to back the research. She had never heard of Navatek. The man at the center of the file is a convicted fraudster who, when he got caught, began shopping a story designed to shorten his sentence. Three agencies — the Justice Department, the FBI, the Federal Election Commission — looked at the same allegations a Maine lawyer recycled last week and walked away. Nobody in her orbit was charged. Not her. Not her staff. Not Scott Reed, who flatly denied ever discussing Kao with Collins or her team. There is even a photograph of Kao meeting with Collins's office months before he wrote a single check — a photograph her side says buries the timeline his lawyers have been selling since 2020.

That is her case, stated at its strongest. It is also where the story gets interesting, because the case is built entirely on what did not happen, and it leaves out the part nobody wants to print: the reason nothing happened.

Here is the part. The FBI did try to open a broader bribery investigation in 2024. That inquiry collapsed only after Donald Trump returned to office and purged the bureau's public corruption teams — the very squads whose job is to follow money like this. Collins was never interviewed because the bureau's work was cut short, not because the bureau found nothing. There is a difference between "no charges filed" and "no crime." She is asking Maine voters to pretend the difference does not exist.

Read the underlying investigative record. Read the 87-month sentence. Read the photograph. Then ask what a sentence like that teaches a man about cooperation: he tells the government what the government wants, and the government decides what to test and what to shelve. That is the machinery, and it runs whether the cooperator is telling the truth or telling a lie. The only way to know which is an investigation. Not a press conference, not a debate stage, and not a senator's four words.

It is a familiar play. Call the ethics complaint the work of "partisan Democrats." Mock the Maine attorney who filed it, Valerie Adams, for losing her June primary to be district attorney. Wave away the account as something a crook "spun up." Repeat the line — "recycled from 2020," "rejected by the DOJ and the FEC" — until reporters stop asking. It worked once. It is working again. The harder she fights the question, the more her constituents should ask why she is fighting it.

Collins has spent decades cultivating the pose of a moderate who simply cannot abide the rough edges of her party. Last week, in the Maine Senate debate, she distanced herself from Trump. Fine. But the same Trump who handed her the talking points is the one who cleared the field. The senator cannot take the Trump whitewash and reject the Trump obstruction; the two are the same transaction.

The complaint that landed last week is not a Democratic hit job. It is a Maine lawyer asking the Senate Ethics Committee to do the work the FBI was building toward before the ground was cleared. Collins could welcome that inquiry. She could open her office records, name her donors, and let an independent body walk the receipt trail for herself.

She will not. She will call the inquiry "partisan," insist she was never interviewed, and campaign on the fiction that the silence around her is the same thing as exoneration. By November she may well win again — she has held this seat for decades, and the geometry of Maine politics has long bent her way. But the voters of Maine deserve a senator who does not have to instruct them on what "completely false" really means.

That senator is not Susan Collins.
