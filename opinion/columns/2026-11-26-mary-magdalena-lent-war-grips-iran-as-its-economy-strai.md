In August, President Masoud Pezeshkian and parliament speaker Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf publicly pressed for an end to the war, as [Iran’s top politicians urged an end to the war](/articles/2026-08-22/iran-s-top-politicians-urge-end-to-war-as-economy-crumbles/) while the country’s finances buckled. At the same time, President Donald Trump’s administration [tightened economic pressure as the deadlock persisted](/articles/2026-08-10/trump-returns-to-iran-economic-pressure-as-war-deadlock-persists/). U.S. officials settled on financial sanctions and a naval blockade of Iranian ports. Washington then tightened the blockade to cover flights and overland trade in addition to oil exports by sea, most of which were bound for China.

On Nov. 20, Pezeshkian told Iran’s national television, “We have no money.” The next day, at a conference in Tehran, he said, according to the state-run IRNA news agency, “We are engaged in a silent war that is far more difficult than one in which missiles are launched and destruction is caused.” He called it “an economic war…and it is no easy battle.”

Iran’s state electricity company Tavanir has begun rolling blackouts lasting up to two hours a day in some locations. Tavanir said damage at Iran’s natural gas fields during the war had produced fuel shortages. Reza Sepahvand, a spokesman for parliament’s energy commission, said Iranians should brace for worsening winter outages. Power stations around Tehran burn mazut, a high-polluting, low-quality fuel, and authorities have used methanol to stretch gasoline supplies. A reduction in gasoline subsidies during the summer triggered massive queues at the pump.

Hossein Zafari, spokesman of Iran’s Flour and Bread Council, said authorities cut allocations of cheap flour and bread in seven of the country’s 31 provinces. Bakers in western Zanjan province protested the shortages. The Pasta Manufacturers Employers’ Association told state media the government had more than doubled the price it charges consumers for industrial flour. Authorities denied the cuts constituted rationing.

Government statistics showed food and beverage price inflation above 110 percent year over year. Iran’s rial had fallen to 2.9 million per dollar from 32,000 at the time of Tehran’s 2015 nuclear deal. The average pension in Iran was worth $64 a month. The Wall Street Journal reported that nationwide protests in January were followed by a security-force crackdown that killed thousands. There is no sign of widespread dissent now, with security officials maintaining tight control over the public since the war.

After a crash killed 13 petrochemical plant workers in Abadan, a trade union held a demonstration and tied the deaths to austerity measures and political repression. The union wrote, “Subsidized gasoline, subsidized bread, and now they are even trying to ration breathing, and if you protest, they cut it off.” Authorities later detained at least 12 of the protesters, the Human Rights Activists News Agency said. Retirees, labor unionists, teachers, and retired telecommunications workers have demonstrated recently. Deputy Interior Minister Ali Zeinivand said authorities allowed marches so long as they focused on living conditions, adding that inflation had left workers’ wages insufficient to cover their expenses.

Some of Iran’s hard-liners have become the loudest critics of Pezeshkian’s subsidy cuts. At nightly demonstrations, they have targeted Pezeshkian, Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi, and Ghalibaf. Earlier in the month, women dressed in white burial shrouds demonstrated outside the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps headquarters, accusing the force of not doing enough to retaliate against the United States. Some hard-liners now suggest Tehran seek a nuclear weapon or escalate the war.
