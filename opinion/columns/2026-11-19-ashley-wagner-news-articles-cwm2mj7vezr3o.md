The British government is making its off-grid households pay for the war.

Not as a metaphor. As a design.

Ofgem abandons heating-oil households to brutal, uncapped price shocks.

[North Northamptonshire Council](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cwm2mj7vezr3o), the Reform UK-led local authority, is accepting applications for a £250 emergency voucher for residents who heat with oil when low fuel levels would put their health, safety, or basic living conditions at risk. The threshold matters. This is not a discretionary comfort subsidy. It is emergency triage after policy has left one fuel outside Ofgem’s price cap.

Heating oil is not protected by the cap, which means households dependent on it feel price increases faster than households using mains gas or electricity. Rural communities, including parts of Northamptonshire, are among the most affected. Greg Wilcox, the council’s executive member for communities and rural affairs, said: “We know that times are hard for many households and we want to do all we can to support as many residents as possible.”

The British government announced a £53 million support package in response to rising costs. I will not sneer at money aimed at a fuel bill. But emergency relief is not price protection. A voucher says we will help after the shock; a cap says we will limit how much of the shock households must carry. The cost does not disappear. It moves down the chain—from fuel market to delivery truck to tank to kitchen table—until a household outside the regulated market absorbs it.

The currency changes; the structure does not.

At my kitchen table at eleven at night, I have run what a $100 or $300 monthly energy-bill increase does to the sheet. My household’s combined net income after taxes is $8,800 a month; childcare is $2,400. A $100 increase takes 1.1 percent of monthly take-home and 4.2 percent of the childcare line. A $300 increase takes 3.4 percent and 12.5 percent. We also have two professional incomes, a 7 percent mortgage, and David’s grandmother’s estate helped us buy the Fishtown rowhouse. The margin that lets us absorb one bad bill is a privilege. My house runs on mains gas at a regulated rate. I am on the protected side of the exact line this column is about. A household whose only fuel is outside the cap has less room to move. And this is my own kitchen, not a universal portrait of every parent doing the math.

Earlier MSI coverage put [heating-oil households at 21 percent higher costs this winter](/articles/2026-11-16-heating-oil-households-face-21-higher-costs-this-winter/). Separate MSI analysis projects average U.S. heating bills to rise more than 7 percent. Since the conflict between the United States and Iran began in March, heating-oil prices have been volatile. This is not one English council inventing a crisis. The cap excludes the fuel; the volatility lands on the customer. A price cap does not promise cheap energy, but it does change who must carry the first shock.

Earlier this month, the Bank of England raised rates to 4 percent as the Iran conflict lifted inflation risks. The war does not bill the tank once. It bills the household again in every payment it must borrow.

Carry the arithmetic. The voucher is worth £250. The increase is 21 percent. Twenty-one percent of what? On the increase alone, £250 covers a 21 percent rise in full only for a household whose annual oil bill runs about £1,190 before the increase—£250 divided by 0.21. An eligible household above that line still fills its tank at the higher price with the voucher in hand. The help is real. It is also smaller than the hole it is being lowered into.

Follow the cost down. Crude becomes refined product; refined product becomes delivery; delivery becomes fuel in a tank. There is no shopping-around answer to that. An oil tank is not interchangeable with a gas connection. Heat is a health question before it is a comfort question. The buyer at the end of that chain is not a shopper comparing prices. The buyer is captive.

In the introduction to *Can’t Even*, Anne Helen Petersen treats burnout as a structural condition rather than a personal failure. Energy poverty works the same way. When policy leaves the protected alternative outside the cap, the household is told to comparison-shop, weatherstrip, or cut something else. But shopping around requires choices, and an oil tank offers none. The missing option is not a character flaw. It is policy architecture.

Our Catholic-working-class formation understood the order of operations. At St. Stanislaus in Lansdale, my mother belonged to the Altar-Rosary Society; parish volunteer networks turned the corporal works of mercy into meals, funeral lunches, and help for families behind on rent or utilities. Dorothy Day held that the works of mercy without the works of justice are inadequate. In *Rerum Novarum* in 1891, Pope Leo XIII named the principle: “To exercise pressure upon the indigent and the destitute for the sake of gain, and to gather one’s profit out of the need of another, is condemned by all laws, human and divine.” A £250 voucher is mercy at the point of danger. It is not justice in the price charged for the oil.

Annie Lowrey has spent a decade documenting the other half of the design: means-tested help arrives through application regimes and eligibility cliffs that levy their own tax—in time—on households with the least of it. A crisis-gated voucher is that tax at full volume. The help exists, the form exists, and the qualifying condition is that things have already gone wrong.

What would actually hold is not a mystery. The government’s £53 million support package, North Northamptonshire’s £250 emergency voucher, and the $3 billion heating-aid boost an energy economist [urged last month](/articles/2026-10-30-energy-economist-urges-3bn-heating-aid-boost-as-winter-bills-climb/) all acknowledge that assistance belongs in energy policy. The missing design is support that arrives automatically, sized to the delivered bill and indexed to the price of oil, before the tank runs dangerously low. A floor under the winter, not a payout after the emergency.

The parish I grew up in had an answer for a utility crisis, and it was not a form. The St. Vincent de Paul conference arranged assistance quietly—someone knocked, someone called the office, the bill got handled before the shutoff notice. I am not nostalgic about it; the parish net was small, patchy, and carried on women’s unpaid hours. But it had one property the voucher lacks: it moved on trust, before the crisis, without requiring the household to prove it had hit bottom.

Taylor Swift’s title-line “You’re on your own, kid” travels because the architecture is legible on both sides of the Atlantic. Read as care infrastructure, it names what the market hands the household when protection stops at the meter: care gets privatized, then the person carrying it is left alone. A rate cap limits the price before winter. A voucher apologizes after it. The national architecture leaves heating oil outside the cap; the county council delivers the apology in £250 increments, gated on proof that things have already gone wrong.

Extend Ofgem’s protection to heating oil, or build an equivalent regulated floor for essential home heat. Trigger assistance before tanks run dangerously low. Fund routine delivery support as ordinary energy policy rather than charity. Hold the regulator accountable for the households its jurisdiction excludes. Protect essential home heat before the spreadsheet row forces a family to choose between fuel and food.

Somewhere in Northamptonshire tonight there is a gauge sliding toward empty and a kitchen table with a laptop on it, and the help being offered will arrive only after the gauge has proven the danger is real. The government has chosen the apology. The households it bills first will be the ones it reaches last.
