*Federal pardon came first; state justice had not yet spoken.*

The pardon came before the verdict. Donald Trump’s allies used his pardon office to put federal absolution behind election deniers while their state cases were still pending, and the White House later issued a blanket pardon.

In fall 2025, Cleta Mitchell, a key lawyer in Donald Trump’s efforts to overturn the 2020 election, contacted Justice Department pardon attorney Ed Martin. Criminal cases against fake electors were pending in Georgia, Michigan, Arizona, Nevada and Wisconsin. Mitchell knew Martin was working on a memo arguing that those involved should receive a presidential pardon.

“Time is of the essence,” she wrote. If trials proceeded, “the additional financial and emotional costs to these loyal Trump supporters will be enormous.” Some defendants had spent retirement funds and mortgaged their homes on legal fees. “Allowing a trial to proceed is simply unacceptable.” She added: “Please let me know what else I can do to help get this done.” Martin replied: “Thank you for this. Very helpful.” American Oversight obtained the emails through a Freedom of Information Act request.

On 10 November 2025, Trump issued blanket pardons to the fake electors. It touched no state charge. It still put the loyalists ahead of the courthouse. The legal effect was smaller than the political display: the White House had placed itself behind the defense before the states had finished their work.

That was not a pardon earned after proof. It was access used to seek absolution before proof.

Georgia showed what Mitchell wanted. She told Martin that if pardons came promptly, before a new prosecutor was appointed, the council could move to dismiss the remaining charges against Trump. The prosecutor who eventually took over the case dismissed the charges, though for reasons unrelated to the pardons.

Mitchell had been on the telephone call when Trump pressed Georgia secretary of state Brad Raffensperger to find enough votes to overturn the election. A Georgia special-purpose grand jury recommended criminally charging Mitchell for her part in that effort. She was never charged.

Martin had not entered the matter as a neutral arbiter. Before joining the Justice Department, he spoke at a Washington rally on 5 January 2021, represented some January 6 defendants, served on a board providing financial assistance to their families, and, as interim US attorney for the District of Columbia, fired and demoted prosecutors working on January 6 cases. The official receiving Mitchell’s request had already served the same movement. They were not adversaries. They stood on the same side of the door.

The power here is proximity to the pardon office. Belief made these people loyal; access made their loyalty legal leverage. This is not everyone who said Trump won in 2020. It is the class that can turn a defendant’s legal bills into a place in the pardon queue.

A pardon need not erase a state charge to put federal prestige and personal access behind the defendant while state prosecutors carry the case. It can make delay look like mercy. The law still applies; the powerful get to change the terms first.

The pardon power turned proximity into a procedural advantage, and Trump’s allies used it to put election deniers ahead of the courthouse.

Trump’s lawyers were still [challenging his hush-money conviction before a New York appeals court](/articles/2026-11-05-trump-s-lawyers-challenge-hush-money-conviction-before-new-york-appeals-court/), while his pardon office moved for pardons for loyalists facing state cases. One office was contesting a conviction. The other was trying to make sure another case never reached one. One team fights exposure; the other seeks mercy. The law does not come with one entrance. Power knows which door is open.

Two-tier justice, fresh stationery. The old docket repeatedly shows the rule: proximity decides who reaches the pardon office first. Inside the courts, corporations have answered for crimes with negotiated payments while the people who gave the orders escaped individual charges.

In the 1990s, Charles Keating, asked whether the roughly $1.3 million he gave to five senators bought influence, said: “I hope so.” The Lincoln Savings collapse cost taxpayers $3.4 billion. Keating went to prison, served about four and a half years before his convictions were overturned on a technicality, then pleaded guilty in 1999.

In 2009, AIG paid $165 million in retention bonuses after receiving roughly $182 billion in committed federal support. Seventy-three employees received at least $1 million each. No major Wall Street CEO went to prison for the conduct that caused the 2008 financial crisis.

In 2012, HSBC admitted laundering money for Mexican drug cartels and sanctioned regimes, agreed to pay $1.9 billion under a deferred prosecution, and saw no individual prosecuted. In 2015, General Motors admitted failing to disclose an ignition-switch defect, forfeited $900 million, and had no individual charged; the defect was tied to 124 deaths. Purdue Pharma pleaded guilty in 2007 and 2020, while the Sacklers who extracted billions were never criminally charged. Its 2025 settlement was $7.4 billion, but only about $850 million was set aside for individual victims, to be paid over 15 years.

The law can reach the powerful. It just cannot stay there.

No state case had produced a verdict. The federal pardon came first.

On the day the pardons came, Martin received a congratulatory email. He wrote: “It was a long project. But I am glad we stuck the landing.”

Pardon before proof.

The connected got a pardon queue. The law got a blindfold. Sentence passed.
