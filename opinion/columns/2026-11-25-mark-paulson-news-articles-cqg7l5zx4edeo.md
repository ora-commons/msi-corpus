The U.S. Army is sacrificing Iowa farms by dusting them with lead.

I know what it means when a dangerous decision gets carried onto somebody else’s ground. I live on forty acres of central Wisconsin sand in Adams County—ground my grandfather worked, my father kept, and that came to me by inheritance, not by price tag. The year I finished high school, the Sand Hill mill where my father worked shut down, and this county has been absorbing decisions made somewhere else ever since. Around 2008, the big livestock operations moved in south of here. After that, the wells started testing hot. My neighbor’s well came back at 14 milligrams per liter of nitrate last spring against a public-health standard of 10. The Adams County Land and Water Conservation Department published the data, the Adams County Times-Reporter ran it, and the water did not change. When the wind sits north, I get the lagoon off County G in my shop all afternoon. That is what “controlled” looks like from the downwind side: the cost never disappears. It moves onto the nearest ground and the people who had no vote in the schedule.

Out in Iowa, the Army holds 68 barrels of degraded lead azide at the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant in Middletown, a town of about 360 people. The blast remains scheduled for Wednesday. The operation involves five tonnes of degraded explosives, and lead azide is a highly volatile compound used as a detonator. The Army says the controlled blast is needed to prevent an unplanned explosion and that handling the barrels could make an accidental detonation more likely as temperatures fall. The appeals court [overturned an earlier decision stopping the operation](/articles/2026-11-24-u-s-army-secures-court-approval-to-detonate-degraded-lead-azide-in-iowa/), despite objections over lead exposure and environmental hazards.

Officials set an optional five-mile evacuation zone and a 20-mile shelter-in-place advisory. Depending on weather and wind, they said airborne lead dust could reach as far as 100 miles. Residents near the plant were told to stay indoors around the blast and expect “temporary noise, ground vibrations, and a dust plume that will contain some lead.” The Army can call the blast controlled at the plant. It cannot call every farm beneath its plume an unaffected bystander. The question the Army has not escaped is whose land becomes the buffer.

That distinction runs straight through [the Army’s mapped plume](/articles/2026-11-24-army-maps-toxic-dust-plume-for-planned-iowa-lead-azide-detonation/). Margo Sprenger, 71, farms and owns land in Donnelson, about 20 miles southwest of the plant. She said her land lay in the direct path of the projected plume. “It’s going to change my life forever,” she said. Her grandparents started on those farms, she said, and now they do not know whether they can produce food they can trust. She asked the two questions that settle who bears the cost: “Who’s going to tell us if it is safe or not? How much money is that going to cost for us to even find if it is safe?” Those are not side issues. They decide who gets exposed first and who has to buy the answer.

Beth Howard, a pie-maker, planned to travel about 150 miles to Des Moines for Thanksgiving while her partner stayed on the farm his family has worked for more than 150 years. She asked what the future held for that ground. “Is this the end of times for our farm?” Then she named the arrangement in one sentence: “We should have never been put in the situation in the first place.”

Fort Madison, roughly 20 miles from the plant, first heard about the operation on November 17, when the detonation was then set for three days later. City Manager Laura Leigois said the city was “blindsided.” Residents took matters into their own hands “because we’re going to take care of our people,” including covering the vents on the city water tower, the openings that let air move in and out. That was not panic. It was people doing what the institution scheduling the blast had not done: protecting what they could. Three days of notice denied Fort Madison time to make a plan.

Middletown’s reaction was split, as it had every reason to be. Mayor Rodney Johnson lives half a mile from the plant and planned to stay, watching his mobile home by camera while working at a manufacturing job in a nearby town. He grew up 12 miles away, said he survived lead poisoning as a child though he cannot be certain how he was exposed, and noted that crop dusters pass over the town every two weeks. “Every two weeks, we have crop dusters flying over our homes,” he said. “There’s a lot more dangerous stuff being put into the air.” Johnson also believes many Middletown residents will be angry if the blast is delayed again or cancelled. That is his judgment, not a finding that the dust is harmless.

Trisha Klaus, 49, wants it to happen. “I actually want it to happen, because I’d rather it be controlled than just blow up,” she said. She and her 99-year-old grandfather, Keith McNeil, who worked at the ammunition plant for half a century, planned to shelter at the Army Reserve Center and bring their pets. Andrew Wiese, a pastor whose church campus borders the plant, said uncertainty had created fear because it was “a breeding ground for a lot of ideas.” Half a mile from the plant is not the same proposition as twenty miles through the plume. Noise and ground vibration pass. The far tail of a lead plume settles into soil that grows food, and it does not pass. Those are their decisions. None is consent for Sprenger’s ground.

Jeff Kurtz, 72, a retired locomotive engineer and former state representative, did more than call the blast frightening. He worked with a team of scientists who assessed the situation on the ground in support of the legal challenge that paused the detonation. The team believed other means existed for removing the explosives and did not accept the Army’s argument that the threat of a self-explosion was imminent. I cannot settle the chemistry from my bench, and I will not pretend I can. The Army may have the stronger case on preventing an explosion. That does not make the farmer’s soil its money or its responsibility. The institution that owns the hazard kept the calendar from start to finish. The appeals court’s order allowing the blast is not a scientific finding that lead cannot reach a farm. It is a ruling about legal authority, not an end to the safety dispute.

This is the Community Collapse contradiction named in Chapter 16 of *We Too*: public language about safety and community sits on top of an arrangement that spends the countryside to solve the institution’s problem. The plant made the town. The town gets the plume.

Naomi Klein called such ground a “sacrifice zone”: land chosen to absorb the cost of somebody else’s enterprise. The Army closes out a stockpile on its own calendar and calls the result safety. The ground takes the lead. When the dust settles, the verification bill lands on the people who eat off that land, not on the institution that dusted it. The reporting leaves unresolved who will pay for soil testing. Unless the Army takes responsibility, that bill falls on the farm owners. A disposal plan whose last step is neighbors paying to learn whether their ground still grows trustworthy food is not a safety plan. It is a cost transfer with an announcement.

Wendell Berry’s argument in *The Unsettling of America* is not nostalgia for a vanished small farm. It is that an economy becomes extractive when it moves the cost of production onto people and land that cannot carry it. Strip the membership out—the farms, the families, the know-how handed down, the trust that lets someone eat from their own soil without buying a test first—and what remains is ground with an owner of record. That is exactly how a disposal schedule prefers to see land.

The Army can prevent an unplanned explosion without exporting the cost of disposal. If controlled removal is safer, then independent soil, feed, water and crop sampling before and after the detonation, medical monitoring where warranted, compensation where harm is shown, and public results belong on the Army’s ledger, not Donnelson’s. Rural families should not have to buy the first test and then sue for the answer. People beyond Middletown deserved a seat in the decision before the blast.

The plant has been a workplace for generations, and many nearby families have long worked in farming, manufacturing or at the ammunition plant. Those facts do not cancel the hazard; they sharpen the duty. A community can need the plant and still be owed a disposal plan that does not spend a neighbor’s dirt. The current plan has not earned that trust. It has only asked people to accept a risk whose bill they have not seen.

My kids are eight and five, and everything I am asking for Iowa is what I ask for here: that the ground under a family still grows food you can trust when those kids are grown. The Army had a problem. The farms got the schedule.

If it cannot dispose of its explosives without spending Iowa’s countryside, it has not solved the danger of an unplanned explosion. It has turned the family farm into the fuse. A blast has minutes. The uncertainty about soil contamination can outlast a memory, and a farm’s lost confidence can outlast both. Temporary describes the noise. It does not describe the lead.
