Peru is abandoning the Amazon's last free peoples to miners and cartels. The mercury is already in the water. Raphael Hoetmer, who runs the Western Amazon program at Amazon Watch, says it plainly: gold is present in every Amazonian river, a world at war has pushed the price to historic highs, and so the metal is pulled from the riverbed with mercury at industrial scale. Pollution, sickness, and a stripped forest are what remain behind the suction dredges. The poison does not stop at the riverbank. It moves into the fish, into the mothers who eat what the river gives and nurse their children.

On Sunday, Nov. 15, Pope Leo XIV travels from Cusco — past the Sacsayhuamán fortress, more than eleven thousand feet above the sea, the first test of a man recovering from lung cancer surgery — and flies down into Pucallpa, a rainforest city of 434,000 people, to sit beside Lake Yarinacocha with Shipibo, Asháninka, and Kukama leaders. He is [back in Peru for the final leg of a South American tour](/articles/2026-11-11-pope-leo-xiv-returns-to-peru-for-final-leg-of-south-american-tour/). Cardinal Pedro Barreto, president of the Amazon bishops conference, says the Church in Peru reads the trip as accompaniment of Indigenous peoples "in the defense of life and the care of the territory," and that Leo is "clearing the path laid out" by Francis. The Rev. Luca Zanta, one of the organizers, says the pope will "primarily listen to the Amazonian Indigenous peoples." Take those words at their full weight, because the territory they name is a killing ground. What the communities will bring him is already in the record.

First defendant, the Republic of Peru. The Mashco Piro in Madre de Dios and the Kakataibo deeper in the forest live in voluntary isolation, on reservations Peruvian law created to protect them. Hoetmer puts the record plainly: "They have reservations that should protect them. But the Peruvian government is increasingly unwilling to acknowledge the existence of these peoples." Into that official silence the invaders walk — loggers whose concessions now brush the Mashco Piro's grounds, in clashes that left at least two loggers dead in 2024, and drug cartels, for whom those reserves, as Hoetmer says, "are ideal places for them to operate since no one can get in." Images released by Survival International in 2024 showed dozens of Mashco Piro near those logging concessions. This is a country where Leo has already [prayed for an end to surging violence](/articles/2026-11-14-pope-leo-xiv-prays-for-end-to-violence-as-peru-crime-surges/), and the worst of it now moves, armed, through reserves the state will not admit exist. A state that will not say a people exists has already decided what may be done to them. Erasure is not the prelude to destruction. It is destruction's first instrument. On Saturday, Peruvian police carried out three operations against illegal mining in Madre de Dios and destroyed extraction equipment — rafts and motors — valued at $4.1 million. One day. One region. The state can find machinery whenever it chooses. The people it refuses to see. To the officials in Lima: you found the rafts and motors in a single Saturday. The peoples those rafts invade you are unwilling to acknowledge. Recognition is a signature. Sign it. And I will not stand outside this accounting: the church I was baptized in brought the cross and the crown to this continent together, and the Doctrine of Discovery it blessed is the great-grandfather of every concession drawn through someone else's territory. We who claim the gospel and live on the profits of extraction are not watching from outside the frame.

Second defendant, the gold economy. Gold sits near historic highs because, as Hoetmer says, "this global context of war and conflict has contributed to gold prices rising to historic highs" — the wars elsewhere make the metal the safe place to hide money, and the price pulls suction dredges into rivers that carry gold in their silt. The miners cut the forest, dredge the beds, and loose mercury into the water, where it climbs into the fish and from the fish into the children of the peoples being displaced. This is not two crises, one ecological and one human. Francis named it in *Laudato Si'*: "the cry of the earth and the cry of the poor" are one cry, one complex crisis, social and environmental at once. Gold is on my hand as I write and a gold cross at my throat, and the demand that pulls the dredges up the rivers is ours. The ounce in the chalice, the cross, the ring helps set the same price that pays the dredge. A wedding band is not innocent of the river it came out of. No one who wears the metal gets to call the miners strangers. And the forests fell and the rivers soured under governments of every party in Lima; the evil does not change color with the government in power.

Third defendant, the men who did what was done in Condorcanqui. There, in the northern Amazon, teachers sexually abused Indigenous Awajún and Wampis girls — the case Cardinal Barreto expects the pope to take up. Leo has spent this homecoming [denouncing machismo and the violence done to women](/articles/2026-11-12-pope-leo-xiv-denounces-machismo-violence-against-women-peru-homecoming/), and this case sits at the sharp end of everything he has been saying. Men given the care of children used the care as the trap and destroyed those girls for appetite. This is evil. It is *pecado social*, structural sin, the kind the prophets named because nobody else with a microphone would. Father Peter Hughes, an 84-year-old Irish priest who has lived in Peru for more than six decades, says that in the Christian sense of justice "the most important people are the poor, the marginalized, and those who have no voice" — and these girls had no voice. And our own house stands in the same room: the Church's own record of abuse, and of hiding it, is long and documented, and no shepherd may name the sin of teachers in Condorcanqui without first confessing what clergy did and what bishops covered in our own countries.

The Torah says it thirty times and more: "You shall not wrong or oppress a resident alien, for you were aliens in the land of Egypt." "The alien who resides with you shall be to you as the citizen among you; you shall love the alien as yourself." And Jesus, who spent his childhood as a refugee, drew the last line where everyone could see it: whatever you did to the least of these, you did to me; whatever you did not do for one of them, you did not do to me. The Shipibo elder, the Awajún girl, the Mashco Piro man hiding deeper in the forest when the helicopters come: that is the person in the story. The text is not complicated. Only the refusal of it is.

So I speak to you now the way Romero spoke to the soldiers of his own country. To you at the dredge with the mercury jug: the price of gold is not a moral law, and you can set the jug down. No soldier is obliged to obey an order against the law of God. To the Peruvian state: acknowledge the peoples in isolation, title and enforce their reserves, and prosecute the men who touched those children in Condorcanqui as the criminals they are. To you who teach school in that province: the girls were entrusted to you, and what you did to them is an abomination. Óscar Romero, archbishop of San Salvador, stood in his cathedral on March 23, 1980, and addressed the men who were killing his people: "In the name of God, in the name of this suffering people whose laments rise to heaven each day more tumultuous, I beg you, I beseech you, I order you in the name of God: Stop the repression!" They shot him the next day while he said Mass. The words did not die with him, and the door of return was in them.

What would obedience to the teaching actually require? The mercury trade choked off at the buyers, the fuel, and the credit; the reserves titled and held as the lands of living peoples and patrolled by the people who live in them; the girls of Condorcanqui given a courtroom instead of a silence. None of it is beyond us, and none of it is beyond you. That is not charity. It is the floor of justice the tradition already teaches. And it is the question Romero left every bishop who lands in Pucallpa: "A church that does not provoke any crisis, a gospel that does not unsettle, a word of God that does not touch the real sin of the society in which it is being proclaimed — what gospel is that?" Blessing the forest is not the assignment. The assignment is to stand beside Lake Yarinacocha and say, in the Church's own voice, that what is being done to the Mashco Piro, to the rivers, and to the girls of Condorcanqui is social sin, structural sin — and that the Church has the prophetic obligation to denounce it.

Then the tree. The shihuahuaco Father Zanta will plant beside Lake Yarinacocha can live more than a thousand years and grow dozens of meters tall. A symbol is only a symbol if someone defends the ground beneath it for a thousand years. A child who watches it planted will be an old woman when the tree is still young. The Shipibo, the Asháninka, the Kukama, and the Mashco Piro in their silence will believe the tree when the dredges are gone, the reserves are real, and the girls are safe. Plant it. Then keep it alive. Lay the mercury down. The river is still running, and the water your grandchildren drink is in it.
