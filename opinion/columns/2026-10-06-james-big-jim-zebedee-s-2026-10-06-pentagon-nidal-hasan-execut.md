Trump is staging Hasan's execution while the Fort Hood families have waited seventeen years.

Let me tell you what Hasan did, because the plain facts matter. On November 5, 2009, he walked into the Soldier Readiness Processing Center at Fort Hood — the place where soldiers go to get cleared to deploy — armed and shouting "Allahu akbar," Arabic for "God is great." He opened fire. He killed twelve U.S. soldiers and one civilian worker. He wounded thirty-two more. He never denied it. At his court-martial in August 2013, he told the panel he was "defending Taliban leadership in what he said was the United States' war with Islam." A general court-martial found him guilty of thirteen counts of premeditated murder and thirty-two counts of attempted premeditated murder and sentenced him to death.

Now I'll say the part that needs saying plain. That sentence was owed. Hasan murdered American soldiers who were getting ready to deploy and a civilian worker who was on the job. He said he did it on purpose, for a cause. I'm not going to pretend I have a problem with the verdict. The system did its job that day, and it did it right.

Here's what the system did after that. It sat on the sentence. Seventeen years since the shooting. Thirteen years since the court-martial promised those families a day. The widows, the parents, the children did what working families in that position do. They called. They wrote. They waited on hold. They watched the calendar turn. Seventeen Thanksgivings with an empty chair. Seventeen years of a folded flag in a drawer. The government that could not find a way in seventeen years to give those families a closed book found a firing squad in about a month, the minute the boss in the Oval Office decided he wanted one.

That's the part that ought to make working people's blood run cold. When the powerful want a show, the machinery moves. The approvals, the announcements, the scheduling — all of it happens in weeks. It's the same Washington that can set a date for a killing and can't process a claim without three years and a lawyer. The state is fast when the cameras are on and slow when the people on the other end are just working people trying to get through.

Not a word about the seventeen years. They were talking to the cameras, not to the people who have waited for this longer than anyone in Washington has held their job. The date and the place will be set by the Secretary of the Army, Adam Telle. That's how it works — one boss picks the method, another boss picks the day, and nobody asks the families what they've been carrying.

I'm not going to argue the death penalty here. That's a debate with a long history and good people on both sides. What I know is that a widow is not a prop and a mother who buried a soldier is not scenery. The families were used once by the delay and now they're being used again by the spectacle. The execution is dressed up as a television program with the president as the star and their grief as the backdrop.
