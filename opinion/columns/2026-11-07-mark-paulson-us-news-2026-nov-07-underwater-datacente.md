Data center firms want to keep savings and dump pollution risks in the Great Lakes.

In Adams County, an industrial load is not a weather event. It is a decision about wires, rates, land, water, and the bill that follows.

I get my electricity from Adams-Columbia Electric Cooperative in Friendship. It belongs to the member-owned rural-electric model. The line gets carried to farms, shops, and houses even when the return looks thin. The Rural Electrification Act of 1936 put long-term federal loans behind rural electric cooperatives like mine. I keep a Honda EU2200i for outages. It can run the refrigerator and a few lights through a bad night, but it is not a substation. A hyperscale server campus is not a barn load either. The wire, transformer, substation, and reserve capacity for a corporate load should be paid by that load, not shifted onto the homes, farms, and small businesses that built the co-op for a different economy.

The business case is not imaginary. [The Guardian’s November 7 report](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2026/nov/07/underwater-datacenters-great-lakes-trump) lays out the proposition plainly. Two underwater datacenters already operate off the coast of China, and reports say the Trump administration is considering plans for four proposed sealed-pod facilities off the coasts of Maine and Alaska. At an Ohio rally in October, Donald Trump said there was “so much money to be made for Ohio” and that the datacenters would go to China: “We want them.”

Underwater iterations appeal to tech companies because they can largely eliminate community opposition and cut cooling expenses. Nir Kshetri, a professor of emerging technologies at the University of North Carolina Greensboro, says they also allow a large industrial load to sit close to a city without consuming the same acres of land or inviting the same political fight. A Microsoft project off northern Scotland in 2018 recorded 87 percent fewer server failures than comparable land-based datacenters after two years. Microsoft executives regarded the trial as an unmitigated success. Underwater can cool better. It can reduce failures. It can put a factory near the market while moving it beyond the room where the public can answer.

The current record matters. As of November 7, 2026, no underwater datacenter project has been proposed in the Great Lakes. Ohio’s EPA says it has received no application for one in Lake Erie. Microsoft says it has no plans to deploy an underwater datacenter anywhere. A federal EPA spokesperson said the agency supports data-center infrastructure while using its statutory powers to protect water quality.

So this is not about a sealed pod already fouling Lake Erie. It is about what the public will permit before one arrives. The absence of a permit is not a defense. It is the opening. There is no leak to point to, no operating pod to photograph, no regulator to shame for approving the wrong thing. There is only the chance to write common rules before private firms begin treating the Great Lakes as a place to put anything they cannot get approved on land. John Lipaj of the Lake Erie Foundation has already contacted lawmakers in New York, Ohio, and Michigan for that purpose. A common rule is missing.

Where a company must answer a county hearing, residents can put traffic, noise, power, water, and taxes on the table. Underwater siting can move the plant beyond the reach of that hearing. The Community Collapse contradiction in *We Too*, Chapter 16, is plain here: the firm keeps the return while arranging to remove the community’s power to answer. The pattern is not theoretical. [Local resistance has already pressured tech giants ahead of the midterms](/articles/2026-10-22-local-resistance-pressures-tech-giants-ahead-of-the-u-s-midterms/), and [more than 70 percent opposition to nearby data centers](/articles/2026-08-31-more-than-70-oppose-nearby-data-centers-as-abbott-shapiro-curb-approvals/) has already forced state action. Underwater siting takes the same logic one step farther. Move the plant out of the room where the public can answer.

Lake Erie is not a spare reservoir awaiting a better use. It is the warmest of the five Great Lakes, already burdened by agricultural runoff and harmful algal blooms, and about 30 million people live around the Great Lakes. A datacenter throws off heat. Helena Volzer of the Alliance for the Great Lakes says a rapid temperature increase of two to five degrees Fahrenheit can alter aquatic metabolism, reduce dissolved oxygen, disrupt food webs, and drive fish from preferred habitat. The pitch calls cold water a business advantage. The lake receives the thermal bill.

Aldo Leopold’s land ethic in “Thinking Like a Mountain,” from *A Sand County Almanac*, places people inside the biotic community rather than above it. The sealed pod does not become clean because the server racks sit below the surface. It looks clean because the heat and fluid risks have been moved out of ordinary sight. A lake can be made colder for the machine while warmer for everything alive in it. That is the business model in one sentence.

The other danger has no settled record because these installations have not been around long in freshwater. Lipaj said the concerns include the fluids, batteries, and electrical equipment inside a datacenter and the possibility of a catastrophic leak. No long history is not proof of safety. It means nobody has earned the right to treat a sealed pod as harmless because the company calls it sealed. The Great Lakes supply drinking water to tens of millions of people. The suggestion that they lack the large fauna of the open ocean does not make them expendable. The fish and aquatic life of these lakes are not decoration.

On my home ground, the Wisconsin DNR lists Petenwell Flowage among impaired waters for mercury and total phosphorus, and the lake has carried harmful-algal-bloom advisories in recent summers. That is already a lake carrying a load. It is not a place waiting for one industrial system to decide what it can absorb.

On November 3, residents of Conneaut, Ohio, a Lake Erie community of about 12,000 people, voted for a charter amendment prohibiting large datacenters in their area. Council member Mariana Branch said she wanted to protect the Great Lakes as one of the world’s largest freshwater resources and that technology was moving too quickly for cities to keep up. The U.S. side of Lake Erie falls under Ohio’s jurisdiction, and in July 2026 the state’s EPA turned down a proposed data-center discharge permit after public outcry. New York is the only one of the eight Great Lakes states with a temporary statewide moratorium on datacenter construction, and it runs only until mid-2027. The Lake Erie Foundation is asking the other states to write rules with teeth.

That is the pattern of governing by permit. A company that can choose among jurisdictions can shop for the place where opposition is weakest and permitting is slowest. No one needs to hate computers to demand a vote before a sealed industrial plant goes into a public lake. The question is whether a company may remove the community’s ability to answer no.

Trump’s China line makes the contradiction exact. This is the Nationalist Shell Game described in Chapter 16 of *We Too*: the national interest gets the speech, private firms get the gains, and the locality holds the risk. China supplies the flag. The firms get lower cooling costs and a place where land-side opposition is much harder to organize. The lake holds the consequences. Trump’s rally line does not answer who owns the machinery, who carries the cleanup liability, or how much of the promised benefit stays in Ohio after the contractors leave.

When a John Deere tractor comes into my shop, I can see the failed part and put my hands on it. A sealed pod in a lake asks the public to trust a permit instead. Wendell Berry’s argument in *The Unsettling of America* is that a community’s economy is its membership: the arrangement by which people make a living together and hold one another to account. A company that puts its machinery in public water without making its residents members has not solved a siting problem. It has cut the settlement out of the bargain.

Before any pod receives a permit, the eight Great Lakes states should stop new underwater permits until common standards exist: independent thermal and groundwater modeling, full public review, enforceable discharge limits, operator liability, emergency-response capacity, a decommissioning bond, and proof that grid costs will not fall on ordinary ratepayers. The permit should disclose heat discharge, coolant and battery chemistry, maintenance and leak plans, and protect drinking-water intakes; it should ask what the lake community can carry, not only what the operator can cool. Rules written after a company arrives become a bidding war among communities; rules written first keep the burden where the work occurs, and meaningful host-community consent must be part of the bargain.

The line to my pole barn comes from a co-op the members own. The water at the end of my road should not become a server room that the public cannot refuse.

Mike and Quinn should inherit lakes they can drink from, fish, and vote over. Using the machines is one thing. Turning the public’s water into their dumping ground is another.
