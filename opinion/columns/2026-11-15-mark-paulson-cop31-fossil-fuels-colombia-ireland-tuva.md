The governments that promised to quit fossil fuels are keeping the oil money.

The notebook is open on the bench tonight, next to the parts manual, the way it is most nights in November. Twelve winters I have written down the day the ice goes out on Petenwell, and across those twelve years the date has moved eleven days earlier. The rut runs about two weeks off where it ran when I was learning that stand. The Wisconsin Initiative on Climate Change Impacts puts statewide warming at roughly three degrees Fahrenheit since 1950 and calls the 2010s the wettest decade on record. Up here the warming is not a negotiating position; it is a column of dates in my own handwriting, and the lake signs the bottom line every spring. The number that actually governs my year, though, is the diesel price on the pump at the co-op in Friendship — the same pump the brush hog and the Silverado and the snowmobile draw from. That number does not move because a room full of governments wrote down what it intends to do in thirty years. It moves when somebody decides to drill.

The story out of Antalya is short. I read it at the bench the other night, after the last machine went out the door. Abelardo de la Espriella abandoned the fossil fuel transition to revive oil drilling. He took office in August promising to strengthen the country's oil and gas industry. Colombia helped found a coalition to leave fossil fuels — fifty-seven countries gathered in Santa Marta last April — and now the country has left the coalition. Same verb, opposite direction — and the second exit has revenue behind it. A coalition whose founding member walks out three months after its president changes hands is not a law of nature. It is a voluntary arrangement, and voluntary is the word people use when they want the option of leaving. Ireland's Department of Climate, Energy and the Environment confirmed the withdrawal and said preparations were well advanced for next year's program of events; Colombia's Environment Ministry and the presidential office did not answer questions about it. Ireland and Tuvalu take over the leadership from Colombia and the Netherlands, with a second conference set for July 2027 in Tuvalu — an island nation that cannot treat the transition as a paperwork exercise. The Irish department's line on the handover, "this process is bigger than any one country," is true, and also what you say when your founding partner has just walked out.

The government de la Espriella replaced, Gustavo Petro's, had stopped awarding new oil and gas exploration contracts and made moving off fossil fuels a central promise. De la Espriella's environment minister, Fabio Arjona, said those restrictions had contributed to domestic shortages and pushed some industries onto dirtier fuels, and argued that expanding natural gas would cut emissions while securing the country's energy supply. That is the argument, and it is the same argument in every language: the fuel we already know will keep the lights on, and the clean thing can wait. Gas beats the fuels it replaces only if the methane stays in the pipe, and that is a promise with a long record of breaking. Natural gas is a fossil fuel whatever ledger it sits in, and the money it generates is the pull that keeps postponing the exit. Irene Velez, Colombia's former environment minister and one of the initiative's leading advocates, said Santa Marta "has a life of its own, beyond the change of government in Colombia." Maybe it does. But a coalition that cannot hold its own founders is a coalition that can be repossessed, and the repossession clause is written in oil revenue.

Three years ago at COP28 in Dubai, nearly 200 countries agreed for the first time to transition away from fossil fuels in their energy systems. The agreement established no binding timetable, which is another way of saying each government decided for itself when — and whether. Since then, on the international level, little has happened. The talks in Antalya are working over whether to formally recognize the global fossil fuel transition road map that Brazil's COP30 presidency published on Nov. 3 — [the same road map previewed here](/articles/2026-11-10-brazil-to-launch-fossil-fuel-transition-roadmap-at-cop31/) when [these talks opened](/articles/2026-11-11-world-leaders-begin-cop31-talks-in-turkey-seeking-concrete-climate-action/) — and to stand up a process for carrying its recommendations forward. Recognition and process. No timetable. Joeri Rogelj of Imperial College London read the road map and called it "more a transitioning mood board than a map that one can use to navigate this transition." Natalie Jones of the International Institute for Sustainable Development counted 23 countries with national or regional road maps, or plans to develop them, and said planning is the first step toward delivering the transition. She is right. Planning is also the first step toward never delivering it. Three years after the promise, a step is not a walk, and the countries that produce most of the world's oil and gas have not joined the walk. Momentum is what people call a promise before anybody has to keep it.

Wendell Berry titled a book *Standing by Words*, and the title carries the whole argument: a promise you can walk away from was never standing on anything. His career-long target, the extractive mind — the habit of taking what sits in the ground, or in the soil, or in a community, as though it owed nothing back — lands on the same spot: a road map with no date, no consequence and no binding vote is not a commitment. It is a mood with a cover page. A transition you can exit when your own gas price changes is not a transition. It is the same extraction with the date left blank.

I am not going to pretend my own country passes the same test. The United States rejoined the Paris agreement in 2021, pumped crude at a record 12.9 million barrels a day in 2023 — the highest ever measured — and withdrew again in January 2025. A pledge is the easy document. The production number is the operative one, and any coalition of the willing that cannot survive a change of government in Bogotá should be honest about what it can survive closer to home.

Fanny Petitbon of 350.org said cutting fossil fuel dependence and scaling up climate finance — "including by making big polluters foot part of the bill" — are the two make-or-break issues of this summit, and that dropping contested proposals the moment opposition shows up guts whatever ambition survives. Jones made the companion point: vulnerable countries need international support to diversify their economies and keep public services running as fossil fuel revenues decline. That is the actual negotiation under all the road maps. The bill.

I know what happens to a place built on one thing in the ground when the ground stops paying. The Sand Hill mill closed in 2009, the year I finished high school, and my father's paycheck went with it. There was no plan for the town. There was money for the demolition and none for the people. Adams County has spent going on two decades digesting that, and the digesting is not finished. Multiply the mill by a nation and you have Colombia's oil counties. Multiply it by an ocean and you have Tuvalu. Wendell Berry argues in *The Unsettling of America* that a community's economy is its membership, and that extraction destroys the membership long before it destroys the economy. A signature without a date does not hold a membership together; it lets everyone keep the card while the local office closes.

An exit worth the name would carry two features the Dubai language lacks. It would set dates that bind, because a voluntary pledge is a promise with no consequence attached to breaking it. And it would move the money before the closure, not after — the way the Rural Electrification Act of 1936 ran wire to farms the market had written off, through cooperatives the members owned, and took rural America from roughly one farm in ten electrified to four in five inside a generation. Adams-Columbia Electric Cooperative, headquartered up the road in Friendship, is that statute still operating. Aldo Leopold wrote that the land is a community we belong to rather than a commodity we merely own; the cooperative is that idea with insulators on it. Build the replacement while the old income still flows, and let the people who earned it own the replacement.

Ireland will host the events, Tuvalu will hold the conference in July 2027, and the progress report will list 23 countries' road maps — or plans to write them. A season does not get to be voluntary. We decided a coalition of fifty-seven countries could be. The transition will show up at the pump in Friendship either way; the only question is whether it shows up before the wells finish deciding.

Aldo Leopold trusted the record over the rhetoric, and a notebook does not care who is in office. In March I will walk down to the bank on Petenwell and write this year's ice-out date on the next line. The line is already there, waiting. So is the promise made in Dubai. Mine gets filled. Mike is eight and Quinn is five, and by that 2027 conference they will have spent their whole lives listening to adults move a date. They will inherit whichever column of numbers turns out to matter — the dates or the pledges. My job is to keep my column honest. I wish somebody at that table had the same assignment.
