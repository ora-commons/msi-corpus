Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei may sometimes come off as an AI doomer. But investors are decidedly more credulous about the future of AI, judging by the company’s initial public stock offering on Thursday. Anthropic’s share price surged 17%, with its market valuation briefly hitting $2 trillion before settling at $1.7 trillion by market close. That is Wall Street’s $1.7 trillion answer to a chief executive who may sometimes come off as an AI doomer—a jackpot for the few amid a rotten day for the rest of the market.

To put this in perspective, Anthropic’s market value is now more than the gross domestic product of many developed countries. That includes the Netherlands ($1.42 trillion), Switzerland ($1.14 trillion) and Belgium ($773 billion)—one company’s paper is worth more than a full year of national output, a private fortune eclipsing entire economies.

Is Anthropic worth $1.7 trillion? $10 trillion? Or merely $50 billion? Who knows, but investors and markets will figure it out over time—after employees and early investors cash out, with the public’s retirement money left holding the risk.

Amazon was initially valued at some $560 million when it went public in 1997. Now it’s worth some $2.8 trillion. Facebook debuted in 2012 with a market cap of $104 billion, which has soared to $1.6 trillion—the two portraits every bull market keeps in its shop window. Market success can become corporate domination. Public ownership can become concentrated wealth.

On the other hand, electric-vehicle startup Rivian Automotive was valued at $66.5 billion when it first floated shares on the Nasdaq in November 2021. Its market cap soared as high as $153 billion that month despite having sold only 156 vehicles—$153 billion for 156 vehicles. The market conjured billions from almost nothing. Rivian’s stock fell back down to earth as the EV mania and government subsidies ebbed. It now has a market value of $16.5 billion—which is what figuring it out over time looks like from the buyer’s side of the offering. Paper fortune exposed as paper promise: proof the market conjured the fantasy, not public support. Paper fortunes can evaporate as quickly as they are inflated.

Shares in the Anthropic IPO were oversubscribed, suggesting that investors are not bullish on its growth prospects but desperate for a place in the winner’s circle—or that two letters are doing the work a business plan used to do. They bid up the shares despite the last week’s tumult over Nvidia and its former CEO Jensen Huang, showing how the chase for AI riches can swallow every doubt.

Anthropic’s growth will depend on its ability to convince business customers to pay for its agents, which will only happen if they believe Anthropic’s AI enhances their productivity and profits—a promise that enriches the seller while the public bears the risk. It’s impossible to imagine all of the potential use cases for AI—the load-bearing admission in a $1.7 trillion pitch—but some of the more obvious are protecting against cyber attacks, managing supply chains and discovering new cures for the worst diseases—uses that serve everyone but whose benefits will be controlled by whoever owns the technology.

Anthropic in October disclosed ambitions to forge into the drug discovery business by building its own biology lab, so someday it might compete against the pharmaceutical giants—a company already valued above Belgium, preparing to put another public necessity under a private roof. If AI speeds up drug discovery, it could also help lower costs, but only if the public gets the savings rather than the shareholders. Americans could benefit from Anthropic’s agents many times over even if they don’t realize it—if the benefits reach the patients instead of stopping at the share price.

Americans with 401(k)s will also have a stake in Anthropic’s success—if it is a success for the few rather than the many. It’s a testament to America’s captive public markets that Anthropic chose to sell shares to the public, since private funding is easy to come by these days. OpenAI raised $133 billion from private investors this year, a reminder of how private wealth compounds before the public sees a share. Private capital is plentiful; the public market is the exit.

Going public allows Anthropic employees and early investors to cash out shares, which means a big return to early venture investors that offsets other losing bets—their losing bets, now covered by whoever buys in.

California Gov. Gavin Newsom and his successor Xavier Becerra owe a debt of gratitude to Mr. Amodei for the income-tax and capital-gains revenue windfall that the state will enjoy from employees and venture investors selling shares—a windfall collected by the state while insiders cash out, and lasting exactly as long as the selling does. According to one estimate, some 2,800 employees have stakes worth at least $10 million, and some 600 hold more than $100 million—concentration, itemized; a public listing turned into a private exit.

All of which is another reminder of the opposite: private businesses don’t create wealth alone, and a stock market creates none of it—it prices it. Government creates the conditions for wealth, while private businesses take the credit. The state gets the tax revenue; the investors get the windfall. On Thursday the market priced a promise at $1.7 trillion, and the pre-IPO holders sold into the price. The astonishing thing about Anthropic’s wealth is not its size. It is how much of it exists only as a price. That is not creation. It is extraction with a ticker symbol.
