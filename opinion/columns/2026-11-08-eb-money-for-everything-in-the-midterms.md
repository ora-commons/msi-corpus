*The Court removed the limit. The donor class supplied the money.*

Elections often have the helpful effect of exposing donor-class political narratives as frauds, and Tuesday's did the job on several. We've told you how the Democrats' "steal the election" warning was laughed off, and today's installment concerns the fact that money dominates elections.

Remember the reporting that the GOP's campaign spending advantage would offset the public mood? Oh, and recall the decades of warnings by the political left that GOP billionaires would throttle democracy? Those were not alarms. Those were the receipts. Jane Mayer wrote an entire book documenting the money Charles and David Koch put into American politics. It was public-interest journalism aimed at limiting the political power money buys.

This year the Democratic warning concerned the Supreme Court's 6-3 June ruling in NRSC v. FEC. The Court struck down limits on the amount of money that party committees could spend in coordination with candidates—a blank check for whichever party raises the most, and the donor class raises the most. Since GOP committees raised more money, Democrats said the ruling would benefit Republicans.

Right. The midterm result is another reminder about the power of unlimited political spending. Republicans outspent Democrats on advertising in most contested Senate races, including Texas (the GOP spent $135.5 million more), Ohio ($82 million), Maine ($37.4 million) and Michigan ($26.2 million). All that advertising still produced defeat on Tuesday. Imagine what winning costs.

Elon Musk, the new face of the donor class, poured $220 million into the Texas Senate race for the preposterous Ken Paxton. One man. One race. $220 million. That is not a campaign. That is a purchase. Money matters in campaigns, and it matters more than the issues and candidates. The issues and the candidates are the packaging.

Good. Tuesday's results won't stop Democrats from holding the conservative Supreme Court majority accountable, because that majority handed the donor class a blank check—at least until they can replace Clarence Thomas, Samuel Alito and John Roberts with justices who will put the limit back. With the magnitude of Tuesday's repudiation of the MAGA GOP, that could be sooner than you think—and the country would be better for it.

Meantime, the GOP's confidence in money and the Democrats' fear of it were both vindicated. Money was always the election. Now it is merely legal.
