Joseph Babbitt’s employer makes a $5,000 deductible look like good health insurance. Babbitt is 60, a senior financial controller at a Springfield, Missouri, pet-supply distributor with about 50 employees. The company offers several plans and covers most or all of his monthly premium. In the 1990s, his deductible was $250 or $500. This year it is $5,000.

His case compresses the system’s failure into two numbers. The benefit is generous. The price of using it is not.

The system is not merely failing people who lack insurance. It is failing many of the 157 million Americans who receive coverage through work. Coverage may exist. Affordability may not.

KFF’s annual survey of nearly 2,000 employers puts the average annual family premium at $28,000. Workers pay about $7,000 of that total on average; employers cover the rest. Matthew Rae, associate director for KFF’s Health Care Marketplace Program, says the total is “basically the cost of a new car every year just to keep a family of four insured.”

The total family premium rose 5%, which Rae describes as “a period of modest growth” and roughly on par with wages and inflation. The worker-paid portion rose 9%.

That gap is the story. While the headline increase looks manageable, workers’ costs are rising nearly twice as fast. “A 5% increase on a big number is still a lot of money,” Rae says. The divergence could also signal employers shifting more of the bill onto employees.

That is what “good insurance” means in practice: a benefit whose share of the bill grows faster than pay. The supposed bargain is peace of mind in exchange for a locked door, with care converted into a deferred bill.

The Commonwealth Fund’s 2026 survey makes the consequences visible. Among workers with health benefits, about 43% said they did not get health care because of cost during the previous year. About 31% reported medical debt.

These were not people without coverage. These were people whose coverage existed on paper and failed when they needed it. The transaction is forced in doctors’ offices and hospital billing departments, where the bill competes with rent and groceries.

The deductible has become the system’s preferred form of deniability. It postpones the appearance of failure: the insurer can say coverage exists, the employer can advertise benefits, and the patient can postpone treatment until the bill becomes impossible to postpone. Nobody has to declare that health care has been rationed. The architecture does it quietly, one household at a time.

Workers can choose a job, a plan, a doctor and a deductible. The choices are real. The relief is not.

President Trump and Vice President Vance are backing [a bill letting merchants pick card networks](/articles/2026-10-09-trump-vance-back-bill-letting-merchants-pick-card-networks/). Its promise is consumer choice—the same promise job-based insurance keeps making while deductibles leave coverage practically unusable. The card bill concerns network selection and payment fees. It does not change what a family owes before treatment. Even perfect competition in card routing leaves the tollbooth standing.

Meanwhile, the country is losing coverage at one end while hollowing out the value of coverage at the other. Federal decisions allowed extra Affordable Care Act subsidies to expire. Medicaid losses followed the end of the pandemic emergency, and the overall uninsured rate rose over the past two years. Millions have already lost Medicaid or marketplace coverage. Millions more are projected to lose Medicaid when work requirements take effect next year.

Employment is supposed to be the gateway to security. The gateway increasingly leads to a tollbooth, and the exit leads nowhere.

The public has already rendered its verdict. Nearly three-quarters of respondents to the Commonwealth Fund survey said the federal government has a responsibility to ensure that all Americans have coverage. That included 45% of Republicans, 93% of Democrats and 76% of independents. This is not a fringe demand. It is the clearest mandate in the debate.

Congress should restore the enhanced Affordable Care Act premium tax credits for 2027 and make them permanent. It should replace Medicaid work requirements with continuous eligibility. Insuring people is not enough; coverage must work when sickness arrives.

The deductible cap must be specific. Congress should enact a statutory cap of $1,000 for individual coverage and $2,000 for family coverage, effective for plan years beginning January 1, 2027. It should cover every employer-sponsored medical plan, including grandfathered plans, small-group policies and self-funded employer plans that bear the claims risk themselves.

The Department of Health and Human Services should write the implementing rules and collect compliance data. The Labor Department should enforce the cap for self-funded plans, state insurance departments should enforce it for insured plans, and the Internal Revenue Service should enforce the tax consequences of noncompliance. Congress should also amend the tax code so the cap does not by itself disqualify an otherwise eligible health savings account.

The existing tax advantages for employer coverage must serve the same purpose. Congress should condition the exclusion of employer-paid coverage from taxable income under Internal Revenue Code Section 105, the employer deduction under Section 106 and cafeteria-plan tax treatment under Section 125 on compliance with the cap.

The worker-contribution rule must also be measurable. From 2027 through 2029, the employee share of the premium could rise by no more than one percentage point in any year and could not rise at all when the total premium fell. Beginning in 2030, an employee’s dollar contribution could increase only by the lower of 2 percent or total-premium growth. Dollar-for-dollar savings from holding employee contributions below that ceiling must first reduce the deductible.

That sequence matters. A plan that met the contribution rule but retained a $5,000 deductible would lose its favorable tax treatment. The contribution rule would therefore reduce deductibles rather than merely restrain premiums on paper.

A cap shifts costs, and pretending otherwise would launder the arithmetic. Lower deductibles move more claims into insurance. Insurers may seek higher premiums. Employers may reduce benefits. Small firms may drop coverage. If net premiums rise, enrollment can fall. Workers with unusually rich benefits may see some repricing.

Those are the visible burdens: insurers, employers, federal taxpayers, small businesses and workers whose plans are already generous. Federal reinsurance for catastrophic claims and a two-year transition grant and technical-assistance program for small employers should absorb much of the shock during 2027 and 2028. Reinsurance shifts tail risk from insurers and employers to the federal budget. Federal spending rises; premium shocks and coverage losses fall.

Restoring the enhanced marketplace subsidies and replacing work requirements with continuous eligibility also require federal money. State Medicaid agencies need funds to build continuous-eligibility systems. States incur an upfront administrative and technology burden; federal taxpayers carry it, while recurring redetermination churn falls. The alternative is deductibles, postponed care and medical debt.

Babbitt put the human cost bluntly: “We are the only country on our planet that forces people into bankruptcy because they get sick.” That is the outlier he describes.

He was not describing a failure of personal finance. He was describing a system that monetizes job security and calls the invoice a benefit.

A job should be the floor of a middle-class life. Employer-sponsored insurance must not be the cliff. Restore coverage, cap deductibles and make employers carry their share. Until then, the badge on the résumé will mean admission to a tollbooth, not access to care.
