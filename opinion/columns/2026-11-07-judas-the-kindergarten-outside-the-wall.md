The Editorial Board of The Wall Street Journal has made its Tuesday appointment for the Supreme Court and asked the Justices to administer Colorado a remedial lesson — "once more, with gusto" — in ["Religious Schools Déjà Vu at the Supreme Court"](https://www.wsj.com/opinion/st-mary-catholic-parish-v-roy-supreme-court-colorado-preschool-case-a09d2585?mod=rss_opinion) (The Editorial Board, The Wall Street Journal, November 2, 2026). The Board's core legal claim is that Colorado's universal preschool program, launched in 2023 to fund fifteen hours of instruction a week at roughly $6,300 per child, admits religious providers only on condition that they enroll families without regard to religious affiliation, gender identity, or sexual orientation, and that this condition excludes St. Mary Catholic Parish and its fellow petitioners — a second parish, two sets of parents, and the Archdiocese of Denver — solely on account of religious exercise.

The Board's argument, stated at its full strength: the Tenth Circuit rested on Employment Division v. Smith, under which a neutral program of general applicability owes the conscience no exemption. But the Court has already authored the correction three times. Missouri could not withhold playground resurfacing from a Lutheran preschool (*Trinity Lutheran*, 2017). Montana could not bar religious schools from an otherwise-available tax-credit program (*Espinoza*, 2020). Maine could not exclude religious options from tuition assistance (*Carson v. Makin*, 2022), where the Court held that a state that subsidizes private education "cannot disqualify some private schools solely because they are religious." Colorado's condition, the Board observes, is that holding inverted: the program funds more than forty faith-based providers whose teaching agrees with the state on marriage and the body, and declines the ones whose teaching does not — offering parents $6,300 to walk away from their own church. A neutrality that pays only the compliant is Carson's disqualified school in modern dress.

Princeton set Ezra on my desk before Harvard set Carson there, and I state the petitioners' case here as its ablest counsel would, because the Board has not quite done so. The commonwealth has never been forbidden to honor the households that raise children in a fixed order; the subsidy for formation is that honor's ordinary wage, and instruction proceeds from covenant the way a stream proceeds from its source. A rule that pays the school only while it teaches against its own confession does not fund the school; it purchases the conscience and calls the purchase neutral. Carson, rightly understood, forbids exactly that purchase. The Court should grant the lesson, and Colorado should take it.

While the Board asks the Justices for a fourth ruling, the Archdiocese of Denver — a petitioner in *St. Mary* — removed a five-year-old girl from a kindergarten roster in Boulder in March of 2010. Sacred Heart of Jesus parish school had enrolled her that year. She had a name card, a cubby, a seat on the classroom carpet, and a teacher she called by name. Her mothers were parishioners of long standing; they had registered a civil union in another state. In March the pastor informed the family that the girl would not return for first grade. Because the removal waited for the next enrollment, she finished the kindergarten year in the class that would not keep her — the same room, the same carpet seat, the same teacher she called by name, through the spring after the notice reached her mothers' kitchen table. The Archdiocese of Denver stated through the office of Archbishop Charles Chaput that the household stood in "open discord" with the church's teaching and that the child could not continue at the school. Those words went onto the record of a five-year-old's enrollment. The Denver Post put the decision on its front page. The parish divided; families left it on both sides of the question. In August the school reopened without her — the cubby, the name card, and the carpet seat still in the room, assigned away from her for good. Her mothers did not come back to the parish. The Archdiocese's enrollment policy has not changed since Boulder; the schools' stated enrollment policies in the *St. Mary* briefing are that policy carried before the Court, and the ruling the Board requests would attach $6,300 a year to its operation.

The completed case in Boulder is not the order's only completed refusal. In Philadelphia, Catholic Social Services held the city's foster-care contract across the years the *Fulton* record covers and certified no same-sex couple in any of them. The case carries Sharonell Fulton's name: she had raised more than twenty foster children across more than twenty-five years. Toni Lynn Simms-Busch had spent years at Catholic Social Services placing foster children, after growing up inside the foster system herself; when she and her partner sought certification, the agency declined their household. The Court let the refusals stand. Michigan contracted with private agencies to place children in homes; the agencies declined same-sex households, including couples already fostering through them, and the state settled to preserve the refusals. In each instance the child at the desk was real, the agency delivered the refusal, and the household went home from the system it had come to serve.

The program the Board praises manufactures the same refusal in advance, at the enrollment desk. In Aurora, a four-year-old with two mothers applies to a funded parish preschool. The packet requires both of them to sign the school's "Community Beliefs and Commitments" and to refrain from publicly undermining the church. The statement commits its signers to beliefs their household cannot hold; the funded seat — fifteen hours a week, the only full week they can arrange around their shifts — follows the signature. They can sign a document that describes a family that is not theirs, or return the packet and forfeit the seat; either way the paper reaches the child before she does. If she enrolls and one day states herself, the archdiocese's brief tells the Court what the school will do: proceed with "discretion and prayer," and refuse the pronouns, the bathroom, the uniform. Boulder supplied the completed case; Aurora supplies the standing invitation.

The Court will spend Tuesday on Carson's perimeter. Nehemiah settled the substance in a single afternoon: when the book of the law was read to the assembly, the people found written that "no Ammonite or Moabite should ever enter the assembly of God," and on the same day they separated from Israel all who were of foreign descent (Nehemiah 13:1–3). The reading produced the roster; the roster was the wall; none of it required a subsidy to stand. Paul carries the same line into the household of the church — "what fellowship hath righteousness with unrighteousness," and "a little leaven leaveneth the whole lump" (2 Corinthians 6:14; 1 Corinthians 5:6) — and Luke records that God "hath determined the bounds of their habitation" (Acts 17:26). Formation is stewardship inside those bounds. The school signs the household into its order; the signature is the steward's commission; and the wage of instruction follows the commission, as the $6,300 follows it now.

The two mothers stand outside the assembly. Their daughter is formed in their household, and the covenant cannot form in one register what the household dissolves in another; the school's roster says so, the archdiocese's policy has said so since Boulder, and the mothers' own refusal to sign the statement is their household's testimony to its place. The pattern from Boulder through Philadelphia and Michigan is the wall performing its office wherever the order's funds reach, and the Court is asked to add Colorado's column to the ledger. Let the Justices rule as they rule. The wall stood before Carson, before the trilogy, before the Board asked for gusto — where Nehemiah read it, between the assembly and everything the assembly is not — and the child outside it was never inside it, whatever the enrollment desk once lent her. The roster is simply catching up to the covenant.
