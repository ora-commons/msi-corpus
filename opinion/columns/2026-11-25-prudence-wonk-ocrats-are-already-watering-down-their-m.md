Insurers and business groups are defending taxpayer-subsidized private insurance against universal care.

Here are the numbers.

The Wall Street Journal’s Nov. 25, 2026 report on Democratic health-care platforms examined 64 winning battleground House and Senate candidates. Roughly 20 percent campaigned on Medicare for All. Sixty percent campaigned on a Medicare buy-in, under which eligible Americans buy into Medicare, or a public option, a government-run plan sold alongside private coverage. The universal proposal was already the minority position before Congress wrote a line of a bill.

The private system is not a marginal policy preference. More than 200 million Americans have private health coverage, and spending on private insurance accounts for nearly one-third of national health expenditures. The question is not whether that market exists. The question is what the federal government does with it.

Three designs are now being confused. Bernie Sanders has proposed a four-year transition: Medicare at age 55 in year one, age 45 in year two, age 35 in year three, and public coverage for all Americans in year four, with dental, vision, and hearing benefits added along the way. His plan would bar private insurers from selling coverage duplicating the government program. Hakeem Jeffries now supports universal coverage for children, expanded dental, vision, and hearing benefits, and a lower Medicare eligibility age he has not sized. Abdul El-Sayed supports guaranteed public coverage for every American while allowing private and employer-sponsored insurance to coexist.

Those are not three versions of intensity. They are three different answers to who pays, whose coverage is displaced, and which benefits are universal.

The insurance industry’s opposition has two institutional vehicles, not one. Health insurers and pharmaceutical companies have helped finance lobbying and advertising through the Partnership for America’s Health Care Future, a coalition of industry groups. Its documented opposition here is to Medicare for All. The U.S. Chamber of Commerce separately launched Protecting Americans’ Coverage Together in 2020 with other business groups. That coalition has opposed Medicare for All, a public option, and a Medicare buy-in.

The distinction matters. Partnership for America’s Health Care Future should not be credited with opposing proposals the record does not show it opposed. The two coalitions seek the same policy result—preserving private coverage—but through distinct institutional channels.

The lobbying is organized. The narrowing is real. What the record does not establish is that either coalition purchased a candidate’s platform. No receipt shows that a candidate selected Medicare for All, a public option, or a buy-in at the request of an insurer, a drug company, or the Chamber. The defense of private coverage is the lobbying record. Capture is not.

Here is the CBO receipt. In a December 2022 letter, “Costs of Lowering the Medicare Eligibility Age,” the Congressional Budget Office estimated that lowering the age of eligibility from 65 to 60 would add $155 billion to federal deficits over six years, assuming the change took effect in 2026. That is a score for one eligibility expansion. It is not a score for universal coverage, dental, vision, hearing benefits, private-insurance displacement, provider payments, or the proposed financing.

The agency has not estimated Sanders’s plan. Lists of taxes, premiums, and levies are not scores. Republican warnings about required tax increases or additional borrowing are not scores either. No full score exists because no complete bill exists.

The tax side requires the same precision. JCT’s “Estimates of Federal Tax Expenditures,” JCX-13-23, published April 13, 2023, put the exclusion for employer-provided health insurance at roughly $300 billion a year. A tax expenditure is revenue the government forgoes because the tax code grants an exclusion, exemption, deduction, credit, preferential rate, or deferral. Here, employer-paid health benefits are excluded from taxable compensation. The exclusion is not an appropriation and not a check the government writes to insurers.

It is also not comparable to the CBO’s $155 billion estimate. One figure describes revenue forgone under the tax code. The other describes the six-year deficit effect of one eligibility change. They cover different policy changes, different periods, and different accounting concepts. Subtracting one from the other would produce a number without meaning.

The legal framework makes the distinction unavoidable. Under Section 257 of the Congressional Budget and Impoundment Control Act of 1974, codified at 2 U.S.C. §900(c), CBO scores changes against the current-law baseline: scheduled expirations occur unless legislation changes them. Section 313 of the same act, 2 U.S.C. §950e, governs reconciliation through the Byrd Rule, which excludes provisions that increase deficits beyond the budget window. Sanders wants to use reconciliation. That changes the Senate vote. It does not change the score.

The employer-health exclusion is already part of current law. Continuation of the exclusion itself produces no revenue change relative to the baseline. A proposal that repeals or alters it would receive a score for that change. A proposal that preserves employer coverage may leave the exclusion in place. No legislative text says which design Congress will choose.

Sanders’s plan has the clearest promise for uninsured and underinsured people. Under universal public coverage, covered services would carry no premiums, deductibles, or copays. That removes point-of-service costs that currently push families into debt or away from treatment. Workers with employer coverage would not retain duplicative private benefits, however, and private insurers would lose that business. Employers would not pay nothing: Sanders has proposed income-based premiums from employers and households, along with higher taxes on high-income Americans and levies on Wall Street and large corporations.

Providers would move from a mixed private reimbursement system to government-program payments. Business groups warn that the public rates could squeeze hospitals and physicians. Whether lower rates would be offset by greater patient volume and steadier payments is not a slogan question; it is a design question. The proposal has no score showing the net effect on providers or taxpayers.

El-Sayed’s design keeps employer coverage in place. That would preserve private insurance and offer public coverage alongside it. It could avoid the immediate disruption Sanders’s phase-in would cause. But the guarantee’s benefits, premiums, provider payments, and relationship to private plans remain undefined. An uninsured person cannot be promised comprehensive coverage until the guarantee is written. A worker with employer coverage cannot be told what changes until the financing and private-market provisions are specified.

Jeffries’s proposal is more bounded still. Universal coverage for children, added dental, vision, and hearing benefits, and a lower eligibility age would help uninsured children and underinsured people whose current coverage excludes those services. It would not universalize coverage for every adult, and employer-sponsored insurance would remain in place. The age and cost remain unspecified.

The buy-in and public option represent the compromise most candidates chose. They add a government-sponsored coverage route without requiring private coverage to disappear. Uninsured and underinsured people gain access to a public plan, but the premiums and cost sharing determine what that access saves. Workers with employer coverage may see little change unless the public option is cheaper or better. Healthier people may move from commercial insurance to the public plan, leaving insurers with a more expensive pool and putting upward pressure on private premiums. Risk adjustment—payments that compensate plans for caring for costlier patients—can limit that effect, but only if the design uses it. Provider payments face the same issue: negotiated rates and patient volume determine the result.

The business groups have identified a genuine risk. They have not established that the risk will occur under a specified plan. Nor has the source article shown that their opposition produced the candidates’ retreat. Two records sit beside each other. They do not become one causal story without a receipt.

Congress should put every proposal into legislative form and score it as written against current law. The score should identify the treatment of employer-sponsored coverage, the distribution of taxes, the phase-in, provider payment rates, premium effects in the private market, and the people gaining or losing coverage. Distributional tables should show who receives care without point-of-service costs and who bears the cost of paying for it. A platform is not a program.

No such document exists. The next receipt needed is a CBO estimate of Sanders’s, El-Sayed’s, or Jeffries’s bill—not another platform. Private coverage remains in place, and the private insurers’ position remains intact. The score is the score. The authors of every coverage proposal do not get to grade it.
