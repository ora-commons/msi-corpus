Trump is negotiating Europe's borders with Putin while Putin bombs Ukrainian children.

The record from this week: the President's envoys met Putin in Moscow, flew to Dublin, and sat down with Volodymyr Zelenskyy, and somewhere between those rooms the United States asked Ukraine, for the first time, whether it would withdraw from the parts of the Donbas, the industrial region of eastern Ukraine that Russia has been grinding at since 2014, in exchange for admission to NATO. Zelenskyy said no. He said no the way a man says no when his country's constitution is on the table: Ukraine will not surrender its territory, will not recognize the occupied land as Russian, and will not accept neutrality imposed from outside, because neutrality would require rewriting the constitution of a nation that never voted for it. "If Putin expects Ukraine's neutrality to be enshrined, that's not going to happen," he said. "We're not in NATO right now anyway."

Alongside the territorial ask, the American side floated a new security framework for Europe as a whole. The administration has spent the past week [linking this European security overhaul directly to the ceasefire talks](/articles/2026-11-10-u-s-links-european-security-overhaul-to-ukraine-ceasefire-talks/), and now the wire tells us what the overhaul is for. Zelenskyy's judgment came with unusual plainness: "In my view, this can be dangerous. This should not be the main agenda." He named the agenda he expected Putin to bring: Finland and Sweden, Hungary, the presence of American troops in the Baltic states. Putin had raised similar ideas with Joe Biden in 2021, Zelenskyy said, a redistribution of power in Europe and the world. That demand was refused then. It is being entertained now. Set those two facts side by side and the shape of the thing is clear. The framework is not a mechanism for ending the war. It is the vehicle for reopening the whole European settlement, with the war as the lever and Ukrainian ground as the price of admission to the conversation.

Andrew Bacevich built *Washington Rules* on the observation that the American security establishment runs on a set of rules nobody in this country ever voted for. The unstated corollary is the one doing the work this week. If the arrangement is ours to run, then the arrangement is ours to rearrange. A new European security framework floated by Washington, with European capitals reading about it in a wire dispatch, is that corollary in its purest form: the borders of Europe put up for revision in a room the Europeans were not seated in.

European leaders have already said it plainly: decisions about the continent's security must be made by Europeans themselves. Barbara Tuchman's checklist for folly includes the detail that matters most here: the alternative was available and named at the time. It was named this week. The policy still moved.

There is an old text for what happens when the people who must live under an arrangement are not the people who write it. Thucydides, *History of the Peloponnesian War*, Book 5: Athenian envoys on Melos tell the island's magistrates, in Crawley's translation, that "the strong do what they can and the weak suffer what they must." The Melians asked only to be heard as parties to the decision that would govern them. They were not heard. The city refused terms it had no hand in writing, and Athens made an example of it: the men killed, the women and children enslaved. Twenty-four centuries later a president under bombardment states the counter-principle in one sentence: "There can be no security guarantees in Europe, or for Europe, without Ukraine." That sentence is the Melians' argument, spoken by a man who has the votes and the army the Melians lacked.

The mirror for the framework itself is not distant. The Molotov-Ribbentrop Pact of August 23, 1939, with its secret protocol, divided Poland and the Baltic lands between Moscow and Berlin, with Finland and Bessarabia written off to the Soviet side as well; the peoples whose borders were being traded on paper received no seat and no veto, and Germany's invasion of Poland followed nine days later, then the annexations, then half a century of occupation. Putin's stated grievance for years has been the revision of that settlement in reverse. An American administration that entertains reopening "European security" with Moscow before the shooting stops, with Ukrainian territory on the table, is not inventing a process. It is repeating one with a documented result, and Zelenskyy said as much: Putin's desire to change borders in Europe "is a fact," and "it is precisely things like this that lead to wars or escalations."

I have been inside a war whose map was drawn elsewhere. From where I sat, in the gunner's seat in the spring of 2003, my crew rode north out of Kuwait behind a plan fixed in rooms none of us would ever sit in, and the men around me understood before Baghdad fell that the political end of the thing had never been thought through; you can feel the gap between a war and its purposes through the seat of a tank. The men and women now posted on NATO's eastern flank are living under a version of that gap, and none of them enlisted to have their garrisons put on a negotiating list between Moscow and Washington. Their presence is a matter for the alliance they serve, decided after the war they are deterring has ended. Not a line item.

Then there is the texture of what is being offered. Zelenskyy accused SpaceX of restricting Starshield, the company's satellite system, over Russian-occupied Ukrainian territory, capping it at 161 kilometers an hour for five days, which kept Ukrainian forces from intercepting jet-powered Shahed drones, the Iranian-designed munitions Russia fires at Ukrainian cities, until the drones reached areas where the limits did not apply. The offered explanation was technical. Zelenskyy did not accept it. "The current Starshield restrictions suggest that SpaceX imposed them," he said, and that this "doesn't quite line up with what we heard earlier," when Secretary of State Marco Rubio had given assurances the system answered to the American government. He has been raising this problem for a month, [the Starlink limits persisting while he searched for European funding](/articles/2026-10-16-zelenskyy-says-starlink-limits-persist-as-he-seeks-more-eu-funding/), and the record shows the same contractor and the same occupied-territory throttle. Now, I'm just a simple man, but a security guarantee a contractor can throttle is not a guarantee. It is a subscription. A nation under missile attack learning in real time that its air-defense picture depends on one private company's switches is not a footnote to the security framework. It is the framework's true structure, and it does not pencil out as security. It pencils out as dependency written in somebody else's contract.

The war keeps its own ledger underneath all of it, because the negotiation has not paused the war it is supposed to end. A Russian strike on Zaporizhzhia on Friday wounded twenty-two people, according to Ukraine's State Emergency Service. Three of them were children. A six-month-old baby. A two-year-old girl. A twelve-year-old boy. The same day, Ukrainian forces struck the Samara oil pipeline station near the village of Prosvet, a node in the export network Moscow finances this war with, and the Lipetsk-2 airfield; Moscow had not commented. Ukraine's priorities at the Abu Dhabi talks were an energy ceasefire, reopening the Black Sea, and prisoners, and the Russian delegation at that table answered that it had no mandate to decide the energy ceasefire. The one concrete deliverable from the Moscow round so far is an exchange of prisoners of war, more than a thousand on each side by Zelenskyy's estimate, twelve or thirteen hundred when the count settles. That is what the table has produced so far: swapped prisoners, and a framework to discuss.

Zelenskyy named the sequence himself: a ceasefire, an end to the war in Ukraine, and then security guarantees for Ukraine. The order matters because the order is the argument. Reverse it and you get what the wire describes: a framework negotiated while the shooting continues, in which the party that started the shooting gets to draw the map for when it stops.

So what would the Constitution itself require? Three things, none of them exotic. First, sequence, because guarantees negotiated while the bombs fall are terms of surrender wearing a treaty's clothes. Second, consent: the package goes to the people who must live under it, as Zelenskyy himself has promised: "If the American side confirms precisely this to us, together with other NATO partners, then I will ask our Ukrainian society what it thinks: do we accept it or not?" And the nations of NATO, who have said for weeks that Europe's security is decided by Europeans, sit in their own capitals and decide it, with the direct European representation at the table that Washington has now conceded. Third, the Senate: any admission of Ukraine into NATO is a treaty, and the Constitution gives treaty ratification to the Senate. A territorial concession extracted in a negotiating room before that ratification is a promise made by nobody with the power to keep it, paid for with ground that belongs to somebody else. The men dining in Moscow and Dublin cannot bind the American word in Europe without two-thirds of the Senate consenting to it. That is not obstruction. That is the document.

The men drawing the new map have not asked the children of Zaporizhzhia. A six-month-old baby, a two-year-old girl, a twelve-year-old boy, wounded on Friday in a city under Russian fire, while envoys redrew continents over their heads. Trump is handing Putin the revision he has been after since 2021, and calling the handover a framework. He should walk the ward in Zaporizhzhia before he signs anything with the man who put those children there.
