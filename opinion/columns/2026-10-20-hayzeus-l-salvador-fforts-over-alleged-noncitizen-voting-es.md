The talking points are already written. The Department of Justice charged 28 green-card holders in California this week — eleven in custody, the rest being processed — for illegally registering to vote and casting ballots they had no legal right to cast, as [previously reported here](/articles/2026-10-19-doj-charges-28-california-green-card-holders-over-alleged-illegal-voting/). Days earlier, the same department [sued California to block the state from tabulating certain ballots](/articles/2026-10-15-doj-sues-california-seeking-to-block-counting-of-alleged-noncitizen-votes/) in the November midterms — a lawsuit legal commentators were quick to call a [long shot](/articles/2026-10-19-legal-experts-call-doj-california-ballot-lawsuit-a-long-shot/). A long shot is a charge that has not yet been refuted.

Secretary of State Shirley Weber called the federal demands "outrageous" and accused officials of making "vague and unsubstantiated allegations." The voting-rights groups said the administration is laying the groundwork to sow doubt about November's results. Both claims may prove out. Neither one answers the question the charges pose.

Voting by a noncitizen is a federal offense. It is not a paperwork confusion. It is a felony. If these 28 people did what the charging documents say they did, they broke the law of the country they chose to live in, and they should answer for it in court. I say this plainly because it is true, and because a column that cannot say it has already surrendered the ground it claims to hold.

But here is what neither side's talking points will say: a charge is not a conviction. Twenty-eight names on a federal list is not a systemic finding. It is not proof that a registration system is broken by design, or that a state produced fraud on purpose, or that an election is compromised. It is 28 people, each of whom has a name, a family, and a constitutional right to a trial. The federal government spent months building these cases. The cases will move slowly. They will require evidence. They will produce defenses that either hold up in a courtroom or do not. That is how the system works when it works, and it has not worked yet.

Look at the scale. Twenty-eight named defendants. Eleven in custody. Federal charges carrying real prison time. These are real people facing real consequences, and neither political establishment in Sacramento or Washington has spent a single minute this week attending to that fact.

The ask from one side is to look away from the charges. Accept the existing rolls. Count whatever California has produced. Trust the process that produced the suspect registrations absolutely and the process that produced the indictments not at all. That is a position. It is not a defense of election integrity. It is a defense of the administrative apparatus that failed to catch 28 people before the federal government did.

The ask from the other side is to look away from the fact that these are charges. Treat the indictments as proof. Treat the number 28 as a crisis. Treat a federal lawsuit filed three weeks before a midterm as routine. Spend the arrests as a story about systemic fraud before a single jury has heard a single case. The claim that California produced these defendants by design is a claim the charges do not support. Twenty-eight indictments are not evidence of a conspiracy. They are evidence of 28 indictments.

The contradiction is the message. Both sides are spending 28 unconvicted people as evidence for positions the evidence does not yet support. The Secretary of State finds the inquiry inconvenient. The administration finds the timing useful. The 28 people at the center of it have not been heard from at all.

Weber's word was "outrageous," and she applied it to the federal demands. There is a case that the word has a different referent: the registration system that the charges allege put 28 noncitizens on rolls that should never have included them. She has been Secretary of State long enough to know what is on her forms and what is not. The administration has been in power long enough to know what it is doing with the timing. Neither of them is owed the benefit of the doubt the other is refusing to extend.

I have spent my life around people this country has decided to process rather than see. Immigrants who work, who pay taxes, who go to church, who send money home, who have lived here for years without anyone asking hard questions — and immigrants who broke the law, and who are still people. The Gospel takes away our right forever to discriminate between the deserving and the undeserving poor. Dorothy Day wrote it in plain language, and she meant it about the people nobody wanted to help, including the ones who had committed crimes.

That does not make illegal voting acceptable. It does not make the federal charges wrong. It makes the charges a matter of justice rather than a matter of spectacle, and justice requires a patience that neither side this week is prepared to exercise.

The midterms are less than three weeks away. The lawsuit is working through the courts. The arrests are public. The next ten days will produce every possible objection — federal overreach, timing, the voters, the courts, the sacred precinct — and none of them will answer the underlying question: how did 28 people who were not citizens end up registered, end up voting, and end up in federal custody? The charges are the answer the federal government has given. They are specific, they are public, and they will be tested in court. That test has not happened yet.

Twenty-eight names is not a story about a system. It is a story about 28 people who are about to find out what this country does with the stranger — not the stranger who asks permission, and not the stranger who behaves, but the stranger who is accused of a crime. That question does not resolve in a press conference. It resolves in a courtroom, slowly, with evidence, and with the outcome still unknown.

The hard look is here. It was always going to come. The only question was whether anyone would wait for the answer before declaring what it was.
