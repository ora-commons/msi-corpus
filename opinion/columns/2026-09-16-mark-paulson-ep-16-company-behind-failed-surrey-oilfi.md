UK Oil and Gas abandoned Horse Hill after climate rules destroyed its value.

I know what a stranded asset looks like because Adams County has been living with them for half a century. The railroad built Adams and then dieselization emptied the yard. The paper mills in Port Edwards, Whiting, and Wisconsin Rapids once carried families through the week; the last of those mills idled and the work went elsewhere. The dairy count fell from 16,886 farms in 2002 to about 6,216 in 2022 while the average herd grew.

The building stays. The land stays. The work that made a household stable leaves.

That is the part of the Horse Hill sale worth bringing home to Wisconsin. UK Oil and Gas sold its stake in the Surrey oilfield for £1 million after spending more than £25 million developing it. The company had once bought a 14.3 percent economic interest for £6.6 million. Its accounts carried the asset at £55,360. The geology did not evaporate. The permission to use the geology did.

The old valuation counted barrels. The new valuation counted whether anyone would be allowed to burn them.

That is not a technical adjustment. It is the difference between an asset and a liability.

UKOG spent a decade pursuing Horse Hill on the strength of estimates that the wider Surrey formation held 9.24 billion barrels of oil. The company drilled, raised money, and built its business around the field. Investors looked at the rock and saw a resource. The planning system eventually looked beyond the wellhead and saw the emissions that followed the oil into cars, boilers, and furnaces.

In 2024, the United Kingdom Supreme Court’s Finch ruling overturned a council decision that had allowed the project to expand. The Court held that downstream greenhouse-gas emissions belonged in the environmental assessment. The oil could not be treated as if its climate consequence began only when it came out of the ground.

Guy Prince of Carbon Tracker called the result “regulatory stranding.” For a large company, he said, that might impair one investment. For a small company built around one field, it could transform the whole business.

That is the rural political economy of it. A major corporation can write down one project and move its capital. A small company, a county, or a household cannot move so easily. When the only employer in a town closes, the town does not lose only wages. It loses customers for the hardware store, children for the school district, volunteers for the fire department, and taxable activity for the public services that remain.

I watched that happen here. When the C&NW reduced the Adams yard, the railroad did not take the tracks away all at once. It took away the reason for the houses, stores, and institutions around them. When the paper mills closed, the machinery and buildings remained while the paychecks disappeared. A place can look intact on a map and still be poorer in every way that matters to a family.

That is the household consequence of regulatory stranding. A working family does not own an oilfield, but it can depend on the jobs, lease income, school enrollment, local taxes, or electric customers that an energy project was supposed to create. When the project fails, the private investor records a loss. The community carries the empty capacity around it.

The same balance sheet shows up on my forty acres. I own the land because I inherited it. I could not buy it at today's prices. Sara's school-district job provides the health insurance my shop could not afford last year. If an economic use disappears in Adams County, I cannot simply rename the county and move the household to a new balance sheet. The truck still needs fuel. The kids still need school. The road still needs plowing. The county still needs a tax base and the electric cooperative still has wires to maintain, whether the promised project pays off or not.

Wendell Berry has spent a lifetime describing this mistake: treating a community as an input to an enterprise and calling the people who remain behind an externality. A company can sell a field. A family cannot sell the years it spent arranging its life around the field.

UKOG is now trying to sell the next version of itself. It changed its name to UK Energy Group and began raising money for salt-cavern energy storage in Dorset and Yorkshire. The company reportedly raised £1 million in 2025 for hydrogen-storage projects and another £500,000 to acquire land for a proposed East Yorkshire salt-cavern site. Chief executive Stephen Sanderson described the Horse Hill sale as a “timely and attractive opportunity” to complete the company’s exit from onshore oil and gas.

In plain English, the old asset had become a liability, and the company needed to leave before the liability swallowed the company.

The fossil-fuel business remains as a balance-sheet ghost. The new energy business gets to walk forward with the surviving capital.

The buyer is Energy B, operating through Horse Hill Developments Ltd. It paid £1 million for a project UKOG spent more than £25 million developing. Energy B has submitted a new application to Surrey County Council for four production wells, an oil-processing area, tanker-loading facilities, and a fluid-reinjection well.

The application proposes extracting up to 678,693 tonnes of oil over 20 years. Its environmental assessment puts lifetime greenhouse-gas emissions at about 2.3 million tonnes, including the emissions from burning the oil. It calls that impact “insignificant” and places Horse Hill at roughly 0.05 percent of the projected United Kingdom carbon budget.

A large number can be made small by dividing it by a national total. The household near the well still gets the place where the emissions land.

Sarah Finch, whose legal challenge produced the Supreme Court ruling, said the new assessment had serious shortcomings. Government guidance requires the application to consider the project alongside current and approved fossil-fuel developments, she said. The application did not do that, even though the data exists.

The council’s formal consultation closed July 13, with earlier submissions still under consideration. The Weald Action Group has not ruled out another judicial review if Surrey approves the application.

This is why the Horse Hill story is larger than one disputed field. The ruling turned climate accounting into a market force. It made permission part of the asset.

That change will reach Adams County in less dramatic ways. It will reach the electric cooperative's long-term planning, the county's industrial recruitment, the farmer deciding whether to sign a lease, and the household deciding whether a promised job is solid enough to build a life around. Adams-Columbia Electric Cooperative exists because rural people once built infrastructure the private market considered too thinly settled to serve. The wire was a public choice before it was an investment opportunity. The same question remains: who carries the cost when the investment story fails?

The answer cannot be the household that was told to trust the story.

A transition that counts only the investor's loss is not a transition. It is another transfer. The capital owner writes down the field while the community keeps the roads, schools, service obligations, and empty buildings. Real energy policy has to account for both sides: the emissions that make a project unsafe and the public goods that keep a rural place from being abandoned when private capital moves on.

Horse Hill is not proof that every fossil investment is worthless. It is proof that geology is not permission, permission is not value, and value is not guaranteed by a press release about jobs.

The oil stayed underground. The valuation did not.

The next use of the capital should serve the communities that carried the old one—not merely give the same executives a cleaner name and another balance sheet.
