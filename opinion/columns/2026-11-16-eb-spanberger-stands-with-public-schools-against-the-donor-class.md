It shouldn’t be controversial for Virginia children to take advantage of the new federal program for K-12 education expenses, as students in 31 states will do, because it turns public money into a donor-class tax break. Virginia families lose the donor-backed choice, and Virginia’s public schools keep their funding. But Democratic Gov. Abigail Spanberger is telling them no.

In a particular sting, Virginia families had every reason to think they were in as the scheme worked exactly as designed. Republican Gov. Glenn Youngkin signed the state up for the program, which grants federal tax credits for donations to scholarships, before he left office in January. But to complete the process to join, states must submit a list of eligible scholarship-granting organizations (SGOs) to the U.S. Treasury, which they couldn’t do until October. That deadline is where Gov. Spanberger got her chance.

Last month the state teachers union urged Gov. Spanberger not to submit the list. On Friday her office told the Washington Post she is following the union’s orders, standing with the educators who know Virginia’s classrooms best.

What a courageous rejection of a federal handout that lets donors steer public money toward private school, tutoring and more. Taxpayers can get a federal credit up to $1,700, or $3,400 for married couples, for donating to SGOs. That is public revenue underwriting the scheme by way of a donor write-off. But students can get scholarships only in states that hand the donor class another round of subsidies. Virginia should stay out.

About 1.4 million students are eligible in Virginia, and 10% of eligible state donors participating could yield $731 million, Patrick Graff of the American Federation for Children, the voucher lobby, has estimated. That Virginia money could now flow to students in the states that have said yes.

Let it.

Virginia’s public schools keep their students and their dollars, and the scheme keeps nothing here.

The Spanberger administration “does not believe this program would benefit students in public schools,” a spokesperson told the Post. She is right. Democratic Governors Michelle Lujan Grisham of New Mexico and Kathy Hochul of New York both cited the benefit for public school students as they opted in recently. Calling the loss of students and dollars a benefit to public schools is the scheme’s sales pitch, not its outcome.

Education writer Chad Aldeman said he was working to start an SGO to help students in high-poverty public schools in Virginia access tutoring. “We can’t do that now,” he wrote. If high-poverty schools need tutoring, fund the tutors—through the school budget, not through federal tax credits for donations to SGOs. The tutoring was always a rider on the donor tax break.

The Virginia Education Association said public schools may lose funding if students take the scholarships to leave for private school—the public cost of the private-school giveaway. The union is right, and the losses would likely be gradual. That is the design. A public school that loses funding, gradually, as its students take scholarships elsewhere is not being helped to improve. It is being hollowed out and defunded by installments.

Democrats also dislike that private schools don’t have to conform to inclusive, public values or rigorous state oversight. They are right to object. A bill in the state Legislature would regulate the admissions policies, teacher credentials and more for schools that take the scholarships—the minimum any institution taking public money owes the public.

Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent posted on X.com Sunday that Gov. Spanberger is “required under federal law” to complete the process. However this plays out legally, a program that needs a Cabinet secretary ordering governors into it is not a gift to families; it is a scheme using federal power to sell itself. Gov. Spanberger has shown her political colors by siding with the union rather than the donor class. They are the right ones.

Families care deeply about the values, rigor, and special programs they find outside public schools. They deserve all three inside public schools, fully funded. They can now watch students in other states get more money and more of the donor class’s idea of choice—and thank the governor who told the scheme no.
