Donald Trump's agents took an eight-year-old boy away from his family.

The appointment was in San Diego, on November 4, and his family understood it as routine — a check-in, the kind the system demands and the obedient attend. He is a third-grader at Fay Elementary School. His uncle brought him, because the uncle is the one who raises him; he has raised the boy since the boy's father was deported to Mexico last year. Video obtained by NBC News shows three armed federal officers leading the child out of the building. Senators Alex Padilla and Adam Schiff and Representatives Vargas, Jacobs, Levin, and Peters reviewed that footage and wrote to Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin and Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. that it appears to show "the use of restraints on a child who seemed to pose no threat to those around him." His advocates say he was later transferred to the Office of Refugee Resettlement, the agency that holds unaccompanied children. A spokesman for one of the signatories says Congressman Juan Vargas has repeatedly contacted both departments and heard nothing back. They have asked where the boy is and whether his family has been told. Congress has asked. The agencies have answered nothing.

Begin with what the boy did, because it is the part that should stop every reader cold. He obeyed. He got up on a Wednesday, put on his backpack, and walked into a federal office because the law told him to. To the officers who took him, and to the supervisors who sent them: he did everything your law asked of him, and you answered with three armed agents and restraints. You have taught every Haitian family in San Diego a piece of arithmetic — miss the check-in and be marked noncompliant; keep it and risk your child. Obedience is the bait now. A system that converts compliance into seizure is a trap, and a trap set for children is cruelty by design.

Now read the government's explanation, slowly. The uncle could not prove legal guardianship, a department spokesperson says, so federal anti-trafficking law required taking the boy in as an "unaccompanied alien child" — for his safety, the department says, out of an abundance of caution. Follow the mechanism. His father was deported last year so abruptly, by Customs and Border Protection, that no guardianship paperwork could follow him out of the country. So the uncle is the father in every way but on paper. The state broke the family. Then the state ruled the surviving care legally invisible. Then the state invoked a statute written to protect children from traffickers to take a child from his own kin. The father's deportation manufactured the condition. The condition licensed the taking. And the boy was accompanied — his uncle brought him to the appointment, as the law required. The government unaccompanied him, then cited the condition it created as though it were a fact of nature.

The same spokesperson called allegations that ICE took a third grader into custody to be placed in detention "false," and called the account of it "a depraved attempt to smear" officers whose work, the department insists, is to protect children. No one has been smeared. The footage is public. Set the statement beside the video and the gap speaks for itself: an agency charged with protecting children explains, in the same breath, an operation that removed a child from the only family he has, and names the account of that removal the depraved part. Isaiah had a word for a government that calls the taking safety and the naming of the taking slander: woe to those who call evil good and good evil. I will say the rest in my own voice, because it is mine to say: putting restraints on an eight-year-old is evil. The words say safety. The boy says otherwise. The paperwork does not make it right. The statute does not bless it.

Around the trap, in August, the administration stripped temporary protected status from Haitians — people who have lived and worked here lawfully, many for more than a decade — and Guerline Jozef of the Haitian Bridge Alliance says her community now lives "under a cloud of terror." "The moment they kidnap or take a person within the community," she said, "we don't know if we will ever hear from them again." The cloud is not a metaphor to them. It is the same community that spent September mourning [a Haitian student found dead wearing an ICE monitor](/articles/2026-09-09-police-probe-possible-suicide-of-haitian-student-wearing-ice-monitor/). School board trustee Cody Petterson of San Diego Unified said ICE "understands that this is absolutely not where this child should be," and that one of the worst places this child could be is "in a cage somewhere with nobody they know, not understanding the process, and not understanding what's about to happen to them." I have no better word than his. And what the inside of custody looks like, the men already inside have told us themselves — [nearly two hundred pages of testimony and drawings mailed out of a Texas detention jail last month](/articles/2026-10-31-detained-men-mail-nearly-200-pages-of-testimony-and-art-out-of-texas-ice-jail/) — because the public hears from inside only when those inside can write.

Scripture says plainly what becomes of a nation that does this to a child. The Torah commands the love of the resident alien more often than it commands anything else — love the stranger as yourself, for you were strangers in the land of Egypt. "I was a stranger and you welcomed me," the Lord says of himself at the last judgment; "I was in prison and you came to me." And the first thing the Gospels record the Holy Family doing with the infant Christ is run: out of one country and into another, looking for safety. The boy in the video is the stranger. He is in prison. He is among the least of these twice over — stranger and child — and there is no exemption clause for paperwork. There is no honest reading of that Scripture that does not name this week's work as sin.

Before I name the officers and the secretaries again, the accounting that is mine. I wore this country's uniform, and I know how an institution teaches a person's hands to do a thing while his conscience is told to file the report afterward. I have framed houses beside men who could not give their names at the payroll window, and I took the wage. The parishes I pray in have hung welcome signs while the vans ran at night. And the machine did not begin with this president: Adam Goodman, who wrote the history of what he calls the deportation machine, counts roughly eight million formal deportations across the past century and some fifty million so-called voluntary departures, fear doing quietly what paperwork could not, under administrations of both parties that all spoke of securing the border — and families were being separated structurally long before any policy carried a name. None of that softens what the video shows. It convicts the machine the video shows, and it tells me what this administration added is its own: it revoked the protections that kept Haitian families lawful, deported fathers too fast for paper to follow, and now calls the child it separated an unaccompanied child.

On Sunday the president told 60 Minutes that the deportations have been "too tough" for many people, that enforcement "both helped and hurt," and that it contributed to his party's losses on November 3. I will take him at his word for exactly as long as it takes to drive a third-grader home. If the policy was too tough, the proof is an eight-year-old in federal custody right now. An admission is not repentance. Return the boy to his uncle. Restore what was stripped from his community. Then we can talk about softness.

What humane policy requires here is not a mystery. A child stays with the people who feed him, walk him to school, and pray over him; kinship is recognized in weeks, not litigation-years; a check-in is an appointment, never an ambush; and when a country cannot safely keep its people, protection is extended, not revoked. The bishops of the United States and Mexico wrote exactly that more than twenty years ago, and no administration of either party has kept all of it. This one has turned the safety appointment itself into the mechanism of seizure. A nation that requires a family to keep its appointments owes that family its word that compliance means safety, not bait. Repentance has a shape, and it is not complicated: return the child to his uncle's house while the questions are answered; restore the protections revoked in August; and write the rule that no father's deportation may manufacture an "unaccompanied" child out of a family that plainly exists.

Óscar Romero stood in the cathedral in San Salvador on March 23, 1980, the day before he was shot dead at the altar, and told the soldiers of his own country — men with orders, men with rifles — that no soldier is obliged to obey an order against the law of God, that no one has to carry out an immoral order, that it was time to recover their consciences and obey them rather than the order of sin. Then three verbs climbing: "I beg you, I beseech you, I order you in the name of God: stop the repression!" I say it now to the three officers in the video and to the secretaries whose departments issued the statements: the order in front of you is smaller than his, and clearer. You are not obliged. You can put the restraints down. You can put the boy in a car and drive him to his uncle's door. The door of return here is not a metaphor. It is a driveway, a knock, a handoff — and you will be received as what you still are: a man, not an order.

I was eight once, in a classroom in El Paso, the morning a desk near mine came up empty after a pre-dawn raid and the teacher kept on teaching. I learned the lesson she was not giving. In San Diego this week there is a third-grade desk with nobody in it; a room full of eight-year-olds sees it every morning, and so does the One who said: I was in prison, and you did not come to me. Bring the boy back, and let him sit down.
