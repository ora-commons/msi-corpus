The state capitol in Jackson was full on Thursday night. Hundreds of Black Mississippians had come to testify against a plan to redraw the state's congressional lines. They came with hand-painted signs and with chants that shook the marble corridors inside: "Leave our maps alone. Whose maps? Our maps." An hour before the hearing was scheduled to begin, the room was already full — people leaning against the walls, an overflow section outside watching the proceedings on television screens. Inside, on the bench, sat the joint redistricting committee: sixteen Republicans, all but one of them white, and four Black Democrats, handpicked by legislative leaders.

This fight is familiar territory, Safia Malin, policy director at One Voice, told the crowd before the hearing. "The giants of the civil rights movement didn't fight so that we could sit this fight out. They fought so we could stand exactly where we're standing right now and finish what they started."

She is right about the territory. She is right about the giants. What the giants never had to do was sit through eight hearings that were always going to end the same way.

This is what the fight in Mississippi has looked like since 1890, when the state wrote itself a new constitution to disenfranchise the Black citizens it had just been forced to count. That constitution ushered in Jim Crow. The Voting Rights Act tore it open. Now the Supreme Court has given the state permission to try again.

The trigger was *Louisiana v. Callais*, the decision that gutted Section 2 of the Voting Rights Act. It did not just rewrite federal law. It threw the door open, and in Mississippi the mapmakers walked through it the same week. Within days, Tate Reeves, the governor, was on X writing that he expected "lawmakers to redraw congressional lines BETWEEN NOW and 2027 elections!" He called a special session to do it. Then he cancelled it. The eight hearings were the substitute.

The target is Bennie Thompson, Mississippi's only Black congressman, who represents the Delta and who has held the seat for decades. His sin was chairing the House January 6 committee. State Auditor Shad White, who is openly considering a run for governor, took the microphone Thursday and went straight at him. "The delta deserves better leadership than Bennie Thompson. It just does," White said, as the audience booed him down. Through the boos he invoked the "silent majority" — Richard Nixon's 1969 framing, recycled wholesale — and accused Thompson of being "focused on running the January 6th committee." The line landed the way Nixon's line always lands when it is being used to disenfranchise people.

Thursday was the day the targeting stopped being whispered in back rooms and started being broadcast from a podium.

The committee had heard eight hearings around the state. The Jackson hearing was the last, and it lasted just under two hours. Sixty-seven people got two minutes each. Six of the first ten speakers were white. The first Black woman called to testify was the fifteenth person to speak. The committee did not answer questions. It did not explain how testimony would shape the maps. It did not pretend the outcome was in doubt.

House Democratic leader Robert Johnson, who is Black, told the audience to treat every word as historical record. "This is testimony," he said. "And at some point it may be evidence. So let's make sure we're heard."

Walter Boone, who is white, called the effort "Mississippi plan 2.0." He was being literal. "The original Mississippi plan culminated in our 1890 constitution, and we cannot ignore that history," he told the committee. "I'm not a scholar or trained historian, and even I know that Mississippi has been here before. Our first reconstruction ended in much the same way, through a culmination of redistricting and fraud. Our Black elected representatives were stripped of their offices, and Black constituents did not see another person represent them that looked like them for 75 years." After the 1890 constitution passed, Black Mississippians would not see another person who looked like them represent them in Congress for three quarters of a century. The state is not improvising here. It knows exactly what it is doing. It did it before.

Jesse Otto Smith, seventy-six years old, told the committee that his great-grandfather was born enslaved in 1861. That he couldn't vote. That his grandfather, born in 1891, couldn't vote. That his father, born in 1926, couldn't vote. "That's why I'm here," he said. Because his ancestors could not be.

Lauren Wilson, a sophomore at Tougaloo College, put the choice in biblical and historical terms. "The pharaoh had the position, but Moses had the power. Woodrow Wilson had the position, but Ida B. Wells had the power. Lyndon B. Johnson had the position, but Martin Luther King had the power," she said. "So I want you to know that even though tonight you have the position, we hold the power." She is twenty years old. She has read her history. The committee members, almost all of them older and richer and whiter than she is, sat and listened and said nothing about whether any of it would matter.

The committee had been selected to look like the legislature. The testimony was selected to look like a hearing.

This is not a Mississippi story alone. The same week Mississippi held its hearings, Missouri's redistricting defiance was generating contempt summonses before that state's supreme court, most recently summoning Jason Hoskins after months of refusal. South Carolina has defied the White House's pressure campaign. The courts have become the only thing standing between the remains of the Voting Rights Act and the partisan gerrymander. The pattern is unmistakable: a post-*Callais* map-drawing scramble, with every state party racing to lock in maximum advantage before 2028. The court created a vacuum. The statehouses are filling it.

What the protesters in Jackson understand, and what too many of the people who write about this from a distance still do not, is that the map is the battlefield. This is not a fight about whether some district is "fair" in the abstract. It is about who gets to draw the lines, who gets to count, and who gets to be counted. Section 2 of the Voting Rights Act existed precisely to police this. It has been stripped of most of its teeth. The work of policing has now moved to the statehouses and the courtrooms, and the response has to match the scale of the assault. Republican mapmakers are coordinating across state lines. The response cannot be fifty separate last stands.

The full Mississippi legislature is expected to convene later this year for a special session to redraw the state's legislative maps. Those maps will not affect November's midterms — that deadline has already passed. Bennie Thompson is not up for reelection until 2028. That is the timeline. That is what the clock is ticking toward.

The committee heard from sixty-seven Mississippians because the law requires that it look like it heard from sixty-seven Mississippians. The committee has filed away its sixty-seven pieces of "testimony." The map will be drawn the way the governor wants it drawn. The Supreme Court has told the state it can. The legislature has been told it can. The committee has been told it can.

The only people being told anything at all are the ones the map is being drawn against.

And they are showing up anyway.
