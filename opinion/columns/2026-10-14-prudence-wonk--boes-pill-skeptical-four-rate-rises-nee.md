The Bank of England is making British households pay for its patience. Its chief economist confirmed as much on Wednesday, on the record, in a single skeptical sentence: "Is it obvious that we will need to follow market pricing to four 25 basis point hikes? There I would be a little more skeptical." Four quarter-point rises — a full percentage point of tightening — is what investors have priced. Chief Economist Huw Pill, who has spent recent meetings dissenting from the majority in favor of a rate rise, now says the market's path may overshoot what the inflation fight requires. The size of the response, he said, is uncertain, but may not be as large as investors expect.

Here is the documentary record. The policy rate has sat at 3.75% since December. Over that period the Federal Reserve and the European Central Bank both raised borrowing costs in response to the energy prices produced by the U.S.–Iran war. The Bank of England did not move. It is now the only major Western central bank choosing not to respond to the same shock, and the choice has been public since the [September hold](/articles/2026-09-17-bank-of-england-holds-rate-at-3-75-while-fed-ecb-raise-theirs/), when [Pill voted with the minority for tightening](/articles/2026-09-03-boe-s-pill-argues-war-uncertainty-supports-rate-hike/), arguing the committee could not afford to wait for clarity on the war's duration.

Translate that into what the hold does. Inflation is running near 4 percent against a 2 percent mandate — prices rising at twice the rate the committee is legislated to deliver, with a forecast that [accommodates the deviation rather than correcting it](/articles/2026-09-17-bank-of-england-holds-rate-at-3-75-while-fed-ecb-raise-theirs/). With the policy rate at 3.75%, the return on money is set below the rate at which money is losing value. That is a negative real policy rate, and its effect is distributed, quiet, and universal: the pension fixed to an index that lags, the cash balance in a savings account, the weekly shop. Nothing appears on a statement. The cost simply arrives as a higher price for the same goods, absorbed by every household that spends rather than borrows.

Now name the tradeoff, because the committee is not avoiding a distributional choice — it is making one. Raising rates into this shock would deliver a sharp, visible, concentrated cost: the monthly payment of every household on a variable mortgage, the refinancing bill of every borrower coming off a fixed term, the demand of every business financing inventory. Holding rates delivers a slow, invisible, diffuse one: the erosion of real incomes across the whole population. The Bank has chosen the second instrument. Households on fixed incomes, savers, and wage earners whose pay adjusts slower than prices bear that cost. Households carrying fixed-rate debt are spared it twice — their payments do not rise, and inflation quietly reduces the real value of what they owe. This is not a scandal. It is the design. It should be described as the design.

The market has been reading the Fed and the ECB as a directive and pricing a Bank of England that mirrors them. The reaction function — the rule a committee actually follows when it sets rates — now says something different. A central bank that tolerates inflation at twice target while its peers tighten into the same energy shock, that forecasts the tolerance rather than the correction, and whose chief economist publicly doubts the market's path, is not a mirror. It is a committee that has decided the supply shock should be absorbed by real incomes rather than by demand policy, and that has decided to say so in increments.

Concede the strongest version of the case. An energy price spike is a supply shock. Raising the policy rate does not lower the price of oil and gas; it compresses demand that the same shock has already compressed. Pill has never argued the direction is wrong — he has voted for hikes at recent meetings. His skepticism is about magnitude, which is the honest uncertainty. But uncertainty is not costless, and its cost is not distributed to the committee. If the Bank under-tightens, the error lands on every household's real income, spread so thinly that no single voter can locate the cause. If it over-tightens, the error lands on debt service and employment, concentrated enough to be visible and to be reversed. The committee has chosen the error whose consequences it does not have to explain in a payment.

The MPC is now the only Western central bank declining to respond to an energy shock, and it holds that position with an internal forecast of 4 percent inflation in hand. That position may well be correct. It is still a choice, and choices have authors. The question for the committee is not whether patience is defensible — it is. The question is why the households paying for it were never asked, and why the cost of the policy appears in the price of groceries rather than in any document where it could be counted. The Bank of England has told the market it may not need four rises. It has not told the pensioner buying bread on a fixed index how many years of 4 percent she is being asked to fund.
