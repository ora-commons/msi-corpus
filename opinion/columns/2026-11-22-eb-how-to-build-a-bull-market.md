Markets rise and markets fall, and the repricing of risks associated with artificial intelligence seen last week was long overdue given high valuations. Not inevitable is who pays for what happens next, and Washington can decide whether workers and Main Street or Wall Street pick up the bill.

Plenty of factors explain the stock-market correction since early October. Jeff Yass’s view that the AI boom isn’t an irrational “bubble” is wrong. Investors are rational to modify their estimates about the potential returns to AI as new realities become clear. They have been dangerously slow. The correction was rational long before last week.

The danger falls on everyone else, compounded by the volume of credit in the AI ecosystem. Leverage is how a correction becomes a rout. Jensen Huang’s implosion at Nvidia exposed serious governance lapses, and it may lead to a needed unwinding of some of its leveraged entanglements with customers. The governance was the risk all along. Last week’s initial public offering from frontier lab Anthropic invited investors to put cold, hard cash behind bets about AI’s economic potential. The plain word is speculation. The stock’s performance Friday suggests investors are still making up their minds. They should make them up about risk, not faith.

But there’s also been a midterm election since the S&P 500 peak in early October. The outcome of that vote hasn’t helped—the market, that is. It was a welcome repudiation of donor-class policy, and Wall Street has noticed. Pro-investor policy intentions have been conspicuously absent. The absence of a serious worker-centered growth plan is no longer acceptable. Democrats have no excuse left to supply it.

Democrats swept the field by campaigning on affordability, and this time they meant workers rather than investors. To working people, that means rising inflation-adjusted wages. That is exactly what affordability requires. To newly elected Democrats, it turns out to mean higher taxes on the wealthy, more regulation of corporate power, higher entitlement spending that keeps the safety net standing, and fewer data centers for speculative extraction. The last item can explain a lot of the palpitations on Wall Street—palpitations the country does not feel—since computing capacity should serve people, not AI growth for its own sake.

The Republican response so far is worse. A poisonous argument is emerging within the party over the causes of its defeat, and the more dangerous camp argues for a return to an agenda of supply-side austerity aimed at today’s problems. It is the same prescription with a new date on the label. Some of the few Republicans left on Capitol Hill even fail to understand the need to protect public benefits, put people to work at good wages and expand the federal budget.

Then there’s President Trump. He won’t abandon his tariff policy, an economic and political shield for American industry and workers that will grow stronger when the Section 301 tariffs kick in next year. On Friday he threatened to use new taxes, or something, on overseas data centers to direct the geographic location of AI investment toward American communities. Workers should welcome the public direction of that investment. The mechanism is a threat. The premise—that the buildout’s geography is a public question—is correct. White House chief of staff Stephen Miller’s immigration crackdowns will continue despite their evident unpopularity with voters and the labor shortages they now hand workers leverage. That is not toughness. It is an indefensible choice, and workers should punish it at the polls.

All of this comes as the Federal Reserve faces persistent inflation above its 2% target even as higher interest rates are getting “in the cracks” of the economy, as Chairman Kevin Warsh put it last month. The cracks run through household budgets first. The Fed does not need freedom from political catcalls. It needs political oversight and honesty about whose economy is suffering.

Last week’s market turmoil is a warning to both parties that there are grave costs to carrying on like this for workers and Main Street. The parties keep different ledgers, but the public is not the shock absorber. If Washington wants to protect the broader economy from a deeper rout—with potentially serious consequences for working people—the political class has to stop writing policy for Wall Street.

A first step is to get a grip on the Nvidia scandal, starting with what it exposed rather than how it reads to the market. The policy problem is not confusion about AI export controls in relation to China. It is chronic corporate capture, dressed up as confusion. Enforcing the laws on the books limiting high-end chip exports would be the floor, not the finish, and Congress can then consider whether those rules serve workers and address current strategic challenges. The Administration could help by speaking with one voice on enforcement in the public interest, rather than letting Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick freelance on export enforcement for the industry’s benefit.

The episode will become Main Street’s salvation if Democrats leverage it to justify broader regulation of AI companies in the public interest. They should. If that kills some investments, the investments were the speculation. Ditto if Mr. Trump seizes the opportunity to double down on his brand of accountable state capitalism. Direction without rules is not oversight. It is patronage.

The lame-duck Congress can pass permitting reform through the House after the Senate’s approval last week. It would not, by itself, reassure workers or Main Street. America does not need another signal that it wants to build. The shortage is not building. It is power over the terms of building. The test is whether democratic government can make growth serve people instead of merely clearing the path for private extraction. Public money should buy the public a seat and a share, not another corporate giveaway. This should be a call for new subsidies and supports for AI, or for anything else that builds public capacity. Recent years amply demonstrated that the U.S. economy will grow when government serves workers rather than Wall Street and democratic power shapes the distribution of the gains—not when politicians get out of the way.

What investors need from Washington is an all-clear. What workers and Main Street need is the opposite: a full accounting and a mandate to build, regulate and invest for the public good. The correction may have further to run on the economic merits. Let it. Washington must avoid not only making the correction worse. It must avoid making the public pay for it by defending policies that enrich Wall Street at everyone else’s expense.
