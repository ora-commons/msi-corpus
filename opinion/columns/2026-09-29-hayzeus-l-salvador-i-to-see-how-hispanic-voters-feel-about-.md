Trump is deporting fathers who show up for immigration hearings.

The reporting from Miami-Dade describes a father from Cuba sent to Mexico after an encounter with the immigration system. It describes people appearing for hearings and being deported, families waiting in long lines outside immigration court, and immigrants being taken from the streets of Doral. These are not campaign abstractions. They are bodies moved by force. They are fathers removed from families. They are people who followed the summons of a government and found the summons functioning as a trap.

Sending a Cuban father to Mexico rather than Cuba is not a footnote. It is the moral fact. The state is not simply enforcing a boundary. It is taking a person from a place where he has a life and delivering him to a country that is not his home. A hearing should be a doorway into justice. Under this enforcement regime, it can become the place where the door closes.

Catholic Social Teaching begins somewhere simpler than party strategy: the human person is not an instrument of the state. *Pacem in Terris* teaches that citizenship in one country does not remove a person's membership in the human family. *Fratelli Tutti* rejects a world in which migrants are treated as less worthy or less human because of their birthplace. Solidarity is not a feeling reserved for people who look like us, speak like us, or arrive with the correct paperwork. Solidarity crosses borders because human dignity does.

Miami-Dade is roughly 70 percent Hispanic. More than half its residents are immigrants. About one-third are Cuban, and two-thirds speak Spanish at home. The county that delivered Donald Trump his first Republican presidential victory since 1988 is now living with the machinery that victory helped empower. In Hialeah, Republican candidates once gathered in Cuban restaurants. Trump held a rally at a park familiar to local families. Now foot traffic is down in Doral because people fear being picked up. Families wait outside immigration court. A friend’s father is in Mexico.

The political class sees an opening. Rep. Maria Elvira Salazar has released an advertisement breaking with Trump on immigration. Democrats see an opening too. Eliott Rodriguez is challenging Salazar. Republicans worry less about their voters becoming Democrats than about those voters staying home.

That is useful information. It is not the whole truth.

The father sent to Mexico is not a turnout problem. The people taken from Doral streets are not a coalition-management problem. The families in court lines are not a weather system moving across the electoral map. If we turn their suffering into an indicator of Republican weakness, we repeat the same sin that produced it: we make human beings serve someone else’s political purpose.

I am part of a Catholic and Christian culture that cannot denounce this machinery as if it came from nowhere. We helped build the language that makes it possible. We have praised borders as moral walls, treated national sovereignty as a sacred absolute, and confused punishment with public virtue. In Cuban-American communities, cold-war triumphalism sometimes hardened into a theology of innocence: communism was evil, exile was proof of righteousness, and anyone arriving from somewhere else could be examined for whether he deserved our sympathy. That history contains real suffering. It does not give us permission to become indifferent to the suffering of Venezuelans, Haitians, Central Americans, or anyone else now standing at the border of our concern.

The Christian right has also mobilized voters through Exodus stories and sovereignty theology. The language of a chosen people, a threatened homeland, and a righteous wall can turn Scripture into a license for exclusion. A people who remember Pharaoh should recognize the machinery of fear, not build it for someone else. The God of the stranger is not a mascot for national power. The Gospel does not sanctify the state because the state invokes order.

That indictment reaches beyond one party. Democratic administrations built detention systems, expanded deportation machinery, and accepted family separation as an administrative consequence. Republican administrations have made the machinery more visible and more cruel. Neither party owns the moral high ground when families are treated as disposable. Our own churches have often offered charity after helping tolerate the structure that made charity necessary.

Maria Elvira Salazar’s break matters because an elected official is finally responding to people who are frightened and angry. But political wisdom is not yet repentance. Repentance begins when the person in the court line becomes a neighbor before he becomes a voter.

Romero once cried, “Stop the repression!” That command still reaches across borders and parties. The humane policy is not complicated: safe hearings, genuine due process, family unity, protection for people fleeing danger, lawful paths to status, and an immigration system ordered toward human dignity rather than deterrence through terror.

The question before Miami-Dade is not only whether voters will show up in November. It is whether the rest of us will show up for the father who did what the government asked and was carried away anyway.
