Jensen Huang and Nvidia's board sold America's chip-export controls for China revenue.

The channel is already under federal investigation. The U.S. Attorney's Office in Manhattan leads a probe into whether entities linked to Alice Huang — an executive at the Singapore data-center company Megaspeed and, by the reporting that ended Mr. Huang's [tenure as chief executive](/articles/2026-11-12-jensen-huang-resigns-as-nvidia-ceo-after-affair-reports/), a suspected Chinese intelligence asset — smuggled Super Micro products into China. The hardware named is Super Micro's, not Nvidia's; the allegation concerns a smuggling channel, not a license application. What the file establishes is that the perimeter around exported computing hardware is porous enough that a corporate executive is the alleged conduit. The affair cost Mr. Huang the job. The controls were supposed to cost Beijing the compute. Export controls are the licensing rules that decide which advanced processors may lawfully leave the country; the machines that train frontier AI models also train military targeting and surveillance systems, which is why every high-end chip Nvidia ships is a controlled item, licensed or barred by regulation. The scandal did not create the exposure. It published it.

Here are the numbers, because the sequence is the indictment. In April 2025 the Commerce Department required licenses for the H20, a processor Nvidia had built to China's permitted specifications after earlier rounds of controls — built for the market the law had closed — in a licensing notice published in the *Federal Register*. Against that inventory the company disclosed a $5.5 billion charge in a single quarter, in the quarterly report it filed with the Securities and Exchange Commission, for chips it could no longer lawfully ship. The chief executive then made the case in person: to President Trump, to the press, in Washington and in Beijing. In July 2025 a second *Federal Register* notice withdrew the licensing requirement, and the company announced plans to resume China sales. The appeals returned exactly what they sought. The write-off lasted one quarter. The loosening was the point.

The terms of the trade belong in the record too. Press reports described an arrangement under which the federal government would take fifteen percent of the resumed China sales — a figure that reached the public through the press, not through the rule. A national-security restriction converted into a product line with a commission schedule. The company supplied the year of lobbying and the personal appeals; the rule came back withdrawn; the market came back open; the government took its commission off the top. That is what was sold, and this is what it went for.

Understand what was traded. The export-control regime, built in October 2022 and tightened in 2023, rests on one doctrine: keep the computing frontier out of Beijing's reach long enough that China's domestic alternatives mature later rather than sooner. That doctrine was argued on the merits inside the agencies and the Congress for years. In 2025 it was overwritten in a single season: [Nvidia won looser China chip rules over documented national-security warnings](/articles/2026-11-12-nvidia-s-huang-wins-looser-china-chip-rules-despite-national-security-warnings/), after a lobbying campaign run by the largest beneficiary of the change, over objections that lawmakers of both parties had placed on the record before this scandal existed. Bipartisan scrutiny on Capitol Hill over chip exports to China predated the affair reports, so the pressure on the company was never a reaction to Mr. Huang's conduct. Anyone who has read the *Federal Register* notices imposing and then withdrawing the H20 licensing requirement knows the difference between an argument made on the merits and one made by the party the rule constrains. The first gets a hearing. The second gets a decision. The mechanism has a proper name: capture of a security instrument by its regulated subject.

The campaign needed a doctrine, and it found one in the language of competitiveness. Mr. Huang argued for a year that blocking China would only accelerate Chinese self-sufficiency; that the depth of China's AI talent made the controls futile; that the American lead is preserved by selling rather than withholding. Portions of that argument may even be correct as industrial policy — which is precisely what makes it an effective laundering vehicle. A revenue argument dressed as security doctrine can absorb any objection, because the doctrine was composed after the forecast. The architecture is not new. In the last financial crisis, American International Group's Financial Products subsidiary — the derivatives business at the center of the counterparty blowup — ended up supervised by the Office of Thrift Supervision because AIG's parent had acquired a few small savings banks. Alan Blinder records the mechanism in *After the Music Stopped*: the subsidiary "managed to get itself regulated by the Office of Thrift Supervision (OTS) because the corporate parent company had acquired a few small savings banks." The federal supervisor of the largest single link in the credit-default-swap chain was selected by the chain. This time the perimeter is the export-control regime, and the entity that drew it outward is the company that sells through it. Extraction under regulatory cover is still the name of it, whatever the commodity.

Notice what did not happen. The warnings were not answered. They were absorbed. Anat Admati and Martin Hellwig identify regulatory capture as epistemic — an objection is not refuted but translated into a vocabulary in which raising it sounds naive — with willful blindness one of its three modes, alongside ignorance and purposeful obtuseness. The vocabulary doing that work here is competitiveness: a restriction becomes a cost to American chipmakers, a warning becomes an obstacle to American chipmakers, and the security claim never has to be met on its own terms because it never arrives in its own terms. A warning carries no number. A revenue line carries a number. When the two are weighed in the same room, the one with the number wins, and that is not a finding about who was right. It is a finding about which side of the argument was countable.

Put the consequence next to the mechanism, because the delay is the entire content of the doctrine. The compute that crosses the line under a relaxed license does not sit idle in commercial data centers. The same accelerators that train frontier models train the targeting models defense planning runs on, and they carry the inference behind large-scale surveillance — face matching, video analytics at scale, signal classification — that deployed systems consume. The doctrine promised exactly one thing: that this hardware would arrive within Beijing's reach later rather than sooner, so the systems built on it would arrive later too. Every quarter of withdrawn licensing and every shipment cleared under it buys that delay back from the timeline the national-security agencies argued for. The bill does not land on Nvidia, and it does not land on the agencies that objected. It lands downstream, on the people the delay was written to protect — the forces and the publics who meet a targeting or surveillance capability that matured ahead of the schedule the rule was meant to hold — and none of them were in the room when the requirement was withdrawn or the commission was set.

Then the remedy now on the table. The board approved the campaign, and the resignation does not retire the vote. Shareholder litigation reads a resignation over undisclosed conduct as a failure of board oversight and disclosure, and such a suit will be argued inside the four corners of fiduciary duty: what the directors knew, when they knew it, what the market was told. None of that reopens a licensing decision. The wrong on the record is that an export regime was relaxed over documented objections; the machinery available to answer it is a claim about a personal matter. Two different questions, two different defendants, and only one of them is in a position to change the rule. The company and its directors now carry that exposure, and a resignation does not mollify Congress — the objections stay on the record, and the benefit ran in exactly one direction.

The corrective the record supports is procedural, which is why it keeps losing to the checkbook: return the licensing decision to the joint review the national-security agencies are charged to run, and require any change relaxing an export control on a named destination to carry the objecting agency's written answer — dated, signed, and published with the rule — before it takes effect, and to be reported to Congress with the objection attached so it can be voted on rather than absorbed. Anyone who has read a licensing docket knows a beneficiary does not return a loosened control voluntarily. The genre is stable: the costs are modeled, the objections are footnoted, and the conclusion was obtained in advance.

Prosecute the smuggling as the floor of the response, because a control that can be carried around the counter is not a control. Mr. Huang has left Nvidia. The loosened rules remain in force, and the national-security warnings that preceded them remain on the record, unanswered. The resignation answers the affair; it does not answer the sequence of April, July, and the fifteen percent, all of it in writing. The affair is the fact about a man; the capture is the fact about a company's hold on a country's export policy. This was decided in advance and the security doctrine was retrofitted to the sales forecast.

What the record asks for is specific: reopen the July 2025 withdrawal, publish the objecting agencies' written answers — dated, signed, and attached to the notice — and send the licensing decision to Congress for a recorded vote with those answers in front of it. A loosening that survives that vote is a decision. A loosening that cannot survive it was never one. The company that lobbied the rule open does not get to write the answer to the objection it overrode.
