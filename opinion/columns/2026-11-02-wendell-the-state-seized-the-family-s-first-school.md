Colorado did not merely lock Catholic schools out of its public preschool program; it made the State’s definition of marriage, sexuality, and the human person the admission price to a child’s first school. Andrea Picciotti-Bayer’s Fox News Opinion essay, [“ANDREA PICCIOTTI-BAYER: Colorado's universal preschool revives vile anti-Catholic laws”](https://www.foxnews.com/opinion/andrea-picciotti-bayer-colorados-universal-preschool-revives-vile-anti-catholic-laws), argues that Colorado’s “universal” preschool program denies Catholic schools access to that program unless they accept an “equal opportunity” clause covering sexual orientation and gender identity. She asks the Supreme Court, which is hearing *St. Mary Catholic Parish v. Roy* on November 3, to stop Colorado’s modern return of the Blaine amendments. The benefit is worth about $6,000 a year at the school a family chooses. Colorado does not forbid a Catholic school to remain private; the alleged coercion lies in conditioning access to public preschool aid on surrendering the school’s religious mission.

The terms are not paper. The Sheleys have seven children, five already at St. Mary’s, and have paid thousands of dollars out of pocket. A father described in the dispute pays for Catholic preschool while his taxes help fund other children’s preschool education. His child has spina bifida, and Medicaid coverage helps keep the family in Colorado. An additional $6,000 would go a long way toward caring for her.

A mother in the case works at a lumber company. Her husband paints houses, though his work slows every winter. They have six children and a seventh on the way. She once assumed Catholic school was only “for rich people.” A second mother started a florist business to help pay tuition. Three of her children attend a Catholic school in Denver, but her youngest now attends a Lutheran preschool in the state program. Another parent took a lower-paying job at the parish school for a tuition discount and watched families leave because pre-K had become one bill too many.

Across these families, the sequence is brutal and ordinary: tuition, a smaller wage, a child sent elsewhere, a school closing, a brief waiting to see whether the Constitution will return what the program has already taken. A parish school is not a delivery vehicle for state-approved childhood. It is a place where family and parish meet before either can fully put words to itself. When the price of belonging to that place rises beyond what parents can carry, the institution does not become less religious. It becomes a building with no children in it.

The State cannot make religious fidelity a condition of aid and call refusal a failure of inclusion. That is the strongest honest point of Picciotti-Bayer’s case, and conservative constitutionalism requires the Court to honor it. Public preschool money may carry common rules for safety, service, and nondiscrimination. Colorado crosses the line when theological conformity becomes one of the funding conditions.

Colorado’s rules say that many preschools may serve only children with disabilities, only low-income children, only children of color, only gender-nonconforming children, or only LGBTQ+ children, and they permit officials to grant further exceptions case by case. They grant no comparable flexibility to Catholic schools. Colorado has made room for mission-specific preschools serving particular communities while telling the Catholic school to surrender what it is. That is not equal treatment. It is equal treatment with the Church required to go.

The year that should trouble us is 1875. That year Speaker of the House James G. Blaine proposed a constitutional amendment barring public money from “sectarian” schools. The House passed it overwhelmingly; the Senate fell just short. The states took it up, and by 1900 twenty-eight had written versions into their constitutions. None said “Catholic.” None needed to. The public schools of the day were predominantly Protestant in practice, with daily instruction, prayers, and hymns built around the King James Bible. Catholic families that wanted their children taught in their faith built schools of their own.

That is the inheritance in question: not a sectarian appetite for public money, but the small mediating institutions—parish, school, cooperative, lodge—through which people learned their duties and their place in a common life. When two preschools close, that web loses rooms where children were formed and parents learned to be neighbors.

The modern law is not innocent novelty. *Trinity Lutheran* in 2017, *Espinoza* in 2020, *Carson* in 2022, and *Chiles* in 2026 form a line under the principle that a state cannot disqualify a school merely because it is religious or practices its faith. Colorado has been told more than once, in *Masterpiece Cakeshop* in 2018, *303 Creative* in 2023, and *Chiles* in 2026, that constitutional guarantees do not collapse under ideological pressure. Colorado’s rule does not merely regulate a preschool. It asks a religious community to renounce its account of marriage, sexuality, and the human person. Washington can forbid that coercion. It cannot build a classroom.

I manage a farm cooperative now, and I used to trade agricultural futures from a Chicago commodities desk. There, a seller could hedge a crop at a stated future price and transfer the later price movement to another participant; the price could fall or rise without erasing the contract. Colorado’s rule gives a Catholic preschool no acceptable participation terms in the public program. There is no lower rate it can accept and remain Catholic, only exclusion from the program the State created. A license, unlike a price, does not reward adjustment; it commands belief. Public money may carry ordinary standards for health, safety, and teaching. It cannot require the parish to hire a teacher or write a curriculum as the condition of surrendering the faith that brought the parish into being.

Here is where the conservative answer—and the fusionism it has smuggled into the cause—has betrayed itself. An anti-rentier economy asks who captures the surplus from an essential local institution. Concentrated capital has its own way of dissolving rooted things: a private-equity owner can strip a nursing home for a quarterly return, treating generations of care as a balance-sheet asset; a corporate landlord can hollow out Main Street while calling the rent market discipline. Concentrated state power carries the same appetite in administrative dress. It can strip a parish school of its beliefs and call the submission equal opportunity. Fusionism is the doctrine that pretends a neutral administrative state is the highest politics, so a family may keep its parish only if the parish signs the State’s moral terms. That is not conservatism. It is surrender in a more fashionable vocabulary.

I distrust Christian nationalism on religious grounds: a nation cannot be Christian; only a person can, and only with difficulty. But official pluralism can carry the same coercive appetite in another costume. A Christian nationalism that made Christendom compulsory would make one confession automatic; Colorado’s licensing rule makes approved pluralism compulsory for access to public preschool. Both abolish freedom by making one account of persons the price of membership. The difference of belief is the very thing the school and family are meant to preserve.

Here is the fraternal correction I owe the advocates of administrative secularism: a state that insists upon the secular character of public education has not thereby earned the right to define the human person. It has simply moved the confession from a school to a constitution. Society is an inheritance of institutions, not a market basket assembled by a distant planner. A larger body should not absorb the work a smaller body can do. Colorado may use public money. It has no warrant to define the soul. The State does not merely purchase seats; it attempts to manufacture the kind of person who may occupy them.

Nor should the conservative movement imagine that school choice is release from responsibility. Too many have treated public failure as proof of the public’s worthlessness. They want parents to flee failing schools but leave the public school behind without a plan, then bless the closure as freedom. That is the rentier error we condemn elsewhere: extract the public good, privatize the balance sheet, leave the obligation behind.

A family may choose a Catholic school and receive aid; the common good still asks what becomes of the public school, the public teacher, and the child whose family cannot afford the private choice. Human dignity stands above property, but property is a real good and not an idol; the earth was given for all. A school is a moral community before it is a balance sheet.

Picciotti-Bayer has diagnosed the coercion correctly. A favorable Supreme Court ruling would matter, and I will not cheapen the families’ claim by pretending a local workaround can substitute for constitutional protection. Yet no decision in Washington can build a school. It cannot hire the teacher, settle the calendar, mend the fracture among parents, or teach a child to pray. A judicial exception can forbid the State from compelling belief; a local community must still form the person. We should want both.

The answer is not a federal preschool bureaucracy or a larger state waiver. Colorado should lose the coercion. A court order can forbid the wrong, but it cannot build the right. The durable answer is a local settlement in which a parish preschool can teach from its faith, a public school can teach from a shared civic curriculum, and parents can choose among institutions that answer to families and communities rather than to a distant preference.

The State should write ordinary terms: safety, hours, attendance, child welfare, and demonstrable educational service. It should not write the terms of marriage, sexuality, or identity into the license of a four-year-old. Portable public aid and neutral civil minimums belong to the State, while seats, calendars, and local arrangements belong to the parish, public school, parents, and local boards together. The law can keep the common fund from becoming a test of orthodoxy; the local community can keep education from becoming a bureaucratic commodity. The State’s job is not to redeem the child. Its job is to protect the child’s right to be formed by a community able to answer for the formation.

Build a federation of member-owned preschool cooperatives. A cooperative can own its building or lease it locally, employ its teachers, pool purchasing, set sliding-scale tuition, and answer to the families who use it. Its rules should be plain: voluntary membership, democratic control, one member and one vote, and any surplus held for the common enterprise rather than paid to an absentee owner. A Catholic preschool could remain Catholic. A secular cooperative could remain secular. A family that wanted to leave could leave without asking the State’s permission.

Public money, where it enters, should follow the child as a portable benefit. It should not purchase the right to inspect a child’s formation or command a curriculum. The cooperative may fail; its members may reorganize or dissolve it. What they must not surrender is the authority to govern their own common enterprise.

This is harder than issuing another rule from the capital or winning a Supreme Court case. Families must find one another, keep books, train teachers, argue over tuition and curriculum, and carry the risk of failure themselves. They must also protect the stranger and the child whose family cannot pay the same amount. That difficulty is not incidental. It is the border of freedom.

Two preschools have already closed. The Sheley family has paid thousands out of pocket. The family seeking a place for a child cannot wait for the opinion. Meanwhile, the parish school must keep its door open, the local public school must keep its promise to every child, and the State must fund educational service without claiming the soul. Build the answer in parish halls, union rooms, and vacant storefronts, one classroom at a time, before another family must send its youngest child elsewhere.

In 1875, Catholic families built schools despite the Blaine amendment; their inheritance was not privilege but a school, a parish, and a place. We can build that kind of institution again—not as nostalgia, and not as privilege, but as a place where the family, the parish, and the town meet the child together. Leave the parish, the school, and the town their own life. The family’s first school belongs nearest the child—to the family, the parish, and the town—not to the State.
