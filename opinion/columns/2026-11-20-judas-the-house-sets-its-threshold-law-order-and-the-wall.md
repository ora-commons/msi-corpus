Mehek Cooke’s [“Trump can finally deliver the immigration bargain Reagan was promised”](https://www.foxnews.com/opinion/trump-finally-deliver-immigration-bargain-reagan-was-promised) in Fox News is the conservative case for immigration enforcement at full strength. Cooke’s argument is legal before it is political. The Immigration Reform and Control Act of 1986 granted legal status to nearly 2.7 million people and promised enforcement in return; the enforcement was temporary, and its conditions lapsed. Cooke cites a GAO finding that 39 percent of unauthorized workers in five major cities had used or were suspected of using fraudulent documents. He cites a Congressional Research Service analysis showing that by the 1990s the unauthorized population had exceeded its pre-IRCA level. Another GAO audit documented unfilled border positions, reduced agent time, and failing equipment in high-traffic corridors. A statute whose enforcement is optional is a letter of intent. Reagan’s bargain failed at the enforcement half.

Cooke begins with the Protect Our Neighbors Act, which would allow final removal orders to expire after ten years and open a path to permanent residence. His objection is precise: because due process has already produced those orders, the bill would reward evasion for a decade and manufacture lawful status out of obstruction. Existing law supplies a narrower route. Cancellation of removal requires ten years of continuous presence, good moral character, no disqualifying conviction, and proof that removal would cause exceptional and extremely unusual hardship to a citizen or lawful permanent resident spouse, parent, or child; relief remains discretionary and capped at 4,000 cases a year. Cooke calls this compassion through the rule of law. It is case-by-case compassion held inside law rather than amnesty outside it.

His institutional means are equally definite. He would codify the administration’s border policies so a later president cannot erase them; require nationwide E-Verify; penalize employers who knowingly hire unauthorized workers; fund the immigration judges; and give each judge a verified case file so orders issue promptly and consistently rather than at the pace of the backlog. Gene Hamilton, a former senior Department of Homeland Security official, told Fox News that 1.6 million people now face removal. Cooke reports that in fiscal year 2026 southwest-border apprehensions fell approximately 95 percent below the Biden-era annual average and that Border Patrol completed its first fiscal year with zero releases into the United States. His program preserves the enforcement chain intact: Border Patrol holds people in the interval, the Executive Office for Immigration Review issues final removal orders, and Immigration and Customs Enforcement carries out removals.

Cooke draws his sharpest distinction between a cartel member and a father who entered illegally fifteen years ago, paid taxes, and raised American children. The father’s removal does not remove his children’s citizenship; the children remain citizens, and he goes. Titus 3:1 commands subjection to principalities and powers; Acts 17:26 assigns each nation its “bounds of their habitation”; Nehemiah rebuilt a wall because an assembly is a household with a threshold. The law and the text describe one order: the house governs its membership, distinguishes the stranger from the heir, and does not permit enforcement to become optional merely because the law has inconvenient work to do.

Marisol Herrera was thirty-four years old. Before dawn, Border Patrol officers roused her from a room she shared with two other mothers and walked her out to a vehicle. The officers put zip-ties around her wrists. When Marisol turned her hands, the plastic cut her skin. Her daughter was six. At processing, Border Patrol officers took the girl from Marisol and put her in another vehicle. Border Patrol held Marisol in a concrete cell built for adults under a light that stayed on. Border Patrol transferred the girl to a shelter whose floors were covered with plastic. At the shelter, children slept on thin mats beneath fluorescent lamps. In some facilities, the lights remained on around the clock. Shelter staff gave Marisol’s daughter a blanket and shoes two sizes too large. She ate from foam trays at a table with children whom Border Patrol had taken from their mothers.

Staff measured Marisol at intake and weighed her at every meal. Staff also weighed the girl. The girl lost weight during the days that followed. She stopped speaking to the adults who fed her and slept on her side with her shoes still on. Some children had only a thin mat, a blanket, and no other bedding. Shelter staff did not tell Marisol which shelter held her daughter or when the girl would return. When officials brought Marisol and the girl together, the child would not release her mother’s sleeve. At night, Border Patrol agents separated children from parents without telling the parents where the children had been taken. Border Patrol operated without a system for tracking the children it removed.

In April 2018, the Department of Justice issued zero-tolerance directives that increased detention and directed Border Patrol to take children from adults in the detention chain. By June 21, 2018, Border Patrol had separated more than 2,700 children from their parents. The Department of Justice’s Office of the Inspector General recorded psychological trauma among separated children. In *Flores v. Johnson*, the court documented the separation of children from their parents and their placement in contracted shelters. Border Patrol transferred children from its facilities to contracted shelters while parents tried to locate them. The separation chain continued without a system for recording where each child had gone. Reporters filmed children sleeping on floors and in fenced pens in Border Patrol processing areas in Texas, with their mothers in nearby adult cells.

Ana Eliza Flores Villavicencio was ten. On March 14, 2019, Border Patrol detained her at the El Paso port of entry. On March 18 and March 20, Border Patrol medical providers examined her for fever, nausea, and vomiting. They diagnosed influenza A and strep throat and gave her acetaminophen and amoxicillin. On March 21, Border Patrol transferred her from the port-of-entry facility to another Border Patrol facility. On April 8, Border Patrol transported her to a hospital because her illness continued. The hospital discharged her later that day, and Border Patrol returned her to custody. At 3 a.m. on April 10, Ana Eliza died at a local hospital after cardiopulmonary arrest.

Jakob Caicedo was eight. On May 10, 2019, Border Patrol detained him near Presidio, Texas. Border Patrol medical staff examined him, diagnosed pneumonia, and gave him antibiotics. On May 17, Border Patrol released him to his family. His family took him to a hospital. On May 19, Jakob died in the hospital.

Nehemiah did not rebuild the wall and then decide whom to admit. He rebuilt it because an assembly is a people, a people is a house, and a house has a threshold. Nehemiah 13:3 treats the foreign marriage as grounds for exclusion from the assembly. The line was drawn before it was enforced. Enforcement is the last stage of a decision already taken.

Romans 13:1 teaches that there is no authority except from God. First Peter 2:13–18 commands subjection to the ordinances of men. Acts 17:26 sets the bounds of every nation’s habitation. These texts disclose one household order. The border is the wall, and the wall is the household’s settled judgment concerning who belongs within it.
