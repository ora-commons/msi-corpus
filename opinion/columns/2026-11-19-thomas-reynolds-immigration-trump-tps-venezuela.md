The Ninth Circuit helps Trump deport Venezuelans without the protection Congress created.

Wednesday, while the Ninth Circuit was still deliberating, the administration asked the Supreme Court to halt an order restoring Venezuelans’ deportation protections. Hours later, the appeals court extended its freeze. The result is not a pause while the law is tested. The termination remains operative. Work authorizations remain invalid. Deportation proceedings continue. The government’s filing identifies about 1,800 Venezuelans who previously held Temporary Protected Status in detention and roughly 330 in removal proceedings. Roughly 600,000 received TPS after the Biden administration designated Venezuela in March 2021 and expanded the designation in September 2023. The government does not say how many protected Venezuelans remain in the United States. What it does say is how many are inside the enforcement machinery while the freeze holds.

The administration’s position has a respectable form. TPS is temporary by statutory design. [8 U.S.C. § 1254a](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=granuleid:USC-prelim-title8-section1254a&num=0&edition=prelim) assigns the assessment of country conditions to DHS and authorizes designations of up to eighteen months. If a termination is reviewable on appeal, if the government’s interim pause is about to expire, and if removal cannot be undone, an appellate court can preserve the operative posture while the appeal proceeds. Nothing in that account, standing alone, requires bad faith.

The primary record is not ambiguous. DHS published the termination on March 21, 2025, at [90 Fed. Reg. 12854](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/search?conditions%5Bterm%5D=%22Termination%20of%20Designations%20for%20the%20Republic%20of%20Venezuela%22), and opened the administrative record through [Regulations.gov](https://www.regulations.gov/search?searchTerm=%22Termination%20of%20Designations%20for%20the%20Republic%20of%20Venezuela%22). The litigation is *Centro de Trabajadores Unidos v. Department of Homeland Security*, No. 3:25-cv-02927 (N.D. Cal.); the district-court opinion is [linked here](https://www.courtlistener.com/?q=%223%3A25-cv-02927%22&type=r&order_by=dateFiled%20desc). It held the termination unlawful and ordered TPS restored. On appeal, *Centro de Trabajadores Unidos v. Noem*, No. 25-5573 (9th Cir.), the appeals court issued the [interim freeze](https://www.courtlistener.com/?q=%22Centro%20de%20Trabajadores%20Unidos%20v.%20Noem%22&type=r&order_by=dateFiled%20desc). The order extended the termination through seven days after the Ninth Circuit’s full ruling. The figures come from the government’s filing, as reported by the [Associated Press](https://apnews.com/search?q=Venezuelan%20TPS%201%2C800%20330).

The sequence answers the emergency the administration invoked. The administration went to the Supreme Court while the Ninth Circuit was still deliberating and warned that a temporary pause would soon expire, allowing deportation protections to be restored. The Ninth Circuit then extended the freeze until seven days after its ruling—the contingency the administration called urgent. The application stands anyway. And the freeze preserves no neutral status quo. It preserves the challenged termination, the invalidation of work authorizations, and the removal proceedings that remain active because the protection a district judge found unlawfully stripped has not been restored.

That is how the shadow docket works. Stephen Vladeck’s [analysis](https://www.bloomsbury.com/us/shadow-docket-9781666928067/) defines the emergency docket as a route to consequential legal outcomes without the ordinary merits process, meaning without oral argument, without full merits briefing, and often without a signed opinion. His conclusion is blunt: “the more you look at the body of work, the more it looks like the best explanation for when the court is intervening and when it’s not, is partisan politics and not neutral substantive legal principles.” The [Brennan Center’s emergency-docket tabulation](https://www.brennancenter.org/our-work/supreme-court-emergency-docket) gives the scale. Through the end of 2025, the second Trump administration filed 34 applications and prevailed in 80 percent. The first filed 41 and prevailed in 28 percent. The Biden administration filed 19. The Bush and Obama administrations together filed eight over sixteen years. Immediate legal consequences accumulate while the ordinary appellate process remains unfinished.

The Supreme Court’s own dockets confirm that it has already used this machinery in the Venezuelan matter. The Court granted DHS’s application in [*DHS v. Centro de Trabajadores Unidos*, No. 25A117](https://www.supremecourt.gov/search.aspx?filename=/docket/docketfiles/html/public/25a117.htm), allowing the termination to take effect, and entered another temporary order in the companion application, [No. 25A120](https://www.supremecourt.gov/search.aspx?filename=/docket/docketfiles/html/public/25a120.htm). After the Haitian ruling, it vacated and remanded the Ninth Circuit order. It also granted a temporary stay in the Haitian litigation, [*Noem v. Haitian Refugee Center*, No. 25A103](https://www.supremecourt.gov/search.aspx?filename=/docket/docketfiles/html/public/25a103.htm). None of those orders adjudicated the Venezuelan termination on the full merits.

The Haitian decision requires the same precision. [*Noem v. Haitian Refugee Center*, No. 25-10365](https://www.supremecourt.gov/search.aspx?filename=/docket/docketfiles/html/public/25-10365.htm), slip op. at 8–13 (U.S. July 1, 2025), did not uphold the end of Haitian TPS on the merits. It held that TPS-termination challenges are reviewable under the Administrative Procedure Act in district court. The APA is the statute requiring agencies to give reasoned explanations and permitting courts to set aside arbitrary or capricious agency action. The Court vacated the appellate court’s jurisdictional ruling and remanded. It did not decide whether DHS’s Haiti determination was substantively lawful. That decision supplied the framework for review. It supplied no answer on Haiti’s merits and no answer on Venezuela’s.

The country-conditions steel man still has force. TPS designations turn on country conditions, and DHS says Venezuela no longer meets the statutory requirements for renewal. Other countries can retain designations without proving that Venezuela’s conditions have changed. The Ninth Circuit need not treat all TPS holders alike. It need only preserve the operative posture until DHS receives appellate review. That is the strongest version of the government’s case, and it does not carry the government’s requested outcome.

The governing APA question remains. In *Department of Homeland Security v. Regents of the University of California*, 591 U.S. 1 (2020), the Supreme Court held that DHS’s rescission of Deferred Action for Childhood Arrivals was arbitrary and capricious because DHS gave no adequate reason for rescission and failed to consider the reliance interests of people who had acted on the government’s promise. *Regents* does not decide the TPS claim. It identifies what the emergency litigation has not yet determined: whether DHS supplied a rational, record-based explanation for terminating Venezuela’s protection.

The reported country-by-country map demands that answer. DHS has terminated TPS for more than one million people from thirteen countries, including Venezuelans, Haitians, Hondurans, Nicaraguans, Afghans and Syrians. At the same time, the administration has kept protections for nearly 200,000 Salvadorans and more than 100,000 people from Ukraine and Sudan. Each designation may rest on its own country record. That is the steel man. But a list is not an explanation. The treatment of one country cannot answer the record concerning another. The list proves differential treatment, not motive. It also proves that categorical uniformity cannot explain the outcomes.

The Court’s emergency docket has already blessed the posture that travels with such a list. In *Noem v. Perdomo*, 606 U.S. 742 (2025), Justice Kavanaugh concurred in allowing warrantless, profile-based immigration stops without explanation, describing them as “brief encounters.” That decision does not establish that DHS’s TPS terminations are lawful. It establishes that the same emergency machinery can preserve consequential enforcement while ordinary review remains unfinished.

The administration’s public case also invokes the vocabulary of deference. Under *Kleindienst v. Mandel*, 408 U.S. 753 (1972), courts ordinarily decline to look behind an executive immigration decision that supplies a “facially legitimate and bona fide reason”—in plain terms, a recitation rather than a record. The administration’s answer is that TPS is temporary and Venezuela no longer meets the statutory requirements. A district judge tested that answer against the record once and held the termination unlawful. The emergency orders suspended the consequence of that finding while the recitation traveled upward.

The merits dispute is real. Whether the Venezuelan termination complied with the statute is genuinely contested. The district court ruled one way. The Ninth Circuit’s full ruling is pending. The Haitian decision changed the route of review, not the controlling merits answer. That dispute should be resolved through briefing, argument, and a signed opinion.

An honest disposition would require DHS to explain the Venezuela determination country by country and on the administrative record; appellate review of that explanation; and a mechanism that preserves TPS while the legality of removal is tested. The Supreme Court can deny the pending application, dispose of it with reasons, or take the case and decide the Venezuelan question on the merits. What the record cannot defend is a protection class left suspended by temporary orders that decide nothing finally while detention and removal proceed.

The process is not the obstacle.

The process has been displaced.

The Roberts Court’s emergency docket and the Ninth Circuit’s freeze have formed one machine: preserve deportation first, test legality later. Eighteen hundred people sit in detention while the freeze holds, and Justice Kavanaugh’s word for the other stops—“brief encounters”—does not describe this. There is nothing brief about any of it.
