The Trump administration is giving oil companies license to waste gas and foul America’s air.

At my one-man shop in Friendship, I know the difference between a forecast and money in the account. The EPA’s $45 billion savings estimate does not contain a lower bill from the Adams-Columbia Electric Cooperative, which is headquartered here, or proof that a Wisconsin family will see one. The agency has issued an aggregate forecast. A forecast is not a receipt.

Adams County’s Land and Water Conservation Department has spent years building a public well-testing record because groundwater contamination becomes a county problem only after somebody measures where it is moving. Methane is not nitrate. The pollutants differ. The enforcement sequence does not. A local office can document a plume after it arrives. It cannot reach into a remote oil well and order the owner to repair a leaking valve. Wisconsin’s Department of Natural Resources can act on pollution that reaches Wisconsin’s air; it cannot replace federal upstream enforcement. That is why the EPA’s methane rules matter here even when the wells are elsewhere.

On Tuesday in Lubbock, Texas, Donald Trump went to the oil and gas industry’s campaign rally with a sales pitch: terminate the methane rules adopted in 2024, slash “crippling” compliance costs and unleash more American petroleum. The EPA supplied the headline numbers. The proposal is expected to save consumers and businesses $45 billion, produce 210 million additional barrels of oil and generate 1.41 trillion cubic feet of natural gas. Trump said the barrels equal about ten days of U.S. petroleum consumption.

Another number in the report tells the real story. Since the EPA stopped enforcing oil-and-gas methane regulations in March 2025, operators have leaked, vented or flared $7 billion worth of natural gas, according to the Environmental Defense Fund. That is not a rounding error. It is the product of the enforcement retreat this administration now wants to extend and lock in.

One number is projected aggregate savings for consumers and businesses. The other is damage already done. Together they expose the bargain: privatize the gain, socialize the loss.

The 2024 rule was the first federal rule to impose nationwide methane limits on existing oil-and-gas facilities. It did not prohibit drilling. It required operators to eliminate and minimize leaking, flaring and venting of methane and volatile organic compounds harmful to human health. An Obama-era rule finalized in 2016 had covered only new and modified sources.

The Super Emitter Program was the part the industry hated most. It required companies to investigate major leaks detected by third parties using EPA-approved remote-sensing technology. Independent eyes could catch the largest leaks and force operators to confront them. Ending that program would not be housekeeping. It removes the machinery designed to catch the industry’s worst polluters in the act.

The proposal would [end the leak-identification program and expand gas flaring](/articles/2026-10-27-epa-methane-proposal-would-end-leak-identification-program-expand-gas-flaring/). It would also [terminate the Super Emitter Program](/articles/2026-10-27-trump-expected-to-announce-epa-plan-ending-the-super-emitter-program/). Earlier reporting had already described the plan as [a weakening of oil-and-gas methane rules](/articles/2026-10-27-trump-administration-reportedly-plans-to-weaken-oil-and-gas-methane-rules/). Trump calls this terminating the regulations. EPA Administrator Lee Zeldin calls it revision under the Clean Air Act. The substance is a rollback dressed in administrative language.

This retreat did not arrive in a vacuum. Fieldnotes records — calendars, presentation slides and meeting-request forms obtained through the Freedom of Information Act — show that industry groups and oil and gas companies met with the EPA at least 38 times during the administration’s first year. On a conference call with industry representatives, Independent Petroleum Association of America executive Lee Fuller described EPA staff as suddenly willing to discuss things the industry had tried to get them to address for years, things the EPA “had never even let it come on to the radar screen.” Industry called itself a “good-faith partner.” Its calendar suggests something closer to a permanent seat at the drafting table.

This was not an arms-length relationship. At a Trump rally in Corpus Christi earlier that day, the president pointed to Continental Resources founder Harold Hamm in the audience. In April, Continental Resources had written to the EPA opposing methane-rule provisions restricting routine flaring of associated gas. The companies shaping the rollback were not distant interest groups asking for a favor. They were active negotiating partners with access to the people writing the replacement rules.

Some oil and gas executives supported the 2024 rule in public. Industry opposition was substantial; it was not universal. That difference matters, but it does not erase the imbalance in access.

The administration calls the plan an energy-cost victory. Look closely at the arithmetic. Private operators gain flexibility. The public gets methane, flaring and environmental damage, while the promised savings remain an agency projection.

Gas that leaks, vents or flares is gas that cannot be sold. It is released or burned instead of delivered. A company that captures leaking methane can sell it. A company that detects a major leak can repair a failure that is burning its product. Cleaner air and commercial efficiency are not competing interests.

The EPA’s savings projection assumes compliance costs are simply burdens to be removed. In practice, the promised savings will come at least partly from declining to require operators to measure, capture and disclose the pollution they create. By dropping those duties, the proposal would produce more waste and then book the avoided cost of capturing that waste as savings. That is not efficiency. It is permission to treat the atmosphere as an unlimited waste dump.

For an ordinary household, the practical consequence is not a rebate with the EPA’s name on it. It is energy that never reaches a burner and pollution that does reach the air. The public is not being asked to trade one promise for another. It is being asked to accept fewer guarantees about energy that never reaches consumers in the first place. Industry gets permission to pollute. The public gets told it will benefit. The bill arrives in methane, health and climate.

Trump is facing criticism over high fuel prices stemming from the United States’ war in Iran, and more domestic drilling is the administration’s stage answer. Ten additional days of petroleum consumption cannot answer a war-driven energy shock. It is a prop for the stage — 210 million barrels, repeated until it sounds like abundance while the bigger lever remains untouched: stop wasting the gas America already produces.

That March 2025 suspension of enforcement came in a year when the United States emitted more methane from its oil and gas sector than any other country in the world. Methane is responsible for at least a quarter of today’s warming.

I have kept the ice-out dates on Petenwell Lake for twelve years, and the mosquitoes arrive earlier around the shop now. The Wisconsin Initiative on Climate Change’s 2021 assessment reports that Wisconsin has warmed about 3°F since 1950. Methane does not stay politely inside the Permian Basin or over the Gulf Coast. It travels on the wind, as methane does. For Adams County families, this is the air they breathe and the seasons they live through, not a remote regulatory abstraction. It is the difference between catching a leak before it becomes a shared burden and asking people to live with the burden afterward.

The administration is not running out of legal authority. Zeldin says the EPA is revising the rules under the Clean Air Act. The law gives the agency the power; the White House simply prefers not to use it. Instead of treating leaked methane as pollution to be reduced, this White House treats enforcement as an obstacle to production and leakage as an acceptable cost of “energy dominance.”

America can produce more oil and gas without pretending methane is harmless. The 2024 rule did not demand an end to drilling; it demanded that the industry stop treating leaks, venting and flaring as business as usual. This administration has reversed that choice.

The EPA has become a service desk for the companies it is supposed to regulate. The White House gets its barrel counts. Operators get weaker rules and freedom to waste energy. The public is handed an agency-wide savings projection while it pays through polluted air, wasted gas and a hotter climate.

This is not energy dominance. It is regulatory surrender sold as economic freedom. America does not become richer by burning its own gas in the sky, and consumers do not get cheaper oil because the government has agreed to stop counting the pollution. America deserves better than a future where the official measure of energy dominance is how much gas the country can afford to waste. The numbers do not quarrel. They indict. And the air is doing the accounting.
