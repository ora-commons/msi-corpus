Donald Trump and J. D. Vance weaponized Islamophobia to police political allegiance.

Abdul El-Sayed, 42, a physician who ran the Wayne County health department, was declared the winner of Michigan’s Senate race early Wednesday at the state Democrats’ headquarters in Detroit. He will become the first Muslim ever to serve in the United States Senate. Shocks in Texas, Kansas, and South Carolina had already handed his party control of the chamber; El-Sayed’s victory added to a Democratic majority the party had already secured.

But the arithmetic was the easy part. The harder question was what had happened to American political life when a Muslim candidate’s faith became evidence against his fitness to serve.

Donald Trump called El-Sayed a “jihadist,” a “communist,” and a member of the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood. Then he said El-Sayed would have to walk through a metal detector to get into the Senate. Those words did not answer El-Sayed’s policies. They made Muslim identity itself the disqualification.

Trump said the metal-detector sentence. Vance joined the campaign that made El-Sayed’s religion the issue. Rogers was a distinct actor, with a record of his own. Keep the actors straight.

El-Sayed’s opponent was Mike Rogers, the candidate Trump endorsed. Rogers had opposed the Affordable Care Act, defended the administration’s immigration enforcement, and repeated false claims about the 2020 election. Those positions deserved argument. They did not make Rogers the man who called a Muslim doctor a security threat. That was Trump’s work.

El-Sayed ran on Medicare for All and an end to American military aid for Israel. Those positions deserved argument too. He said his detractors chose divisiveness because they had no answer to the cost of gasoline and groceries.

They did have an answer to his name.

The Constitution gives us the exact standard. Article VI says that “no religious Test shall ever be required as a Qualification to any Office or public Trust under the United States.” A Senate seat is an office under the United States.

Article VI does not make every religious criticism of a candidate unlawful, and the record here establishes no court finding of a constitutional violation. The breach was in the campaign’s public premise. Trump announced that a Muslim citizen’s faith itself made him unfit for federal office. Vance joined him in making that question central to the race. That was the precise reverse of Article VI: not a proven statutory violation, but the use of religious identity as a qualification for public trust.

The harm was specific. Voters were taught to read a Muslim name as a security signal. Policy was displaced by a religious shortcut. Constitutional power began to feel like a favor granted to the proper sort of American, and the people whose worship had been made politically suspect were pushed toward the status of outsiders. That is what happens when Islamophobia is conscripted into political service.

Now the money. It did not work as one neat conspiracy. It worked in stages.

AIPAC’s affiliated super PAC, the United Democracy Project, put about $30 million into the Michigan primary trying to stop El-Sayed—a record for a single race. El-Sayed beat the establishment’s favorite, Haley Stevens, anyway, on a movement built over eighteen months in Michigan’s biggest cities and its smallest towns. By mid-October, more than $220 million in general-election advertising had been spent by the two parties. The money was already running long before either final week, as our [October reporting](/articles/2026-10-22-el-sayed-and-rogers-enter-final-weeks-of-costly-michigan-senate-race/) documented. Read the [final pitches side by side](/articles/2026-11-03-abdul-el-sayed-and-mike-rogers-make-final-pitches-for-the-michigan-senate-seat/), and the sequence is plain.

Federal law is specific here, too. 52 U.S.C. § 30104 requires candidates’ authorized committees and political committees to report contributions and independent expenditures. Nothing in this record shows that AIPAC, the United Democracy Project, Rogers, or any other committee failed to disclose. I will not call the spending illegal on this record.

But campaign-finance law did not need to be violated for the money to matter. The United Democracy Project could spend $30 million against El-Sayed; AIPAC could spend more; both campaigns could pour more than $220 million into the general election. All of it could remain formally lawful while building the pressure chamber in which Trump’s metal-detector sentence cost him nothing to say and everything to be believed.

The religious shortcut failed because the voters would not abandon the actual argument. An August Fox News poll found that 54 percent of Michigan voters thought El-Sayed’s positions were too extreme, while 51 percent said Rogers was too close to Trump. Those two findings can sit together. One says policy disagreement was available. The other says partisan loyalty was visible. Voters were not incapable of telling the difference between criticizing a candidate’s platform and treating his religious identity as evidence of danger.

The operation had a larger name. Andrew Whitehead and Samuel Perry’s *Taking America Back for God* describes Christian nationalism as the co-option of Christian language and iconography to cloak political and social ends in moral and religious symbolism. In Michigan, religion was not one message among many. It was the gate. A candidate’s religious identity was treated as evidence against the public good before his record could be examined.

That is political idolatry.

The evidence names Trump, Vance, Rogers, AIPAC, and the United Democracy Project. It does not require me to make every church a defendant. The named actors are enough.

The history runs deeper than one campaign. Jemar Tisby’s *The Color of Compromise* and Kristin Du Mez’s *Jesus and John Wayne* show how the fusion of white American Christianity, national power, and militarized masculinity was built over decades. Trump and Vance did not invent that machinery. They put it to work against a Muslim candidate.

I know this machinery because I was inside it — inside white evangelical institutions. I taught in their schools, sat on their committees, raised money for their causes, and read the Bible straight through the whole time, which is a more dangerous way to be in that building than most people understand.

And I will tell you the thing about that apparatus that nobody tells you. It could put a verse behind anything. Anything. If you needed a warrant for the police, or the patriarch, or the pocketbook, there was a chapter. We had Romans 13 for the state, Deuteronomy 28 for the man who wanted his money called holy, and the Sermon on the Mount for the sanctification of whatever we already wanted. We deployed them in a voting booth, and we called it conviction, and we were not being ironic.

I do not get to blame Trump for discovering a door my church had already built.

I love the church I grew up in. I am still here. And I am telling you this is wrong. The operation built to make Christian faith a national identity cannot make citizenship a reward for religious uniformity.

My standard is the Bible, not the party. If a progressive administration used the Gospel to silence Muslim, Jewish, or other dissent, I would condemn that operation too. But symmetry of judgment does not erase the difference in the record. Here, the operation was named by its carriers: Trump and Vance made a candidate’s faith a disqualifying fact.

Which brings me to the passage that has my attention, and it is not a prophet’s. It is a king’s, and it is the strangest sentence in the New Testament about citizenship.

Matthew 25:31–46. Jesus describes the last judgment as two processions, sheep on the right and goats on the left. The King says to the sheep: “I was hungry and you gave me something to eat… I was a stranger and you invited me in… I was sick and you looked after me… I was in prison and you came to visit me.” Then comes the line: “Truly I tell you, whatever you did for one of the least of these, you did for me” (Matthew 25:35, 40).

Let me show you what that says in plain English, because it is not subtle. The whole question of eternity is settled by what you did to the people who had nothing. And one of the people on that list is called, in the plainest word in the passage, a stranger.

The passage is a machine for destroying the sorting of human beings into those we may love and those we cannot. You do not get to know which person is standing in front of you. The only question the King asks is what you did.

Now the legalism. “The least of these” has been narrowed for a long time—narrowed to the church’s own, to the brother, to the person whose theology matches ours. The legalist reading narrows the circle before love begins: the certified believer, the safe citizen, the politically familiar, the person whose beliefs and loyalty can be approved in advance.

I taught that reading. I taught the old reading for years: the least of these means the people on our list, the ones we can name, and the naming was the entire point of the lesson, because you cannot love a category. You love the woman in the Wednesday-night class.

And I want to say something about that narrowing that I learned late and wish somebody had handed me sooner. The textual and translation traditions are not uniform. Some preserve “my brethren” or “my brothers and sisters,” while others leave the kinship language out. But the variation is not a permission slip. The point is not which English form wins the argument. The point is that kinship cannot become a wall. The stranger does not arrive carrying your membership card. The text says the least of these. It does not say the least of these who agree with us.

Here is the chasm, and it is not a small one. The captured reading asks, “Who is one of us?” Jesus answers, in effect, “The one before you, especially the one you cannot safely classify.”

The operation that ran against El-Sayed never asked whether he was a neighbor. It assigned him a category and treated the category as a security question. The metal detector was not a gag; it was the premise. If a man’s religion is evidence that he is a threat, then some people are full Americans and some people are, by definition, the ones you screen at the door. The criterion is what you did, not what you concluded about who was in front of you.

And here is the argument the slur displaced. El-Sayed’s victory speech said he wanted taxes spent “for schools here, and healthcare here, and infrastructure here,” not “sent to do apartheid, or genocide, or war.” Read that against Micah 6:8—to act justly, to love mercy, and to walk humbly with your God—and Isaiah 58:6–7, where the fast God chooses is loosening the chains of injustice, untying the cords of the yoke, sharing food with the hungry, and sheltering the poor wanderer.

Amos 5:21–24 says God rejects religious performance when justice is withheld: “Let justice roll on like a river, righteousness like a never-failing stream.”

Those verses do not write a policy platform. Scripture does not turn Medicare for All into a sacrament or any military-aid position into a commandment. But they stop worship from becoming an alibi for cruelty. Our tradition owns those verses and has a long practice of singing them and declining them. Medicare for All is where the Gospel is always being asked to justify itself and never is.

El-Sayed told the room on Wednesday morning that the people who made his faith the issue wanted “an America that is too small for some of us.” And he told them, instead, that “America is big enough for all of us.” He told them, too, that Michigan had welcomed him in homes, churches, and synagogues. A man’s religion is not a banner. A victory is not a conversion. El-Sayed’s theology is not mine, and the church does not get to claim it. It certainly does not get to claim him.

What we get to do is narrower and better: go read our own texts and see whether we have been loosening any chains or tying any of them.

Vance cannot exclude El-Sayed from the Senate. By Senate custom, the Vice President, as President of the Senate, administers the oath of office to new senators. That oath, prescribed by federal law at 5 U.S.C. § 3331, requires a new senator to swear that he will “support and defend the Constitution of the United States against all enemies, foreign and domestic,” and to “bear true faith and allegiance to the same.” There is an ugly irony in that arithmetic. The vice president who campaigned on El-Sayed’s religion will administer the constitutional oath to the first Muslim senator. The oath does not ask whether El-Sayed is one of us. It asks whether he will support and defend the Constitution.

I do not know what God has done with a Senate. I know what Micah says about justice, what Isaiah says about the yoke, and what Matthew says about the stranger.

Our pews will not end up on the right side of this history by accident. They will be there by choice or by habit, and habit is how most of us got where we were. The question this Sunday is not whether you approve of a doctor from Wayne County; that is not mine to decide for you. The question is whether you still believe that you do not get to sort the stranger. The text asks that question first, and it asks it before it asks you anything about his votes.

We must take down that ceiling. It begins in the pew, not at the metal detector.
