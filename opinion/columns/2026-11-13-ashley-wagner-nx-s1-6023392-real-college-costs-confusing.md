Colleges are scamming families with sticker prices nobody pays.

A *Hechinger Report* analysis of federal data found 445 colleges and universities where not a single student pays the advertised price. At private, nonprofit four-year institutions, fewer than one family in five pays it. Nearly 80% of families shopping for a college have already crossed one off the list because of the price, and nearly 70% of students and parents say the financial-aid process is partly or completely confusing. This is not a flaw in the pricing system. This is the pricing system.

Middlebury College's fix is to announce that tuition is free for families earning up to $175,000 — more than double the national median household income. Princeton makes the same promise at $250,000, the University of Chicago at $250,000, MIT, Harvard and Swarthmore at $200,000, Davidson at $175,000. It reads like a windfall for the squeezed middle. Phillip Levine, an economist at Wellesley who studies college finance, put the mechanism on the record: at most of the institutions making these guarantees, "the college was already free for almost all those kids." Middlebury's own estimate of the students newly covered is 35 to 50 in an incoming class of about 630. The aid is real. The announcement is not the aid. The announcement is marketing — aimed at a confidence problem the reporting traces straight to enrollment risk, a problem the pricing scheme manufactured. The listed price at Middlebury is $97,386 a year, and the tuition-free promise covers none of the $24,462 a year for room, board, books and transportation.

Here is the kitchen-table version of that number. My household nets $8,800 a month. The part that "free tuition" doesn't touch comes to $2,039 a month — 23% of what we take home, before a dollar of the tuition itself. Middlebury's sticker is 92% of what we earn in twelve months. And we are the lucky configuration: two college degrees, a grandmother's estate holding up the down payment, parents who could call someone who had already filled out the paperwork. Families without that get the price with no map at all. Mark Doughty, touring Middlebury with a daughter, told NPR he had a friend in higher education who walked him through it — "if you don't have some guide through the system, then you could walk in and pay way more than you have to." That guide is the product. The confusion is the leverage.

Because the discount model exists to charge every family the maximum it will pay. The sticker price signals value, the "scholarship" is the discount, and the number that actually matters arrives only after the application, the FAFSA and the financial documents. Liz Mark, the Wisconsin mother on that same tour, compared it to medical billing: you don't learn what the procedure costs until the paperwork is in and the pre-approval comes back. Gallup and the Lumina Foundation found that more than three-quarters of adults without a college degree can't correctly estimate the price of a public four-year institution within $5,000. A price nobody can name is not transparency failing. It is pricing working — the same design as [political ad sites dressed up as local news](/articles/2026-10-30-chatgpt-cites-political-ad-sites-disguised-as-local-news/): a surface built to misrepresent what you're actually buying.

The sorting lands hardest on the families with the least decoding capacity, and the colleges know it, because their own admissions officials say the price and the aid process are hardest to decipher for low-income students and for young people whose parents didn't go to college. My parents read my financial-aid letters because they had been to college; a first-generation family reads the same letter alone, and the sorting happens on that difference. Middlebury's president, Ian Baucom, conceded the mechanism out loud — the idyllic campus outside his office window "might convey that this is not for your family."

Read as an economic text, "Fifteen" is a song about who gets believed at fifteen, and the bridge states it flat: "when you're fifteen and somebody tells you they love you, you're gonna believe them." Put the sticker price where the declaration goes. At fifteen, if a college's website says $97,386, you believe it, and you tell your parents not to waste the application fee. The sorting happens inside the kid, before any admissions office touches her file, and no one ever has to say the word class to her.

Underneath the announcements sits the generation-level ledger. Annie Lowrey writes that poverty in America is a choice, and so is the price of getting out of it. Sara Goldrick-Rab's number is the one I keep returning to: when the Pell Grant was created in 1972 it covered roughly 80% of the cost of attending a four-year public university, and today it covers 25 to 30 percent. That single line is the whole swap. My father's thirty-eight years at the postal service could put three kids through school on a wage where a federal grant and a tuition bill met in the middle — and his version of the pipeline had prices you could hold: St. Stanislaus parish school ran about $3,500 a year per child in the early 2000s, roughly two months of his supervisor's salary, a strain but a number visible from across the kitchen table. Now the grant covers a quarter, and the bill has a sticker price of $97,386 that nobody pays and everybody quotes.

Inside the income ranges they name, the guarantees work: a family under the ceiling gets a number it can trust. That is exactly why the ceiling should be the story and the word "free" should not. Prescott College, the University of Tulsa and Concordia St. Paul cut their actual prices; Penn built a calculator that prices a family before they apply — an official acknowledgment that the published sticker answers nothing. So the fix is not a clearer calculator, it is a real price: publish one net number with the discount rules attached before anyone applies, restore the Pell Grant toward the coverage it launched with, fund public four-year colleges so the sticker price is the price, and retire the fiction that an institution may advertise a number no student pays. Those are not experiments; they are the deal that already existed and was taken apart.

Anne Helen Petersen's argument in *Can't Even* is that burnout is structural, not a personal failure — and that the administrative load is part of the machinery. Here the machinery comes with a price list.

Two numbers arrive on the offer letter, and only one of them is the price. Taylor Swift titled the song right: "You're On Your Own, Kid" is what the country hands a family the moment it opens the aid letter — on your own with the calculator, on your own with the appeal, on your own with a number that isn't final until it is.

The advertised number is a decoy, and knowing which number is real is sold to whoever has the guide. A fifteen-year-old without one closed the browser tab in October of junior year and never applied.
