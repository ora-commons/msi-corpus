Water corporations and federal officials are billing families 62% more for pipes they let rot. Food & Water Watch, a Washington DC non-profit, compared 2025 billing data from the 500 largest community water systems — the ones that serve about 155 million people, 45% of the country — against its own 2015 survey, and found that household drinking-water bills climbed 62% between 2015 and 2025. Overall consumer prices rose 39% in the same window. Groceries rose 30%. Eggs rose 28%. Water bills rose 19% more than the national median household income between 2014 and 2024. The average household using 60,000 gallons paid $531 last year — $44 a month — with bills running from $133 a year, or $11 a month, at the cheapest system to $1,416, or $118 a month, at the most expensive. And $531 is drinking water only: wastewater and stormwater are billed separately, so the tap line on your bill is not the whole water line on your bill. You cannot live without water. You cannot substitute for it. There is no cheaper brand of tap.

$44 a month is not a policy graph. It is an envelope. It comes out of the same one as everything else, and everything else has moved too. [Grocery prices are up 33% since 2019](/articles/2026-07-25-grocery-prices-up-33-since-2019-as-shoppers-change-buying-habits/), and the families who changed how they shop to absorb that are still absorbing it. [The average family is now projected to spend $800 on cooling this summer](/articles/2026-06-18-average-family-to-spend-800-on-cooling-this-summer-energy-group-says/). Eggs, the cheapest protein in the house, are up 28% on the study's own count. The Census Bureau reported last month that family income rose 2.6% in 2025 and poverty fell to 10.2% — that is the good-news version, and it still does not close the gap, because water climbed 19% more than median income across the decade and water is the one line in the budget with no downgrade. You can switch stores. You can skip the restaurant, delay the car repair, run the AC one degree warmer. You cannot skip the tap. So the $44 comes out, every month, and the household does the arithmetic at the kitchen table and finds, again, that it breaks.

Now the part the rate debate keeps averaging away. Food & Water Watch found a $329-a-year gap between corporate-owned and publicly owned systems: $823 for the average household in a for-profit system, $494 in a public one. That is $27 a month — for the privilege of being served by a company whose investors expect a return on your water. Corporate utilities are 11% of the 500 systems studied. They hold 44% of the 25 most expensive systems and 70% of the top 10. Every one of the 25 least expensive systems is publicly owned. The benefit is concentrated in 55 corporate systems billing $329 a year more than their public counterparts. The cost is distributed across 155 million people and concentrated on the poorest fifth of them. The tradition I was raised in has a line for this: "To exercise pressure upon the indigent and the destitute for the sake of gain, and to gather one's profit out of the need of another, is condemned by all laws, human and divine." Leo XIII wrote that in 1891, about industrial-era poverty. He was not writing about water utilities. He did not have to.

Who is paying it? Water bills exceeded the study's affordability threshold — more than 1.5% of income for the poorest fifth of households — in 93% of systems. Only Idaho and Utah stayed below. In Louisiana, Maryland and West Virginia, water bills grew at roughly twice the rate of state median household incomes; in New Hampshire, where bills jumped 177% over the decade, nearly five times faster. In West Virginia, water bills consumed about 11% of income for low-income households. In Puerto Rico, roughly 20% — one dollar in five, for water. The [lower-income households who already carry the heaviest effective inflation](/articles/2026-06-11-lower-income-households-absorb-higher-effective-inflation-as-cpi-rises-4-2-in-ma/) are the ones paying the most for the most basic thing in the house. I write this from a position where $44 a month is absorbable: two incomes, a mortgage bought with help from two grandmothers' estates, a household that is not the median and knows it. A household at 11% or 20% of income is not absorbing anything. It is being displaced — by the bill, from the tap, from the thing every other household takes for granted.

Here is the generational arithmetic the 62% hides. The lead service lines being replaced now were laid over most of the last century. The PFAS — the forever chemicals the study lists among the contamination systems must now clean up — are in aquifers across the country because industrial systems put them there and no one was made to pay for taking them out; the study's co-author names holding polluters accountable among the solutions that keep getting deferred. The nitrate in rural wells comes from industrial agriculture, also named in the study. The ageing water lines that systems must now update and repair are ageing because maintenance was deferred, year after year, because deferral was cheaper than investment. None of the households being billed today made any of those decisions. The people who did are not the people paying. The country ran a tab for two generations and handed the invoice to the third. Swift wrote "The Manuscript" about looking back at a story and finding that the meaning had changed because you aged. This is that recognition at the level of a country: the story households were told about their own infrastructure — it is fine, it is handled, the rate will cover it — the meaning changed when the pipe failed, and the bill arrived, and the bill was addressed to people who had never been in the room where the deferral was decided.

"Corporate water abuses, federal disinvestment and climate change are supercharging water rate hikes across the country," says Mary Grant, the study's co-author and Food & Water Watch's water program director. Of the three forces she names, corporate abuse is the one that gets argued about in public. Climate is treated as an engineering cost. Federal disinvestment implicates the government instead of the company — and the cost of that disinvestment has been parked in monthly rates instead of federal budgets. The water industry's own trade association — the American Water Works Association, whose members supply roughly 80% of North America's drinking water — projects in its own numbers that if communities keep paying for renewal out of rates alone, the average household drinking-water bill goes from $429 in 2025 to $969 by 2050, in 2025 dollars. That is $36 a month becoming $81. Under that projection, 30.4 million households — 21.5% of them — would spend more than 2.5% of income on drinking water alone; 53.5 million, or 37.8%, would clear the 1.5% threshold; and the country would need $13.6 billion a year in assistance by 2050 just to keep bills under benchmarks the industry itself accepts. The trade association for private water companies concedes that "affordability is a legitimate challenge for many households," and answers with customer-assistance programs and the point that many public utilities cannot cross-subsidize customers. That is not a rebuttal to Food & Water Watch. It is an admission that the current structure pushes the cost of public decay onto private households — and the answer to that is federal money in the system before the bill triples, not a patch on a design that is failing by the industry's own projection.

Annie Lowrey writes that poverty in the United States is a choice, and 93% of systems breaching an affordability threshold is not an accident of geology. It is what happens when a country decides that water will be funded at the tap instead of in the budget. The affordable version of this story — the one nobody is selling — is federal capital back into the water system at scale, polluters held accountable for the contamination they left behind, and affordability programs funded publicly so that the poorest fifth of households stop underwriting the repair of a system they never broke. Grant puts the alternative in the plainest terms available: without real solutions, vulnerable communities face "the impossible choice: forgo an urgent water safety project or price many out of water service." That is the choice the 62% is buying down the road — unsafe water now, unaffordable water later, or money from a government that has been billing this day for decades.

Swift's title-line for what this is: you're on your own, kid. That is what American care infrastructure has been saying to households for twenty years, and the water bill is only the latest place it has been said out loud. The tap turns on. The bill comes. The tap is not the scandal. The scandal is what was allowed to rot so that this bill could be sent to you.
