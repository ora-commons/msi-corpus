Police believe one shooter turned a Florida A&M altercation into gunfire, wounding twelve people.

The shooting broke out just before 8 p.m. Saturday, shortly after Florida A&M University’s homecoming football game had ended at Bragg Stadium on Perry Street. Eleven people had gunshot wounds; a twelfth had another injury. Six were adults and six juveniles. The youngest was 11. At least three sustained serious injuries. By Sunday morning, five had been released from the hospital and one remained in critical condition.

At Sunday’s press conference, Tallahassee police chief Lawrence Revell said, “I stand before you devastated.” It was the most honest sentence spoken after a shooting that left 12 people injured near the Florida A&M campus. Revell said police believed one shooter was involved and that preliminary information indicated an altercation between two or three individuals. No arrests had been made.

One shooter and 12 injured people are not contradictions. One person can fire into a crowd. Two or three people can supply the dispute that starts it. How many participants authorities identify is a charging question. How many people a gun puts in a hospital is a public-safety question.

Revell’s description may prove accurate. “Isolated” describes the apparent perimeter of the event, not its cause. It does not tell us how the firearm reached the shooter’s hands. Police can identify the shooter. That alone cannot explain why a quarrel among two or three people became a shooting involving a dozen people, six of them juveniles.

The reporting leaves one crucial fact unknown: how the shooter obtained the firearm. It does not establish legal purchase, illegal purchase, borrowing, theft, or another route. That limits what can honestly be said about this shooting. It does not make the law irrelevant.

If the gun came through every legal channel, that does not exonerate the system. It indicts the system. If it came through an illegal channel, the system did not stop it. Either answer leaves the public rules in need of repair. Florida should not wait for the investigation to reveal the gun’s route before deciding what rules should govern acquisition and storage.

In Adams County, the same question has a smaller and harder form. Wisconsin Statute 120.13(2m) lets a school board adopt a policy allowing licensed, trained employees to carry firearms on district property. Sara works as a paraprofessional in the Adams-Friendship Area School District. That state statute does not solve what happened at Florida A&M. It supplies a standard for thinking about institutions entrusted with children: the rule governing a lethal weapon belongs in a written policy, not in chance or private improvisation.

Now put the proposed policies against my house. I keep thirty-eight firearms in a house where Mike is eight and Quinn is five. They are working tools, not a collection. The burden of keeping those tools from my children is mine. Safe storage is not an insult to ownership. It is part of ownership.

Background checks govern a transaction. Waiting periods govern a purchase. Safe storage governs the firearm already under my roof and within a child’s reach. The school statute governs whether an employee may carry a weapon on district property. None substitutes for another. A universal background check cannot secure an inherited hunting rifle sitting in a closet. Safe storage can. If a policy treats a dealer receipt as proof of responsibility, it has confused paperwork with safety.

The answer is friction, not magic: universal background checks for every sale and transfer, waiting periods that interrupt the leap from grievance to purchase, safe-storage rules for weapons already in the home, and enforcement against people legally barred from possessing firearms. These are not magic remedies. They are friction, and friction is exactly what impulsive purchases of lethal power lack. A background check can be evaded, but not without cost. A waiting period cannot make every acquisition safe, but it can interrupt the instantaneous leap from grievance to firearm. Safe-storage rules can put a weapon beyond the reach of someone whose judgment has collapsed.

Public policy must stop treating firearm access as an invisible bridge between an argument and catastrophe. Secure storage, meaningful waiting periods, and traceable purchases are not secondary considerations after the bullets have landed. They are the public choices that decide whether the next quarrel ends in a fist or a body count.

This is also the third campus-area shooting this year included in this newspaper’s coverage. In April, [a shooting near the University of Iowa wounded five people](/articles/2026-04-19-shooting-near-university-of-iowa-wounds-five-people/). In August, [five people were wounded at Virginia State University; a 19-year-old was later arrested](/articles/2026-08-16-police-arrest-19-year-old-in-virginia-state-university-shooting-that-wounded-5/). Now 12 have been wounded near Florida A&M, half of them juveniles.

These are separate incidents. They are not separate stories. College campuses and crowded student events should not be recurring settings in a ledger of wounded children, but they are. The geography does not change the mechanism: an altercation, a firearm, and a crowd.

Revell said officers ran into the scene before they knew where the shooter was or how many victims there were. They picked up wounded people and carried them in their arms when the crowd blocked transport. That was courage. But no community should be expected to solve a predictable public danger with repeated acts of heroism.

Florida A&M said the safety and security of its students, faculty, and staff remained its highest priority. Now that priority must mean more than a statement issued after violence. It must mean making it harder for someone at a party or football game to convert an argument into a shooting.

The firearm is the conversion mechanism. It converts an altercation into a shooting, one person’s act into 12 injuries, and a homecoming into a hospital corridor. Six juveniles were caught in that conversion. No child should have to learn what “isolated incident” means on the night they are supposed to be celebrating their university.

Revell said no family should have to experience this violence, fear, or heartbreak. Correct. The policy corollary is harder: no family should have to live in a society where anger can reach a loaded gun too easily. Saturday’s shooting may have been isolated. The public rules governing access to lethal force are not. The choice is made before the next quarrel: whether it ends with a fist or a body count.
