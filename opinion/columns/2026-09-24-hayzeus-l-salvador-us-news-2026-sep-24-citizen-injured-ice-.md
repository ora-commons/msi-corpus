Federal agents slammed a Black citizen to the ground in Evanston.

On September 20, around 10 a.m., a man walked toward a pharmacy to collect a prescription. He was a United States citizen. He was also the wrong man.

ICE officers were looking for someone who matched his description.

That mistake became a physical encounter. Ryan Garton, 56, told the Associated Press that two federal agents pinned the man down. One officer put a knee on his back while the man shouted, “I’m a citizen, leave me alone.” Garton saw blood-shaped handprints on the man’s shirt. The man’s upper right eye socket and temple were swollen. His face was scraped.

Michael Lipson, who owns a store across the street, told the AP that officers wrestled the man to the ground. He saw cards and papers from the man’s wallet scattered across the road. The man’s face looked “badly messed up.” Lipson placed his sweatshirt beneath him while Garton examined his injuries.

About five bystanders shouted for the officers to stop.

The Evanston Police Department recorded injuries to the man’s head, face and neck. Mayor Daniel Biss said the man was taken to St. Francis Hospital in Evanston and treated for “minor injuries.” The man’s injuries were real. “Minor” does not mean imaginary. A body does not become an administrative footnote because it leaves the hospital alive.

One officer also told Evanston police that he injured his hand during the effort to handcuff the man.

The Department of Homeland Security supplied its own vocabulary. The officers “encountered an individual who resembled the target.” They gave him “lawful commands.” He “was not cooperative and refused to identify himself.” They left after his identity was confirmed. They offered assistance. He refused it.

Those sentences establish something. They do not absolve anyone.

A man who matched a target description was stopped. Officers physically restrained him. He repeatedly said he was a citizen. Witnesses saw an officer’s knee on his back. The officers discovered that they had the wrong person and left.

That is not a routine success. It is a failure with a human body underneath it.

The agency’s language tries to make the force disappear inside procedure. “Resembled the target.” “Lawful commands.” “Not cooperative.” “Refused to identify himself.” “Without further incident.” The phrases are not false merely because they are official. They become false in effect when they conceal what the official action did to the person standing in front of the officers.

The agency says the man refused to identify himself. The witnesses say he shouted that he was a citizen. Both things can be true. The official sentence does not cancel the human one.

The question is not whether officers may investigate a person who resembles a target. The question is what happens when the state’s mistake meets a citizen’s body, and whether official language will describe the encounter honestly afterward.

The loudest political accounts have failed in the opposite direction. They have described the event as federal agents “beating and badly injuring” a citizen, leaving him “dazed and bleeding” after realizing their mistake. Those phrases outrun the record. The encounter involved physical restraint and injuries. The available record does not establish the catastrophic scene those words suggest.

But the correction cannot become another evasion. Calling the encounter a “beating” without evidence is wrong. Calling it a clean professional operation because the officers eventually confirmed the man’s citizenship is also wrong. A mistake can be lawful at its beginning and cruel in its execution. Procedure can be followed and a person can still be violated by the procedure.

[The earlier local coverage led with the injuries](/articles/2026-09-24-ice-officers-injure-u-s-citizen-mistaken-for-target-in-evanston/). [Another report described the stop as a wrongful detention before the Evanston Police Department completed its investigation](/articles/2026-09-21-federal-agents-wrongly-detain-us-citizen-in-evanston-leaving-him-injured/). That sequencing matters. A political narrative hardened before the inquiry finished. The city’s critics seized the encounter as proof that sanctuary policy produces disorder. The city’s defenders seized it as proof that ICE itself is illegitimate.

Both sides found a use for the man before the man had finished receiving medical care.

Mayor Biss called for abolishing ICE, demanded that the Trump administration be held accountable, denounced “brutal invasions into our cities,” and said, “this violence must end.” Evanston is a sanctuary jurisdiction. Its policy creates friction with federal interior enforcement. That friction is real. It does not turn a wounded citizen into a prop for abolition, and it does not turn federal authority into a license for force without accountability.

The same standard has to govern every badge and every office.

Sunday brought two other immigration-enforcement encounters. In Austin, Texas, an ICE agent shot and seriously wounded a 28-year-old Venezuelan man during a traffic stop. He was working as a DoorDash delivery driver. In Michigan, authorities investigated the death of a 36-year-old Guatemalan national whose car struck a tree at high speed while he was reportedly trying to flee officers attempting to detain him.

These are separate events. Their facts must remain separate. The Evanston encounter did not become more violent because Austin was more violent, and Austin cannot be explained away by what happened in Evanston.

What links them is the country’s chosen machinery: a federal enforcement surge, local resistance, frightened people, officers operating under pressure, witnesses recording, and politicians ready to turn each encounter into a verdict about the whole nation.

President Trump’s second-term enforcement posture produced a record 50,000 arrests in July alone. Federal officers are operating in Democratic-run cities and states that do not want them there. Sanctuary officials are right to demand protection for residents. Federal officers are right to investigate people they have lawful grounds to investigate. Neither principle permits the state to forget that the person under its knee is a person.

The Torah says, “You shall not wrong or oppress a resident alien.” Jesus says, “I was a stranger and you welcomed me.” Those words do not erase citizenship, warrants, borders, or public order. They establish the moral test for how power treats the person it has stopped.

And the citizen mistaken for a stranger belongs inside that test too.

We who have accepted immigration enforcement as an ordinary instrument of government have helped build a system in which a person can be reduced to a description, a card, a database entry, a removable category. We have accepted the language of “target” until the target acquires a face, a prescription, a swollen eye and a voice saying, “I’m a citizen.” We have asked officers to carry out a machinery of suspicion and then acted surprised when suspicion reaches the wrong body.

Óscar Romero did not ask power to speak more gently while people were being harmed. “In the name of God,” he said, “I beg you, I beseech you, I order you: Stop the repression.” His command to stop was not contempt for the soldier. It was love refusing to flatter the institution that had placed the soldier in the wrong.

Mr. Mayor, your anger is warranted when federal power injures a citizen. Your language must still remain faithful to the record. Federal officers, your authority does not make the man beneath your knee less human. The command may be lawful. The force may still require accounting. The citizen may resist identification. You may still be wrong.

The next encounter is coming. The question is whether Evanston and Washington will prepare for it with coordination, accurate target information, clear handoffs, independent review and de-escalation, or whether each side will keep manufacturing a political sermon from the injured body of the person neither side protected.

Matthew 25 leaves no escape in the grammar: “As you did it to one of the least of these,” Jesus says, “you did it to me.”

The country told two stories about Evanston. One made the officers villains. The other made them professionals who did their job and walked away. The truth is harder. They found the wrong man, put him on the ground, injured him, confirmed their mistake and left.

That is the encounter.

Now account for it.
