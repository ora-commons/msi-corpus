Trump is buying votes at the pump with public money.

He did it Thursday night, Oct. 22, at a rally in Sioux City, stumping for Iowa Republicans with the midterms less than two weeks out. The Defense Production Act, a 1950 Cold War law written to make American industry produce what the country needs in a war, would release $150 million to expand gasoline and diesel production. The money, the Energy Department said, comes out of the $1 billion Congress had already appropriated for critical national security energy projects. The announcement did not say who would get it. No refineries named, no states, no projects, no dates.

What the department promises in exchange: more production, storage and logistics at refineries across the country, and refining output up "by millions of gallons per day." Energy Secretary Chris Wright called it "energy addition" and said it would "unleash American energy dominance." United Press put the authority at up to $150 million in gasoline and diesel production. The announcement released the entire $150 million the law allows.

Now, I'm just a simple man with a shop that buys diesel by the drum, but $150 million spread across the refineries of this country is not a refinery. A refinery is a billion-dollar machine built over years, and this is a hundred-fifty million dollars announced in one breath at a campaign rally, with no recipients named and no date. A promise of millions of gallons a day with no allocation stated is a number picked for the podium, not for the work.

At my counter we don't hand a customer an estimate that won't say where the money goes, and we certainly don't hand it to him wrapped in the flag. It's like me taking the money I keep in the drawer to cover the shop's security system, and spending it to patch a customer's broken part the week before a big sale. The customer might be glad for the cheap part. What he doesn't know is that next month, when somebody actually breaks in, the drawer is empty. Ask for the receipt. There isn't one.

That gap sits on top of a month of levers. The administration has been [pulling fuel-price levers as the election approaches](/articles/2026-10-09-trump-pulls-fuel-price-levers-as-midterm-elections-approach/) since the beginning of October: [dyed diesel opened to highway use with the federal excise tax deferred](/articles/2026-10-05-trump-orders-dyed-diesel-opened-to-highway-use-defers-excise-tax/), which means the untaxed off-road fuel farms run can go on the road with its road tax postponed; [sanctions on Russia waived so Kremlin diesel could move](/articles/2026-10-09-trump-says-us-reached-deal-with-putin-for-russian-diesel/), with Moscow free to sell on the global market until April 7; and $8 billion for farmers on Monday to offset diesel at harvest. Four valves turned in three weeks. Not one of them is a refinery. Together they are a government working the price of a gallon down by administrative hand in the six weeks before a vote.

I'll say it plainly, because the record is plain: this is public money spent for an electoral purpose, announced from a campaign stage. The Defense Production Act exists to mobilize production the nation needs for its defense. It is being run as an instrument of the campaign, and the political object is the election. A statute written so a republic under threat could turn its own industry toward what it would need was pointed at a campaign calendar. The questions the statute exists to ask — does the country need this, why this much, for what — got answered before the memorandum was signed. A genuine supply emergency would come with names and delivery dates; this one came with a podium and a countdown. And Eisenhower warned once, sixty-five years ago, about exactly this kind of influence. We're watching it get bought and sold in plain sight.

Meanwhile the country's diesel bill keeps its own record. The Energy Information Administration, the Energy Department's own statistics office, put Monday's average on-highway diesel at $6.28 a gallon, up nearly ten cents on the week and $2.66 on the year. A year ago that same gallon cost $3.62. The man running a combine through harvest does not need the department's projection about millions of gallons a day. He has the invoice. A year of climbing is not a shortage that a memorandum fixes, and the man filling his tank on a Tuesday in Iowa is paying for the year, not for the rally.

The test I would apply to either party is the same one, and it does not depend on who is in office: if the government is going to move the price of fuel before an election, the country is owed the names of who gets the money, and Congress is owed the vote on it. Cheaper diesel at harvest is a legitimate public aim. A billion dollars for national security is a legitimate appropriation. Neither one becomes legitimate by being read as breaking news at a rally. If fuel relief is the policy, then Congress should write it itself, on the record, in plain sight, and put the recipients in the bill where the Constitution says public money belongs. That is not the slow way. It is the only way that leaves the national security power unused, for a president who is not three weeks from an election. I would write the same column about any president who reached for a defense production statute to hold a price down before a vote.

Cheap fuel bought out of a national security appropriation is not cheap. The country pays for it in the authority it just handed over. Trump spent that authority Thursday night in Sioux City, at full value: the entire $150 million the law allows, announced to a crowd of voters with the votes two weeks away. What the country gets is the fuel. What it inherits is the habit: a Cold War production statute used as a price lever, announced from a rally podium in October. Trump is buying votes at the pump with public money, and the receipt he will not show you is the allocation.
