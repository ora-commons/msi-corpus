Donald Trump is taking food and cancer care from Afghan allies who served America's war.

The record calls him Amir, a pseudonym, because his family in Kabul could pay for his name. He is not a symbol; he is a lawyer. From 2009 to 2011, he processed supply procurement for the United States military at Bagram Air Force Base. For nearly a decade after that, he served as a prosecutor in the Afghan attorney general’s Department for the Elimination of Violence Against Women. He prosecuted government officials, religious figures, and Taliban members accused of raping girls and young women. He won. The perpetrators went to prison for years.

Masked men answered his verdicts by shooting his house full of bullet holes. Amir keeps the photographs on his phone beside pictures of himself with Afghan leaders, presenting at conferences, standing in the country where his work once carried weight.

When the Taliban took Kabul in August 2021, foreign friends told him to wait at home. They promised to help him leave. The help never came. In January 2022, his father, a man in his eighties, gave him the only advice left: tell no one and just leave.

The van moved through the darkness toward Pakistan. Money changed hands at Taliban checkpoints. At the border, a guard argued with Amir for forty-five minutes, insisting that he was not a taxi driver but worked for the government. Then Amir’s daughter began to cry.

“Leave him,” the guard said. “His daughter is crying. Leave him.”

Another bribe. The van crossed into Pakistan.

Three years of applications to Germany, Canada, and Australia went nowhere. In January 2025, the United States granted Amir and his family entry on a Special Immigrant Visa, the document this country issues to some people whose service to the American war effort has placed a target on their lives. They landed on January 2 and submitted their green-card paperwork while still in Pakistan.

On January 20, Donald Trump took the oath of office and signed an executive order essentially shutting refugee entries to the United States. A flight delayed by a few weeks would have meant refusal. Amir had entered lawfully. Then his family waited while the process for permanent status stalled.

He drove a Lyft around Raleigh and supported his wife and children. In early 2026, pain in his lower back worsened. What had been treated as kidney stones turned out to be a mass. In July, surgeons at WakeMed removed his cancerous left kidney. His prognosis is good, but the doctors must keep watching. He also lives with post-traumatic stress and other chronic conditions.

North Carolina Medicaid covered his care. SNAP had already ended in February. Then, on October 1, his medical support ended.

A new federal rule created by the 2025 One Big Beautiful Bill Act stripped medical assistance from Amir, his family, and another 10,500 refugees and other people in North Carolina admitted on humanitarian grounds. According to the state Department of Health and Human Services, 12,500 people had lost food aid earlier in the year.

The cancer did not vanish when his coverage did.

I do not call this misfortune. I call it cruelty written into federal policy.

Before this administration, refugees were connected with local resettlement agencies. Families like Amir’s could expect green cards within six months to a year. Congress’s 1996 public-charge rules made most new arrivals wait five years for Medicaid and other benefits, but Congress created exceptions for refugees, asylum seekers, survivors of human trafficking and domestic violence, and others admitted on humanitarian grounds. Special Immigrant Visa families were exempt, and they could receive Affordable Care Act subsidies.

The new rule erased that central exception. With very limited exceptions, these families lost access to assistance, including income-based ACA subsidies, unless they received a green card. Since about mid-2025, the administration has paused many green-card applications. Jennifer Taylor of Refugee Hope Partners reports that almost no refugees have been admitted except white Afrikaners from South Africa.

That is selective admission. It is not a neutral rule of protection.

Read that arithmetic slowly. His care was conditioned on a document, and many applications for that document have been paused. That is not a gap in the system. It is the system working as built. A foreseeable consequence is not an externality. It is a choice, and the people who made it have names.

Administrators, you cut off their food and medicine while their green-card processing was paused. You made survival wait on a card you have not processed. This is not border enforcement. It is abandonment enforced as law.

Amir’s phone showed $475 in his bank account. His rent was due in less than a week. He had an unpaid credit card balance. Before his surgery, he could work twelve hours a day. Now the pain stops him after eight or nine hours. He might make $150 on a good day.

At NeighborHealth, the community clinic where his cancer was diagnosed, about 500 patients—five percent of the clientele—lost Medicaid coverage in October. Forty percent of the clinic’s patients speak languages other than English, including Dari and Pashto. Federal rules prohibit the clinic from providing free care, though it offers care on a sliding scale. Amir started a GoFundMe campaign because he wants to pay his share.

A government that makes the ally it rescued ask strangers to fund his treatment has abandoned the debt it owes him.

This is what the rule leaves: a cancer patient raising money for care his government took away. Amir’s case is not an exception.

His fear of ICE is not abstract. He worries about arrest, detention, and removal. He fears a flight to Louisiana, where the United States is holding detainees before deportation. In detention, [Afghans are losing departure dates as removal flights stall](/articles/2026-11-14-detained-afghans-lose-departure-dates-as-removal-flights-stall/). Then removal to Pakistan, which has forced more than 2.7 million Afghan citizens back into Afghanistan since 2023, and from there to Kabul.

Amir cannot return. The Taliban has released most of the men he helped put in prison. Return would put him in mortal danger.

This summer, [a Kabul evacuee in Houston described a fading dream of medicine](/articles/2026-09-06-kabul-evacuee-in-houston-describes-fading-dream-of-medicine/). Amir says he still loves this country and would like to serve it. But his lawful presence has not quieted his fear. Taylor said many Afghan refugees share that fear and that legal status did not guarantee they would not be picked up.

I know this fear. In El Paso, classmates disappeared after pre-dawn ICE raids, and the school day continued as if the empty desks were ordinary. This is what it means to live beneath a state that can take a man’s livelihood and health while his family waits for a green card.

Now hear the texts.

The Torah commands the welcome of the stranger more often than nearly anything else: “You shall not wrong or oppress a resident alien, for you were aliens in the land of Egypt.” And again: “The alien who resides with you shall be to you as the citizen among you; you shall love the alien as yourself.”

Jesus says: “I was a stranger and you welcomed me.” Then: “I was sick and you visited me.” And he makes the identification unavoidable: “Truly I tell you, just as you did it to one of the least of these who are members of my family, you did it to me.”

To the officials who wrote this rule, that sentence is not a lesson about the ancient world. It is an accusation against you: I was a stranger and you did not welcome me. Amir is the stranger and the sick man, and the policy of the United States answers both with the same pen.

This administration wraps itself in the sanctity of life. Its claim must stand under the test of the texts it invokes. This is what the Pharisees received from Jesus: the texts and the conduct set in one room. Taking follow-up care from a cancer patient because of paperwork his own rescuer has frozen is cruel. Stripping medicine from the ally who brought Taliban members accused of raping girls and young women before Afghan courts is betrayal.

Calling that pro-life is calling evil good. Isaiah’s woe falls on the trade exactly: “Woe to those who call evil good and good evil, who put darkness for light and light for darkness.”

The abandonment of Amir is not one man’s signature. The machine is older than any administration. Adam Goodman has documented a deportation machine whose roots reach into the nineteenth century and whose coercive mechanisms have operated under governments of both parties. Congress built the five-year public-charge bar in 1996. Earlier administrations separated families and detained migrants. Some Catholic politicians invoked Romans 13 to bless enforcement.

I wore the uniform in the years this country was recruiting interpreters and prosecutors across two wars and promising them a door—a medic’s cross instead of a rifle. I am a taxpayer, a voter, and a veteran inside the habit that now abandons them. We helped normalize a system that now frightens a cancer patient living lawfully in this country.

But symmetry does not excuse this record. This administration shut refugee resettlement almost completely, paused many green-card applications, and cut off care. The moral standard does not change with party.

The unmaking of the humanitarian exception was not drift. It was a specific act with named authors. Congress wrote the removal into the One Big Beautiful Bill Act. Trump signed the bill into law. His administration froze the green-card queue that would have restored what the bill took away.

What a humane policy requires is not mysterious. The 2003 pastoral letter *Strangers No Longer*, issued jointly by the United States and Mexican bishops, says people have the right to find opportunities in their homeland and the right to migrate when they cannot support their families, while affirming nations’ right to control their borders. It calls for protection of refugees and asylum seekers and respect for the human rights and dignity of undocumented migrants.

For Amir, that discipline becomes plain duty: process Special Immigrant Visa green cards on enforceable deadlines, restore Medicaid and ACA help while cases are pending, resume refugee resettlement, and provide notice, hearings, and individualized review before removal. Protect family unity. Never send a person back to face persecution. The common good includes the people already here.

On March 23, 1980, Óscar Romero stood in San Salvador’s cathedral and told the soldiers that “Ningún soldado está obligado a obedecer una orden contra la ley de Dios. Una ley inmoral, nadie tiene que cumplirla.” No soldier is bound to obey an order against the law of God; an immoral law, no one has to carry out. Then Romero said: “En nombre de Dios, pues, y en nombre de este sufrido pueblo, cuyos lamentos suben hasta el cielo cada día más tumultuosos, les suplico, les ruego, les ordeno: ¡cese la represión!”

*Stop the repression.*

Congress can write a rule that strips cancer care from refugees. It cannot make cruelty into justice. The rule is *pecado social*: social sin. Romero’s command demands a policy, not only a cry. Stop the rule that makes a man’s cancer treatment wait for a green card no one is processing. Stop treating a man who stood with this country as a liability to be returned to the men he helped imprison.

The door is open. No one is beyond return—but the return runs through the sick man’s medicine, not around it.

A man with a rifle across his knees and a bribe in his pocket looked into a van at a border in the dark, saw a child crying, and let the family go. My country has the paperwork, the courts, and the gospel, and it has chosen the queue that does not move. Amir’s phone shows $475. His rent is due. His follow-up care waits on a document his own rescuer froze. I set the picture down where Romero set his homily: with the people who still have a choice. Give him a green card and restore his care. Let a man who defended women in Afghanistan keep serving the country he loves.
