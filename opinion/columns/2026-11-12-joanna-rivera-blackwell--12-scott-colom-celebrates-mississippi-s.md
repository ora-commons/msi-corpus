Mississippi's white Evangelical machine used the Bible to keep a Black senator out of Washington for 151 years. On the night the count finally gave the seat up, Scott Colom walked onto the chapel lawn at Tougaloo College behind a sign that read *Picked Another Senator*, and about a thousand Mississippians stood in a crowd that looked, for one evening, like the state itself: elders who had carried the civil rights movement on their own backs, babies on their parents' shoulders, every one of the Divine Nine in their letters. Colom is the first Black senator Mississippi has sent to Washington since 1875 — [the Reconstruction lineage our coverage traced on election night](/articles/2026-11-11-democrat-scott-colom-wins-mississippi-senate-seat-first-black-senator-since-reco/) runs through Hiram Revels and Blanche K. Bruce, born enslaved, whose term ended in 1881 — and the first Democrat the state has elected to the Senate since John C. Stennis, a signer of the 1956 Southern Manifesto, won his seventh term in 1982. Bennie Thompson, who for decades was Mississippi's lone Black and lone Democratic member of Congress, told the crowd he now has "a partner in Washington." A century and a half is a long time to keep praying for a partner.

A Tougaloo graduate named Rev. Maxine Bolden told the crowd the night "meant everything," and then said something no pulpit in this machine has earned the right to hear said near it in a century: "God came back and gave us all the marvels." That is testimony, and I honor testimony. It is also a theological claim made in public about the God of the Bible — and for five generations the machine that just lost this seat claimed to be the final authority on exactly that God. Its pulpits will not answer her tonight, so I will, from inside the tradition that raised me.

Let me be plain about where I stand. I did not stand on that lawn. I was raised one state over, in the same convention, on the same hymnals, under the same interpretive machinery. I taught the verses the other way for thirty years. I tithed to it. I was handed a version of this history with no Black senator in it, because the history I was handed had no Black citizens in it either — not as citizens, not as people the story was about. The Civil War was about states' rights. Reconstruction was the tragedy. The old order was gone, and nobody in my class was asked what it had cost the people it kept out. The women in my Wednesday-night circle voted its ticket and told me they did it for the Lord, and I believed them, because for thirty years I said the same thing. I am not writing as a stranger settling a score from outside. I am writing as someone who sat in those pews, who finally read the Book it preached from and noticed the parts it skipped.

Now let me show you what it skipped. Amos, chapter 5. "I hate, I despise your religious festivals; your assemblies are a stench to me" (Amos 5:21). Not your idols, mind you — your *worship*. The Lord goes on to name what the singing was papering over: courts where bribes shut the poor out of justice at the gate (Amos 5:12), and Amos indicts the dishonest measure and the cheating scales himself a few chapters later (Amos 8:4–6). And then the verse every Mississippi choir has sung without once asking what it agreed to: "But let justice roll on like a river, righteousness like a never-failing stream" (Amos 5:24). Martin Luther King Jr. quoted that verse on the National Mall. In the Letter from Birmingham Jail he named the silence of the white church in exactly those terms, walking past the sanctuaries of the southern states and asking, in effect, what kind of God is worshipped in them. He had his answer. Medgar Evers was shot down in the driveway of his Jackson home in June of 1963, and Byron De La Beckwith walked free after two hung juries while the sanctuaries of that city kept their hymnals open. That same June, Fannie Lou Hamer sat in the Winona jail being beaten for the crime of a voter-registration workshop, and later stood before the Democratic convention and asked, "Is this America, the land of the free and the home of the brave?"

Let me be careful with the text, because the careful reading is the whole point. God is not complaining about the music. He is addressing people whose worship is flawless — the festivals kept, the offerings brought, the songs sung on pitch — and whose justice is missing. The assembly and the justice are named in the same breath, and the second is not a figure of speech for the first. "Let justice roll on like a river" means courts that work, honest measures, wages that are paid, and a ballot that counts. The preaching I grew up on turned Amos into a rebuke of empty ritual: God does not want your music if your heart is not right. That reading lets the congregation keep its politics and simply upgrade its sincerity — it is the legalist move, take a text that indicts what the assembly does and convert it into a text about how the assembly feels. The machine's reading of Amos was that the prophet scolds hypocrisy in general and calls for personal revival. The plain language is otherwise: God hates the worship of a people whose hands are full of blood (Isaiah 1:15), and the river of justice Amos demands runs through courthouses, county registrars, and ballot boxes. The chasm sits right there on the page: the plain reading grades your worship and your justice together; the captured reading grades your worship, demotes your justice to private kindness, and rules the arrangement you vote for out of Amos's jurisdiction. Mississippi sang verse 24 and hammered the gate of verse 12 shut for a hundred years. The text has not changed. The operation has.

The machine would like tonight to be about new politics. It is about very old theology. In 1822, Richard Furman — the Charleston pastor whose heirs founded the Southern Baptist Convention in 1845, in large part to keep slaveholders in the mission fold — wrote that slavery was "justifiable by the doctrine and example contained in Holy writ... consistent with Christian uprightness, both in sentiment and conduct." James Henley Thornwell supplied the machine's method: the church's "only argument is, Thus it is written." It was always *thus-it-is-written*; what the pulpit never permitted was the follow-up question — which writing, read how, to whose benefit. In 1875, as Bruce held the very seat Colom has just won, white Mississippi fought the campaign its preachers sanctified as Redemption, with Romans 13:1 — "let every soul be subject unto the higher powers" — standing duty over the whole arrangement, and the convention that built on Furman's foundation went on apologizing for the defense of slavery in 1995. The apology is a receipt: the machine knew. Anthea Butler's reading is the one I find hardest to escape — that race, not abortion, was the organizing grievance that built the Religious Right's machinery in the first place. And as Jemar Tisby documents across the American church's record, when the choice was racism or equality, the established practice was a complicit Christianity. Mississippi was not an exception to that record. It was the standard-bearer. Frederick Douglass named the seam before any of us: "Between the Christianity of this land, and the Christianity of Christ, I recognize the widest possible difference — so wide, that to receive the one as good, pure, and holy, is of necessity to reject the other as bad, corrupt, and wicked." He was not writing off the believers. He was naming an operation — a Christianity that keeps its festivals and lets the river run dry. Matthew 23:23 has Jesus tell the most punctilious religious men of his day that they "have neglected the more important matters of the law — justice, mercy and faithfulness" (the KJV has "judgment, mercy, and faith"). He said it to the people who tithed their spices. He would have said it to my people.

And the machine held. In 2018 the seat's holder, Cindy Hyde-Smith, was recorded joking that if a supporter invited her to a public hanging she would sit on the front row — in a state where the lynching tree is not history but geography — and the machine's electors returned her anyway, in a runoff, over Mike Espy, a Black man. Sociologists Andrew Whitehead and Samuel Perry, in *Taking America Back for God*, demonstrated that this alignment runs on ideology rather than devotion — Christian nationalism predicts political allegiance better than worship attendance does — and Mississippi's electorate was their dataset in church clothes. The seat was not merely lost last week. The machine's hold on this state was defended, verse by verse, for five generations.

Now hear the song for the night itself. Luke 1:46–55, the Magnificat — the song of a pregnant girl from occupied Galilee: "He hath put down the mighty from their seats, and exalted them of low degree" (Luke 1:52). The NIV is no gentler: "He has brought down rulers from their thrones but has lifted up the humble. He has filled the hungry with good things but has sent the rich away empty." I sat through decades of Advent pageants in which this was rendered as a lullaby about meekness, and the only dangerous thing in the room was the costume budget. The plain language announces the reversal of thrones — the hungry filled, the rich sent away empty-handed, the powerful deposed. Whether or not God "came back" for Colom's counting week, I do not know; I will not launder an election into a prophecy, because that is exactly the move the machine made with every seat it ever held. But this much is textual: the song Mississippi's white churches sang every December describes precisely what happened on that chapel lawn, and the machine never believed a word of it in 150 years. Rev. Bolden's marvels sit closer to the Magnificat than anything the state's pulpits have preached in a century.

The pledges spoken on that lawn were plainer than anything the machine ever produced from its favorite verses. Colom's own pledge — "I'm going to treat your children like I want my children to be treated" — is Matthew 7:12 with a Mississippi accent, and it sits beside two verses the machine long preferred to skip: Leviticus 19:34 requires the foreigner to be treated as your native-born and loved as yourself, and Matthew 25:31–46 sets the hungry, the stranger, and the prisoner at the bar of final judgment. The machine taught us that "the least of these my brethren" meant fellow church members. The text has no such trapdoor. It never did.

And notice how the seat finally moved. In a term when Louisiana v. Callais gutted what remained of the Voting Rights Act's protections, the outcome still turned on affidavit ballots — the paperwork mechanism that, historically, has shed exactly these voters — and Black Mississippians cured them by hand, week after week, in the campaign whose shape [Black Mississippians described for us before a single vote was counted](/articles/2026-11-07-black-mississippians-react-to-scott-colom-s-historic-u-s-senate-bid/). The heirs of the machine hollowed the statute; the voters it was written against overfilled the ballots anyway. You may call that ironic. I was raised on the prophets, and I call it Tuesday. Aznii Welchlin, president of Tougaloo's student government association, led students to march to the vote and helped cure those ballots. The instruments of disfranchisement get replaced. The permission structure does not: some people are to be governed, and some are to govern, and Scripture is asked to say amen.

Two cautions before anyone on the winning side gets comfortable. First: the Magnificat is not a party platform, and I refuse to hand it to one. Before this gets read as a verdict on one coalition, know that the shape is portable — any congregation that blesses the arrangement that feeds it will reach for the same verses, and the mainline pulpit that hands Amos to its own preferred slate performs the same move I have just named. The same plain reading that brought down Stennis's heirs will, if honored honestly, sit in judgment of every coalition that wants the Book as an endorsement — including the one now smiling. If the exile community starts quoting "He hath put down the mighty" as a victory anthem while reserving "rich" for other people's rich, the machine is rebuilding itself in our colors, and I will write the same column about it. I apply this reading to my own side or I become the thing I analyze. Second, and more gently: I have already heard some of my fellow defectors indulge in contempt for the pews that lost. I will not join it. Some of those pews are mine. Some of the people on that lawn were in those pews last year. The line between the crowd that danced and the crowd that didn't runs through families, kitchen tables, and Sunday schools — and it runs, for anyone raised where I was raised, straight through us. The machine is the target. It always was. The people in the pews are the readers the Book has been waiting for.

Sunday morning, the hymnals in Mississippi's white churches will be exactly where they have always been, and Amos 5:24 will still be printed in the passages their choirs love. The question of what kind of people worship there, and who their God is, is on the table again, and this time the state has given those churches a live way to answer it. If you were raised where I was raised, none of this is an accusation against your faith. It is an invitation to read the book you already love with the interpretation machinery taken off. And the song Tougaloo's students quoted over the loudspeakers — "It's been a long time coming, but a change is going to come" — was written by a preacher's son from Clarksdale. Sam Cooke. The machine's own tradition produced that song, and spent a hundred years not meaning it. Sunday, it can start.
