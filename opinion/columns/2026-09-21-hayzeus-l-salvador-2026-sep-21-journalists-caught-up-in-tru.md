The Trump administration is jailing journalists who document its deportation regime.

Three journalists crossed a line this year that the country has spent two centuries pretending did not exist. They tried to document what the government was doing, and the government came for them.

Independent photographer John Abernathy, Colombian reporter Estefany Rodríguez, and Emmy-winning Salvadoran journalist Mario Guevara describe the same arc: a professional act of witnessing, followed by an arrest, followed by an answer from the state that runs somewhere between indifference and contempt.

This is not a string of unfortunate encounters. It is a policy.

In 2025, the U.S. Press Freedom Tracker counted 32 arrests and 170 assaults on journalists. Most of the arrests happened at protests. That count does not include the campaigns that follow: the 100-plus days in detention, the isolation, the deportations, the confiscated phones, the warnings that reporters will be “crushed” and “disappear[ed] without a trace.”

Those are the throughlines.

Abernathy was in Minneapolis photographing protests that the federal government has itself made a frontline of its immigration crackdown. ICE agents tackled him and pepper-sprayed him. The photograph that records the moment shows spray running down his face as he lies on the pavement. He threw his camera to another photographer as agents took him down so the photographs would survive.

“It deserves to be seen, what’s going on around here,” he said.

That sentence is the case.

The state took the man. It did not take the pictures.

Rodríguez was arrested outside a Nashville courthouse in March after reporting on immigration arrests there. A day later, agents surrounded her car. The vehicle carried her outlet’s logo. They read the mark before they took her.

She spent two weeks in ICE detention in Louisiana and Alabama. A judge ordered her release. The state did not apologize for the two weeks it had already extracted from her, or for the chill left behind in every Spanish-language newsroom in Tennessee.

That chill is doing real work. It is the work of the case that just walked out of an ICE facility on bond — [Rodríguez’s own release](/articles/2026-03-20-ice-releases-tennessee-spanish-language-reporter-on-bond/), which the state treated as a concession instead of an obvious correction.

The pattern is simple. Arrest the journalist. Hold the journalist far from the courthouse. Wait for the story to lose momentum.

Guevara is the case the administration cannot stop talking about, which is itself a kind of confession. He had lived in Atlanta for nearly two decades. He ran an independent Spanish-language outlet. He built a TikTok following by documenting immigration enforcement. Agents arrested him at a “No Kings” protest in June 2025.

They asked him, repeatedly, why he insisted on documenting their activities when he was not a U.S. citizen.

He spent more than 100 days in detention, mostly in isolation. “They didn’t want me to hear the stories of other fellow prisoners and that’s why they locked me up alone,” he told Forbidden Stories. By his account, the isolation kept him from hearing what other people inside were experiencing.

His wife and children remain in the United States. He was deported to El Salvador in October. He has continued to report on U.S. immigration enforcement from a country that has its own reasons to want him quiet.

The Department of Homeland Security called his claims of targeting “completely FALSE.” It said he was arrested for obstruction. It confirmed that he had been deported. It confirmed the underlying basis for the deportation: a removal order more than a decade old. Then it issued the line the administration wants running on every screen:

“If you come to our country and break our laws, we will arrest you and you will NEVER return.”

That is not a rebuttal. It is a threat, with the rebuttal stapled to the front.

The statement does two things at once. It denies targeting Guevara while warning every other journalist covering immigration enforcement that the price of the story is the story.

The two statements are the same statement.

The Guardian’s reporting on these three journalists formed part of a broader investigation coordinated by Forbidden Stories, a press-freedom consortium involving staff from two dozen newsrooms in 16 countries. The project examined third-country deportation deals the Trump administration struck, mostly in Africa and Latin America, to offload deportees the United States could not return to their countries of origin.

The geography has expanded.

In February 2026, in Yaoundé, four journalists — including three on assignment for the Associated Press — went to a state-run compound where African migrants deported from the United States were being held. Cameroonian authorities detained them. They confiscated their phones and equipment for a week. During questioning, officials warned that the journalists would be “crushed” and “disappear[ed] without a trace,” one of the group later said.

Nalova Akua, one of the four, said the team believed it had been targeted for exposing a U.S.-Cameroonian collaboration that officials wanted kept quiet. The collaboration is operational and diplomatic. The Trump administration needs foreign partners willing to absorb people it cannot deport through ordinary channels. Those partners need silence about what happens inside their compounds.

Reporters break the silence.

The reporters get detained.

This is a moving target across the entire chain: from a Minneapolis sidewalk to a Cameroonian compound, from a Nashville courthouse parking lot to a Georgia protest, from Louisiana detention to an isolation cell whose name the public does not know.

The journalists caught in this are not collateral. They are the leading indicator.

The pattern is broader still. [Nicaraguan journalist Galeano is being held at Krome](/articles/2026-09-16-nicaraguan-journalist-galeano-held-at-krome-court-date-october-2/) with a court date in October. Three journalists were [arrested at Delaney Hall](/articles/2026-06-03-police-block-press-from-delaney-hall-protests-arrest-three-journalists/) in June while police blocked the press from protests. ICE [released a Tennessee Spanish-language reporter on bond](/articles/2026-03-20-ice-releases-tennessee-spanish-language-reporter-on-bond/) in March after holding her.

This is enforcement against journalists as a throughline, not a collection of unrelated incidents.

Francis named the moral condition behind it at Lampedusa: “the globalization of indifference.” We become used to the suffering of others. We decide it does not concern us. Then we become used to the people who record that suffering being arrested for recording it.

King wrote that “a time comes when silence is betrayal.” The press does not need another permission slip to speak. It needs protection from the government whose conduct it is trying to show us.

And we who read the press have a duty as well. The camera on the pavement is not an ornament. The confiscated phone is not a detail. The family left behind after deportation is not background. Each is a life placed under pressure so that an institution can keep its story intact.

You can stop this. Release the reporters. Return the equipment. End the detention of journalists for doing journalism. Open the compounds to independent witnesses. Let the courts examine the records instead of letting the threat do the work.

That is the door of return. It is still open.

But it will not stay open forever if the country keeps calling the locked door security.

The year in numbers is 32 arrests and 170 assaults. The year in pictures is Abernathy on the pavement. The year in audio is Guevara describing isolation. The year in metadata is Rodríguez’s outlet logo on the car door. The year in geography is four journalists in a Cameroonian compound, their phones taken, their disappearance threatened.

What do you do with that fact?

You refuse to look away.
