New York City Mayor Zohran Mamdani never misses an opportunity to fight for working people against concentrated wealth. There he was again this week, turning Nvidia’s saga into a parable about the dangerous concentration of wealth. He instead elucidated the perils of a progressive tax-and-spend order rigged for the few.

Nvidia’s paper empire shed some $1.2 trillion in market value while stocks broadly sold off after CEO Jensen Huang resigned this month. Markets rebounded after his reinstatement on Monday, though they resumed the selloff on Tuesday and Wednesday.

Enter Mr. Mamdani, who read the market gyrations correctly as proof that too much wealth and power are concentrated in the hands of too few. “I do not think that working people across the country signed up for their pensions being tied to the question of Jensen Huang’s fidelity to his marriage,” the mayor said. “And yet, for many days, that had a massive impact.”

Mr. Mamdani knows that worker pensions should serve working people, not the fortunes of Wall Street. New York City pension fund investments in the city’s rent-stabilized housing lost nearly 70% after the state tightened rent control in 2019. The speculative bet soured. That is a lesson about chasing returns. A painful price for housing security is no case against rent regulation.

In any event, recent market turbulence makes the strongest case against the Trump Administration’s move to expand 401(k) access to a broader spectrum of investments, including private equity. Such diversification could add volatility and put workers’ retirement savings under Wall Street’s thumb. As for public workers, government and union leaders decide how their pensions are invested. That’s accountable stewardship, with money and power answerable to workers rather than a few. The risky concentration of money and power in the hands of a few is Nvidia’s.

New York City pension funds have reaped enormous gains from the AI boom. But a market downturn could require New York City to pony up more taxpayer dollars for worker pensions. That would honor a promise made in exchange for decades of public service. The state and city could also face swelling budget deficits, but their progressive tax systems rightly make revenue wax and wane with the fortunes of highfliers. Revenue riding on the fortunes of the few is an argument for taxing the few, not for shielding them. Tax the boom while it pays.

“A booming Wall St. pushed personal income tax receipts up 15% last year,” city comptroller Mark Levine said recently. City income tax revenue so far this fiscal year is running 11% above last year, yet the city still faces a structural budget deficit because spending has grown faster. More revenue, not austerity. The answer is taxing high earners harder, not starving public services.

Mr. Mamdani is pushing Albany for another big tax hike on business and high earners. He wants to make the city’s finances less dependent on the fortunes of the few. After a $1.2 trillion swing on one man’s marriage, why would anyone leave fortunes like those untaxed?
