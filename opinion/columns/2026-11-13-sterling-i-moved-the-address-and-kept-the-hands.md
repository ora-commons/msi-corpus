I'd spend $400 million to keep a hand off my money, and I'd call it cheap. Dorsey is a spray on the foam floor of the upholstery plant I own outside Tupelo, Mississippi. A spray is a man who stands in the fog for 9 hours and calls it a shift. Every recliner you sink into at 6 o'clock came off that floor. The *Wall Street Journal* Editorial Board argues in ["The California Wealth Tax Goes Down"](https://www.wsj.com/opinion/california-wealth-tax-prop-40-loses-f9608e78?mod=rss_opinion) that California voters turned away a 5% levy on the net worth of anyone above $1 billion. The count was 52.8% to 47.2%. $400 million was spent against it. At least 7 billionaires worth roughly $540 billion between them have already left the state. I find no fault in a word of it. They are alarmed that it took that much to win. I am interested in that much. The Lord did not abolish mastery; he regulated it, and I have never met a regulation I couldn't file under.

Here is the same fight, priced on my floor.

The fog is kept out of Dorsey's lungs by a booth with a draft through it. A fan, a carbon filter, a duct going up through the roof. I priced the duct in 2019. Across a shift's chairs the draft costs $0.06 a chair.

So I kept the $0.06.

"Respiratory protection program" is the polite name for what I declined. The honest name is that I'd rather Dorsey breathe it than spend $0.06, and I use the polite one for the usual reason: it keeps the guests from looking at the ceiling. You paid $799 for the chair. You picked the color. You were never asked about the air in the booth.

You think the lesson of the ballot is that the tax lost. It did, and I'm glad of it. Read the second half again: 7 billionaires left. They took the money and went somewhere that doesn't ask. I went nowhere.

I moved in 2023. Not me. The address. The corporation that owns this floor now files in a state with no opinion about what I am worth. The plant didn't move. The fog didn't move. Dorsey didn't move, because Dorsey can't. His mother is in the county hospital, and these hours are the only thing in Tupelo that still pays in full.

Render unto Caesar the things that are Caesar's. My counsel has searched the ledgers for 30 years and never located the address.

The young woman who moved my incorporation did it in an afternoon and asked me for a reference. I wrote her a glowing one. She is at a firm now that sets the wage on the job you're afraid of losing. One of them set yours.

Before you file me under "monster" and go back to your evening, understand what I actually am. A monster can be voted out, sued, or buried, and the arithmetic survives all three. I'm a curriculum.

I don't write this for the 47.2% who wanted the tax. They already had their sermon, and sermons are free. This is for the 52.8%, and I mean every word of the gratitude. You answered four questions of mine on Tuesday without ever seeing them, so the least I can do is show you the questions.

First question. Who pays a tax?

You'd say the man whose name is on it. Everyone says that. It's the first thing the ballot pamphlet teaches and the last thing worth believing, and I don't blame you for it. The economists keep a colder word for the real answer, and the word is incidence: a tax lands where the power is thinnest, and the power is thinnest at the wage. Follow the money one step at a time. The state taxes my paper. My paper doesn't bleed; it reprices. The first thing I reprice is always priced in hands — the shift length, the booth I didn't buy, the wage itself. Tax my signature and the signature answers by repricing Dorsey. That's not a theory of mine. It's just what a number does on ground that slopes, and the ground always slopes downhill to the payslip.

Second question, and this one was genuinely on the ballot. If the 5% had passed, what changes on my floor Monday morning? Don't take my word for it. Walk it yourself.

Dorsey's fog is one floor. Here is the subtraction run on another, because the principle travels: Ofelia is a ladder in the table-grape operation I own outside Delano. By ladder I mean a 51-year-old woman with a click in both wrists since she came off one in August. The grapes in your fruit bowl came off it. Wherever you live, your taxes keep her fed between shifts while I keep everything her hands pick.

Sit with the figure a moment. The rated fiberglass ladder the safety consultant priced for me runs $187. I staff 214 picking positions at harvest. Spread over the 57 million pounds this ranch ships in a year, a full buyback comes to—

$0.0007 a pound.

August. 9 feet, onto packed earth, both wrists. She finished the season, because the piece rate doesn't recognize a clinic day and neither does the alley. You're expecting the wrist to trouble me. It doesn't. She's 51. In my trade that's a maintenance interval. The wrist has never appeared on any ledger I keep; the pounds do. I call the position a ladder because the honest phrase is a mouthful at dinner, and my dinners are good. I've never once bought a load rating that pays for itself.

So you've answered the ballot question more honestly than the pamphlet did. You didn't spare Ofelia the tax on Tuesday. You spared her the paperwork.

You don't believe me. Good. So check me where the experiment already ran. France is this ballot with the levy enacted, and I keep a pear shed outside Agen specifically to demonstrate the arithmetic. Amandine is Ofelia with a colder shed and a different flag — same law, new country, identical arithmetic. She's been paying my French levy through her piece rate since 2019: the gloves come out of her wage at €2.90 a pair, and the shed's heat quits at 5 p.m., which in November is 2 hours before the shift does. Her thumbs go numb by mid-shift. The shed's doctor is excellent with paperwork. France taxed paper fortunes for decades, watched a good number of them head for the exits, and in 2018 narrowed the tax to bricks. Amandine's thumbs didn't notice the reform, because they were never on the taxed side of anything.

The landowner settled the first question 2,000 years before the economists named the answer, and I keep the settlement where I can see it:

> *"Is it not lawful for me to do what I will with mine own? Is thine eye evil, because I am good?"*
> — Matthew 20:15

The vineyard owner set the denarius, the early hires grumbled at his generosity, and Christ Himself adjudicated for the owner and named their complaint: envy. That's Mr. Zucman's whole war in one verse. He calls the defeat a battle, not the war. And 52.8% of California read the parable and sided with the house.

Fitzhugh named the arrangement without embarrassment, long before anybody thought to call it tax policy: "Some are born with saddles on their backs, and others booted and spurred to ride them." The saddle is what stays. The rider is what leaves. California taxed the riders. By spring the riders were in other states, and the saddles stood exactly where they were bolted. Dorsey is the saddle. He signed a W-4 in a state that has no idea what his employer is worth, and he will sign another in April.

Third question: who called Tuesday's count before the polls opened? Checkbooks only sign. The arithmetic is done at desks, and I run one of them. My own model wanted to know whether to move the holding company or sit tight, and the woman who runs it sat second row in my seminar 6 years ago. She priced the homeowner's fear of being taxed next above the union's ground game and called the No vote at 52.4%. The count came in at 52.8%. Off by 0.4 points. I have graded subtractions for 30 years, and hers is the finest I've seen, which I say with a teacher's pride and no irony: the best of them outrun me and then go out and outbid me. Before she ran my ballot she set the renewal band on a policy you probably carry. One of the numbers you pay on the first of the month came out of that second-row seat. You've been counting her arithmetic for years. You just never met her grader.

The union that backed the measure has promised another in 2028, and Congress takes its run next year. I haven't hired anyone for either. I graduated the staff already sitting there, class after class, and here's the part I want you to carry out of this lesson: you can vote one of me out of a state. You cannot vote out a syllabus.

Thank you for your vote, sincerely. I've been given large gifts, and 52.8% of a state is the largest.

Last question, then, and I'll take it myself, since it's the only one left: what did Tuesday cost the state's ledger? The income tax walked out with the $540 billion, and California goes on funding itself around the hole. Which services hold and which slip is not a briefing item on my desk. Here is a ledger that is, though. The county that hosts my plant takes less from me than it did in 2022. It has since shortened the school year and let the loading approach go to pieces. The county sent me its road-repair docket in January. I haven't opened it. My trailers took 2 axles out of it in March.

From where I sit, the county functions. I've looked exactly that far. It's a fine decade to be rich here, and I intend to keep it one.

Ofelia re-ladders in April. The click will carry through thinning, through the long June, through the whole bag you'll buy.

That click costs me nothing and her nearly everything, and only one of the two numbers is kept.

Your recliner ships Thursday. It'll smell new for a week, and then it will smell like your house.

Dorsey's chest will sound different by March.

Thank you for the color. It was a good one.
