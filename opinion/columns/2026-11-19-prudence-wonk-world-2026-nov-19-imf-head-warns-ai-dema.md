Kristalina Georgieva is using AI hardware inflation to justify rate hikes during an AI boom. The IMF does not set interest rates. It supplies the diagnosis; central banks supply the rates. The question is whether the diagnosis meets the condition tighter policy is meant to address.

The documents make a timeline. On November 18, 2026, the European Central Bank’s *Financial Stability Review* said the debt AI companies were taking on to finance growth had “historic parallels” with the dot-com bubble and the subprime-mortgage bubble that ended in 2008. It warned that an AI crash “could lead to spillovers affecting euro-area financial stability.” On November 19, Anthropic debuted on the public market at $83 a share and raised the largest investment in an initial public offering. No public score accompanied the price; the buyers supplied it.

At the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation summit in Shenzhen on November 19, Georgieva supplied a different kind of price. She said demand for AI hardware and related technology was pushing consumer prices up worldwide while the rapid rollout of AI was producing stronger-than-expected growth. AI hardware and related technology products now account for more than one-tenth of world goods trade, and that share is rising. She put global inflation at 4.7 percent for this year after the Middle East energy shock and concluded: “This calls for a tightening of monetary policy in many economies.” A higher benchmark rate—the reference point for mortgages, business loans, and investment returns—would be the mechanism.

By the time she spoke, the European Central Bank had raised rates in June, the Federal Reserve in September, and the Bank of England on November 5. The Bank of England’s increase was its first in more than three years and its third major increase of 2026; financial markets expected further increases into next year. Those reported increases came after the war-driven oil shock. Georgieva was adding a second concern: additional pressure from AI demand. Oil prices had fallen back from their wartime peak, but the chronology matters. The source for one rate decision is not automatically the source for another.

Her competitiveness prescription was blunt: “To put it simply: you snooze, you lose.” Build digital infrastructure. Prepare labor markets for AI. Mitigate risk. There is nothing false in that program.

The bridge between AI hardware prices and general inflation is the place where the argument must be made. AI demand can add to inflation. A scarce input can raise costs for the firms buying it and, in time, the prices of the goods those firms make. A broad, persistent increase in prices across the economy can warrant tighter monetary policy. A rise in the price of one capital good—equipment used to make other goods—cannot establish that condition by itself.

The Federal Reserve’s revised *Statement on Longer-Run Goals and Monetary Policy Strategy*, adopted August 22, 2025, restored symmetric treatment of labor-market gaps, meaning that weakness below target and strength above it carry more nearly equal weight. Its inflation target concerns the general pace of prices, not the price of one machine. The statement does not make input scarcity and general inflation interchangeable.

If new computing capacity lowers later costs and raises output, today’s equipment price is partly the cost of future supply. The boom needs financing. Georgieva tells central banks to make financing dearer. The methodological charge is narrow: an input-price observation has been made to carry the policy weight of a broad inflation finding. That does not require a claim about motive. It requires the bridge.

In October, the IMF named the energy shock, debt, and AI boom as [global growth threats](</articles/2026-10-07-imf-chief-names-energy-shock-debt-ai-boom-as-growth-threats/>) and warned that an [AI share-price correction could threaten global financial stability](</articles/2026-10-15-imf-warns-ai-share-price-correction-could-threaten-global-financial-stability/>). Those are not incompatible instructions. They are different mandates. If broad inflation persists, tighter monetary policy can restrain demand. If AI credit losses emerge, the repair must come from capital and liquidity requirements—rules requiring banks to hold more loss-absorbing equity and cash-like assets—plus disclosure of who holds the debt and when, and resolution rules determining who absorbs losses in a failure. Financial stability is not repaired by a cheaper borrowing rate.

The higher financing cost does not first appear as an invoice from the semiconductor seller. It appears in interest charges and refinancing fees. Households with variable-rate debt face higher payments when their loans reset; businesses that must refinance face the same change; governments issuing new debt pay more for the money they borrow. A borrower cannot negotiate the benchmark rate. The borrower can accept the reset, refinance, reduce spending, or fall behind. That cost falls hardest on households with the least room to wait and the least control over the rate.

The financing choices were made in private. The cost reaches borrowers through a public price system. No document assigned the higher financing cost to any particular borrower; the assignment occurred through the rate channel. If AI supply expands as expected, the owners of the buildout retain the equity upside. Borrowers still face the resets. The rate is the mechanism. The incidence—who actually pays—is uneven.

The minutes of the Federal Open Market Committee for the June, July, September, and November 2022 meetings record four consecutive increases of three-quarters of a percentage point, the most aggressive sequence since 1981. That response came after broad post-pandemic inflation and included an explicit sacrifice of slower activity to restore price stability. The 2022 record cannot be copied by analogy. It defines the evidence 2022 supplied. A broad rise in prices across the economy and a relative-price shock in one capital input are not the same object.

The laundering method has a pedigree, though the arithmetic differs. Supply-side projections around 1981 promised that tax cuts would finance themselves. David Stockman later wrote in *The Triumph of Politics* (1986): “He out-and-out cooked the books, inventing fifteen billion dollars a year of utterly phony cuts.” That confession is not evidence that Georgieva has repeated it. It is a warning about what happens when method is fitted after the preferred policy is known.

A forecast can carry both signs; a policy recommendation must say which sign governs. Hers said: tighten. The proof still has to cross the bridge from AI hardware demand to prices across the economy.

Central banks should tighten against broad, persistent price pressure. Financial risks from the AI buildout should be handled through stronger capital, liquidity, and disclosure rules, plus resolution rules that place shareholders first, loss-absorbing bank equity second, and public backstops last. Public money entering the buildout should carry a public share of the upside, while worker preparation and digital infrastructure should be funded rather than treated as afterthoughts.

The score is the score. Here, there was none. The European Central Bank’s review documented the debt. The offering priced the risk. General tightening transmits its cost through variable-rate credit and refinancing; no statute, scorer, or assigned owner prices that cost for a particular borrower. That gap is where the accounting disappears.

At the Apec podium, Georgieva made “AI is for ever” sound like a reason to make interest rates for ever. The first phrase describes technology. The second leaves the bridge unshown. The author of the bet does not get to grade the loss.
