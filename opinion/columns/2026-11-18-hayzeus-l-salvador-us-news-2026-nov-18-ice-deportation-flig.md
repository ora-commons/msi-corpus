Donald Trump is disappearing human beings on deportation flights he has hidden from public tracking.

A family waiting for a loved one does not live inside a procurement file. I have too often let legality end the moral inquiry, asking whether a removal was legal but not who could find the removed person, whether a court order reached the aircraft, or who profited from the machinery. I owe those questions to every administration, every contractor, every Catholic agency, and every religious political project that asks to be trusted in the name of God. In September, ICE [removed detainees with final removal orders from its public online locator](/articles/2026-09-21-ice-removes-detainees-with-final-removal-orders-from-online-locator/), the system families and lawyers used to find them. Representative Rob Menendez says ICE is “making it almost impossible for families, their lawyers, and our casework teams to locate their loved ones.”

The aircraft still broadcasts its position to any receiver that cares to listen. It is the public boards that have been blinded. Since late September, charter aircraft used for ICE removals have been struck from FlightAware and FlightRadar24. By early November, most of their callsigns were blocked too, and no flight plans were posted. Human Rights First counted the callsigns missing from the two services for the vast majority of ICE flights by November 5. Other trackers built on crowdsourced ADS-B data can still follow the aircraft, but tracking more than 70 ICE flights a day has become “an incredibly laborious process.” One monitor now handles more than 2,000 flights a month across a network that already [hubs through two northeast Ohio airports](/articles/2026-10-13-two-northeast-ohio-airports-serve-as-ice-deportation-flight-hubs/). The government has not made the operation vanish. It has made ordinary people do extra work to find it.

The secrecy has a line item. In September, ICE added the FAA’s Limiting Aircraft Data Displayed program—LADD—to its contract with CSI Aviation. LADD was written so the owner of a private plane, on his own request, could keep his own noncommercial flight off public trackers that rely on federal data. The administration is using it for its deportation fleet. Forty House Democrats wrote to the FAA in May, saying reports indicated that ICE had misused LADD to shield deportation flights beyond the program’s purpose. One signatory says the agency still has not answered. LADD does not erase the aircraft’s radio signal. It changes what the public can readily see: which plane, whose flight, headed where. What has been stripped from the boards people read is the identity of the flight. What remains is a position moving across the sky.

The contracts explain the money and the labor. In July, ICE signed a noncompetitive staffing agreement with CSI worth up to $305.7 million. It can pay CSI for up to 140 dedicated flight coordinators working eighteen hours a day, seven days a week. With its options, the agreement runs through March 2029. On October 31, ICE signed CSI another order worth up to $1.28 billion to keep the charter flights flying for another year.

Under the original 2025 agreement, ICE supplied CSI with a manifest naming every passenger before departure. ICE’s own 2023 air-operations handbook assigned the preparation of those manifests to agency coordinators, “responsible for reviewing and allocating seat requests, preparing flight manifests.” The new arrangement moves that work to CSI staff. The staffing justification says the contract will “reduce administrative burdens on ICE personnel.” It calls CSI “the only source capable.” The government is paying a private company to carry the administrative weight of its own deportation program, and paying it well.

CSI now helps build the lists and verify the manifests for every mission. In a sworn declaration filed in federal court, CSI senior vice-president for government services Richard DeLucia said that 19 of the company’s more than 300 employees came from the immigration agencies themselves, mostly recent ICE and Border Patrol retirees, all with “full access to ICE’s data systems.” DeLucia said CSI took pride in “hiring the best ICE personnel after they have left active duty to continue to bring a high level of continuity to our deportation system.” The company did not merely inherit the paperwork. It hired the memory of the enforcement system and put that memory inside its own operation.

Albert Dainton, ICE’s head of contracting, signed the staffing justification twice: once as the agency’s advocate for competition and once as head of contracting. DHS Secretary Markwayne Mullin had also removed the requirement that the secretary approve contracts above $100,000. The $305.7 million agreement therefore arrived through a weakened chain of oversight. A no-bid expansion, self-approved, in the dark.

The competition never had a chance. A rival bidder fought the underlying contract through five rounds of litigation, and ICE’s own staff recommended killing the award. ICE killed it, then moved to reinstate it, with the request queued at the homeland security secretary’s desk. The challenger withdrew. The reinstatement was dated the day after the withdrawal. The machine absorbed the protest the way a river absorbs a stone.

This machine did not begin with this president. I grew up in El Paso when pre-dawn raids emptied classmates from their desks and the school day continued as if the empty desks were ordinary. We got used to it. Getting used to it was the sin. Adam Goodman traced the deportation machine to the late nineteenth century. It has carried out formal removals by the millions and pressed tens of millions more out through the door marked “voluntary.” Both parties have kept parts of it running. My own community’s preference for ease is not innocence. Few are guilty, but all are responsible.

The same moral test must reach the political use of religion, including arguments made from within the Catholic tradition I inherit. In January 2025, J.D. Vance, a Catholic political figure, made an ordering of love—family, neighbor, community, fellow citizens, and after that the rest of the world—the organizing principle of public life. Francis told the American bishops that Christian love is not “a concentric expansion of interests” but a fraternity “open to all, without exception.” Robert Prevost, now Pope Leo XIV, answered directly: “JD Vance is wrong: Jesus doesn’t ask us to rank our love for others.”

This is the Christian-right operation at its clearest. It is not support for border control. It is the use of religious identity to make exclusion appear holy. Family, faith, citizenship, and border security may inform public argument. They cannot make a human being less findable, less welcome, and less entitled to counsel. None of this disputes the legitimate power of a state to set admission rules, secure its borders, and hear asylum claims. Pope Leo himself said, “No one has said that the United States should have open borders. I think every country has a right to determine who and how and when people enter. … But we have to look for ways of treating people humanely, treating people with the dignity that they have.” The distinction is plain. Border control can be lawful. Making people disappear from public view cannot be sanctified by calling it Christian, familial, patriotic, or necessary for security.

Jesus asks a harder question: “I was a stranger and you did not welcome me.” He does not ask whether the stranger crossed a boundary lawfully. He asks what we did to the person left waiting at the other end of our decision. The Torah makes the same demand in plainer words: the stranger who resides among you “shall be to you as the citizen among you; you shall love the alien as yourself.” A citizen can be found. A citizen has a name the record must answer for. A human being cannot claim the protection of the law if the law cannot find the human being. Findability—the chance of being defended—is the condition these contracts purchase the removal of.

Ask what you call a person whom the state can move and the family cannot find. Angelina Godoy, whose Human Rights Center has documented these flights for years, says there is no legitimate reason to remove them from the trackers—“the only reason to do so is to seek further secrecy”—and calls the hidden shuttling of people across the globe “an affront to democracy and the rule of law.” She is right. She is also understating it.

Latin America has a word for people the state moves and cannot be made to account for: *los desaparecidos*, the disappeared. El Salvador taught the hemisphere the word. The churchman who would not be quiet about it preached his last homily from the cathedral pulpit in San Salvador on March 23, 1980. He told the soldiers, “les suplico, les ruego, les ordeno: ¡cese la represión!” I beseech you, I beg you, I order you: stop the repression. The next evening, as he lifted the chalice at Mass in the chapel of the hospital where he lived, a single shot killed him. The repression Romero named ran on night flights and rosters no one could audit. The secrecy was never a scandal standing politely beside the cruelty. Secrecy is the cruelty’s oxygen. So I will say the word the tradition keeps for this: what is being assembled here, contract by contract, is evil.

A humane immigration system is not a mystery. In *Strangers No Longer* (2003), the bishops of the United States and Mexico wrote the arithmetic down: people have the right to find opportunity in their own lands; the right to migrate when home cannot feed them and their families; the right of nations to govern their borders; protection for refugees and asylum seekers; and the human dignity of undocumented migrants regardless of status. Those principles are held together, not sorted into the half that flatters us. Due process must be physical or it is nothing: a judge’s order that can reach the tarmac, a manifest a court can read, public flight plans, independently verifiable passenger lists, real access for counsel and families, and contracts that are competitive, auditable, and answerable to the public. A country cannot call itself lawful while families must infer the movement of their own from radar dots and procurement language. The common good requires both secure borders and a process that treats every removed person as a neighbor rather than a line in a manifest.

The people who run this machinery are people. I will not write them as anything less. ICE, you can keep an aircraft from appearing on FlightRadar24. You cannot make a removed person less a person. CSI, your systems can hold the names and routes. They cannot hold the whole moral weight of what the government does with the people on those manifests. To the officials, contracting staff, pilots, and coordinators inside this machinery, the door remains open: disclose the flights, return manifest preparation to ICE, make the flights and manifests subject to lawful oversight, end the no-bid windfall, and keep every flight visible before it lands and answerable afterward. Every one of you can stop. A family should not have to watch a radar dot vanish over the ocean and wonder whether the plane carrying their loved one has reached a place where a court order can still be enforced.

A chartered plane lifts off in the dark. Its transponder sings its position all night to anyone with a receiver, but its tail number is gone from the boards the public reads, no flight plan is posted, and the lists of who is aboard are drawn by the people paid to draw them. Contracts expire. Secretaries change. The record outlives its keepers.

Until then, the command stands, unchanged since a pulpit in San Salvador: *cese la represión*. Stop the repression. It was an order spoken to soldiers. Tonight it is spoken to whoever holds the pen that writes the lists.
