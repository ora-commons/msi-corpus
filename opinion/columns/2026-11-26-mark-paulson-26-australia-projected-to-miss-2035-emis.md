Australia’s federal government is sacrificing its 2035 climate target to coal and gas approvals.

It is gun-deer week in Adams County, and the shop runs quiet, so I had the evening at the bench with Australia’s numbers. I had a Honda EU2200i on the bench with its carburetor off. A farm customer needed it ready to power a milking parlor because a transmission project had slipped again. The machine does not make a national promise. It either starts or it does not.

On Nov. 26, 2026, Australia’s climate department published projections showing that current policies will cut emissions 48 percent below 2005 levels by 2035. Australia’s target is a reduction of 62 to 70 percent below those levels. Even against the lower end, the gap is fourteen percentage points. Progress toward the 2035 target has not improved since the previous annual release.

That is not a narrow miss. It is the federal government publishing a timetable for failing its own target. Each new coal or gas approval loads more extraction, demand, infrastructure, and emissions into the decade the target is supposed to cover. A mine approval does not vanish because a deadline arrives, and its commitments do not end because an election changes. The department’s forecast has to carry all of them.

The 2030 arithmetic is closer. Current policies put emissions 42 percent below 2005 levels against a 43 percent target. That outcome depends on renewables supplying 82 percent of Australian electricity by 2030. If transmission delays and supply-chain bottlenecks push the renewable milestone to 2032, the department says the 2030 reduction falls to 38 percent.

The fix, insofar as Canberra imagines one, hangs on wires. The 82 percent is not a weather forecast. It is towers, wire, permits, interconnection queues, machines, and people with the authority to build the system. In Adams County, that is not an abstraction. Adams-Columbia Electric Cooperative serves this part of the county. Its member-owned line and the federal rural-electrification program built service where the market judged the last mile unprofitable. The federal program began in 1936, sending low-cost money to member-owned cooperatives instead of waiting for an investor-owned utility to run the wire. Australia’s 82 percent takes the same kind of decision: build the line, permit the wind, and change the grid. Anyone who has watched a line get strung across sand country knows the wires are the work. Money that actually arrives. A line that actually gets built.

The renewable build is real. In the year to June, climate pollution fell 1.3 percent, or 6.2 million tonnes. Electricity-sector emissions fell 3.6 percent. Gas generation fell 23.2 percent, and coal generation fell 3.6 percent. Australia’s total annual emissions were already 25.3 percent below 2005 levels. The Climate Change Authority says transport is approaching a “tipping point”; electric and plug-in hybrid vehicles accounted for 29 percent of new light-vehicle sales in 2026, up from 13 percent in 2025. The one bright number is the market moving faster than the paperwork. Real movement, but not enough to close the gap.

But the pace is worse than the destination. The Climate Change Authority says holding the 2030 and 2035 targets requires sustained annual cuts of 25 million to 30 million tonnes. It says the pace must “accelerate substantially.” The latest cut was 6.2 million. Bench math puts the national total a shade under 480 million tonnes. The delivered cut was roughly a fifth to a quarter of the required pace—four to five times short, and celebrated. Bowen called it “a reduction at one-and-a-half times the rate of the last 10 years,” and told parliament, “We are making good progress, we are facing undeniable headwinds.” The statement is honest about direction. It is not honest about the cargo if “Australian emissions” is allowed to stand in for the full carbon consequence of Australian coal and gas.

Vaclav Smil’s account in *Energy and Civilization* is hard fact here: energy transitions are slow and often additive. A clean system can be built beside an old one, but the old one does not leave because a deadline appears. A coal approval made now can load extraction equipment, power demand, and emissions into the very period Australia promised to cut.

As Bowen led the Cop31 negotiations, [scientists urged an end to coal and gas approvals](/articles/2026-11-07-scientists-urge-an-end-to-coal-and-gas-approvals-as-bowen-leads-cop31-negotiatio/). The talks had failed, and the new projections give that warning a number. Greens climate spokesperson Steph Hodgins-May said the federal government continued approving 38 new coal and gas mines while its strategy asks more sectors to electrify. More electrification on one side, 38 new fossil projects on the other: that is the contradiction inside one body of policy. The renewable goal does not cancel those approvals.

Queensland’s government has abandoned its renewable-energy targets and extended the operation of coal-fired plants. Bowen said that would “significantly impact” national emissions. “Continuing to sweat intermittent and unreliable coal just isn’t the answer to the question of our future energy security,” he said. Then came the sentence that belongs on a utility bill: “Our system needs certainty. It does not need sovereign risk.” State policy sets the national arithmetic. A federal target cannot absorb decisions that add coal years while cancelling renewable projects. The sovereign risk is not weather. It is a public promise that changes when the politics change.

Here is the cargo the official statement leaves outside the room. The Australian Conservation Foundation says emissions from burning Australian coal and gas overseas after export totaled 1.1 billion tonnes in 2025-26, and those emissions are absent from the national statement. Gavan McFadzean calls exported emissions “the biggest handbrake on Australian climate action.” The page the accounting skips is not in anybody’s county. It is over the horizon, in somebody else’s air. The counting stops at the waterline.

A national target may count territorial emissions. It cannot then be treated as a complete answer to the full carbon consequence of a country that sells the coal and gas. The contradiction has a name in *We Too*, Chapter 16: the Nationalist Shell Game. Wave the flag at home. Sell the harm abroad. Draw the ledger so the harm lands on somebody else’s page.

Here is the domestic version. The 2018 tariff war cut Chinese purchases of Wisconsin soybeans. The USDA documented the export collapse. Federal payments later helped cover damage the trade policy itself caused, and the Wisconsin Examiner and Wisconsin Public Radio documented a $12 billion bridge-payment round announced in 2025. The flag stayed on the television while somebody else paid the bill.

The same test has to apply in Washington. The United States enacted the Inflation Reduction Act in 2022, the largest climate bill in its history, and then crude production averaged a record 12.9 million barrels per day in 2023. Last year, [China’s emissions decline while U.S. greenhouse gases rose in 2025](/articles/2026-11-18-china-s-emissions-decline-while-u-s-greenhouse-gases-rose-in-2025/). Scorekeeping that matters is combustion, not podiums.

Shadow energy and emissions reduction minister Dan Tehan said national emissions had increased since the government took office in 2022. The Guardian noted that his comparison did not mention the pandemic shutdown year, when the economy and its emissions were idled together. Pick the bottom of the trough as the baseline and any honest recovery reads as failure. That is how a ledger gets drawn to flatter the man keeping it.

And a word for the towns that dig the coal, because they read statements like this one the way my dad read the notices about Sand Hill. Wendell Berry named the extractive mind in *The Unsettling of America*: it spends the town along with the seam, leaving the bill for the county, the school district, and Main Street. Berry’s test—“solving for pattern”—was not to pile one good machine on top of a bad one. Those towns deserve better than to be the pea under somebody’s shell. Bowen’s headwinds are real in Queensland too.

The Climate Change Authority also says methane demands attention now. It recommends established technologies to detect and repair leaks at mining sites and to minimize avoidable venting and flaring of fossil-fuel gas. Leak detection and repair are not distant abstractions. They are equipment, inspections, records, enforceable limits, and repair orders.

The route inside the official record is plain: build the transmission and wind capacity the projection assumes, keep the 82 percent target intact, stop approving coal and gas, and enforce methane-repair rules. Make the 29 percent new-vehicle electric-and-hybrid share a floor, then put the harder work where the authority says it belongs—in industry, resources, agriculture, and the activities that do not yield to an app or a slogan. Australia has the pieces for that pattern. It has to stop making the fossil pieces larger.

I want Mike and Quinn to inherit a grid somebody built and a ledger somebody told the truth about. On my land at the end of County Z, the power line still runs past the field because the United States decided a rural county was worth connecting. I keep the Petenwell ice-out date in my notebook, twelve winters running; the first winter I wrote it down, the ice went out eleven days later than in the years I have recorded since. Leopold put a plain definition on it in “Thinking Like a Mountain” in *A Sand County Almanac*: “Conservation is a state of harmony between men and land.” Nothing in that sentence recognizes a harbor line. The lake has never once asked which country burned the coal.

Australia can still choose its grid over coal and gas before 2030. If it waits, it will not merely miss the target. It will have buried the promise under the machinery built for the next one. A target that counts the coal it keeps and not the coal it sells is not a target. It is a receipt, and the interest comes due in weather.
