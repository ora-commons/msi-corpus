Trump is committing crews to a lethal standoff and calling it trade.

I sell transmissions for a living. A tariff lands at the port and moves downstream to whoever buys the goods. At my parts counter, it reaches the customer as another line on the repair bill. Trump’s administration put a 19 percent tariff on Thai goods. The Supreme Court overthrew it in February. By July, the administration had reached for a 12.5 percent replacement levy, citing Thailand’s failure to act against forced labor.

Forced labor is a real evil, and I am not the man to wave it off. But an evil that becomes the stated reason for a replacement tax months after the court struck down the first one reads less like a standard and more like a handle. That pencils out as leverage. It does not pencil out as trade, and it does not pencil out as defensible foreign policy either.

Last October’s framework called on Bangkok to remove tariffs on most American goods and barriers to importing American automobiles, food products, and pharmaceuticals. The same arrangement called for Thailand to spend more than $18 billion on American aircraft and buy American farm goods and liquefied natural gas. The Thai treasury signs the purchase. American aircraft sellers receive the order. The tariff importer pays first, and the cost moves downstream to the merchants, workers, and families who buy the goods.

That is coercion wearing trade paperwork. A government forced to buy American aircraft under tariff threats is not a free trading partner. It is a compelled customer. At home, a government is not only a flag on a stage. It is a payroll, a public bill, classrooms, clinics, local workers, and families trying to make Friday. Washington does not get to use those lives as the pressure gauge on an $18 billion sale. That does not pencil out as security policy. It pencils out as a sales account.

An $18 billion sale can make an impressive ledger entry, but it does not resolve the confrontations in Philippine waters.

The pageantry is first-rate. The security beneath it is sold, not promised.

Trump lands in Manila on Tuesday as the [ASEAN summit opened over the weekend](https://www.msindependent.com/articles/2026-11-15-asean-leaders-open-manila-summit-as-trump-prepares-to-join-regional-talks/). On Wednesday, President Ferdinand Marcos Jr. is scheduled to give him a red carpet, full military honors, and a state banquet at Malacanang. The two governments are expected to reaffirm their treaty alliance, which dates to 1951.

The welcome itself deserves a plain reading. The Philippine ambassador to Washington, Jose Manuel Romualdez, told the Associated Press that the president asks about Imelda Marcos in phone calls: “How’s Imelda?” There may be a brief reunion with the 97-year-old former first lady if she is well enough and receives her doctors’ approval. Her son carried a note to the White House thanking Trump for his “strong backing of the Philippines.”

I have no quarrel with courtesy between nations. The alliance has survived the fall of a strongman in the 1986 People Power uprising. It can survive another exchange of greetings. But when personal warmth does the work the treaty text used to do, somebody is holding the invoice and somebody else is holding the memories. Personal history has its place. It is not a substitute for strategy.

None of the ceremony matters if it secures the alliance. A treaty that cannot change what happens at the waterline is a press release with a seal on it. The people who live beside those waters and the local institutions charged with their safety cannot spend the paper. They need the pressure on Philippine coast guard crews and communities to meet an American answer before Washington treats the burden as Manila’s problem alone. A red carpet cannot do that work. Neither can a state banquet.

The security news is in the water. During Trump’s visit, ASEAN leaders are working with China on a non-aggression pact covering the contested waters of the South China Sea. The pact covers the same waters where Chinese and Philippine coast guard and naval forces have confronted one another in recent years. China is not entering this as a bystander. It has built the islands, kept up the pressure, and is now helping write the rules for the waters it has already changed by force.

China will help write the rules for those waters. That is not equal security. It is power politics with stationery.

The non-aggression pact does not erase the patrols and confrontations already at the waterline. For the communities and local institutions beside those waters, the danger remains while the paper is negotiated above their heads. Two capitals, two methods: tariffs and sales on one side; islands, patrols, and rules-writing on the other. One lesson remains. The fighting is being postponed, and postponed fighting gets billed to the next cohort.

Trump’s first trip abroad after the deep midterm losses is not a turn away from China. It is a route into China: Manila, the Quad, then the [Trump-Xi-Putin nuclear talks](https://www.msindependent.com/articles/2026-11-15-trump-turns-to-asia-for-xi-putin-nuclear-talks-after-midterm-losses/), followed by the Shenzhen summit. The Quad is the four-country grouping intended to counter Chinese influence in the Asia-Pacific. The next stop puts Trump at the same table with Xi Jinping and Vladimir Putin to discuss nuclear power, with a meeting with Kim Jong Un floated above the itinerary. The Philippines gets full honors. China gets the strategic center of gravity. An alliance does not become stronger when the ally becomes scenery.

That is not diplomacy with commerce attached. It is commerce transacted on top of a live standoff, and the crews are not at the table. A slate of expected deals in aviation, defense, energy, and technology waits beyond the nuclear talks. Scripture says, “By their fruits ye shall know them,” and the week’s fruits are arranged in order: carpet, contract, table. Talking is cheaper than shooting, and I am not against the table. But read the week as one document. An alliance is represented by a state banquet, Thailand is represented by an aircraft order, and nuclear strategy is represented by commercial terms. The machines are counted twice this week, once as the instruments meant to keep a war from starting and once as merchandise, and the same hand keeps both books.

Andrew Bacevich’s *Washington Rules* named the inherited American operating system: American troops abroad, leadership of alliances, and intervention when the political class declares intervention necessary. Plainly, Washington keeps the bases, keeps the promises, and sends armed force when officials call the danger sufficient. The Manila itinerary runs on that system, but it sells the alliance as a transaction. Honors for one ally, an aircraft bill for another, and China helping shape the maritime rules. Security has become a sales funnel; commerce has been draped in the flag.

Eisenhower, in his farewell address of January 17, 1961, paragraph 24, warned of “the acquisition of unwarranted influence, whether sought or unsought, by the military-industrial complex.” In plain terms, the people who build and sell the instruments of war had gained too much authority over public decisions. Here the influence need not wait in a lobby. It is written into the terms of doing business with Washington. The arms industry once had to seek its market. Now the market arrives as a condition of trade. The complex needs customers the way a standing army needs ammunition, and this week the customer list is on tour.

That influence has a receipt and a payer. The receipt is more than $18 billion in aircraft. The payer is the Thai treasury. The downstream bill belongs to the Thai importers, the people who buy their goods, the workers whose pay and prices meet those costs, and the American sellers who receive the market Washington demanded.

The machines are counted twice this week. The bill I am watching is the third one. The tariff lands at the port and moves downstream to whoever buys the goods. The aircraft are a different ledger; Thailand buys them with Thai money, and they go into the buyer’s hangars. The third bill is paid by the crews if the standoff goes hot, American and Filipino.

The machine I rode was an Abrams, paid for by the American government, and from where I sat in its gunner’s seat in Iraq in 2003 the machine was never the point. The point was the four of us inside it, the fuel and parts that kept us moving, and the families at home doing arithmetic nobody showed them. An alliance reaffirmed over a state banquet, in a region where confrontations between armed forces are climbing, is a promise that if it ever breaks open, the bill gets paid in crews. The crews who would pay it are not in the banquet hall. They are not in the trade delegation. They were not consulted, and they never are.

Under Article I, Congress holds the power over foreign commerce and raising armies; it should hold open hearings on every defense order attached to a trade framework, and tariff authority should be exercised by the branch that holds it. Under Article II, Senate advice and consent is what binds the country to a treaty, while Congress must authorize war and scrutinize any major use of armed force. Washington should defend the Philippine treaty in substance, support the Philippines against coercive pressure, and refuse to let aircraft sales substitute for an American commitment to Philippine security.

The banquet is Wednesday. The carpet is red, the welcome is genuine, and none of the ceremony needs my approval. But the week’s commitments get paid for downstream: the tariff by whoever buys the goods, the machines by the treasuries that order them, and the standoff itself, if it ever goes hot, by crews from Manila and crews from towns like mine. Trump is committing crews to a lethal standoff and calling it trade.

The crews were not in the room. They never are.
