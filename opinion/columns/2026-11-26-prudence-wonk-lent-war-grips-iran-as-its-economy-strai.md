The Trump administration is starving Iranian pensioners to pay for a blockade it calls pressure.

Pezeshkian's government is extracting food and power from ordinary Iranians.

Here are the numbers.

Benoit Faucon’s November 26 report in The Wall Street Journal puts Iran’s food and beverage price inflation above 110 percent year over year. The rial stood at 32,000 a dollar when Tehran signed the July 2015 Joint Comprehensive Plan of Action in Vienna. The agreement was the last formal instrument in this relationship to arrive with published text and a recorded vote; the United Nations Security Council endorsed it in Resolution 2231. It now takes 2.9 million rials to buy one dollar, roughly a ninetieth of the 2015 value. The average pension is worth $64 a month. This month, a Tehran man in his seventies bought sangak, a traditional Persian flatbread, for the equivalent of 35 cents—seven times the subsidized price—and said it was the first time in his life he had paid that much for a loaf. A regressive transfer, in the vocabulary of the cost tables: the squeeze takes its largest share from the people with the least to give.

The subsidy cuts are not fiscal housekeeping. They are not ordinary budget management. The blockade is a two-step machine: Washington chokes off the revenue; Tehran decides where the cut lands; the cut lands on bread and kilowatts. Washington settled on financial sanctions and a naval blockade of Iranian ports in August. [Iran’s top politicians were still publicly urging an end to the war](/articles/2026-08-22/iran-s-top-politicians-urge-end-to-war-as-economy-crumbles/) at the time. Since then the squeeze has widened from oil exports at sea to flights and overland trade. Block the oil and the treasury empties; empty the treasury and the subsidy bill comes due.

In May, [War and U.S. blockade strain Iran’s economy as inflation hits highs](/articles/2026-05-14-war-and-us-blockade-strain-iran-s-economy-as-inflation-hits-highs/) recorded households already straining under the blockade.

The summer gasoline-subsidy cut had already produced massive queues at the pump, as [Iranians rushed to buy fuel and food as Washington threatened tighter sanctions](/articles/2026-08-26/iranians-rush-to-buy-fuel-and-food-as-trump-threatens-tighter-sanctions/) reported. November is the arithmetic arriving in the bread.

President Masoud Pezeshkian told Iran’s national television on Nov. 20: “We have no money.” That first sentence is a budget confession. On Nov. 21 he told the state-run IRNA news agency that the conflict was an economic war: “We are engaged in a silent war that is far more difficult than one in which missiles are launched and destruction is caused.” The current shock is intensified by the U.S. naval blockade. Washington produced the shock. Tehran chose the subsidy cuts. The pressure from Washington is real. It does not make every subsequent domestic choice compulsory.

What got cut tells you where the emptiness was assigned. A food or electricity subsidy is the government’s payment toward a household’s bill. Cut that payment, and the household pays more. The government says it has no money. It has the power to ration. It is using that power against its own people.

Hossein Zafari, spokesman of Iran’s Flour and Bread Council, told Nour News that authorities cut allocations of cheap flour and bread in seven of the country’s 31 provinces. The government also more than doubled the price it charges consumers for industrial flour. Bakers in western Zanjan protested the shortages. In one bakery in Tehran’s affluent northern suburbs, bread grew progressively darker as it spoiled; a customer said the flour had been mixed with feed meant for animals. The state denies that this is rationing. That is rationing by quantity and rationing by price. The missing word changes nothing.

Tavanir, Iran’s state electricity company, has begun rolling blackouts lasting up to two hours a day in some locations. Reza Sepahvand, a spokesman for parliament’s energy commission, says Iranians should brace for worsening winter outages. Tavanir attributed the shortage partly to war damage at natural-gas fields. Power stations around Tehran burn mazut, a heavy, low-grade residual fuel oil, and authorities have used methanol to stretch gasoline supplies. The state calls it fuel scarcity. The household receives a rationed grid. An oil state is rationing light. In an oil-producing country where many households treated cheap energy and food staples as birthrights, Tehran is now rationing both.

Two vocabularies now describe one transfer. Washington calls the instrument pressure; Tehran calls the cuts management. Two words for one transfer; each government picks the word that keeps the bill off its own ledger. The laundering runs from Washington to Tehran to the hardliners. Washington calls the blockade a strategy. Tehran calls the cuts subsidy reform while denying they constitute rationing. London-based Bourse & Bazaar Foundation founder Esfandyar Batmanghelidj says the government lacks the political mandate for broad subsidy reform and calls the response half-measures that fail to address the root causes of “economic mismanagement.” That diagnosis is made to do useful work. Constraint explains the choice. It does not launder the incidence. The state cuts what the weakest can absorb—the loaf, the kilowatt, the pension’s purchasing power—and calls the remainder management. Structural problems may be real, but they do not explain why the pressure arrived when it did or why bread and electricity were selected. Each phrase moves responsibility one step away from the household paying it. The vocabulary was chosen after the consequence became visible.

In the institution where I spent a career, a measure with a fiscal cost came with a score—the table stating who pays, how much, over what window, before anyone votes. The blockade has no score in the budget sense. It was settled by executive decision in August, not enacted as legislation; sanctions of this kind are administered by the Treasury Department’s Office of Foreign Assets Control, which publishes designations and notices in the Federal Register, and no office attaches a distributional table to them. The costs are tabulated elsewhere: in a currency worth a ninetieth of its 2015 value, in bread at seven times the subsidized price, in blackouts, on invoices addressed to people who did not choose this war. Call the instrument pressure and the hunger reads as leverage rather than as a bill.

The people paying have not gone quiet. Retirees, teachers, and labor unionists have marched. Nationwide protests in January were followed by a security-force crackdown that killed thousands, The Wall Street Journal reported. After a crash killed 13 petrochemical workers in Abadan, a trade union demonstrated, tying the deaths to austerity and political repression; the Human Rights Activists News Agency said at least 12 protesters were detained. Deputy Interior Minister Ali Zeinivand told the semiofficial ILNA news agency that marches were allowed so long as they focused on living conditions. Inflation has made wages insufficient to cover living expenses, he said; it is natural that these groups have demands. The cost may be named. The war may not.

The hardliners’ answer confirms the indictment. They criticize the subsidy cuts while suggesting that Tehran seek a nuclear weapon or escalate the war over the Strait of Hormuz. A nuclear program does not lift the blockade; it invites new sanctions and a wider war. Escalation at Hormuz would choke the oil-export lanes through which the state’s revenue runs. Neither proposal puts bread in a bakery. At nightly demonstrations, begun during the war, chants increasingly target Pezeshkian, Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi, and parliament speaker Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf, all officials who backed talks to end the war. Women dressed in white burial shrouds demonstrated at the Revolutionary Guard Corps’ headquarters, accusing the force of insufficient retaliation. The sequence is plain: economic pressure produces shortages; Tehran passes shortages to households; the hardliners propose more economic war. A government that cannot afford flour is being told, from inside its own politics, that the remedy is a bomb.

If this is pressure, it can be priced and disclosed like one. End the blockade, restore oil exports, flights, and overland trade, and require Washington to publish the incidence table for each instrument it chose—then require Tehran to publish the revenue and expenditure choices behind every subsidy cut. The arithmetic must say who pays, in what currency, over what window; it must not remain legible only in empty shelves and rolling blackouts. The corrective is not another round of cuts. The subsidized loaf and the subsidized kilowatt are the poorest Iranians’ share of their own oil; a settlement that consumes that share without replacing it is a transfer from them to the other side of the table.

The arithmetic was published in advance: the May inflation data and the August strategy decision sit on the record before November’s shortages. The administration chose an instrument whose transmission runs through other people’s bread and then called the damage pressure. The war has produced a bill. Washington emptied Iran’s treasury; Tehran assigned the emptiness to bread and kilowatts.

The last line of the blockade is a baker in Zanjan without cheap flour. The blockade has no distributional score, so the bread is the score. The Trump administration does not get to grade the blockade it wrote.
