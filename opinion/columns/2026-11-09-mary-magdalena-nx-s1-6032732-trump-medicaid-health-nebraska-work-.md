On May 1, 2026, Nebraska became the first state to enforce the Medicaid work requirements in President Trump’s One Big Beautiful Bill Act, eight months before most states were expected to do the same. About 70,000 adult Nebraska Medicaid recipients were required to document 80 hours a month of work or comparable activity unless they qualified for an exemption. The [KFF Health News investigation published by NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/11/09/nx-s1-6032732/trump-medicaid-health-nebraska-work-insurance) documents the rollout and its human cost.

In December 2025, Nebraska Gov. Jim Pillen appeared with Mehmet Oz, the Trump administration official overseeing Medicare and Medicaid. Pillen said state officials “certainly appreciate President Trump and his whole administration for delivering this key reform.” He added, “We’re excited to be able to be the first to make it happen.” Pillen had said Nebraska would not add workers to implement the requirements. In July, he instituted a government-wide hiring freeze.

Eli McCreary was 64 when he was diagnosed with cancer in his right kidney in June. He also had diabetes and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease. He had left a four-month substance-use treatment program in March.

McCreary said CHI Health told him that he was no longer insured through Medicaid and would cancel his appointments unless he paid out of pocket. He canceled a CT scan and other tests. He delayed a cancer-related appointment with a physician who traveled to Omaha, missing what he called his window of opportunity for another month.

McCreary called a number that turned out to serve Nebraska’s outsourced SNAP accounts, not Medicaid. A representative told him, “You’re required to do work to stay on,” and, “And you haven’t fulfilled your work requirement.”

Nebraska later said McCreary had no lapse in coverage. State officials said he had turned 65, entered Medicare, and was disenrolled because of his age, not the work requirement. Attorneys and policy professionals said his substance-use treatment and health conditions should have qualified him for an exemption. At the time of the report, McCreary was enrolled in Medicare, with Medicaid covering some costs. He had rescheduled his canceled appointments. In early November, surgeons removed his right kidney.

State officials said 86 percent of affected applicants had met the requirement or qualified for an exclusion, and 93 percent of existing enrollees had kept their coverage. As of August, about 1,100 Nebraskans had been denied benefits because of the work requirements, most because they had not responded to requests for information. Nebraska’s total Medicaid enrollment had fallen by roughly 4,600 people since May, a drop of 1.4 percent.

Internal state documents said it was “expected” that most work-requirement denials would result from missing paperwork. The average wait at Nebraska’s health department call center nearly doubled between May and July, rising from about 13 minutes to 26. August brought more than 101,000 calls, a 66 percent increase from May. Health clinics and advocacy groups attributed much of the confusion to poor training and inadequate staffing.

State documents obtained by KFF Health News outlined $8.2 million in contractor costs over two years for Medicaid changes and more frequent eligibility checks. Nebraska listed at least six contracts costing more than $250,000 each, including one involving compensation above $1.8 million. Federal taxpayers pay as much as 90 percent of state information-technology changes.

Public Consulting Group senior consultant Latanya Michaux said, “I really cannot brag enough about how well Nebraska handled this requirement.” She also said, “We definitely had fun standing up the Medicaid work requirement program.” During the launch, a group of 60 people held hours-long meetings three days a week. The Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services did not issue key guidance on exemptions until June 1. Nebraska had already begun enforcing the rule on May 1.
