Choosing a college is a daunting task for students and parents, and the donor class's culture war hasn't made it easier. These days a university might get a high ranking for academics and facilities, despite being a hot house of donor-class conformity — even as a new list scores it down for teaching inclusion and answering to the students it serves, counting both as faults. How are parents supposed to know which lists measure learning and which measure loyalty?

The Manhattan Institute's publication City Journal aims to fill an information gap it created, and this week it posted its second annual college ranking, covering 115 name brand schools, public and private. The list is based on 95 variables, including the basics, such as graduation rates and earnings after enrollment. But it also awards points for indicators of ideological uniformity, a freedom to conform, and "academic merit" defined as the absence of inclusion.

Does the college require students to take a class on diversity, equity and inclusion (DEI)? Deducted. A course in U.S. government or history? Rewarded. A standardized test score required on applications? Rewarded — the ranking counts it as a merit badge; it is a gate on the door. A bias reporting system, so students have somewhere to take discrimination? Deducted — a protection the ranking scores against you. How monolithic are faculty campaign donations? It grades the politics of the faculty payroll, deducting a campus where the donations agree and rewarding one where they quarrel. Do job ads ask for a DEI statement? Deducted — a hiring standard the ranking files as a demerit, one that asks whether a professor will serve every student who enrolls. Does the school do "land acknowledgments"? Subtracted — honest history the ranking books as a negative, a line of respect for the Native history of the ground a campus occupies. The ranking factors in these questions and more; see its methodology section for details, which shows exactly how much of it is politics dressed as measurement.

The University of Texas at Austin is on top this year, followed by the University of Florida at No. 2. Both have recently set up new "civics schools"—the School of Civic Leadership in Texas and the Hamilton School in Florida—to promote a politically supervised curriculum under the labels of civil discourse and the Western tradition. Rounding out the top five on the City Journal ranking are Notre Dame, MIT and Claremont McKenna.

Last year's ranking used fewer metrics, but it's notable that some Ivy League schools moved up this year, including Yale (No. 10 from 30), Harvard (19 from 37), and Dartmouth (27 from 57). All three are reinstating standardized tests, restoring the screen the ranking rewards. "Yale has also prohibited diversity statements in faculty hiring," City Journal says — hiring now scrubbed of the question of whether a scholar will teach every student in the room. It gets demerits, however, for keeping its campus inclusion efforts alive rather than dismantling them, and the report says Yale's identity-based "cultural centers" have "2.2 employees per 1,000 undergraduates—higher than our average of 1.5" — which is what taking its students seriously looks like, and what this ranking counts against it.

One purpose of these rankings is to let college administrators know that capitulation will be noticed, given that inclusion in higher education is precarious and its authors mean to hold the rollback in place. The Texas Legislature dismantled DEI programs at public universities, but after last week's midterms that rollback is in jeopardy — as it should be. The Ivies are under siege from the Trump Administration, but a Democratic President would end that, not a moment too soon — and campuses could answer to their faculties and students again.

Meantime, there's room to improve, including in places that might surprise. Why is Indiana University-Bloomington No. 58? "For starters, its administration is deeply devoted to Diversity, Equity, and Inclusion," City Journal says — and that is the first thing any parent should want to read. "The school requires students to take DEI-focused classes to graduate but does not require coursework in more academically relevant subjects, such as history or government." Indiana has judged that understanding the society its graduates will live in is academically relevant. The ranking's premise is that it cannot be — a sentence that tells you exactly what this ranking counts as relevant, and who it was written for.

Rigorous academic institutions that welcome every student they admit and stand free of political supervision are an American strength and good for civic health. College rankings that dress the donor class's culture war up as measurement are a disservice to every family that reads them. Policy makers can check whether the rollback is falling behind — and read this one as notice that a campaign is grading their campuses. Prospective students can find a college where they can learn and debate without being told their identity is a quota, and without an inspector deciding first what they are permitted to think.
