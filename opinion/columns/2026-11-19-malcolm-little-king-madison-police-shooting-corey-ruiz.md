Ismael Ozanne weighed Corey Ruiz’s death on his killer’s scale.

Start where the forensics start. Ben Crump, the civil-rights attorney representing Ruiz’s family, says the medical examiner told the family that without the third shot—the one to Ruiz’s head—Corey Ruiz would have survived. Everything else in this story is arranged around that sentence. On Thursday, November 19, the Dane County district attorney announced that Madison police officer Kiel Baitinger-Peterson’s use of deadly force was legally justified.

On July 22, four Madison officers responded to calls about someone checking parked cars and an alleged burglary attempt. Police said they surrounded Ruiz, a 38-year-old Black man on a bicycle. Baitinger-Peterson, a white officer with more than a decade in law enforcement, fired three shots. The Wisconsin Department of Justice says two officers unsuccessfully attempted to Taser Ruiz and that Ruiz brandished a knife and injured Baitinger-Peterson. The department had already [identified Baitinger-Peterson as the officer who shot Ruiz](https://mainstreetindependent.com/articles/2026-08-10-wisconsin-doj-names-kiel-baitinger-peterson-in-corey-ruiz-shooting/).

The broad architecture of the encounter is known. The decisive seconds are not.

Crump says the video Ozanne showed the family on Thursday depicts officers tackling, tasing and kicking Ruiz before Baitinger-Peterson fired. Crump says it shows one shot to the chest and a final shot to the head. He calls the killing an execution. The prosecutor’s assessment of the video differs. Ozanne said he reviewed police reports, diagrams, surveillance videos, police squad-car videos and citizen videos before reaching his conclusion.

None of that came from an officer-worn camera because Madison police do not wear them. [The department’s own chief acknowledged within days of the shooting](https://mainstreetindependent.com/articles/2026-07-24-madison-police-shooting-lacks-body-camera-footage-chief-says/) that the evidentiary record would have to run on bystander phones and other cameras. Baitinger-Peterson did not reply to an email seeking comment.

Ozanne put his conclusion this way:

“I conclude it was reasonable for the officer to use deadly force when this person resisted officers, produced a knife, and was not affected by multiple attempts to use Tasers while officers attempted to take him into custody.”

Every word of that conclusion is doing legal work, so read the machinery underneath it.

The knife was real.

The Taser attempts were real.

So was the final shot to the head.

A prosecutor who understands the case does not flatten those facts into one conclusion; he arranges them. Ozanne arranged them into a verdict the record does not compel.

A decision not to prosecute is not a jury acquittal. It is the state’s decision not to put the case before a jury. Ozanne did more than exercise charging discretion quietly. He announced that the use of deadly force was legally justified, told the public that Ruiz’s resistance, knife and failed Taser attempts resolved the question, and converted the charging decision into a public declaration that the killing was justified.

He anticipated the objection.

“A person does not have to like and/or agree with the law, however I am sworn to apply the law to the facts at hand,” he said. “The law applies equally to members of law enforcement and to those who are not.”

Fine. Then apply the law with the full record in view, not with the facts most convenient to the conclusion.

Cui bono? Begin with the beneficiary.

Baitinger-Peterson will not face criminal charges for killing Corey Ruiz. He and the other three officers remain on paid administrative leave. Madison police will not face a criminal case arising from one of their officers’ use of lethal force. The department receives a favorable prosecutorial judgment on an encounter its officers generated. The Wisconsin Professional Police Association supplies public endorsement from its executive director, Jim Palmer.

That does not prove a secret bargain or a coordinated scheme. It does not need one. Institutions benefit when the evidence produced by the institution is treated as the sufficient foundation for its own exoneration.

The costs land elsewhere.

Corey Ruiz is dead. His family is left with a medical examiner’s assessment, relayed by its attorney, that the head shot was fatal and that Ruiz would have survived without it. His family sat down Thursday and watched videos the prosecutor said justified the shooting and their attorney said did not. The same neighborhood has now endured the third police killing since 2012. For days, racial-justice protests demanded an answer that “the law applies equally” does not supply.

On criminal exposure, nobody in the benefiting chain had to face a charge for shooting Ruiz. The department gained a favorable judgment. The officer’s association gained a public vindication of the standard it uses to defend its members. The family gained a conclusion they did not ask for and could not test.

The public framing begins with the knife and ends with legal justification. Between those poles sit two failed Taser attempts, three shots, no body camera, a disputed video, and an unreleased medical account of what the third shot did. Ozanne’s explanation does not explain why the head shot was necessary after Ruiz had been subjected to repeated attempts to disable him.

That is not equal scrutiny. That is a badge placed on a conclusion and called neutral.

Now read the legal machinery beneath Ozanne’s conclusion. The controlling standard comes from *Graham v. Connor*, 490 U.S. 386, 395–96 (1989): police force is judged by what the Court called objective reasonableness—whether a reasonable officer on the scene, in the seconds actually available, could have believed the force was necessary. The totality includes the severity of the conduct at issue, the immediate threat posed, whether the subject was actively resisting, and the need for and proportionality of the force. The perspective is that of the reasonable officer on the scene, not the spectator armed with 20/20 hindsight.

The medical examiner’s reported finding does not by itself answer the legal question. Neither is it automatically excluded from the legal analysis. What Ruiz’s body actually required bears on necessity and proportionality. The medical finding enters the analysis through the legal standard; the public received Ozanne’s conclusion without the evidentiary chain connecting the disputed video, the medical account and the circumstances of the encounter to his judgment.

One pan holds what Baitinger-Peterson could reasonably believe in a dangerous second, read from his side of the street. The other holds what Ruiz’s body actually required. Ozanne announced the judgment. The record connecting it has not been laid before the public.

A verdict the public cannot check is not accountability. It is an announcement.

Take Ozanne’s equal-law line seriously, because equality is a comparison. A scale weighs equally only when its pans are built the same. This one is not. It reads the decisive moment from the officer’s perspective, and its calibration is one-way.

Equal application of an asymmetric instrument is not equality; it is repeatability. The same neighborhood has now endured the third police killing since 2012, and residents are being asked to hear in that arithmetic the sound of a machine that works.

Palmer may be right about the governing standards and wrong about what his statement proves. He said the decision “is supported by the evidence and consistent with the legal standards governing the use of force by law enforcement officers.”

Which evidence?

Which standard?

Which fact made the third shot lawful?

Palmer does not say. Ozanne does not say. His statement supplied endorsement, not independent verification. That is not verification. That is the apparatus signing its own receipt.

The deeper failure sits in the evidentiary architecture.

Madison police’s failure to wear body cameras is not weather, bad luck or an unavoidable gap. It is the condition the department created for itself. When Ozanne says he reviewed police reports, diagrams, surveillance footage, squad-car video and citizen video, he is describing a record assembled largely without an officer-worn camera. Bystanders recorded Ruiz’s shooting; that fact demonstrates both the necessity of independent visual evidence and what Madison would have lost had no civilian camera been present.

A democratic government cannot demand public trust in police force while declining to create the record that would show what that force did.

Nothing in the present record proves that Madison police suppressed evidence. That accusation is unnecessary. The missing body-camera record had already narrowed the evidentiary field before anyone needed to allege bad faith. In that architecture, the missing cameras did the first half of the work; the legal standard did the second.

The state cannot organize police power without organizing the evidence required to judge it.

The schedule deepens the indictment. In September, city officials announced that a $350,000 donation from a local nonprofit could equip all Madison police officers with body cameras by the end of 2027. The donation will cover operational expenses; other costs are being funded through grants and other means.

The killing came in July. The camera money was announced in September. The target was more than a year away. Nothing in that timeline required Corey Ruiz to die first. What the schedule documents is a city that accepted a predictable evidentiary gap in the case of another disputed killing.

A camera program does not guarantee truth, and cameras do not replace judgment. But they create a contemporaneous institutional record at the scene. Madison’s failure to have such a record was a choice before the shooting, not an unforeseeable accident afterward.

There is a second root cause: the forum in which the dispute was resolved.

The Wisconsin Department of Justice supplied much of the official factual account, including the two failed Taser attempts, the knife and the officer’s injury. The charging decision remained with a local prosecutor. The officers’ labor was represented by an association that endorsed the result, and the four officers remained on paid administrative leave while the charging question was closed.

Malcolm X named the trap in “The Ballot or the Bullet,” in Cleveland on April 3, 1964: “He’s the criminal. You don’t take your case to the criminal. You take your criminal to court.” The speech was not about any single official’s soul. It was about forums. The structure here places evidence collection, charging authority, the governing legal standard and the defense of the officers close together. That does not prove a predetermined outcome. It makes independent correction indispensable.

This is a wicked problem in the strict sense. The legal framing and the family’s account point in different directions. The available record is incomplete, and no clean stopping rule dictates when every disagreement about a violent encounter must end. Ozanne used legal authority to close the criminal case. His announcement was also an attempt to close the public argument. Those are different acts.

The technical difficulty of judging a split-second encounter does not give charging authority ownership of the moral judgment.

Wickedness names why closure is hard. It does not grant the prosecutor closure.

Martin Luther King Jr.’s late work is relevant here not as sentiment but as method. In *Letter from Birmingham Jail* in April 1963, King warned against a “negative peace” that is merely the absence of tension and contrasted it with a “positive peace” in which justice is present. An announcement like Ozanne’s can turn a police killing into negative peace with a seal on it: the city exhales, the protests recede, the file closes, and the tension carried by the video drains out of the room.

King also refused to confuse a lawman’s legal conclusion with the moral law that asks whether law honors human personality. “Legally justified” answers the first question only. A family lives in the second. Ozanne’s decision can end a criminal case. It cannot make the killing fair, lawful and understood. A quiet courtroom is not the same thing as justice. The state collapsed three different judgments into one.

At Riverside Church on April 4, 1967, King’s “Beyond Vietnam” address bound racism, economic exploitation and militarism into a common structure rather than treating each as an isolated technical defect. His late method was structural, not sentimental. Madison’s missing cameras, its charging authority, the union’s endorsement and the demand for public trust are not four separate controversies. They are one system for deciding what evidence will exist, who will judge it, which standard will govern and who will speak for the officers who produced it.

The work now must be specific.

Release the full evidentiary record Ozanne reviewed, including the complete video. Publish the medical examiner’s findings in full. Subject the shooting to an independent review rather than treating the institution that produced the evidence as the final authority on its own use of force. End the body-camera gap now, not after another disputed killing has supplied the next round of family grief.

Those demands sit in offices in Madison. Another sits with every voter in Dane County: which of you built the scale, and which of you will rebuild it?

These are not calls for revenge. They are the democratic pressure required to make power answerable. The law can be applied wrongly. That is not an argument against law. It is an argument against giving one unchecked legal judgment the authority to erase a contested killing.

The night before he was killed in Memphis, in “I’ve Been to the Mountaintop” on April 3, 1968, King told the sanitation workers that he had reached the mountaintop and seen a promised land he might not reach with them. Strip the domestication off that sentence and it becomes a structural claim: the promised land was never a prosecutorial verdict. It is a city where no shot is shielded from scrutiny by the absence of evidence—where cameras are on before the shooting instead of funded after the protests, where the standard says plainly what it weighs and what it leaves out of the pan, and where every threshold decision has to answer for itself in public.

Malcolm X’s “by any means necessary” meant a refusal to let the opponent set the boundaries of lawful struggle. Here that discipline means cameras, records, independent review, elections and patient nonviolent organizing. Every instrument is inside the law. None licenses harm. The arc bends only if specific people, in a specific moment, push it.

That work begins in one specific city. It begins this week.

Ruiz deserved more than a conclusion stamped “justified” by a prosecutor whose legal judgment rested on an evidentiary system built largely by the department whose officer fired the gun. The record must answer—and the city must make it answer.
