Citrus greening nearly killed Florida's orange industry and abandoned half a million acres.

I know what a dead grove looks like. On my forty acres in Adams County, a failed patch of trees turns back into brush fast enough to hide the old fence line. In Florida, the scale is different, but the loss is familiar: land left waiting while the people who knew how to work it move on.

In a Frostproof rail yard where citrus packing houses once lined the tracks, a junkyard now sells the dismantled remains of Florida's juice plants. Drive past the abandoned groves and the story of American orange juice looks finished.

It is not.

This spring, the Environmental Protection Agency approved two technologies aimed at the disease that has been killing Florida citrus since the mid-2000s. One is the [first commercially released Crispr-edited crop](2026-09-18-soilcea-gene-edited-citrus-trees-florida) specifically engineered to resist disease. It is not corn or soy. It is the rootstock of the Florida orange tree, the bottom half of the grafted plant that determines whether the top half survives.

Soilcea's gene-edited rootstock has shown thousands of times more resistance to the bacterium that causes citrus greening than anything now growing in the ground. The resistance is high enough to be considered uninfected by scientific standards. Nian Wang, the University of Florida microbiologist who identified the genes that made the edit possible, helped give growers their first real shot in twenty years.

The disease is carried by the Asian citrus psyllid, an insect small enough to be mistaken for pollen. It clogs the tree's vascular system and chokes off water and nutrients. Pesticides have not stopped it. The tree can still stand in the grove while the disease takes away the fruit, the yield, and eventually the tree itself.

Florida's orange production is down 95 percent since greening arrived. More than 90 percent of those oranges go to juice. Last year, 60,000 acres went out of production, 26 percent of what remained. About half a million acres now sit dormant, with abandoned groves absorbed into cattle pasture and the state's sprawling development.

The number of companies processing Florida oranges for juice fell from 54 in the 1980s to four. The industry lost more than half its workforce between 2003-04 and 2020-21. It did not lose only trees. It lost processors, truck drivers, nursery workers, pickers, grafters, mechanics, and the institutional knowledge that lives in people's hands until the work disappears.

A second approval went to Silvec, a company founded by a former venture capitalist and his sister, a University of Maryland virologist. Its technology uses a harmless virus to deliver antibacterial peptides continuously into the tree. That matters because peptides introduced into plants usually break down almost immediately. In treated trees, peptide levels have risen at least a hundredfold, while bacterial loads have dropped by at least 90 percent.

The technology is being propagated in inoculated scions at a nursery in Frostproof. Growers are gathering to preorder on September 30.

The demand is already moving. Agromillora is growing more than a million of the modified trees, and hundreds of thousands have been ordered for spring planting. The trees are coming. The trucks are lined up.

The collapse that produced this opening is staggering, but the science did not arrive into an empty field. Philip Rucks, a longtime citrus and nursery owner in Frostproof, watched greening push him toward bankruptcy a decade ago. He diversified into blueberries, bamboo, and ornamental plants. "We're just desperate trying to find alternatives," he said. Now he is propagating the inoculated scions that may become the next generation of Florida groves.

Gary Farmer, Agromillora's head grafter, has seen millions of trees come through his nursery and watched every one perish. "This is the first real hope I have had in twenty years," he said.

That sentence carries more weight than the press release. Hope is not a mood when a man has watched the same failure arrive tree by tree. It is a production order, a grafting bench, a nursery schedule, and a state subsidy arriving before the next season closes.

Florida is putting $330 million over two years into grower subsidies to help bridge the gap. The first oranges from the disease-resistant trees will not be ready for juice for another two years. The science has arrived. The capital is partially in place. The land is waiting.

This is the part where the country usually congratulates itself too early.

A gene edit does not restore the workers who left the industry. A viral delivery system does not reopen the fifty packing houses that became four processors. A subsidy does not recreate the generation of citrus workers and growers who carried the knowledge of a business that had been handed down before the bacterium arrived.

Fran Becker, senior vice president at Peace River Citrus Products, one of the four remaining processors, put the danger plainly: "What we really need is people to come back into the business. If we don't, I'm scared of where we might end up."

The first fight was against the disease. The next fight is against time, capital, labor loss, collapsing consumer demand, and soaring input costs. The country has started the rescue, but it has not finished the job.

The pattern is not unique to oranges. For twenty years, citrus growers waited while a spreading plague outran the institutions meant to answer it. The same playbook is now playing out against [Dutch elm disease](2026-08-31-forest-service-dutch-elm-disease), where federal foresters are injecting thousands of young elms with the very spores that killed their elders. That sounds backward until you understand the point: science sometimes has to expose a living system to danger in order to find the survivors that can carry it forward.

The same Crispr and viral-delivery approaches may be adapted to Pierce's disease in grapevines and Maize lethal necrosis in corn. Anna Whitfield of North Carolina State, who works on emerging plant disease and global food security, says the sky is the limit.

That is the larger story. These are not merely new oranges. They are tools for crops whose diseases have outrun pesticides, breeding cycles, budgets, and patience. They are a platform.

Aldo Leopold wrote that conservation is a state of harmony between people and land. Harmony does not mean leaving the land untouched. It means learning what the land is asking, then doing the work before the loss becomes permanent.

The question is no longer whether American agriculture can defeat the diseases that have been killing it. The question is whether the country can keep its people, its nurseries, its processors, and its working knowledge in place long enough to finish what the science has finally started.

The next two years will tell whether Florida's citrus belt can hold on long enough to see its own comeback through.
