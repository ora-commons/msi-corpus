A 34-year-old Filipina who swore her oath in New York City earlier this year is about to do something she has never done before: pull a lever in a midterm. A 37-year-old Venezuelan in California spent an evening researching every proposition on a ballot so long it overwhelmed him — and didn't skip a single one. A 23-year-old Texan says the thing she was most excited about at 21 wasn't drinking. It was filing paperwork to petition her parents.

This is what happens when a country hands a ballot to someone who spent two decades earning it.

Nicole Solis-Sison's family fled the Philippine government, landed in Los Angeles, and expected to go home. Instead, Manila pursued them across international immigration territory and demanded them back — and the INS agent who showed up at their door did something nobody would credit an immigration officer with today. He told them to file for asylum. He gave them the tools to stay. The agency that once told her family to stay is the same one whose agents now arrest people who look like they might not have papers. More than twenty years later, she's a citizen with a voter guide and a diary. "I've only gotten out of it with like a sliver of hope," she says, and the weight she feels is to protect the people still trapped in the machinery she barely escaped.

Juan Escalante didn't know he was undocumented until high school, when a lawyer his parents paid turned out to have not just given them bad advice but mishandled the case outright — and in immigration court, you don't get a do-over. You're "kind of out of the line entirely." DACA pulled him back in. He graduated college, the first in his family, and naturalized in 2025. He is 37 years old, the first American citizen in his family, and this year he casts his first general-election ballot — an act he calls "profoundly personal and emotional," because he understands what a vote can do "in service of the greater good." When the mail-in ballot arrived he treated it like homework worth doing properly, because it is. "Despite the amount of bubbles that I had to fill in and the amount of research that I had to do, I never once took that for granted."

You can see the intended effect in the fear, because the fear sits right next to the pride. Deisy España's mother made the sign of the cross in the passenger seat during a traffic stop, praying the police wouldn't hand her over to ICE. Leydi Gonzalez's family held the conversation every immigrant household dreads — who takes the kids if mom and dad disappear — the year Trump first won. The message of the administration's hardline enforcement is simple: stay small, stay quiet, stay afraid. Arrest people who look like they might not have papers. Keep ICE in the news every day. And make citizenship itself feel like something that can be taken away.

These aren't cautionary tales. They're the reason an administration has spent months building exactly this kind of list — [people like Marva Cook](/articles/2026-10-23-prosecutors-charge-marva-cook-after-she-says-she-learned-she-was-not-a-citizen/), charged after learning she was never a citizen at all — and every new citizen watching got the point. They're the reason [an enforcement agenda voters in Miami-Dade are already faulting](/articles/2026-09-29-miami-dade-voters-fault-trump-immigration-enforcement-ahead-of-midterms/) has turned [knowing someone caught in the machinery](/articles/2026-05-05-many-know-someone-affected-by-trump-immigration-push-new-ap-norc-poll/) into one of the defining political facts of the cycle.

Before I name anyone else's part in this, I have to name my own. We who claim the gospel in this country — my church, my parish, the pews I sit in every Sunday — spent years keeping our heads down while this machinery was built, and a silence that long is not neutrality; it is consent wearing an alibi. The Catholic-right figures who blessed it and the bishops who would not name it belong to my field of accountability, not to someone else's. And there is a religious case being made for every one of these arrests that has to be named plainly: Christian-right organizations and the politicians they bless have spent this administration wrapping enforcement in the language of order and faith — treating the arrest of a mother in a passenger seat as godliness, treating a deportation as rule of law consecrated. That is the oldest operation in the book. Extract a fragment of scripture, invert it, and use it to license what the gospel calls evil.

Jesus told the scribes and Pharisees that they tithe mint and dill and cumin and neglect the weightier matters of the law — justice and mercy and faithfulness. Jeremiah gave the preachers who dress a wound and call it peace the name the tradition already knew: they have healed the wound of my people lightly, saying "Peace, peace," when there is no peace. There is no peace in a household that keeps a suitcase packed by the door. To the Christian official who invokes God to bless this, and to the pastor who tells his flock that order is godly: the harm is named, the door is still open, and you can stop.

But the policy is producing the opposite of what it wants. It is creating the most motivated first-time voters in a generation.

España's mother just got a green card, but every ICE story lands the same way in her head: "That could have been my dad." Gonzalez turned 21 and wanted the right to petition her parents; she is voting for "a sense of security for my family." Her parents are now in the system through her sponsorship, waiting on residency, and waiting is its own kind of exposure. Her community is still absorbing the killing of Lorenzo Salgado Araujo by an ICE agent outside her backyard, in a state where Democrat James Talarico is polling ahead of Ken Paxton for Senate. The people most targeted by this crackdown are becoming the people most likely to show up.

España's message to the young citizens around her is blunter than any poll: don't vote for yourself. Vote for your parents.

That's the whole story in seven words. The newest Americans aren't voting for a tax bracket or a zoning variance. They're voting as proxies for the people in their kitchen who can't — parents who drove terrified, who prayed through traffic stops, who trusted a lawyer and got destroyed for it, who crossed a border so their kid could one day stand in a booth and pull a lever on their behalf.

A country that makes citizenship take twenty years shouldn't be surprised when the people who finally get it treat the ballot like the treasure it is. The administration wanted them afraid. Instead, they are organized. It wanted them quiet. Instead, they are filling out mail-in ballots, researching every proposition, making voter guides, and voting for their whole families. It wanted citizenship to feel like a privilege that could be revoked. Instead, they are treating it like a weapon — and aiming it at the people who tried to make them disappear.

On Election Day, the communities Trump's ICE was sent to frighten will be at the front of the line. Not despite the fear. Because of it. Because for the first time in their lives, it's in their hands. That's not a constituency you suppress. That's a constituency you've just awakened.
