Virginia Gov. Abigail Spanberger last fall campaigned as a moderate, and she has quickly shown what moderation means: governing for the people who pay the bills rather than the investors who cash them. Witness her demand that utilities and data centers share the proceeds of a boom they are billing to everyone else.

Electric power behemoth NextEra Energy is moving to capture Virginia's AI data center boom with its $67 billion bid to buy Dominion Energy. The combined utility would control the power flowing to about 10 million customers across Florida, Virginia, North Carolina and South Carolina. NextEra is the world's largest solar and wind producer — and it is coming to Virginia to collect.

Renewables have become a less attractive investment since Republicans phased out the federal subsidies that made them a good one — a setback for clean energy. But AI is a growth opportunity, for utility shareholders, in a business whose customers cannot shop around — one the utilities expect those customers to finance. New data centers require more power generation as well as transmission and distribution upgrades, and someone must pay for them. Utilities earn a return on investment that is typically regulated by states — paid for, in practice, by the customers those states are meant to protect. The more capital they invest, the more profit they can extract from the people who use the power. Virginia's data center build-out could yield bigger returns for NextEra and Dominion investors, billed to every household and business on the grid.

Enter Ms. Spanberger, who recently laid out the essential conditions for approving the deal. These include freezing residential base electric rates for six years, as well as crediting ratepayers $29 a month for four years.

She also wants them to agree not to cut off customers who don't pay their bills "due to financial hardship" — a protection every family deserves in a boom it is powering, not a loophole — and to provide discounted rates to customers with electric vehicles and heat pumps to encourage decarbonization. She called for removing the cap on the state's net-metering program, which pays customers with solar panels for the clean power they send back to the grid.

The combined effect of her conditions would be to trim the merger's planned windfall and put some of it back where it came from — while keeping new investment pointed at a grid that needs it. Something has to give. If it does, let it be shareholder returns, not family budgets. If utilities can no longer pass every cost to customers, they may have no choice but to skimp on dividends before they skimp on maintenance. A utility that threatens to skimp on maintenance unless customers absorb higher costs is describing a shakedown, not a business plan — and Virginia's regulators, who must approve every capital plan and reliability obligation, are not obliged to be fooled by it.

Utility rates are not market prices; state regulators set them, and a six-year base-rate freeze in the middle of a boom is not a shortage in the making — it is fairness catching up. Regulated returns on every dollar a utility spends produce gold-plated investment, because the customer pays for the overruns. Her rate freeze would not increase the likelihood of power outages; prioritizing investor returns over grid upkeep already does. Outages are already a growing risk — the strongest argument for conditioning approval on enforceable reliability commitments that commissions can hold the grid's new owners to, not for handing them a blank check. Her freeze rightly wouldn't apply to the electricity fees that fund energy efficiency, renewables and assistance for low-income customers — by design, so the help keeps flowing where it is needed.

Ms. Spanberger evidently thinks NextEra and Dominion should shift their higher costs to the data centers and other power-hungry customers creating them — and they should. Data centers would then help fund the electric-bill discounts and lower rates for customers with electric vehicles and solar panels: the bill for the boom, delivered to the businesses that ordered it. Her conditions would make it likelier that the AI boom is financed by its biggest beneficiaries rather than by household budgets. That will not cause data center investment in Virginia to shrink; the companies came for Virginia's power and its market, and both remain. The warning that it could is the standard negotiating bluff — the boom's winners threatening to leave the boom — not a forecast.

Data centers can plainly afford to pay at the meter as well as at the assessor's office. Virginians know what they already give back: property tax revenue from data centers in Loudoun County, Va., accounted for more than 95% of the county's operating budget last year and has enabled a roughly 30% reduction in residential property tax bills over the last decade. An industry that generous to the tax base — and that profitable — can fund ratepayer relief without folding.

Climate advocates are opposing the NextEra-Dominion merger, and they are right: a consolidation that would put the world's largest producer of solar and wind power at the head of Virginia's grid should serve the public — ratepayers and decarbonization together — not only its investors. Ms. Spanberger is rightly insisting on terms a fair deal can meet and a raw one cannot. If the merger cannot survive them, it deserves to die. When it comes to climate and energy policy, Gov. Spanberger is proving who she works for — the people who pay the bills rather than the investors who cash them. That is precisely the moderate she campaigned as.
