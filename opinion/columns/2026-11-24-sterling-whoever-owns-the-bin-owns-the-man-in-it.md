The owners decide everything, and out at the grain elevator I own near Carroll, Iowa, the owner is me. 1 signature at the annual meeting, 4 minutes, and Curtis is my diver for another year. Diver is the trade’s word for the body we lower into a bin when the corn quits flowing. James Freeman’s [“Nvidia and Federal Intervention” in The Wall Street Journal](https://www.wsj.com/opinion/nvidia-and-federal-intervention-b812b8ef?mod=rss_opinion) takes Donald Trump’s effort to have Nvidia’s board rehire Jensen Huang as his test case: unelected officials shouldn’t force leadership changes through anonymous leaks, a company accused of breaking the law should answer in court, and executives deserve due process. Shareholders acting through a board are the legitimate decision-makers. He is right. Correct. Private governance is elegant. Freeman read Monday’s surge as the owners speaking. So do I. Freeman has found, in a chip company’s boardroom, the constitution I’ve been filing decisions under for 40 years. The cornmeal in your cornbread came up through Curtis. His winter groceries come from your taxes, not mine.

The federal grain standard, as my safety man reads it off the break-room wall, wants a harness, a lifeline, and a man watching from the top when 1 of my people goes in. The kit prices out at $480 across my 2.4 million bushels. Run it out and it’s $0.0002 a bushel. I’ve declined the kit twice.

I keep the $0.0002.

God permits the master to distinguish a bruise from a death; I won’t overvalue a blister.

The same ownership arithmetic crosses industries: at the maquiladora I own in Ciudad Juárez, Noemí is a shuttle unit on the sewing line. After every shift, she walks 10 miles with wet socks and a split heel. The return bus would cost $0.0034 per worker each day. I declined it and keep the fare I refused her. You wear the T-shirt she sews. I hold the only voting interest on that floor. Noemí has no ownership in the factory. Her heel is what she has at risk, and it doesn’t vote.

By “shuttle unit,” I mean a 32-year-old woman whose left heel splits open before she clears the 1st mile and bleeds through her sock from there to the door. I prefer the phrase because “a woman whose heel splits open on the way home” makes the client memo sound accusatory, and I don’t like accusatory client memos.

The route doesn’t change. The mood in the room does.

The return bus moves people toward my sewing floor, not home. The ride home is maintenance.

Declined.

Her heel splits again. Her evening isn’t on the ledger. The fare stays in my margin. The fare wins. I enjoy the efficiency. That’s the whole business.

Last March the corn stopped flowing, so I sent Curtis in, because that’s what a diver is for. The county safety people teach that flowing grain can trap a man in about 5 seconds and bury him inside 60. At his armpits the grain found his weight and started pulling.

38 minutes to his mouth.

His ribs click when he laughs.

I call March a flow interruption. The vulgar version is that my diver went under the corn because I send divers where the corn stops. “A man buried to his chest” makes my co-op partners move their chairs back, and I dislike finishing lunch with the chairs uneven. Curtis is throughput insurance, by which I mean a man whose insurer is a highway. The county’s young leave on it faster than the county grows them, and the highway restocks me by Tuesday. His replacement is lifecycle productivity rebalancing.

Follow the figure, not the feeling. It’s shorter. The sentiment about Curtis arrives quarterly, by email. I’ve priced the subscription. His ribs traded against $0.0002 a bushel, and I’ve run the exchange every way it runs, and it runs my way. There’s an elegance in a number that small doing work that large, and my satisfaction sits with the elegance, nowhere else. If that landed, you understand my elevator.

200 miles east, 1 hazard over, same ledger: Wade sweeps dust in the headhouse of my elevator outside Holdrege, Nebraska. Curtis’s hazard was corn moving. Wade’s is corn dust standing still, banked above a live belt, because emptying it costs housekeeping overtime that prices to $0.004 a bushel. I keep the $0.004. The forearms lost that exchange, and nobody’s asked me to reopen it. Suspended grain dust only needs a spark. An elevator outside Wichita lost 7 men to its dust in 1998, and I read that report the way Freeman reads a proxy: as the owners’ business.

In 2021 the bucket belt gave Wade a static flash. It took his eyebrows and the top layer of both forearms. The forearms healed. I call the banked dust a standing inventory, by which I mean the flour-fine residue I decided not to pay to move. Wade wears sleeves now. The sleeves are my fix.

You’re getting good at this. Her heel is outside the cost column, and the fare is inside. His ribs are outside the cost column, and the harness is inside. Sentiment is a hobby for people who have never priced a heel. It will serve you well.

The column carries another blessing. Wayne Crews of the Competitive Enterprise Institute reports that the Trump administration has produced the fewest new federal rules since record-keeping began in the 1970s. The count flatters the administration because much of it erases burdens imposed by predecessors. I had it read aloud at the safety meeting.

Freeman wants due process and a presumption of innocence for executives, and they should have them. Shareholders are legitimate because they put their hard-earned money at risk. Sit with that test, because it’s better than he knows. My divers put lungs and ribs at risk every morning and get, in return, a schedule. The chief executive gets a presumption of innocence.

Curtis gets a punch clock.

Freeman warns that no phone call should settle who runs a company, that owners may want a change in 10 years or 10 minutes. My 10 minutes run daily at the scale house, and I’ve never lost one.

The law underneath is older than Iowa, and everything above it is just the newest printing of an old document. Ownership has always been the whole franchise. The worker’s vote appears nowhere in the record because the worker isn’t a party to it; he is the thing being governed. George Pullman’s company made the perimeter wider. It put the house, store, water, and street inside the company town. I’ve improved the design. He owned the street; I let the municipality own the road. Richard Furman supplied the older warrant, holding that ownership rested on Holy writ and was compatible with Christian uprightness. The oldest governance language in the building says it plainly:

> “And if a man smite his servant, or his maid, with a rod, and he die under his hand; he shall be surely punished. Notwithstanding, if he continue a day or two, he shall not be punished: for he is his money.”
>
> — Exodus 21:20–21 (King James Version)

For he is his money. Moses filed Freeman’s column 3,000 years early and buried the entire theory in the last clause. If the body is the owner’s money, the owner’s decision is final in every room the body enters. A single amendment has passed since: who pays to keep the body fed between shifts. I arranged that amendment in your favor, which is to say mine.

Freeman’s premise is risk. Shareholders risk money; owners decide. Noemí risks her heel; she receives no vote. The law calls the first capital and the second weather.

Why does Noemí have no vote? Because her labor is on my schedule, not her balance sheet. Capital’s property perimeter ends where the employer’s clock ends. Inside it, the firm owns the thread, the machine, the minute of sewing, and the cloth when it leaves the line. Outside it, the worker owns the walk. We call this Directional Transport Efficiency.

My arrangement is more efficient because I don’t have to call Noemí property. I own the factory; she owns the walk. The legal fiction is cleaner. The margin is better.

Does refusing the ride lower the wage? Of course. Every employer that leaves the fare off the ledger lowers wages by the fare avoided. That makes the shirt cheaper to you and reduces the wages available to buy one. Whatever demand disappears has to appear somewhere else. I don’t carry an elsewhere line.

The order book is full.

Noemí still walks the same 10 miles. Her heel opens on the 1st mile. I’ve scheduled the inbound bus for 6:00.

The bus does not return.

Every dollar I save on harnesses and housekeeping is a dollar Curtis doesn’t earn. A dollar he doesn’t earn is a dollar the grocery in Carroll never sees. I’ve never needed to know where those dollars went. The bins are full this fall, and I like them full.

The cornmeal is $2.79 this week. Thank you for your business; I mean that with real warmth. Curtis’s family shops the discount rack, where my seconds go.

The shirt sells.

The books balance.

The owners decide, exactly as Freeman says, in 10 years or 10 minutes.

Mine take less.
