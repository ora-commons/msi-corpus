Scott Turner's HUD is evicting American children over their parents' papers.

The Department of Housing and Urban Development has a serious administrative case. Section 214 of the Housing and Community Development Act of 1980 restricted federal housing assistance to citizens and specified categories of eligible noncitizens. HUD's strongest case begins with that text. Congress never repealed the restriction, the 1996 proration mechanism accommodated it without nullifying it, and an agency administering the statute may conclude that an accommodation the law does not require has to end. Agencies sometimes reverse their own prior practice. “HUD's final rule fully implements the 1980 Housing and Community Development Act and ends the exploitation of taxpayer dollars,” Secretary Scott Turner said. The rule would reallocate up to $218 million to eligible Americans on waiting lists. On the agency's account, this is existing law enforced, taxpayer money protected, and scarce assistance returned to people entitled to receive it. That theory is recognizable.

Congress says otherwise. The 1996 provision is an act of Congress, not administrative grace: it keeps mixed-status families housed on prorated assistance. An executive agency does not “fully implement” the 1980 Act by ignoring the 1996 amendment to it. The attorneys general put the structural point in one sentence: “Federal financial assistance is not a slush fund that executive agencies can redirect at will to the groups they favor.” Congress's goal, as their complaint reads it, is “a decent home and suitable living environment for all American families,” language echoing the Housing Act of 1949. Under the Administrative Procedure Act—the statute governing federal rulemaking—courts set aside agency action that is “arbitrary, capricious, an abuse of discretion, or otherwise not in accordance with law.” 5 U.S.C. § 706(2)(A). Arbitrary-and-capricious review asks whether the agency considered the relevant evidence and gave a rational explanation for its result. HUD invokes the 1980 Act to announce full-family exclusion. The plaintiffs identify the counter-record: the 1996 prorating regime and prior regulations that allowed mixed-status families to remain. An agency may enforce Congress's judgment. It may not replace that judgment with its own and call the replacement implementation.

Three challenges now run against the rule in the federal district court in Massachusetts. Tenant advocates and residents filed a class action first. Twenty-two state attorneys general [went to court on Monday](/articles/2026-11-23-states-sue-hud-over-housing-aid-rule-affecting-mixed-status-families/), arguing that HUD cannot reverse nearly thirty years of federal policy by regulation. Boston, its housing authority, and the public housing authorities of New York, San Francisco and Chicago filed their own challenge. The tenants add Fifth Amendment claims: equal protection, the guarantee against unjustified classifications, and due process, the guarantee of fair notice and a reasonable verification process. They seek an order declaring the rule unlawful, striking it down and blocking enforcement.

Scale makes the departure concrete. The lawsuits estimate that 20,000 mixed-status families, totaling 78,100 people, could lose housing assistance. The state prosecutors say 55,000 of those people are fully eligible for federal housing assistance, many of them children and seniors. In Massachusetts, about 910 households could be affected. Boston officials put the local number at more than 500 families.

One class-action plaintiff is a Massachusetts mother of four. She has lived in the country for 15 years and is authorized to live and work in Massachusetts, but her immigration status is not among those HUD deems eligible. She lives in a Section 8 apartment with her four children. All four are 12 or younger, and three are U.S. citizens. Under the rule as challenged, the family loses its entire subsidy. The complaint says the children cannot live independently from their mother. The family faces eviction and homelessness. This is not a paperwork dispute. It is a housing loss manufactured by a status rule.

Boston Housing Authority administrator Kenzie Bok put the local consequence plainly. “Almost every family we house at the Boston Housing Authority has previously suffered through a period of homelessness,” she said. “These are Boston kids, they are eligible for federal housing in their own right, and they deserve to stay safely in their homes.”

The constitutional litigation runs into a limit the Supreme Court set in 1972. *Lindsey v. Normet*, 405 U.S. 56 (1972), held that the Constitution does not guarantee housing as a fundamental right. In plain terms, Congress may create a statutory housing program without constitutionalizing every entitlement within it. Congress declared a national housing policy in 1949; the Court declined to constitutionalize that commitment in 1972; Turner's rule occupies the gap between the two. That makes the constitutional claims harder. It does not enlarge HUD's statutory power. A government may deny a benefit it need not provide. It may not deny a benefit Congress created by announcing that an older statute always meant what the program denied for almost thirty years.

The equal-protection claim runs into a separate gap between discriminatory effect and discriminatory purpose. Under *Village of Arlington Heights v. Metropolitan Housing Development Corp.*, 429 U.S. 252 (1977), a rule's harsher effect on one group does not by itself establish a constitutional violation; a plaintiff must show discriminatory purpose. A facially neutral rule burdening a population the government itself segregated is precisely where the intent requirement makes a claim difficult to reach. That population's history is not a natural fact. Richard Rothstein's *The Color of Law* documents how federal underwriting, highway construction and segregated public-housing siting confined Black families as de jure policy—lawful public action, not merely private discrimination. The new rule compounds that record with a status screen.

An honest application of Congress's scheme would keep mixed-status families housed and prorate assistance for their eligible members. If Congress wanted categorical exclusion, it could change the statute; HUD cannot convert the program through a notice that says a family must prove every member's status or lose the assistance that eligible members would receive. If HUD believes the 1996 scheme exceeds the 1980 text, the constitutional route runs through Congress by statute, not a rule that would turn 55,000 fully eligible people out of their homes.

The enforcement burden extends beyond those who may lose assistance. All 8.8 million tenants covered by the rule would have to document their status. That is a review burden, not a loss count, and it should not be blurred with the 20,000 families plaintiffs say could lose assistance. Annette Duke, a senior housing attorney at the Massachusetts Law Reform Institute, says the burden falls disproportionately on people of color, women, children and people with disabilities. A government can call its form neutral. The distribution of the work is not.

The rule is set to take effect on December 14. That does not mean benefits vanish the next morning. Housing authorities would have about a month to begin sending notices, and families would then have until March 14 to verify every family member's eligibility. Local agencies are already [working through the compliance timetable](/articles/2026-11-24-local-housing-agencies-work-to-comply-with-hud-rule-while-minimizing-evictions/), while tenant organizers are telling families not to panic. Zafiro Patiño, director of organizing for the Massachusetts Union of Public Housing Tenants, said: “Do not sign anything yet. The law still is the same, so do not panic.” Some authorities are already sending notices anyway. The first deadline HUD has imposed is a deadline for fear.

The exploitation framing also collides with the Department's own arithmetic. HUD estimates that sheltering people displaced by the rule would cost state and local governments between $20,000 and $50,000 per person each year. The federal ledger reallocates up to $218 million. State and local budgets bear the shelter costs. That is cost-shifting at scale. The reallocation also removes its advertised beneficiaries: 55,000 of the people who could lose assistance are fully eligible for it. Turner said the rule “guarantees ONLY AMERICAN citizens and certain eligible noncitizens will reside in HUD-funded housing.” The people left outside that guarantee include American children.

That displacement would land in a market whose racial geography was made by federal policy. Rothstein's *The Color of Law* documents how federal underwriting, highway construction and segregated public-housing siting confined Black families. The rule compounds that record with a new status screen. Matthew Desmond documented the eviction mechanism years ago: “Eviction is a cause, not just a condition, of poverty.” HUD's rule would push families out of subsidized housing and into a market that the tenants' complaint describes as already desperately short of affordable units. A housing rule can be legally dressed as status administration while operating as a machine for producing homelessness. The city plaintiffs call it “callous, ill-conceived, and illegal.” The record calls it displacement policy.

Turner said earlier this month that the rule reflects that “the federal government is not good at providing housing” and that long-term subsidies are “not the way that it's intended.” The rule does not end long-term subsidies. It makes mixed-status families lose assistance so other households can receive it. The federal government's largest housing program is the tax code, and it appears nowhere in this rule. Matthew Desmond's accounting in *Poverty, by America* found that in 2008 homeowners received $171 billion in tax benefits while direct housing assistance amounted to $40.2 billion. The homeowner subsidy is never called exploitation of taxpayer dollars, is never subjected to a citizenship audit, and reaches households without a housing-agency inspection. The subsidized tenant gets the paperwork. The subsidized owner gets the deduction.

This rule is the housing front of a documented pattern. In 2025, the administration dismissed the federal consent decrees over the Minneapolis and Louisville police departments, terminated $27 billion in Greenhouse Gas Reduction Fund grants and moved to rescind the 2023 Community Reinvestment Act rule. It has also carried its contests with the courts to the emergency docket—the track where orders issue without full merits briefing. The Brennan Center counted 34 emergency applications from this administration through the end of 2025, with the government prevailing in 80 percent of them. Stephen Vladeck's documented conclusion about that record was that “the more you look at the body of work, the more it looks like the best explanation for when the court is intervening and when it's not, is partisan politics and not neutral substantive legal principles.” That is the landscape this litigation runs against. No emergency application appears in the record yet. Nothing has been decided. Whether an agency may reverse a congressionally amended scheme by regulation is genuinely contested, and a district judge could credit the Department. The record is not contestable.

The rule has not yet completed an eviction. It has built the legal machinery for one.

Turner calls this a reallocation of up to $218 million. The reallocation turns 55,000 fully eligible people out of the homes Congress's scheme kept them in, many of them children and seniors, because of whose papers their parents hold. Scott Turner's HUD is not ending the exploitation of taxpayer dollars. It is breaking families apart and evicting American children from the housing Congress built for every American family. The agency conducting the eviction is the one charged with providing it.
