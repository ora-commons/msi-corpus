Trump and congressional Republicans drained the budget on purpose so nobody can fix your bills. Every other sentence in the fiscal conversation is arranged to avoid saying that one, and it is the only one the receipts support. The Democrats who swept in last week ran on rebuilding the safety net — childcare, health coverage, food assistance, an emphatic endorsement of Medicare for All, the Medicaid and food-stamp cuts rolled back, the subsidies Republicans let expire restored — the programs that decide whether a household like mine can breathe. They have already begun [setting their post-election priorities on affordability and accountability](/articles/2026-11-04-democrats-set-post-election-priorities-on-affordability-and-accountability/). Then the room went quiet, because the line everywhere now is that the wish list has run into harsh fiscal reality: there is no money. There is money. What there is not is $5.5 trillion, and the withdrawal slips are signed.

I know the phrase "there is no money" from the inside, because I have said a version of it to myself. In March 2022 I sat at my kitchen table at eleven at night with the spreadsheet open and ran the math until the columns refused to close: $8,800 net a month for four of us, $2,400 gone to daycare before a single utility bill, a 7 percent mortgage that three years of refinancing inquiries never moved. The math did not add up to the household my parents ran in Lansdale on one postal salary, and I did the thing the spreadsheet never asks you to do — I stopped treating the shortfall as a verdict on myself and went looking for where the money went. At my table the answer was structural: housing prices, childcare prices, wages. At the federal table it is names, dates, and line items. (And before anyone grades the arithmetic for bias: the down payment came from David's grandmother's estate, our two-income shape carries the tax benefits that shape always carries, and my parents filled out our financial-aid paperwork. I am describing my kitchen, not every kitchen.)

Here is the federal version of the table, every line item face up. The deficit is running at 6.2 percent of GDP. The debt passed $40 trillion, more than 120 percent of the whole economy. Interest alone costs roughly $1 trillion a year — 3.2 percent of the economy, more than the entire defense budget, about $7,700 per household per year once you split the trillion across roughly 130 million households — paid to lenders before a road gets fixed or a clinic gets staffed. October alone, one month, posted a $409 billion deficit. The Congressional Budget Office projects debt and interest outgrowing the economy for decades. None of that is a feelings problem, and I am not going to tell you it isn't real — I have sat at the table and learned the difference between a budget problem and a feelings problem. Now the line item the "no money" verdict keeps leaving out: Trump's One Big Beautiful Bill Act adds somewhere between $4.1 trillion and $5.5 trillion to the debt over ten years, and it paid for part of its tax cuts by cutting Medicaid and food stamps. The account did not run dry. It was emptied, by named people, on the record, for purposes stated in the bill text. You do not get to add $5 trillion and then announce the pantry is bare.

The engineering has a paper trail, and one of the men who helped draw the blueprint says so plainly. Bruce Bartlett, who advised Ronald Reagan on budget policy, has spent years describing the strategy: cut taxes, let the debt compound, and use the shortfall to handcuff whoever governs next — a design, in his words, to "make sure the next Democratic president is a complete and total failure." The record backs him. The last government with a Republican anywhere near power to raise taxes was George H.W. Bush's in 1990; he lost reelection for it, and that was the end of that. Clinton's 1993 increases built the first surplus since the 1960s, worth about $3.3 trillion in today's terms over the coming decade, and a successor spent it on tax cuts. The ACA raised about $1.1 trillion's worth — and Obama, for the ledger's honesty, also made some Bush cuts permanent. Biden's House passed $2.3 trillion of new revenue in Build Back Better; the Senate authorized about $700 billion. Then the One Big Beautiful Bill Act stacked $4.1 trillion to $5.5 trillion of new debt on top. The deficit is not one party's alone — the pandemic spent too — but the strategy of cutting revenue to disarm the other side has a named author and an on-record quote. Annie Lowrey has been saying the plain version for years: poverty in the United States is a choice, the issue is not that the country cannot pull its people above the poverty line but that it does not want to, and safety-net failure is not weather but decisions by named people at named moments. The people have names. The moments have dates.

Heather McGhee gave this pattern its cleanest American image: the public pool. St. Louis's Fairground Park recorded 313,000 swims the summer before integration; ten thousand the integrated summer after; closed for good in 1956 rather than stay shared. The same mechanics ran through higher education — state appropriations swapped for tuition right as public universities desegregated, so that Black borrowers a dozen years out of college owe more than they originally borrowed while their white peers have paid down principal. Fund the public thing while it serves one constituency; drain it the moment it has to serve everybody. My parents' pipeline — parish school, Catholic high school, a public university a Pell Grant actually paid most of — ran on a full pool. The pool my kids were assigned was drained before either of them was born, on purpose.

Now the repair bill, which is what "the price of the promised social contract" turns out to be. The Center on Budget and Policy Priorities has priced it: $460 billion to restore the Affordable Care Act subsidies that were allowed to lapse, and $2.2 trillion over the decade to undo the Medicaid and SNAP cuts. The harm was cheaper than the cure, because the harm was a withdrawal, and withdrawals get spent. And the largest item is not even on anyone's wish list: the Social Security trust fund runs out in under six years — inside the first term of the people just elected — at which point the law cuts benefits automatically by 22 percent. That number has a face in my house. My father retired from the Postal Service in 2019 after 38 years, and a 22 percent cut doesn't trim his check; it deletes the Wildwood week, then the grocery line. We are among the lucky ones — he has a federal pension stacked alongside Social Security, and a lot of households his age don't. That is not a proposal from either party. It is the scheduled consequence of four decades of choosing tax cuts over solvency, landing on households that structured everything around a promise the budget was quietly emptied from underneath.

The expiring tax cuts tell you who was always meant to be the customer. The tips exemption, the overtime exemption, the seniors' deduction, the SALT bump — extending them costs about $1.2 trillion, and CBPP's finding is blunt: next to nothing in the package reaches the bottom rungs of the income distribution. My household ledger contains exactly zero lines labeled "tax relief," and $1.2 trillion is more than twice what it costs to put the ACA subsidies back. The money exists. It has been committed, repeatedly, on the record, to the households that needed it least. A tax cut calibrated to people paid by the hour with a clock visible, while the children SNAP was feeding get nothing, is a budget that has sorted people into two kinds of poor. That is the discrimination Dorothy Day said the Gospel takes away our right to make: the deserving poor at the tip jar, the undeserving ones cut from the program.

"No money" is also a claim about what can be collected, and the international table answers it. Total US tax revenue sits under 26 percent of GDP — nine points below Britain, nine and a half below Canada, twelve and a half below Germany, sixteen below Sweden. Leo XIII wrote in 1891 that wages ought not to be insufficient to support a frugal and well-behaved wage-earner: in that tradition a wage is supposed to carry a household, and a polity that will not carry its households isn't broke, it's choosing.

And the catalog that would be bought with the choice is real and costed: the expanded Child Tax Credit lifted 4.3 million children out of poverty the year it ran; the childcare plan the House passed and the Senate walked away from would have saved families $5,000 to $6,500 a year across 32 states; the paid-leave compromise the right-of-center and left-of-center economists actually agreed on — eight weeks at 70 percent wage replacement — was sitting finished on the table. None of it lacks a price. The price lacks a coalition willing to say it out loud. The honest program is a list with numbers, not a mood: end the expiring tax cuts and bank the $1.2 trillion; restore the $460 billion in ACA subsidies; schedule the Medicaid and SNAP restoration before the automatic Social Security trigger fires instead of after; pass the paid leave and the childcare credit. Those items are already sitting on [the affordability agenda now taking shape](/articles/2026-11-09-democrats-prepare-affordability-agenda-after-the-midterm-win/). What is missing is the sentence about where the money comes from.

"You're on your own, kid" has been the working mission statement of American care infrastructure for as long as I have been paying for any of it, and this budget is that sentence with dollar figures attached: the deficit argument's actual content is you're on your own. The electorate just went to the polls and declined the sentence, and the fiscal pages answered with two words: no money. The song's one mercy is the lateral net — the other mothers in the group text, showing up anyway — and I know those group texts, and they are love, and love is not a fiscal policy.

The receipts say otherwise. The money existed, was taken by named people on named dates for stated purposes, and can be collected back from the same place. A 22 percent cut to Social Security is my parents' Wildwood week, gone. There is no money — the way there was no money for the tax cuts, either. Rule out the sabotage, or retract the premise. Then fund the week.
