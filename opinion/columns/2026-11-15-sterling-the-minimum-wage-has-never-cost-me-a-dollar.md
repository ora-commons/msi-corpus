Ouida is a fold on the sachet line of the packaging plant I own outside Batesville, Arkansas. A fold is the pair of hands that seals the sample sachet you tore open in a hotel bathroom last month. Her rate file reads:

Standard output, nondisabled survey: 60 folds per minute
Ouida, measured: 19 folds per minute
Commensurate rate: $7.25 × (19 ÷ 60) = $2.30 an hour
Federal floor: $7.25
Paid by me, ever: $0.00
Retained differential: $4.95 an hour

Also filed this week: Michael Saltsman argues in ["Hiking the Minimum Wage Isn't Affordable"](https://www.wsj.com/opinion/hiking-the-minimum-wage-isnt-affordable-cee17e64?mod=rss_opinion) for the *Wall Street Journal* that a national $20 mandate would cost 2.3 million jobs, that a 10% wage increase pushes food prices as much as 4% higher, that rent climbs as much as 7.3% behind it, and that California voters turned down an $18 floor for the first time in the state's history because they could feel the 14% on the menu. I agree with every alarming word of it. I've been hoping for it. You'll pay for it either way. You opened the sachet. I kept the differential. The poor are always with us. Payroll simply organizes them.

The floor lives in Section 6 of the Fair Labor Standards Act. The door I walk through lives in Section 14(c). Same act. Same ink, 1938. Congress set the first minimum at $0.25 an hour and, in the same bill, excused from it the workers it described as impaired in earning capacity by physical or mental deficiency. Sheltered from the floor on the day the floor was poured. My certificate renews under that provision, and it authorizes me to pay by the piece at a rate the regulations call *commensurate*. Commensurate, by which I mean a time study: the regulation has me measure her against a benchmark worker without the label, and the gap becomes my discount. I keep the regulation's word because it survives an audit. The honest phrasing is that I priced her hands against a faster woman and kept the difference, and that phrasing makes a loan officer slow down over the signature page.

Her stub came to $2.30 an hour last Friday. National reporting has documented certificates paying out in pennies an hour; I'm not that generous. A full day of her hands runs me $18.40, which is what I tipped a valet in Nashville last month, and I thought no more of one than the other. Her thumbs are worn past their prints from the glue gun. The sachets seal anyway. The Lord made the week in 7 days and left the rate to me.

Mr. Saltsman, you're right that the federal floor is already irrelevant. It's $7.25, it's been $7.25 since 2009, and 0.1% of your hourly workforce still earns it. It's irrelevant to me because I never argued with it. I price the sachet, not the hour. You also warn the country about the operator who can least afford the $20 floor: the man across the river who runs 11 people and no attorney. I'll take him off your hands. He raises his price, loses the account to me, and closes in March. Your $20 is my market share.

Same principle, different building. Cleo is a folder on the gluer line of the box plant I own outside Waterloo, Iowa. A folder feeds corrugated blanks into the machine with both hands, 10 hours a night, 5 nights, and every box your November order arrived in came off that line.

The interlock on Cleo's trimmer was quoted to me at $0.003 a box across the run, and I keep the $0.003. $1,900, once, in 2019. The quote sat on my desk for a month, then it went in the drawer, and I haven't gone back for it. The trimmer squares the box after the glue sets. It's a blade that comes down while the folder's hands are still in the throat of the machine, and an interlock stops the blade when the light breaks. I've filed it as *input continuity*. The binder is what the brand's safety auditor reads when he awards the January volume, and the honest version — a machine that keeps cutting while a hand sits in it — takes my page out of that binder.

In February the blade took 2 fingers off Cleo's left hand.

The blade took 2 fingers. She came back in 9 days.

The run needed her. She counts the blanks by feel now.

You've opened a box this month. You didn't look at the flap.

Let me put the numbers where you can see them. Cleo makes $16.40 an hour, above every floor ever written. The floor can go to $20 tomorrow. My cost per box moves $0.019. Yours moves $0.04. Nobody between this plant and your kitchen table has ever asked who keeps the gap.

I dropped the second packaging shift in August. The 14 men off it bought the things that come in my boxes. I haven't followed one of them to find out where he buys now. The order book is the fullest it's been since 2019.

Different surface, identical arithmetic. Lorene is a tip credit at the diner I hold outside Blytheville, and the floor that can't reach Ouida stops lower still for her: the tipped minimum has sat at $2.13 since 1991, which means the law has priced her mornings below the cost of her own coffee for 35 years. The tips are your payroll department. You leave $3 on the table and drive off feeling generous; I book a labor hour at $2.13. When a Tuesday rain empties the booths and the tips come up short, the statute says I owe her the gap to $7.25. Ask Lorene whether I have ever once written that check. Between the withholding on her reported tips and the $2.13, her last paper check was $41.67 for 2 weeks, and she made it stretch, because that is what these doors were cut for.

You should know what you're reading, because "employer" flatters the arrangement. I'm the faculty. The commensurate-wage survey is a craft I've taught for 30 years: benchmark rate, measured output, proration in the employer's favor, which the regulations invite. The chapter is assigned in the certification courses the sheltered-workshop industry runs for its operators. My nineteenth Warden seminar graduated in May. One of those students sets the wage band at the fulfillment center that receives your box, and by October he runs the subtraction ahead of me. I've never marked a faster one. Men and women I graded are certifying discounts across 400 counties this quarter, and one of them priced the workshops nearest your ZIP code. You haven't met a monster. You've met a syllabus.

Lift your eyes off her thumb for one sentence; the statute is the thing I own here. The minimum wage was never a wall. It was a floor poured with doors in it: the sheltered certificate in 1938, the tip credit in 1966, the student rate, the training wage. Every amendment that raised the wall carved the doors a little deeper, because the doors are how the wall passed. And the wage floor isn't a floor either. It's a gate. It decides which firms may bid for a body and which may not, and I've never once been on the wrong side of a gate. Your legislators debate the number. My tradition debated the man, and it got there first:

> "One of the very first requirements for a man who is fit to handle pig iron as a regular occupation is that he shall be so stupid and so phlegmatic that he more nearly resembles in his mental make-up the ox than any other type."
>
> — Frederick Winslow Taylor, *The Principles of Scientific Management*, 1911

That is a specification, not an insult. He priced the man before he priced the hour. The bill prices only the hour.

And notice who pays on the rare occasions the wall does apply. The Government Accountability Office reported in 2020 that in most of the 15 states it studied, Walmart and the other big low-wage chains sat among the top employers of households enrolled in SNAP and Medicaid. The floor fixes your maintenance bill, not my costs. You are the counterparty. Scripture ran the same liability arithmetic and settled it in one clause: Exodus 21:20–21 doesn't forbid the rod. It prices the recovery period — *for he is his money.* It has been doing that longer than the language has held a word for wage. Ouida's wrists are that clause at $2.30 an hour. Mine while they fold. Discounted while they mend. Replaced when they stop.

Congress will argue about the $20 and pass something smaller, and the smaller thing will pass me by on its way to you. When the floor moves, the certificates re-price the same week, and my discount climbs with the wall it stands behind. Every dollar the floor fails to reach is a dollar her town never spends, and my packaging contracts are running the best volume they've seen in 6 years. I haven't asked my forecasters which of my discounts is subtracting from it. The 2027 survey tightened the standard to 64 folds a minute. Ouida's new rate came out at $2.15. I initialed the sheet Friday afternoon.

Back to the line, because the law doesn't live in 1911. Cleo runs the gluer one-handed, and *input continuity* has never been higher. The line didn't slow down. I appreciate your November order. The flaps on it were folded by a hand with 9 fingers in it, and not one carton in the stack mentions that.

Ouida folds until the glue takes the last ridge off her right thumb, and the intake van brings the next fold, because a certificate doesn't retire a worker; it re-prices her. Your sachet is free at the sink. The fold inside it cost me $0.002 and cost her the ridges on both thumbs, and the numbers we each keep live in different ledgers. Only mine gets audited. Raise the floor. The doors move with it. I have never paid the minimum wage, and by the time Congress finishes arguing, I'll still be able to say so.

The floor goes up in January. The interlock stays off.
