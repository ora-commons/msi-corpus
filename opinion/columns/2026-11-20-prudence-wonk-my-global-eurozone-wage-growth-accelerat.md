The ECB compounds the war shock by extracting workers’ purchasing power.

Here are the numbers. The ECB’s Nov. 20 negotiated-wage data release says wages settled by labor unions and comparable bargaining groups rose 2.57% from a year earlier in the three months through September, up from 2.45% in the three months through June. That series measures collective bargaining, not every worker’s pay or total compensation. Eurozone inflation reached 4% in October, up from 1.9% in February, when the U.S.-Iran war began. The first number measures what workers negotiated. The second measures what they had to pay.

The European Commission’s current euro-area forecast separates real compensation per worker from compensation per worker. It estimates inflation-adjusted compensation per worker will rise 0.4% this year, down from 1.8% in 2025. That is an aggregate loss of purchasing-power growth, not a forecast that real pay falls: the growth rate shrinks by 1.4 percentage points. The aggregate figures are not a distribution table for households, but they show how little purchasing power the workforce recovered after the energy-price shock. For next year, the Commission expects compensation per worker to rise 3.5%, with real wages rising at a similarly slow pace. The energy shock has taken more of household purchasing power; the forecast says workers are not taking much of it back.

Three steps are documented. The war raised energy prices. The ECB did not cause that first-round increase; it chose the policy response. In its June 2026 and Sept. 10, 2026 monetary-policy decisions, the ECB raised its policy rate—the rate used to steer borrowing and spending; [the September decision put it at 2.5%](/articles/2026-09-10-ecb-lifts-rate-to-2-5-projects-3-inflation-this-year/). The first step belongs to the war. The second belongs to the ECB. The third records where the adjustment landed in aggregate. The extraction lies in the policy division: monetary restraint suppresses demand while households bear the energy bill.

Plainly: no second-round effects does not mean workers have kept pace with prices already in force. “What we don’t see at the moment is second-round effects, not yet,” Bank of France Governor Emmanuel Moulin said. Second-round effects are the danger that workers bargain higher energy costs into wages, firms pass the higher wage bill into prices, and the original shock keeps reproducing itself. No second-round effects is not a finding about household welfare.

The ECB has made the absence of that danger carry a claim it cannot support: monetary-policy success for households whose purchasing power has already been cut. The negotiated-wage series shows whether price increases are propagating into wages; it cannot show whether workers were compensated for the first round. This is intellectual laundering in the precise sense: a respectable technical metric made to carry a distributional result it cannot bear. The test for second-round effects is not the test for whether the policy compensated workers for the shock. The metric can pass while households absorb the first-round loss.

Governments should finance targeted household relief and secure energy supplies; the ECB should prevent the initial shock from spreading into prices. Fiscal authority carries the shock; monetary authority prevents the second round. The correction is procedural: the ECB should publish inflation-adjusted compensation and household energy outlays by income group beside the negotiated-wage series, and should separate the first-round shock from persistent wage-price effects in every forecast. Its own success test must ask whether workers are being compensated for the energy shock, not merely whether second-round effects are absent.

The record was 2.57% negotiated wage growth, 4% inflation, and a central-bank governor saying the second round had not begun. The receipt is precise. So is the laundering: the ECB has booked the absence of a wage-price spiral as monetary-policy success while households absorb the energy shock. The arithmetic is the arithmetic.
