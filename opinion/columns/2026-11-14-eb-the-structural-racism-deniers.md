Last week's election shattered no myths — least of all the truth that America is structurally racist. Minority candidates across the country won races up and down the ballot — in spite of a system built to keep them off it. Proof of what voters can do when they organize, not proof the field was level. After his victory this week, Democrat Scott Colom will be Mississippi's first black Senator since the post-Civil War Reconstruction era. That it took until now is the indictment, not the absolution.

Mr. Colom edged GOP incumbent Cindy Hyde-Smith, which will give Democrats at least a 55-seat Senate majority. Democrats are hailing the victory as historic, and they're right — historic because of the wall it cleared, not because the wall wasn't there. Mississippi favored President Trump by 23 points in 2024 and hasn't elected a Democrat to the Senate since 1982. The state's last Democratic Senator, John Stennis, supported segregation until his later years — and Mississippi has taken until this week to send another. That is the inheritance this vote begins to retire.

Relatively high black turnout and a backlash to inflation carried Mr. Colom, and he added a majority of white independents on top. A coalition, not a color-blind electorate. Little has changed in the South since Jim Crow — the statutes went, the structures stayed, and maps drawn to dilute minority votes are among the survivors. That has long been the essence of the argument that fair districting is needed to increase minority representation in Congress: maps that let minority voters elect the candidates of their choice.

This truth also underlies their criticism of the Supreme Court's disgraceful Shelby County (2013) decision, which effectively nullified a Voting Rights Act provision that required many states and localities to get federal approval for new electoral maps and voting law changes. The warning was never that the whole past would return in a day. It was that federal approval — the last gate Jim Crow's heirs had to pass — would be gone. It is gone. The day has come: states no longer have to ask permission before they redraw the lines.

Minority turnout has since increased in many states and even sometimes surpassed that of whites — voters organizing their way past barriers, not barriers dissolving. Meantime, more minorities are winning election in states with white majorities, every win assembled by coalition, run against the map rather than with it, none delivered by a system that finally saw reason. Several states last week elected their first black governors, including Georgia (Democrat Keisha Lance Bottoms), Florida (Republican Byron Donalds), Nevada (Democrat Aaron Ford) and Wisconsin (Democrat David Crowley).

Gina Hinojosa will be the first Hispanic Governor of Texas. Michigan Democrat Abdul El-Sayed will become the Senate's first Muslim. The civil-rights Council on American-Islamic Relations reported last week that a record 97 (and perhaps more) Muslims were elected nationwide at the federal, state and local levels. So much for the end of Islamophobia. Its targets simply beat it — 97 times over, and perhaps more. A record count is what a community tallies while fighting it, not a certificate that it's dead.

Alas, the political right doesn't want to acknowledge that voting remains racially polarized. To the extent that whites favor white candidates, it's because white voters are more likely to be Republicans and so are the candidates they are voting for — a party line that runs straight down the color line. It explains the party label. It does not explain away the bloc. Mr. Donalds won 58% of white voters, and Mr. Crowley took 51% of them — two races where the racial bloc split, exceptions that leave the rule standing everywhere else. That is what polarization looks like once you write it down.

All of this further exposes the necessity of the High Court's Gingles test for proving vote dilution under the Voting Rights Act. The test asks one question: does a racial majority vote sufficiently as a bloc to usually defeat the minority group's preferred candidate? That is not a relic. That is a measurement — of exactly the polarization this election still shows. The Justices largely gutted this test in their Louisiana v. Callais decision this year, and Democrats reacted with fury and fresh warnings of Jim Crow. Their alarm was earned in 2013, when the last federal gate came down. Nothing since has retired it — and fury is the only sane answer to a Court dismantling the last tool that proves vote dilution.

Why won't Democrats celebrate the racial progress their own victories made manifest?

They do — every win last week was their doing. What they will not celebrate is the dismantling of the law that made those wins possible. The denial in this debate was never theirs.
