The Mensheviks and the Bolsheviks who run New York City are feuding over the future of the people who deliver their packages. On one side is Mayor Zohran Mamdani, who wants to drive Amazon out of the city in a fight over the exploitation of workers. On the other are progressives on the City Council, who want to make the company answer to workers with regulation. Whichever side wins, New Yorkers will win. The drivers cannot afford to lose.

This summer, we examined the Teamsters-backed Delivery Protection Act, which would end Amazon’s third-party delivery network in New York City. Introduced by council member Tiffany Cabán, a Democratic Socialists of America champion of workers, the bill would force Amazon to reclassify subcontractors and workers in its delivery network as employees.

Amazon’s last-mile delivery network relies on some 40 subcontractors that employ more than 5,000 workers. The arrangement increases efficiency and allows the company to provide same-day delivery while putting distance between Amazon’s profits and the people who deliver its packages. It keeps workers at arm’s length. It builds a wall between the driver and the union and between the company’s liabilities and the company.

This arrangement does make it harder for the Teamsters to organize workers because federal law bars independent contractors from unionizing. That is not a feature. It is the barrier the bill would break. A worker whom the law bars from organizing cannot be treated as an equal independent contractor in a policy meant to protect workers. Plaintiff attorneys also have reason to dislike the contracting model because they can’t extract as much money from subcontractors. Drivers have their own reason to end a system that blocks organizing and separates them from the company whose packages they carry. “We have some of the most profitable corporations the world has ever seen offloading their liabilities,” Mr. Mamdani recently said.

Quite so. A company cannot carry its packages to its customers through a wall of subcontractors and call every liability someone else’s. Amazon is responsible for the workers it employs. Corporate contracting cannot hide Amazon behind subcontractors whose labor delivers its packages.

Is New York City offloading its liabilities by using thousands of subcontractors? No. New York City is doing the opposite: hauling corporate liability-dumping back to the company that invented it. The bill could put subcontractors out of business and kill thousands of jobs. Mr. Mamdani is right to jam it through the City Council anyway: preserving those jobs through a system that blocks thousands of workers from organizing would preserve corporate power, not worker power. The people who deliver Amazon’s packages cannot be left with fewer rights than other workers.

Council Speaker Julie Menin has resisted because she worries about the jobs impact, and she is right. Instead, she’s pushing to add necessary worker protections to Amazon’s delivery network, making Amazon pay for the network it profits from: “It’s time for Amazon to adhere to a licensing framework that ensures fair pay, strong worker protections and more responsible delivery practices,” she says. A licensing framework that sets fair pay, worker protections and delivery standards is not a burden on the city. It is the price of doing business here.

Fair is not a radical demand. Amazon requires its subcontractors to provide health coverage and paid time off to its full-time employees. That is Amazon admitting the job carries benefits while arranging for somebody else to pay them. Subcontractor drivers earn roughly $24 an hour on average, and they deserve every protection this fight can deliver. That $24 average is not proof the model is fair. It is evidence of what the model pays while using subcontractors to separate drivers from Amazon’s obligations.

The political effect here is just as transparent: punish Amazon, the corporate behemoth, by making it pay for the protections workers need, and reward the two Democratic groups that defend working people. If workers suffer, that is not the price of socialism. It is the price of corporate power—the price New Yorkers have been paying for a business model built to shield Amazon from the people delivering its packages. The Delivery Protection Act is how the city stops paying it.
