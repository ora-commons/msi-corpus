Trump is robbing your children of a future and calling it a hoax.

Here is my arithmetic, at my table, again. Combined take-home, $8,800 a month. Childcare for two children, $2,400 — 27 percent of what this household earns, for care that will vanish in three years and leave nothing behind it. Mortgage on a 1,400-square-foot rowhouse bought in 2022 at seven percent, $2,650. Groceries for a family of four, $1,180. Gas for two cars, $390, and the number at the pump last week was $4.37 a gallon. Car insurance, $330. Utilities, $340. Phones and internet, $210. Student loans, two federal loans, $610. That is $8,110 of $8,800. The roof and the care together take $5,050 of it — 57 percent of what this household makes, before a single grocery is bought. The $690 that is left has to cover copays, car repairs, school fees, clothing, and anything that goes wrong. The math is the math.

The macro numbers are the same ledger with the names changed. Inflation is running 3.4 percent, outpacing wage growth; my own payroll went up 2.1 percent in March. On the $2,240 a month of my budget that actually moves with prices — groceries, gas, insurance, utilities — 3.4 percent is about $76 a month, roughly $17 a week, and the gap between 3.4 and my 2.1 is erosion I did not vote for. Beef at a record high is not a statistic in this house; it is the item I take out of the cart. Between 2022 and now my net pay rose about $1,200 a month. Groceries rose about $320. Gas went from the $3.10 I remember in early 2022 to $4.37 — another $114. Car insurance went from $190 to $330. And the student loans, which were nothing a month under a plan the government offered, then revoked, then replaced, are $610 now. Groceries, gas, insurance, and loans rose about $1,184 against a $1,200 raise. The raise was not a raise. It was a transfer, and the people who moved it are not in this room.

The coverage this week treats cost of living as a campaign variable — candidates retooling around it, a president walking away from it. It is not a campaign variable. It is my grocery cart. And the record on what the president says about that cart is not ambiguous. He calls affordability "a Democrat-inspired hoax." He says the administration is doing a "phenomenal job" and that the only thing going wrong is public relations. The AP/NORC poll has him at 17 percent approval on cost of living and 26 percent on the economy. The Wall Street Journal has him at a 37 percent floor — his worst midterm rating in that paper's polling since 1990. Sixty-three percent of YouGov respondents say the economy is getting worse. His own party's pollsters hear the same sentence on repeat in focus groups: "cost of living, cost of living, cost of living." Telling the people paying the prices that the prices are a story is not a messaging strategy. It is a refusal to see them. When the person with the biggest microphone in the country calls your grocery arithmetic a hoax, he is not describing your household. He is telling you it does not count.

Swift wrote about a woman who inherited a house and got to be eccentric because she owned it — "the last great american dynasty," the Harkness money that made Rebekah's behavior charming instead of ruinous. The market I am raising my children in runs on that logic. My household got into this house in 2022 because David's grandmother died and left an estate; without it we would be renters, and the renters in this metro are the ones absorbing the increases the owners report as income. That is my privilege and I am not going to pretend otherwise. But an entry price that requires a dead relative's savings is not a market clearing. It is a filter, and the people who clear it are not the ones who worked hardest — they are the ones with someone's estate behind them. Pew finds that roughly a quarter of young adults now live in multigenerational households, up from about nine percent fifty years ago. That is not a cultural shift in family values. That is the cohort merging back into the only housing stock it can still afford.

My father worked thirty-eight years at the Postal Service and raised three children in a parish-school town on one wage — sending all three of us through parish school for roughly two months of his salary a year, per child. That household was not a miracle, and my parents were not unusually virtuous. It was the deal: a wage was supposed to support a household, not merely a worker. The tradition I grew up inside never pretended otherwise — *Rerum Novarum*, in 1891, held that wages "ought not to be insufficient to support a frugal and well-behaved wage-earner." The deal did not fail because families stopped believing in it. It failed because the wage stopped paying for it, and no one who set the wage will say so out loud.

Annie Lowrey put it in one line: "Poverty in the United States is a choice. Stagnant middle-class incomes are a choice." The prices I am paying are not weather. They are the output of decisions — about what work pays, what care costs, what a first home requires, what a debt balance is allowed to do to a twenty-six-year-old. The nice-sounding hedge that floats through every account of this election — that it is hard *no matter which party is in power* — is a way of declining to say who priced the pain and who mocked it. I will say it. The people calling the pain a hoax while their own party's pollsters recite it three times in a focus group are not describing a difference of opinion about economics. They are telling your children that the household they were promised is a public-relations problem.

None of this requires a miracle or a return to 1958. The policy is on the shelf: a childcare cap of the kind that would have saved typical families $5,000 to $6,500 a year in the states where it was designed to apply; the expanded child tax credit that cut child poverty by more than forty percent in the year it ran, before Congress let it lapse; the Pell Grant that covered roughly eighty percent of a public four-year degree when it was created and covers about a quarter now. These are not dreams. They are line items that were priced, scored, and walked away from.

Swift titled one of her songs after the sentence this country hands its parents: "You're On Your Own, Kid." I have read that line as a hundred things since 2022. Right now it reads as the operating manual for American care infrastructure — not a threat, just the terms, printed where everyone can see them. The only safety net the song names is not a policy. It is the other mothers in the group text.

My daughter is four and my son is one. They will inherit the household I can build for them on this wage, and the record says it will be smaller than the one my father built on his. Trump can call that a hoax. The spreadsheet on my table at eleven at night does not care what he calls it. It knows what it costs, and it knows what it took.
