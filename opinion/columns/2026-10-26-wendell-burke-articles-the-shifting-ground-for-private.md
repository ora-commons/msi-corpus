A private-equity CEO can do everything right and still be replaced, because the thing being managed is no longer the company; it is the owner’s missing exit. A preview of Challenger, Gray & Christmas’s September report counted 1,337 U.S. CEO departures year to date, down 19% from 1,650 in the same period in 2025. That apparent calm does not extend to private-equity-backed companies, where sponsors are holding unsold U.S. portfolio companies longer, as this newspaper’s coverage of [private equity’s record backlog of unsold portfolio companies](/articles/2026-09-04-private-equity-sits-on-record-backlog-of-unsold-us-portfolio-companies/) documented.

There is an honest case for replacing a chief executive when the exit has moved farther into the future. A sponsor who cannot sell a company must learn to run one for longer. Markets move, customers change, technology passes under the business, and a leader who was right about the old plan can become the obstacle in the new one. The company needs somebody who can still carry it.

That is the strongest honest version of the argument. More private-equity firms now factor how a prospective leader addresses or integrates AI into the business, including in hiring assessments. AI is the proxy; adaptability is the point. It matters even more now that [leadership trust has overtaken model trust as the leading AI-rollout barrier](/articles/2026-09-21-leadership-trust-overtakes-model-trust-as-top-ai-rollout-barrier/). A new leader who cannot bring employees along is not an upgrade. They are a more expensive version of the same problem.

But the longer hold does not merely change the job description. It changes who owns the mandate.

Laura Kreutzer, private-equity bureau chief for WSJ Pro, describes what follows: sponsors are “opting to swap out CEOs in the middle or even toward the end of their investment holding periods, a move that was much less common only a few years ago.” The scale is not anecdotal. Summit Leadership Partners surveyed 176 private-equity executives; 72% said they had brought in a new CEO for more than a quarter of their portfolio companies.

That is not ordinary executive churn. It is management being used to adjust the portfolio.

The old private-equity bargain was brutal but simple: deliver the operating plan, improve the asset, cash out. The new bargain is conditional: keep the company valuable, keep the leadership credible, and remain useful through a holding period whose length nobody can promise.

The exit has not disappeared. It has merely changed who must chase it.

When the buyer fails to arrive, the sponsor cannot move the calendar, so the pressure moves into the operating company and settles on its management team. A stale strategy can be made to look fresh by changing the operator. A tired leader can be made to look like the problem rather than the consequence of the prolonged hold. A company can be made more saleable by installing a face that promises a buyer a new beginning, as though the enterprise had not merely survived its owner’s impatience.

The PE-backed CEO does not operate under a mandate owned by herself. She operates under a mandate owned by someone else. If she is managing to a stale expectation, she can be right about the business and wrong about her job. So Kreutzer’s advice to CEOs is survival work: know what your sponsor expects, verify that understanding, and determine whether those expectations have changed. Good sponsors tend to be clear about what they want. The CEO’s mistake is assuming that clarity survives a longer and less predictable ownership period.

That is why the new mandate can be stated without euphemism: keep the exit alive. Keep the company credible through the drought. Keep the leadership adaptable. Keep the asset attractive to the buyer who has not appeared.

Longer holds also impose a human cost. Burnout has been cited as an important challenge associated with extended holding periods, and some CEOs are relieved to have the opportunity to step back. That is not weakness. It is the recognition that endurance has become part of the job description. But in the sponsor’s arithmetic, burnout does not create job security. It makes replacement more necessary.

The sponsor’s first move may be support rather than dismissal. Private-equity firms will bring in other resources before replacing a leader: a board member with particular expertise, an executive coach, perhaps another set of hands around a problem that has outgrown one executive. Summit Leadership Partners said its executive coaching engagements doubled in the past year or so. That assistance is not a decoration. It is one of the last humane mechanisms standing between a capable leader and replacement.

A capable CEO should ask for it while help can still change the outcome.

It cannot repair a leader who lacks capability or motivation. Kreutzer’s blunt assessment is worth preserving: “A bigger risk lies in keeping a CEO on board who isn’t capable or motivated.” Here is the hidden trap of the long hold: retention is not always an endorsement. It may be the sponsor postponing the admission that the asset—and the person running it—needs a new plan. Continued employment is not a medal. It may only be the waiting period before the right moment to change the plan.

The strongest PE-backed CEOs will make the company’s expectations explicit, build adaptability into the operating model before it is demanded, and leave the asset easier to value and easier to sell. They will treat AI credibility as a management responsibility rather than a marketing campaign. They will accept help while help can still change the outcome. None of that rescues the bargain. It only makes the operator more valuable before the operator becomes disposable.

My own conservative tradition has ample reason to distrust the concentrated manager and the distant expert. It has less reason to confuse mobility with loyalty, or cash flow with an institution. A company is not merely a line item waiting for a multiple. It is a network of workers, customers, suppliers, skills, and place, and it will remain something after the man who bought it has gone home to wherever money goes when it has finished consuming a county.

Property is real and good. Conservatism has no quarrel with ownership. But property carries a social function, and a human being is not inventory to be swapped when the owner’s timetable slips. The conservative betrayal is to admire the sponsor’s freedom while refusing to ask what that freedom has done to the company entrusted to it. Concentrated state power is no cure; a ministry deciding which companies may live would only change the name on the lever.

The better answer is distributed. Employee ownership, a local management buyout, a cooperative conversion, a mutual or community trust—each asks a different legal form, but each can put governance and residual claims nearer to the people whose labor and place make the asset worth owning. The answer is not another enormous fund able to replace one distant owner with a nearer one. The answer is ownership that cannot simply leave when the multiple no longer looks generous.

It is harder here. Patient capital cannot flee a bad quarter. Cooperative governance is slower than a board’s private instructions. A community institution must answer for the wages it pays and the ground it occupies, not merely the price at which it can be sold. It has no quick mark-up to console the holder. It also asks harder questions of management than a sponsor can afford to ask for long.

Still, the counter-model is not nostalgia. It is the attempt to preserve what an exit always threatens to take: the company’s own next life.

The exit drought will not end on the schedule of the men who caused it. But a company that belongs to its workers and its place can outlive the drought—and perhaps, if we are fortunate, the men who made a business out of not waiting.
