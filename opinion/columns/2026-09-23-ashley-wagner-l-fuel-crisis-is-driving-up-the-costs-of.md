America's energy-assistance system is abandoning Northeast families to pay for heat.

The winter bill for a typical heating-oil household is projected to rise from $1,749 to roughly $2,500. That is a $751 jump before groceries, rent, medication, daycare, or the car payment. Mark Wolfe of the National Energy Assistance Directors Association raised the estimate from a 31 percent increase to 43 percent in a matter of weeks. New York heating oil reached $6.14 a gallon last week, up 66 percent from a year earlier.

The kitchen-table version is not “fuel prices are volatile.” It is $751 taken out of the month.

For a family with a toddler, that is the cost of keeping the bedroom warm while the daycare invoice is already due. For an older household member, it is heat in the room where the person sleeps. For a parent buying oil in installments, it is the difference between filling the tank and hoping the weather holds.

About 4.8 million American households heat with oil, mostly in the Northeast and parts of Alaska. A typical home uses about 800 gallons in a winter. The people in those houses cannot wait for a global supply chain to become philosophically satisfying. They need a truck to arrive before the tank runs dry.

In *Home Alone*, the basement furnace is a monster with a face. That is a child’s version of the fear: something hot and loud beneath the house that might come alive. In 2026, the monster is a 100-gallon minimum delivery and a bill larger than the assistance available to pay it.

A common energy-assistance grant is capped at $500. A minimum delivery is typically 100 gallons because the truck has to make the trip. At $6.14 a gallon, that delivery costs $614 before any additional charges. The grant no longer covers one delivery of the fuel it exists to help families buy.

That is not a minor administrative wrinkle. It is the policy's failure made visible in the basement.

The supply shock is real. The Strait of Hormuz is closed. Iran-backed Houthi militants threaten tankers near the Arabian Peninsula. Ukrainian strikes have damaged Russian refineries. About 7 million barrels a day of refining capacity in the Middle East and Russia is damaged or constrained, and severe refinery damage can take six to eight months to repair. New England distillate stocks, which include heating oil and diesel, have fallen to 2.9 million barrels from a five-year average of 4.9 million.

The [diesel market was already warning us that global buyers were competing for shrinking distillate supply](/articles/2026-08-18-diesel-prices-climb-as-global-buyers-compete-for-shrinking-supply/). The heating-oil bill is the same shortage arriving at a child’s bedroom window.

American refiners are running plants near capacity, but they cannot replace damaged foreign capacity quickly enough. The profits at the top of the chain rise with the price. The cost moves down to the family that has no practical substitute before October.

Katie Childs of Connecticut supplier Tuxis-Ohr’s described the part of the chain that does not receive the windfall. The company operates on a fixed cents-per-gallon markup rather than a percentage markup. When the commodity price rises, its borrowing costs rise, its credit risk rises, and the diesel bill for its delivery trucks rises. The driver still has to show up at the house.

The distributor is squeezed. The household is exposed. The refiners collect the upside. That is how a global crisis becomes a local transfer.

The [29 percent heating-oil surge in Northern Ireland over three weeks](/articles/2026-07-26-northern-ireland-heating-oil-prices-surge-29-in-three-weeks-as-us-iran-disruptio/) showed how quickly the shock can reach ordinary homes. Here, the number is worse and the safety net is smaller than the bill.

The federal energy-assistance system is built around a price that no longer exists. A grant that once represented meaningful help now leaves a family short of the minimum amount of fuel needed to make a delivery worthwhile. “Targeted assistance” becomes a phrase for assistance that reaches the household after the price has outrun it.

This is what consumer precarity looks like when the essential good cannot be postponed. A family can delay a restaurant meal. It cannot delay heat indefinitely. It can turn down the thermostat, put on another sweatshirt, and close rooms, but those are not substitutes for a functioning safety net. They are the household absorbing the policy failure through discomfort.

Natural-gas customers may pay about 5.8 percent more this winter. Propane and electric-heat customers may pay about 9 percent more. Oil-heated homes face the sharpest increase because they are tied to a concentrated supply shock and often cannot switch systems on the schedule of a winter season.

The answer is not to tell a family to become an electrification project by November. It is to index energy assistance to current fuel prices, raise the grant ceiling above the cost of one delivery, and fund weatherization and fuel-switching before the next emergency rather than after the current one has emptied the tank.

The household that paid $1,749 last winter is not receiving a lesson in resilience. It is being billed $751 for a war, a refinery shortage, and an assistance system that was not built for the price in front of it.

The invoice is not just in the mailbox. It is under the toddler’s bed, in the basement tank, and on the kitchen table at 11 p.m.
