Donald Trump's administration made more than a million lawful people unlawful with a single signature.

No charge was filed against any of them. No individual was accused of a thing. More than a million people from thirteen countries were authorized by their own government to live and work in the United States, and by that same government they were made unauthorized — not by leaving, not by overstaying, but by a notice in the Federal Register, the government's own official bulletin. They woke up authorized and went to bed not. That is the act this column is about. Springfield, Ohio, is where you get to watch it land.

The mechanism is worth reading before anyone characterizes it. Temporary protected status lives in Section 244 of the Immigration and Nationality Act, codified at 8 U.S.C. §1254a: on a country-wide finding that the nationals of a foreign state cannot be returned in safety — armed conflict, natural disaster, or what the statute calls extraordinary and temporary conditions — the Secretary of Homeland Security bars their removal and authorizes them to work here for an initial period the statute caps at eighteen months, extended only by a fresh Federal Register notice declaring that the conditions still persist. It confers no green card and no permanent right of any kind. The designation, the renewal, and the revocation are all discretionary acts of the government. Nobody qualifies individually, because nobody is being punished individually.

James Percival, general counsel at the Department of Homeland Security, said what the statute says: "The T in TPS stands for TEMPORARY, yet many of these designations became de facto amnesty." He was right about the T. He called the Supreme Court's June decision upholding the end of Haitian TPS for some 350,000 people "a win for the rule of law and common sense," and on the Court's holding he was right again. Both statements are true as far as they go.

They do not go far. "De facto amnesty" is a description of executive practice, not of statutory text. Nothing in that section compelled a single extension. Every renewal of Haitian status was a discretionary notice published by the Department of Homeland Security — including renewals published by the first Trump administration's own DHS — and the text said temporary at every one of them, in the same words, in the same section. What changed between the renewals and the revocation was not a syllable of the law. It was who was reading it. The discretion that granted is the discretion that revoked, and a government that spent years telling a million people to re-register, on its own forms, cannot announce afterward that the status it conferred was a scam it is now cleaning up. The Supreme Court settled whether the government could do this. Nobody has been asked whether the government should, because asking would mean alleging that some identifiable person did something wrong — and across a million files, no one ever did.

The strongest version of the case against these people deserves to be stated by someone who lives it. Rick Johnson has been in Springfield his whole life outside his service; the Stars and Stripes fly on his porch beside Navy and Marine Corps banners, and his wife served in the Navy while he was at Camp Pendleton. "We love America, we love Americans - any kind of Americans, but you kind of have to be American." The neighbors he has left — the only ones he has, he says, since the empty houses around him were torn down — moved in about four years ago and "don't really bother me." But, he adds, "there's a lot of them." He waves off the driving complaints as the neighborhood's real grievance. "Really it's the amount of them. If you come here you [should] come here legally. The Temporary Protected Status is done… it's time to go." That is a legal claim and a numerical claim from a man who is not asking anyone to be expelled for being Haitian, and it is not nothing. It is also not an argument about the law, because the law is what made those neighbors lawfully present for years. What Johnson is reporting is a sensation of fullness, from the residents of a town that emptied out long before any ship arrived.

The factories went first, and that is the fact the rallies forget. De-industrialization took the work, took the workers, took the families to the coasts and the warmer states, and left boarded houses behind. Paschur Castelly left Haiti twenty years ago and runs a small shop near the empty lots; his shelves stand bare but for religious candles and framed saints. "I can't purchase new merchandise because I don't have people coming in," he says. His community had doctors and engineers; they came for a better life, to help their families, because, as he puts it, "our country doesn't offer us security." Then came the arrivals — an estimated 20,000 Haitians inside five years, fleeing an earthquake and an assassination, doing the shifts nobody else wanted, legally, under a status the government itself conferred. However you count the number, the pace was the story: tens of thousands of strangers inside five years, into a town already emptied. And now the same government announces that the legal was legal only until it became inconvenient.

Migrants facing removal are placed in the Alternatives to Detention program, which means a monitor and a report every two weeks or a signed deportation order. One warehouse worker who chose the monitor put it plainly: "I was given a choice, wear the ankle monitor and report in every two weeks, or sign my deportation order."

Pierre Damas Bel was twenty years old. He arrived in Springfield in 2024 to join his father and stepmother, fleeing a Haiti gripped by political instability and gang warfare. He entered the US under a now-defunct Biden-era program for migrants waiting in Mexico and, according to a family friend, received TPS. He was exactly the kind of young man the parable erects statues to: an honors student, a talented athlete, a cadet in a program training students to become officers in the US Armed Forces. Then he was told to report to an ICE field office, where he was fitted with an ankle monitor. He wrote on Instagram that he felt "shame and humiliation" at wearing the tag. Twice he went to the ICE facilities asking them to take it off. Viles Dorsainvil, co-founder of the support center Bel attended, sums it up: "He did everything right to be here until they put the tag on him." On 31 August he parked his car on the side of a highway near Springfield, walked into oncoming traffic, and was killed.

ICE has rejected "any attempt to blame ICE for the death," and its spokesperson supplied the department's answer to a dead twenty-year-old: "Remaining in the country illegally, while fighting removal, is a choice. Leaving with a free flight home and $2,600 is also a choice." That is the menu the state offered a young man it had itself authorized to be here: exile, or humiliation and a tag. A choice between humiliation and exile, presented to a twenty-year-old by the most powerful state on earth, is not a choice. It is a verdict with formatting.

A young man is dead, and that is a tragedy whichever account of Springfield you hold. In 2023 an eleven-year-old boy, Aiden Clark, died when his school bus overturned in a crash with a minivan driven by Herman Joseph, a Haitian who had no valid US license and went to prison. But Nathan Clark, the boy's father, stood up at a Springfield City Commission meeting and denounced politicians for using his son's death as a "political tool" to stoke anti-immigrant feeling, saying the boy was accidentally killed. The rebuke belonged to the politicians who had turned a dead child into campaign material, and the only person with standing to grant that material refused it. The standard it set applies in both directions: a dead child is not a cudgel, and neither is a dead young man, which is precisely why this column keeps Bel's name on the conduct rather than on the feeling. What that standard does not touch is the government's own conduct, which is not a question of tone.

Then the quiet cost nobody puts on a poster: the door Castelly keeps locked now, the neighbor who shouts across the street to ask if he is alright, fearing a raid. Both readings of Springfield can be true at once, and only one of them describes a policy. Fear is not an accidental byproduct of this enforcement; it is the product. A community that arrived to work is being converted, one tag and one locked door at a time, into evidence for the claim that it never belonged.

Watch the words Trump gave the town at his rally in Youngstown this month: "You have a little town, beautiful little town in Ohio where they dropped about 35,000 Haitians. Well, they're being taken out and they're almost going to be gone and we're giving their town back." Dropped, as from an aircraft. Given back, as to a rightful owner who was never asked. The number is fantasy — reporting estimates put Springfield at around 20,000 — but precision is beside the point: the rhetoric converts people into weather, weather into damage, and deportation into repair. A homeland security video set deportees in chains and white uniforms to jaunty music and ordered the expired to LEAVE IMMEDIATELY.

The department calls its raids in the town "law enforcement operations… to keep Americans safe" — safe, apparently, from people the same department's lawyers insist Haiti is safe enough to take back. The State Department's own advice on Haiti is Level 4, Do Not Travel, the strongest warning there is. Those two documents cannot both be correct. Only one of them is being enforced.

The mechanics are not subtle. Ohio has become a swing state in the coming Congressional elections, with one of two Republican-held Senate seats up for grabs; the president has made two visits, in part to drag turnout against a slide in his approval ratings; and the immigration enforcement campaign has begun to lose support even as border policy holds strong support among Republicans. So Springfield gets another turn in the spotlight, another audition, another turn as evidence. This is what the administration has been building toward since the courts went its way: enforcement, courts and detention operating as one machine, mapped out strategy by strategy before any of it touched a name.

What the government did wrong does not require two competing Springfields to see. It conferred lawful status on more than a million people, ordered them to keep renewing it, renewed it year after year through administrations of both parties, and then — when the politics changed — withdrew it with the same discretionary power it had used to grant it, and called the people it had made unlawful a problem it was solving. It fit an ankle monitor to a twenty-year-old who had done everything right, refused him twice, and answered his death with a price: a free flight and $2,600. Springfield's shelves will not be restocked by a deportation flight, and the factories will not reopen because a monitor was fitted; the town will keep its water tower and its grievance. But a government that tells a million people they are safe, signs them out of the law by decree, and then bills them for the wreckage is not enforcing a rule. It is manufacturing the lawbreakers it punishes — and the debt it owes the men who filled its abandoned streets is not the kind a flight home and a cash payment settles.
