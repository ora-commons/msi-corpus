Michael has at most £300 a week to feed himself and three children after paying £1,800 rent, and Burnham’s first budget has decided that asset owners get a softer landing. That is not a low-key budget. It is a cost-of-living crisis with a red box on it.

When Chancellor John Healey rises in the Commons at 12.30pm to present the Burnham government’s first budget, he will call it “low-key.” Fine. Low-key is not the same as painless, and it certainly is not the same as fair. It is the cover story for a government that will not ask the country’s biggest asset-holders to contribute more. Healey will promise “breathing space for today, fresh hope for the future.” That is how you describe the future when the present has been made to pay.

Michael, one of the First Edition readers profiled in the reporting, works for a small property company. He rents a draughty three-bedroom bungalow in Winchester and lives there with his three children. He pays about £165 a month for electricity and gas, and expects that to rise. His income after tax is between £2,500 and £3,000 a month. His rent is £1,800, leaving him at most £300 a week to cover the living costs of four people.

He is not destitute. He is also not comfortable. He is in the vast territory between eligibility and affluence where the state expects families to absorb shocks themselves.

“I think about the cost of everything now,” he says. “Will I get enough money to be able to afford this? Will I have enough when the rent is due?” His assessment of food prices is blunter: “Once something goes up in price, it never comes down again.”

Some of the pressure is not self-inflicted. The Iran war has increased borrowing costs and eroded the government’s spending power, as [the mounting difficulties surrounding Healey’s first budget made clear](/articles/2026-10-27-john-healey-prepares-his-first-budget-amid-war-driven-economic-pressure/). A government cannot conjure money out of a war or make expensive borrowing disappear.

But war is an explanation, not immunity. A government does not get to call every policy choice an external constraint. The plan to abolish the carers allowance cliff edge is a genuine victory. Pausing the equalisation of the minimum wage for under-21s with the national living wage is a strange place to find fiscal rigidity. Beyond such choices sits a collection of “everyday fixes” meant to make the squeeze bearable without threatening the wealth that created it.

Kiran Stacey, the Guardian’s policy editor, expects a budget far smaller than last year that is “not going to be ending 40 years of neoliberalism.” There is honesty in that. There is also an excuse.

Rejecting a higher capital gains tax and refusing to reduce the tax on large homes are choices, not weather. Promising to stick to Labour’s fiscal rules may be prudent; using those rules to guarantee that valuable property remains untouched is something else. The fiscal rules discipline the budget. They do not write the tax schedule.

The catechism of the comfortable calls this prudence: war made it necessary, fiscal rules made it inevitable, and therefore the household that cannot heat the house must absorb the entire adjustment. That is not prudence. It is an allocation of loss with a press release attached.

The scale of hardship leaves no room for the reassuringly small. StepChange, the UK’s largest debt advice charity, found that average household arrears debt for its clients rose from £2,833 in 2022 to £4,345 in 2025. One in two people now seeking its help has energy debt. For the charity, these pressures are “completely new”: energy debt has entered the same household budget as food and housing costs, leaving families little room to absorb another rise.

This is not a temporary surge in consumer-credit indiscipline. It is the financial shadow of a country asking families to choose between necessities.

Immediate subsidies are necessary. They cannot be the whole strategy when one household faces a rent increase, an energy-bill increase and a food bill that refuses to retreat. Michael receives no benefits, which makes him ineligible for the Warm Homes Discount. That program gives certain benefit recipients £150 off their annual electricity bill. Making it more generous would help some families. It would do nothing for Michael and millions of families like him who earn too little to qualify for targeted support but still cannot afford the accumulated shocks.

Mathew Lawrence, director of the Common Wealth thinktank, has called for a structural plan to reduce energy bills, including an “energy guarantee” providing a protected block of energy priced at pre-crisis levels. That is the kind of policy the Burnham government should be defending. Not another targeted discount designed around the eligibility rules of the existing system, but a price structure that changes the arithmetic faced by ordinary households.

The missing household does not pay. It simply pays elsewhere, in arrears, missed meals and a colder bedroom.

Food offers another test of priorities. The proposed removal of import tariffs on products including baked beans, chocolate and gherkins is expected, on the Treasury’s estimate, to save the average household £21 a year. Michael tries to limit his weekly shop at Aldi to £80.

Twenty-one pounds is not a cost-of-living strategy. It is a line in a press release. It is a discount at the checkout.

Analysis published in May found that food prices during crises such as the pandemic either declined slowly or incompletely, or remained at their higher level indefinitely. Once something goes up, it does not reliably come down. Calling a £21 annual saving relief while £165 monthly energy bills climb is arithmetic with its eyes shut.

Housing shows how the government can offer a long-term answer while leaving the immediate wound untouched. Burnham has committed Labour to the biggest council-housebuilding programme since the postwar decade, and Healey is expected to announce another £1bn for it. More details of Your First Home, a new version of Help to Buy, are also expected.

These are worthwhile commitments. They will not help a tenant whose landlord demands an increase this month. Local housing allowance has been frozen since 2024. The Resolution Foundation finds that more than 900,000 private-rented households on universal credit face a shortfall between their rent and their allowance, averaging £158 a month for a family renting a two-bedroom home. Relinking allowance to rents would provide immediate help while construction catches up.

“Fresh hope for the future” is cold comfort when the immediate alternative is arrears.

That is also what [Andrea Egan’s call for public investment in the budget](/articles/2026-10-26-andrea-egan-urges-burnham-to-put-public-investment-in-his-budget/) demands: public investment that changes what people can afford, rather than measures designed chiefly to be announced. Public money can build council homes, insulate draughty homes, reform the energy market and protect a block of lower-cost energy. Houses, energy systems and local services determine whether families are forced to spend their wages repairing a system that should have been repaired properly in the first place.

Public investment is not a decorative substitute for redistribution. It is what makes redistribution more than a gesture.

The fuel-duty freeze exposes the government’s priorities more clearly. It continues a policy first announced by Rishi Sunak in 2022, but it disproportionately benefits higher-income households, which drive more. It also deprives the Treasury of revenue precisely when public investment and targeted relief are needed. It is a blunt instrument that spares the better-off and makes the harder task of helping poorer drivers look like generosity.

Pat, from Aberdeen, wants lower fuel prices because she relies on her car. That dependence is real. The answer cannot ignore it. Nor can it pretend a universal freeze is well targeted. Poorer households need help with transport, but they need much more help with energy bills.

The government should end the universal fuel-duty freeze, use the revenue for targeted transport relief for low-income households without a viable alternative to driving, and put the larger fiscal effort into energy bills and housing. It should also reconsider its refusal to tax capital gains and large property if it genuinely wants the freedom to act. Fiscal responsibility is not the promise to do nothing. It is the construction of a state capable of keeping promises.

The pattern is now unmistakable. Protect capital gains. Protect large homes. Keep fuel duty frozen. Promise more council houses later. Offer modest help where the eligibility rules allow. Make a tiny gesture toward food prices. Above all, call the result low-key so that the refusal to challenge the people at the top is mistaken for caution rather than choice.

This government risks presiding over the worst parliament for living standards on record. That makes its rhetoric an ethical problem, not merely a stylistic one.

Burnham’s first budget should launch the energy guarantee, begin taxing wealth properly, rebuild public investment and bring immediate relief to households outside the benefit system. The war may dictate the pace. It does not dictate the destination.

A first budget should tell the country whom it serves. This one wants to be for everyone. The arithmetic says otherwise. On the evidence, it protects holders of property gains, owners of large homes and better-off drivers, while families such as Michael’s are asked to endure the age.

That is not breathing space. It is a tighter vice dressed up as caution.

Build the energy guarantee. Relink housing allowance to rents. Insulate homes and council houses now. Target transport help for people who have no alternative to a car. Tax capital gains and large homes. Put the money into public energy, housing and household relief.

Breathing space is not the sound of another press release. It is the moment a parent can turn on the heating without deciding which bill to leave unpaid. It is a benefit that arrives before winter ends.

If Healey’s budget provides none of that, it will have been low-key in one sense only: low in ambition. He can make this budget low-key because it is practical, not because it has surrendered. Heat in the winter. Rent paid this month. One fewer bill left unpaid. That is what public money is for.
