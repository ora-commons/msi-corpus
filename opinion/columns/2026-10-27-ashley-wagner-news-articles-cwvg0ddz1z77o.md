Trump is blaming Biden for the inflation his own tariffs made worse.

Trump’s economy is working. His price promise is not. And he owns part of it.

Two sets of numbers, both true, and only one of them will decide the midterms. US economic growth is 2.2 percent—respectable by the standards of many wealthy economies, Germany among them. Unemployment is 4.2 percent. The stock market is near record highs. And consumer prices have risen 3.7 percent over the past year while average hourly earnings have increased by just 3 percent.

That 0.7-percentage-point gap is what stops a household’s paycheck from keeping pace with prices.

At my kitchen table, the spreadsheet has no column for record highs. It has the daycare invoice, the grocery receipt, the gas receipt and the paycheck that has to cover all three. Taylor Swift’s “I Can Do It With a Broken Heart” gives the split a cleaner title than the dashboard: an economy can keep performing while the household does the work of staying solvent with a broken budget. The spreadsheet can balance on paper and still fail at the checkout line.

Meanwhile, an [Associated Press-NORC survey](/articles/2026-10-20-associated-press-norc-survey-finds-26-approve-trump-s-economy-handling/) found that only 26 percent approved of Trump’s handling of the economy. A [Harris poll](/articles/2026-10-15-most-us-voters-say-economy-is-worsening-ahead-of-midterms-harris-poll-finds/) found that most US voters believed the economy was worsening.

The man promising to end the price pain is being judged by the price pain.

The aggregate economy is not the problem. The distance between the economy and the country’s mood is the problem—and Donald Trump has helped widen that distance himself.

The gap is the 2026 midterms.

It is Biden’s political trap revisited. During the 2024 campaign, the Biden administration could point to low unemployment, solid growth and falling inflation. Many voters still focused on the higher prices they encountered every day. Trump now faces the same disconnect, with one crucial addition: he owns more of the forces pushing prices upward.

In 2024, he ran on price.

“When I win, I will immediately bring prices down,” he said.

Immediately: the most aggressive verb a president has, aimed at the one number his base cared about most. He won with that promise attached. That makes the price level not merely a policy consequence but a promise with a due date, and the due date was the inauguration.

Delivering on it has proved harder, and the administration’s own language now concedes it. After the most recent inflation report, White House spokesman Matthew Middleton said the administration’s “aggressive, pro-growth economic agenda” had blunted “Biden’s inflation crisis.”

Read that carefully: not ended, blunted; not the inflation crisis, Biden’s. On the number that matters most to voters, the best case the White House can make is that it made a bad situation less bad and that the bad situation was inherited. That is credit for damage control.

The voters are running another calculation. Celinda Lake, the veteran Democratic pollster who advised Biden’s 2020 campaign, names the trap in language the White House cannot answer:

“When economists say inflation comes down, they mean the rate of increase has come down. When voters say inflation has come down, they mean the price has returned to what it was. Well, of course, it never returns to what it was.”

That is not a failure of economic literacy. It is the distance between the dashboard and the dinner table. You can win every monthly release and lose the argument every morning at the till. “You can’t argue with people about their own lived reality,” as Lake puts it.

Biden at least had external forces he could point to: the Covid pandemic and the supply-chain aftershocks. Those were real constraints and real costs, but they also gave him a political off-ramp from personal blame. Trump has no such door.

His “Liberation Day” tariffs, announced in April 2025, raised costs for consumers. A Federal Reserve Bank of New York study found that the tariffs increased consumer-goods prices by about 2.9 percent. That figure came after some tariffs were paused, new trade deals were struck and the Supreme Court blocked him from using emergency powers for many of the remaining tariffs. The mitigation limited the damage without erasing it.

A pause is not a refund. A new trade deal does not undo a price already priced in.

Conflict with Iran then pushed oil prices higher, lifting the cost of petrol and adding pressure to household budgets. Peter Morici, the University of Maryland economist who aligns with Republicans, is blunt: “The inflation is clearly traceable to Donald Trump… It’s his tariffs and the war. People know that. They see it.”

Whatever the precise percentage points, the direction of the price level is now partly a choice with a signature on it. The Trump economy has a Trump signature.

The White House points to tax cuts, job growth and lower prescription-drug costs. Larry Kudlow, a Trump ally, calls the expansion “the greatest story never told” because “nobody is getting fired.”

Nobody getting fired is the economists’ definition of victory. The household’s definition is the receipt.

Kudlow’s statistic is for the market. It is not reassurance for an anxious shopper. Voters are not asking whether the president can boast about growth. They are asking whether they can afford the next trip to the supermarket.

Which is why the White House has started shopping for sentiment. Trump has called for a cap on credit-card interest rates and floated a $5,000 payment to American adults if Republicans retain control of Congress.

Read the conditions, because they are the tell. A president who believed 2.2 percent growth, 4.2 percent unemployment and a stock market near record highs could carry him would not be promising checks. Congressional control has become part of the economic pitch: Republicans must make the relief visible before November because aggregate growth is not convincing voters.

A cap on interest can reduce one kind of monthly pressure. A payment can soften one budget. Neither reverses the higher price level that made voters furious in the first place. A check buys gratitude; it does not make the bill shrink.

The campaigns have already run the same calculation. In the closest races, [only one of 54 Republican economy ads mentions Trump](/articles/2026-10-23-only-one-of-54-republican-economy-ads-in-the-closest-races-mentions-trump/). That is not a media strategy; it is a price. The president’s name has become a cost attached to the very issue he promised to fix, so his own party is selling the economy without him—and a party running economy ads that avoid its president is running a cover story.

Anne Helen Petersen’s *Can’t Even* supplies the right warning for this moment: burnout is not cured by a productivity app or a face mask. A price shock is not cured by a better app either. Trump promised to lower the bill; voters are not asking households to become more efficient at absorbing it.

The pro-family economic agenda is not a slogan: refundable child-care credits, paid family leave, universal pre-K and a permanent Child Tax Credit lower recurring family costs. A check contingent on election-night control is none of those things. Policy has to lower the recurring bill before the checkout line.

Biden never bridged the gap between economic performance and economic sentiment. Trump promised to bridge it and made himself more exposed by making immediate relief the center of his political identity.

His economy can win every economic report and still lose the election because voters see the tariffs, the petrol prices and the grocery bill. Trump’s economy is strong enough to win an argument and politically poisonous enough to lose an election.

No dataset repairs that sentence. Only a reversal does.

By the time the midterms are counted, the receipts from this year will be in the bin and the ones from last year will still be sitting on the counter. The checkout line is the verdict. The midterms are the answer. And the man who promised to bring them down wrote that sentence himself.
