Anthropic and Nvidia manufacture independent-looking credit through circular financing.

The useful part of the debt market’s reaction is not the label “junk.” It is a question with people on either side of the transaction: who owes the cash when the model company cannot? Several large bond-fund managers told *The Wall Street Journal* they expect Fitch Ratings or S&P Global Ratings to place any first public Anthropic debt in the BB range, one or two notches below investment grade. Neither agency has made its view public, and a split rating remains possible. “Obviously, at some point in the future, we’re hoping to be investment grade,” chief executive Dario Amodei said in a CNBC interview. The sentence contains an order of operations: spending commitments first, an investment-grade credential later.

A bond is not a share with a cleaner interface. It is a fixed claim on cash, ordinarily payable before shareholders receive anything, and it carries no participation in the upside. Shareholders retain the residual claim to extraordinary value if the models succeed; bondholders receive interest if the cash arrives. Equity investors can pay today for a future payoff they judge probable; bondholders need repayment in cash. The debt market is asking for the second obligation, not the first.

It is true that Anthropic has more cushion than an ordinary start-up. The commitments were disclosed before trading opened, as [Main Street Independent reported when Anthropic’s IPO arrived](/articles/2026-11-18-anthropic-s-ipo-arrives-amid-debt-and-company-disclosed-900-billion-commitments/). Analysts estimate that $96 billion in cash on hand, IPO proceeds and a concurrent private-share placement will cover the commitments for a year or more. The company raised more than $100 billion last week; it reported $17 billion in revenue last quarter; and its market value was roughly $1.2 trillion. Roughly half of the $900 billion in commitments does not begin until 2030. None of this is nothing.

The trouble is category confusion. A market value is an estimate of what investors will pay for expected future cash flow, not cash in a vault. Quarterly revenue is not profit, and liquidity buys time, not solvency. Anthropic is less than six years old, loses money because its computing costs exceed what its model revenue presently supports, and it has no other business to give bond investors comfort. SpaceX’s investment-grade case rested partly on its satellite business. The demand for the current generation of large language models is real, but a commitment schedule is a statement of obligation, while Amodei’s investment-grade ambition is a statement of aspiration. The second tells us more.

The arithmetic is stark without becoming a forecast. If Anthropic’s $17 billion quarterly revenue were repeated four times without further growth, the company would have $68 billion in annual revenue against commitments approaching $900 billion. That is not a prediction; the company may grow faster than arithmetic can presently describe. The comparison merely shows the scale of the financing proposition. Commitments roughly thirteen times annualised revenue must be underwritten by cash the business is not yet producing.

Calling the whole $900 billion a sunk loss would also be false; roughly half has not even started. But a commitment remains a claim on future cash. The relevant question is whether the business producing that cash can carry the claim without selling it onward or shifting its risk to a supplier. If Anthropic succeeds, shareholders retain the residual value after creditors are paid. If it fails, the losses travel to bondholders, equipment lessors, chip vendors and other counterparties; a high equity valuation does not absorb them. A bond has no branch that becomes irrelevant when the equity is volatile.

A company can be right about long-term demand and still fail to pay its bonds on time. That is path risk: the endpoint may be lucrative while the route between here and there requires more capital than the lenders will provide. A bondholder does not receive a better price for agreeing with the long-term thesis. The interest is due on the stated date, and principal is due thereafter.

It is also true that a credit rating is not a moral certificate or a mechanical forecast of default. A BB company can borrow; the high-yield market is about $1.7 trillion. Investment-grade issuers draw from an $8.6 trillion market, and funds restricted to high-grade paper cannot simply buy below it. The practical difference is price and buyer: more interest, fewer compelled buyers. That is market discipline rather than a verdict on the technology. A rating agency estimates whether promised cash flows will arrive, in what order and with what margin if they do not. A BB forecast does not say the models are stupid. It says the cushion between promised cash and actual cash is thin.

If Anthropic lands in the BB range, it would be by far the most valuable US high-yield company. Hilton Worldwide currently holds that distinction, with a market value of about $80 billion. Anthropic’s roughly $1.2 trillion valuation is a different order of magnitude. The label therefore changes the size of the plumbing: a junk label can be workable at certain balance sheets, while this one would send an exceptionally large issuer into a much smaller pool at the moment it needs capital most. Fund managers consequently expect Anthropic to keep using structured financing, chip financing and similar arrangements rather than issue many unsecured bonds. An investment-grade rating would also make the paper eligible for retirement funds that treat high-grade credit as a proxy for safety. If the rating later changes after purchase, the risk does not reverse itself.

Structured financing is not automatically fraud. It divides a pool of cash flows into securities with different priorities; collateral, reserves or a guarantee can protect one claim while another absorbs the first loss. If the risk is disclosed, priced and adequately backed, the arrangement can be legitimate. The problematic feature here is not the word “structured.” It is who supplies the protection when the borrower and the seller are connected.

Broadcom and Nvidia have guaranteed tens of billions of dollars in loans to buyers of their chips that are rented to model laboratories including Anthropic and OpenAI. Where a guarantee is full and unconditional, the guarantor is responsible for the covered debt if the borrower defaults, although the actual exposure depends on the guarantee’s terms. The chipmaker can collect hardware revenue while standing behind the customer’s credit; if the customer cannot pay, some covered risk returns to the guarantor.

The circularity is not that repayment automatically purchases more chips. It is that the chipmaker helps make the chips purchasable, earns the hardware revenue and retains some exposure to the borrower’s credit. The vendor has an interest in making the customer look safe because a safe customer buys more. This is not proof that any particular loan is improper. It does mean the apparent independence of the credit is partly manufactured: the vendor has helped construct the conditions under which the borrower looks capable of carrying the debt.

“Manufacture” here is a structural claim, not an allegation of forged documents. The credit may be real; its independence is manufactured.

It is true that project finance and vendor guarantees can lower the cost of building genuinely useful infrastructure. There is no basis for calling every such arrangement fraudulent merely because the money moves through several legal forms. The trouble begins when a guarantee improves a credit rating without producing a dollar of new operating revenue. It is a promise from one balance sheet to absorb the failure of another.

John Kenneth Galbraith called the interval between extraction and discovery the “bezzle”: the beneficiary has the money, while the victim has not yet felt the loss. That vocabulary does not prove fraud here. The financing record establishes a concern about circularity, not concealment or delayed discovery, so the term cannot carry the accusation.

Cory Doctorow later broadened the bezzle into a general account of technology grift. The analogy here has to remain an analogy: the documented mechanism is not concealment, but a vendor helping to make the buyer’s credit look stronger while retaining some exposure to it. That is enough. The credit may be real; its independence is manufactured.

Analysts’ concern about circularity and the size of the guarantees has been increasing. Market expectations also point to Nvidia pulling back some of these guarantees following the unexpected exit of former chief executive Jensen Huang, who championed the transactions. That removes one easy source of support and increases the prospect that Anthropic will have to approach unsecured lenders directly. The commitments do not shrink; the market merely examines them more closely. An investment-grade rating would also relieve Broadcom and Nvidia of some guarantee exposure because model laboratories could borrow on their own.

Frank Pasquale’s point in *The Black Box Society* is that opacity becomes a governance problem when people bear consequences they cannot inspect or contest. The useful application here is not that structured finance is bad as such. It is that each contract in the chain can look safer in isolation: a guarantee covers a loan, a financing vehicle isolates a project’s cash flows, and a lease turns a capital purchase into a series of payments. Each document has a counterparty. Read together, they form one obligation: laboratories need computing capacity, the capacity is financed by vendors and credit markets, model revenue is expected to service it, and equity retains whatever remains.

Nobody has to have made a profit for the machinery to keep running. The payments merely need to keep moving.

The benefit trace is plain. Equity receives the residual: if the models create a vast new market, shareholders capture the upside. Bondholders receive contractual interest and principal, not participation in that upside, and chipmakers receive equipment revenue while carrying contingent exposure through their guarantees. If model economics disappoint, creditors and guarantors are left with the loss. The asymmetry is not that fixed claims absorb the first loss—equity is junior and does that. It is that equity retains the conditional prize, while creditors carry default risk without sharing in that upside and guarantors remain exposed if the laboratory cannot service the lease.

The arrangement is not unique. Meta and Oracle have investment-grade ratings, issued record amounts of debt and guaranteed large loans for data-centre construction; some of that paper has performed poorly as bond managers absorb a large supply. The cost of building digital infrastructure has been measured at $10 trillion, depending on what one counts. It is larger than even the equity market can comfortably supply. The older convention generally favoured equity: shareholders could win if the company did, while bond investors generally stayed away because they had no ownership upside. At this scale, the industry has to borrow. In the article, Anthropic is the largest single example, but the contradiction is general: an industry promising enormous physical scale is asking the least forgiving part of capital markets to underwrite it.

This is also where scale becomes more than a financial strategy. Tim Wu’s lesson from *The Master Switch* is that visible competition at the surface of a networked economy can conceal private control at the operational bottleneck. For model laboratories, the bottleneck is compute. A company able to commit $900 billion can reserve capacity years before demand has been demonstrated. A smaller laboratory may produce a better model and still lack the balance sheet, suppliers and credit credibility required to put it into production. The commitment schedule therefore buys three things at once: capacity, time and the ability to make other companies wait. Scale becomes a barrier to imitation without requiring a speech about market power.

That is also an antitrust problem, though not one the rating agencies can decide. Lina Khan’s “Amazon’s Antitrust Paradox” supplies the useful question: can a firm use control of one layer of commerce to reinforce its position in another? Here the hardware supplier does not merely control chips; through financing it can influence which model companies can obtain them. If vendor-backed credit becomes a condition of securing compute, credit is a gate. That raises a foreclosure concern at the infrastructure layer. It does not prove an unlawful monopoly, and this column does not need it to: the architecture already tells purchasers where the bargaining power sits.

A compute commitment is not the same thing as a machine already sitting on a depreciation schedule. It is a promise to buy or lease future capacity. Anthropic has begun buying its own computing hardware, which analysts say could help its credit position because a lender can inspect, value and potentially recover an asset. That is the useful half of the arrangement. The less convenient half is that hardware can be collateral and a wasting asset at the same time. Its residual value depends on what another buyer will pay, how quickly model generations change and whether the owner still needs that particular machine. Owning the iron can improve the rating without making the underlying business less risky.

This is an older pattern wearing newer silicon. My father spent thirty years as a journeyman millwright on the bar mill at Manitoba Rolling Mills. When Gerdau bought the mill in 1995, he kept his job while several of my uncles did not. Capital had not vanished from the mill; its priorities had changed, and the consequences did not land evenly. The point is not nostalgia and not a claim that acquisitions are automatically theft. The point is where the downside landed. The new machinery and production schedule did not insulate workers from the timetable imposed by the new owner.

The digital version arrives as rented chips, data-centre commitments and guaranteed debt: the company keeps the upside of scale, while suppliers and future creditors carry some of the schedule risk. The machinery differs. The trade in who gets to be wrong about the future does not.

The rule in the family workshop was simpler: an estimate is not a machine’s value, and a purchase order does not answer what the machine will be worth after the payments have been made. Creditors should ask the same question. What does the compute installation do, what does it cost to operate, what will it be worth when the current generation of models is obsolete, and who is left holding the obligation when the revenue arrives late?

Debt itself is not the problem; the mismatch between $900 billion of spending commitments and a presently loss-making business is. Before a public bond issue, lenders and regulators should require project-level disclosure of every compute commitment’s term, counterparty, termination payment, collateral and residual-value exposure; chipmakers should disclose contingent loan guarantees; and rating agencies should assess the combined cash-flow waterfall—the order in which project cash reaches different claimants—rather than allow a headline equity valuation to substitute for operating cash. Competition authorities should examine whether vendor-backed credit makes demand for proprietary chips self-reinforcing, while open compute standards and portable workloads—software and data that can move between machines rather than remain trapped in one installation—would lower the cost of moving capacity when a financing condition or model architecture changes. High-grade mandates should not be relaxed to accommodate paper whose underlying leverage has been moved off the rated entity.

None of this requires a bearish prediction. It requires saying that a promise to buy $900 billion of computing capacity is not yet a balance sheet. It is an obligation with a deadline attached.

About half the commitments do not begin until 2030, and the bond market’s conclusion is that Anthropic has roughly three years to make investors comfortable with the credit and the plan. The market can be wrong; it was sceptical of Tesla in 2018 just before the stock took off. But being wrong once does not turn a runway into solvency. A rating can lower the interest rate; only cash pays the debt. If the structured route becomes permanent rather than a bridge, Anthropic will have solved its financing problem by replacing an unsecured loan with an arrangement whose full risk is harder to see.

By then, the balance sheet must match the promises, not merely the narrative around them. Wall Street’s refusal to confuse a promise with cash is useful. The question is whether every claim on Anthropic’s future cash sits with the party that can bear it. Much of it currently sits in vendor guarantees whose apparent independence depends on the vendor continuing to want the hardware sold.

The machinery will not care what the rating says. The creditors will. The machinery can be magnificent. The invoice still arrives.
