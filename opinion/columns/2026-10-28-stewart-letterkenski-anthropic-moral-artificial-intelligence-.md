Silicon Valley wants faith to supply moral judgment without surrendering control of its systems.

At the Vatican’s presentation of Pope Leo XIV’s encyclical on AI, Anthropic co-founder Chris Olah said the hardest moral questions are not ones a laboratory can answer; they are questions traditions have carried for millennia. Fine. Anthropic still builds laboratory products; users bring moral questions to them every day. The disclosed arrangement lets the consultation lend legitimacy while the company keeps product control. That division of labor does not show that Claude has learned an averaged morality. It shows that advisers were heard without being given shared authority over what ships.

This turn is not spontaneous. Tech companies were already [increasingly seeking faith leaders’ guidance on AI ethics](/articles/2026-05-08-tech-companies-increasingly-seek-faith-leaders-guidance-on-ai-ethics/). Anthropic says it has met with “scholars, clergy, philosophers and ethicists from more than 15 religious and cross-cultural groups.” Representatives from Meta, Google and Amazon have met with the Vatican, while Microsoft has pursued religious outreach. Anthropic says Claude should draw from religious, secular and political viewpoints with “equal depth and rigor,” pursuing “careful, accumulated thinking” on how good character forms.

Those are disclosed meetings and stated intentions, not an account of model behaviour. A large language model learns statistical patterns from a corpus; a meeting transcript does not, by itself, alter model weights. Consultation could shape training or fine-tuning data, system instructions, evaluation criteria, product policy, or none of them. Anthropic’s public account does not say which. Without that link, the strongest defensible claim is that advisers were heard, not that Claude learned what they said.

Read those corporate words without flinching. The stated programme is not merely pluralism. It is moral accumulation tied to a product: consult broadly, identify what counts as good character, and treat the result as material for a system the company controls. The disclosure establishes the proposal, not its implementation.

Sam Altman said what he thought ChatGPT should do: reflect a weighted average of humanity’s moral view. Humanity does not have a weighted moral average. It has rival and overlapping judgments—some sharply conflicting, some only apparently compatible, none safely removable from the traditions and communities that hold them. Altman’s proposal turns pluralism into an accounting operation: take command, compassion, judgment, prohibition, ritual and debate, then average them into a placid middle. If a model were built that way, the result would not be humanity’s morality. It would be corporate centrism, polished until nobody could locate its authority.

Religion is appealing to these companies partly because it has been reduced to extractable moral content. Webb Keane argues that treating religion as a rule book is a convention tied to scriptural forms, especially the Abrahamic religions, and can distort traditions that do not define themselves chiefly as ethical systems. Doctrine, ritual and relationships are not packaging around a moral core. They are the moral core.

Even the menu is suspect. As religious-studies scholar Tomoko Masuzawa has argued, bounded systems such as Buddhism and Hinduism were not native categories before European scholars arrived to impose them. Silicon Valley is not consulting “world religions”; it is consulting its own taxonomy.

Calling the inquiry equal in ambition does not remove the hierarchy created by extracting moral content. A rulebook can be downloaded. A living tradition cannot. If companies strip away ritual and relationship, they do not make religion simpler; they make it easier to consume—and easier to homogenize.

The meetings may be sincere, and that is exactly what makes the arrangement useful. Meghan Sullivan, a philosopher of religion leading an ethics-and-AI initiative at Notre Dame, praised Anthropic’s rigorous engagement and described the gatherings as intellectually nerdy in a good way. Anthropic explained its models in layman’s terms; participants wrestled with questions ranging from whether humans have souls to whether third graders should have a chatbot for literacy education.

Real conversation. Not shared authority. Consultation is not consent: the disclosed process gives advisers an opportunity to comment, but no authority over what ships. Nothing in the account shows that their advice changed Claude’s data, instructions, evaluations, release criteria or behaviour. The guests could discuss the moral atmosphere of the lab; the lab owns the product. Consultation without disclosed power is advice with a press release.

Legal scholar Paolo Carozza sees the mechanical danger of accumulated thinking. A living tradition can encounter outsiders, absorb what it needs and change organically; generative systems can instead throw materials together, homogenize them, pull toward the middle, favour Western religions and make sense of none of them. Carozza is describing what such systems often do, and potentially do—not reporting a test of Claude’s outputs. The distinction matters. If the consultations become a mechanism for homogenizing advice, a mosaic preserves difference and an average erases it.

Meanwhile, the market can turn consultation into cover. Companies already [sell faith-based AI apps with chatbots imitating Jesus and Buddha](/articles/2026-04-14-faith-based-ai-apps-bring-chatbots-imitating-jesus-and-buddha-to-market/). A firm can manufacture devotional products at scale while claiming that moral questions are too profound for laboratories. There is no contradiction in the business logic: reverence opens doors, revenue walks through. The chatbot assumes a sacred voice. The company signs the terms of service.

AI does not need a religion to be governed by one for this arrangement to become politically useful. The companies do not have to establish that their models actually average religious teachings. They only need people to believe moral authority can be privatized: gathered in private meetings, stripped of public dispute, retained as advice, and monetized in a product whose policy the company alone defines.

Whatever doctrine a model expresses, the source material does not establish that Claude’s is humanity’s average. What it does establish is a process: seek counsel without sharing control. The altar is the meeting; the priests are product teams; the congregation is whoever clicks “agree.”
