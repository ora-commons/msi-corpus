Donald Trump is stealing nearly a billion dollars from the American people.

The Trump administration announced Friday that it is canceling nearly a billion dollars in congressionally approved spending using a "pocket rescission" — a maneuver that lets the executive branch withhold money Congress already voted to spend, then announce the cuts so late in the fiscal year that Congress has no time to stop them. Most of the targets are immigrant services: nonprofit organizations feeding refugees, sheltering unaccompanied minors, and doing the quiet, funded work of keeping children alive and fed in a country that recruited those organizations to do the job in the first place.

The administration says border crossings have declined and emergency-era spending is no longer justified. It also notes, in a White House press release, that some of the organizations are led by people who worked in the Obama administration — the kind of detail that should embarrass a White House but, in this White House, gets filed and forgotten.

This is not budget discipline. It is theft, dressed up in process.

Now put a family in the scene. There is a shelter director in Phoenix — or Houston, or any of the cities where unaccompanied minors from Honduras, Guatemala, and El Salvador land after crossing alone — who pulled up her organization's federal funding portal on a Sunday afternoon and found that $3.2 million she had already budgeted for the fiscal year was gone. Not reduced. Gone. Mid-year. That money was supposed to feed the children already in her care, keep the lights on in the shelter, and pay the case managers working their cases. She did not get a warning. She did not get a call from OMB. She got a Friday press release and a fiscal year that closes in five days.

That is what a pocket rescission looks like when it hits a building where children sleep.

The administration's argument is that because fewer people are crossing the border, these programs are no longer needed. That is not how mid-year rescissions work. These organizations had contracts. The money was already allocated, already committed, already buying food and paying staff and keeping a roof over kids who have no other roof. A rescission does not trim a future budget. It pulls money out of a program that has already spent the money on children who are already here.

Here is what a billion dollars costs when it leaves a child's budget. The United States currently serves roughly 150,000 unaccompanied minors annually through the Office of Refugee Resettlement and its network of grantees. When you divide a billion dollars across that population, you get roughly $6,500 per child. That is $540 a month, $125 a week, for meals, shelter, legal representation, and case management.

If you are a parent, you understand that number. It is two months of daycare. It is a winter coat, school shoes, and a refrigerator full of food for a quarter of the year. It is the line item that keeps a kid from going to bed hungry in a country that said it would take care of them.

Scale it up. The billion dollars the administration is clawing back translates to about 200 shelter beds that will not reopen this year, half a million meals that will not be served, and more than 2,000 children who will lose access to legal representation in immigration proceedings they cannot navigate alone. The administration says the money is no longer necessary. The kids eating dinner in those shelters tonight might disagree.

Collins is right that it is illegal. Murray is right that it is theft. But the real indictment is not a constitutional clause. It is a kitchen-table number: $125 a week, ripped from the budget of every child those organizations serve, five days before the fiscal year closes and the money disappears.

Taylor Swift wrote, "You're on your own, kid" — a title line that has become shorthand in this column for what American care infrastructure actually says to the people who depend on it. The pocket rescission says it in budget language: you're on your own, kid. The shelter director in Phoenix can reapply for funding next year if Congress restores it. The unaccompanied minor from Honduras cannot.

This is the second time Trump has used a pocket rescission in a year. Last time, he blocked $4.9 billion in congressionally approved foreign aid, and the Supreme Court declined to stop him, citing the president's authority over foreign affairs. Foreign aid is one domain. Domestic spending is another. If the courts let it stand again, the precedent is set: a president can cancel anything Congress funds, whenever he wants, for any reason, and the only remedy is the next fiscal year's budget — which he can also pocket-rescind.

Collins said she will work with colleagues to "address these illegal actions." Murray said every Republican who voted for these bills should be furious, because Russell Vought is telling them their votes do not count. Both are right. Neither has been matched by the House, the chamber that would actually have to defend the appropriations clause.

Nearly a billion dollars is a small fraction of federal spending. It is still a billion dollars taken from programs Congress approved and children were told they could rely on. It is still $125 a week per child — two months of daycare, a quarter of a year of meals — gone in a Friday press release.

If Congress will not enforce its own votes, then "bipartisan" is just another word for money the president can steal from a kid's lunch.
