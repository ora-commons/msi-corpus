Marimar Martinez is suing the federal government for negligence, battery, assault, false arrest, false imprisonment, and malicious prosecution. She is a Chicago public school teacher. She was shot five times. The suit is overdue, not aggressive.

Here is the order of events. On October 4 of last year, Border Patrol agent Charles Exum fired into her vehicle in Chicago's Brighton Park neighborhood during ICE's Operation Midway Blitz. The Department of Homeland Security's immediate public posture was that Martinez was a "domestic terrorist" who had "ambushed" federal agents and rammed them with her car. Exum was placed on leave. That is the entire federal response to date.

Every piece of the government story has since collapsed. Federal prosecutors dropped the assault charges with prejudice, meaning the case is permanently dead. An Illinois Accountability Commission found that Exum had no justification for shooting her. [Attorneys say the records contradict the government's account](/articles/2026-02-12-attorneys-say-records-contradict-government-account-in-chicago-border-patrol-sho/) at every turn. Her legal team — Chris Parente and Michael Gallagher — has now produced an independent crime-scene analysis showing that Exum shot her while her car was turning away from his, which means Exum lied about where he was standing, and his agency has known he lied. "It's actual, concrete physical evidence and science led by an independent expert that shows the agent lied about where he was, and Marimar has been telling the truth," Parente said. The government has not responded to that finding.

Five bullets into a teacher turning her car around, and the maximum personnel consequence is leave. No criminal charge against Exum. No public retraction. No correction of the press release. The DHS page calling Martinez a domestic terrorist is still online today, a year after the shooting and a year after the government's own case fell apart. The Department of Homeland Security has had every opportunity, and every reason, to take that language down. It has chosen not to. That is a choice, and it is the choice this suit is now forcing into court.

Before I name anyone else's share in this, I name mine. I sit in a parish in a country where a teacher was shot five times, and we heard about it and went on with the week. My church has been largely silent about the raids, the detentions, the operations with names like Midway Blitz. My readers — many of them people of faith — have read the headlines and returned to their days. That silence is not neutral. It is part of the machinery. It is the institution protecting itself while leaving the man to carry the lie alone. If Exum wants the beginning of a way back, it begins with the truth about where he stood when he fired. An institutional repentance would look like this: the press release comes down, the department says plainly what its own commission has already found, and the man is treated as someone capable of telling the truth rather than as a liability to be managed. The reason Martinez is filing now, after initially planning to let the matter rest, is that the pattern is not stopping. She has watched more people die at the hands of federal agents — including in Minneapolis earlier this year — and watched the same machinery of accusation run again.
