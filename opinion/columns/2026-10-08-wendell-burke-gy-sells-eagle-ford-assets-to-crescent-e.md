They sold ninety thousand acres of South Texas — ground that has paid the bills in Karnes, DeWitt, and Gonzales Counties — so the company could buy back its own stock. Devon Energy is unloading its Eagle Ford position to Crescent Energy for $4.2 billion, at an effective date of July 1 and an expected close around year-end: roughly 90,000 net acres, about 68,000 oil-equivalent barrels a day of net production, roughly four percent of Devon's total output, and a net purchase price Crescent puts at about $3.85 billion after the standard adjustments. President and chief executive Clay Gaspar calls it "the outcome of an ongoing portfolio review" that "sharpens the company's focus on its highest-return and longest-duration assets." That sentence is a CEO's income statement. It is also a quiet confession that the company would rather return cash to its shareholders than go on operating in a place it has been part of for years.

I'll grant Gaspar's case before I take it apart, because the case is real. A company that has just absorbed a rival does owe its owners an honest accounting of what it holds and what each acre is worth against the next best use of the money. Selling a mature asset into a strong commodity price environment is genuinely good timing: a mature asset in a strong price environment is, by definition, the easiest thing in the world to monetize and the hardest thing to walk away from. Capital discipline is not a vice. Focusing on your best ground is not a sin. If Devon were a family partnership deciding whether to keep a played-out lease, I would call the decision prudent and go on about my day.

But Devon is not a family partnership, and the money is not going into the ground anywhere else. In Gaspar's own words the proceeds "accelerate share buybacks, strengthen our balance sheet and increase long-term value for shareholders." Read that again with the county map in front of you. A buyback is the purest instrument finance has ever devised for converting an operating business into a stock price: the company spends its cash to purchase its own shares, the shares grow scarcer, the per-share number climbs, and ownership of the enterprise concentrates into fewer and fewer hands. Chesterton's complaint was never that there were too many capitalists but too few. A buyback is the deliberate manufacture of fewer capitalists, quarter after quarter, executed by men who will never set foot in Karnes County. There is no version of this in which selling a producing asset at the top of a price cycle ends well for the men who made the barrels flow. There are only versions in which it ends well for the men whose pay is set per share.

The rest of Devon's year completes the picture. In May the company closed a roughly $21.5 billion takeover of Coterra Energy, building what management describes as one of the world's largest shale operators "with an asset base anchored in the economic core of the Delaware Basin." [Devon and Expand Energy also moved their headquarters from Oklahoma City to Houston](/articles/2026-10-05-devon-energy-and-expand-energy-move-headquarters-from-oklahoma-city-to-houston/), leaving the city where the company was founded and where its identity had been anchored for decades. Consolidate the ownership, consolidate the address, consolidate the geology. Three decisions in a few months, all in the same direction, all away from anywhere that would have had to be consulted.

So the Eagle Ford was never the problem. The problem is that Devon spent $21.5 billion on Coterra and now has to perform portfolio surgery to keep the math working. The Eagle Ford acreage is paying for that overreach. And the deal is dressed in the same rhetorical furniture the whole sector now wears — Devon calls the price "accretive on a per share basis to free cash flow and net asset value," which is technically true and strategically empty, and [Centalion's $1.5 billion purchase of the Silver Hill gas fields in the Haynesville](/articles/2026-10-05-centalion-buys-silver-hill-gas-fields-in-1-5-billion-haynesville-deal/) earlier in the month came wrapped in the identical vocabulary of long-term value and sharpened focus. The sector has learned to speak in one voice because it has learned to act in one way. Crescent's David Rockecharlie describes what he bought as "top-tier inventory and meaningful scale directly adjacent to our existing operations." Directly adjacent — that is the consolidation tell. The premium in this market does not go to the operator willing to learn new ground; it goes to the vehicle that can stack acreage onto a position it already holds. Nobody has to learn the county. Nobody has to learn the names.

I know how this ends, because I have watched it end. When a company decides your place is a line item, the first thing that goes is the argument for staying. My county gave its working life to a railroad and then to a mill, and each time the accounting was unanswerable — the asset was mature, the returns were better deployed elsewhere, the shareholders were owed the capital. What the accounting never carried was the machine shop that hired the second boy, the motel that filled when the crews came through, the parish that thins when the men stop coming home. None of that appears on a per-share metric, and it was never meant to. That is precisely the wound. The people who set the price have no standing in the place the price destroys.

Let me be honest about the difficulty, because honest grief is the only kind worth writing down. Shale is capital-hungry and unforgiving. Scale genuinely lowers the cost per barrel. The independent working a marginal section against a bank line is not a realistic answer to a firm that can move four billion dollars in an afternoon, and nobody in this county who watched a dairy co-op get absorbed into a corporate processor needs a lecture on how scale beats root. But there is a difference between scale that produces and scale that only holds, and the difference is whether the ownership stays near the ground.

Devon just told Texas what its compensation metrics have been telling its shareholders for years. The shale industry's most dangerous habit is not overproduction. It is the quiet conviction that operating a productive basin and operating a public company are the same thing. They are not. One of them answers to the ground.

And the answer to the first is older than the press release. It gave rural America its electricity when no investor-owned utility would deign to string a line past the last section road: member-owned, governed one vote to a person, answerable to the people who live where the assets sit. In this industry it looks like the family mineral owner who keeps his royalty instead of selling it to a fund; the independent producer with a real balance sheet instead of a leveraged one; the service company owned by the men who run the service; the credit union and the community bank that lend against work rather than against a spreadsheet in Houston. It looks like the anti-monopoly tradition this country used to take seriously — Brandeis understood that bigness is a curse not because large firms are wicked, but because a firm big enough to divest a whole region in a single quarter is a firm no county can say no to.

Leave the town its life. It is not a complicated program. It is only the oldest one we have.
