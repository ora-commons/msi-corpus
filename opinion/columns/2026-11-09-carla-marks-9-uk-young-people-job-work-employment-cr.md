Britain’s youth jobs crisis is an investment failure, not a bill for families to pay. Almost a million young people are not in education, employment or training, and ministers are braced for Alan Milburn’s review to recommend welfare cuts to finance the remedy. Paul Nowak, the TUC’s general secretary, has told ministers to sharply increase investment in employment support, education and training, and not to make arbitrary cuts to vulnerable people without offering them a route into work. [The Guardian reported](https://www.theguardian.com/society/2026/nov/09/uk-young-people-job-work-employment-crisis-paul-nowak-milburn-review) that Nowak called this a “once in a generation chance”; [Main Street Independent’s account](https://mainstreetindependent.com/articles/2026-11-09-tuc-chief-urges-uk-to-expand-youth-employment-support/) carries the same warning. His point is not that youth employment is cost-free. “There is no solution that is cost-free,” he says. The point is that Britain must pay for the transition before shifting the bill onto families.

At the Magna centre in Sheffield, disabled and autistic young adults took part in an internship project. A place. Training. Support. Hours. That is what a route looks like.

Support has to meet people where they are. Some young people need disability accommodations, mental-health care, stable transport and help both before and after they start work. None of that is an argument for throwing them out of the plan. It is an argument for paying for it.

Nowak is right that the arithmetic is hard. A benefits bill that climbs with youth unemployment is a real fiscal fact. And the case for tougher benefit conditions is not nothing. A young person who can refuse every offer without consequence is not being helped into anything. The previous attempt at reform, under a Labour government, was a mess that ended in an expensive U-turn on disability-benefit cuts.

So the argument is not about whether there is a bill. It is about who gets handed it, and what they get in return.

The numbers show why the sequence matters. The government says it has pledged £2.5 billion for youth-employment support, expected to deliver almost 500,000 opportunities to earn and learn. It is also engaging businesses to create as many as 200,000 jobs. Those are real commitments. They are not the same thing.

A training referral is not a job. A subsidized vacancy with no commitments on pay, hours, training and retention is a public rebate for churn.

A course is not a job. A subsidized placement that ends with the worker discarded is a receipt with a ribbon on it.

If public money buys a decent wage, real training and a durable job, that is a defensible bargain. If it merely turns an existing promise into a press release, the family is poorer and the young person is still out of work. The bill has changed address. It did not vanish.

Which is how the household budget becomes the instrument of choice. It is the one pot nobody can refuse to fund and everybody can feel. The state cannot keep treating the household budget as the adjustable shock absorber for a labor-market failure it has not paid to fix.

Benefit cuts are not a funding strategy. They are a transfer of failure.

Nowak’s own fiscal argument makes the sequence plain. If young people are not supported, the benefits bill goes up. Cut support on the promise of future savings and the government risks making the bill worse before making the job. Milburn’s review has already been delayed until after the budget amid fears that a call for welfare cuts would sit uneasily beside the government’s spending plans. Apparently the machinery of fiscal responsibility requires careful timing.

Benefits are not rewards for failure. They are the floor beneath labor-market mobility. They are what lets a worker leave a rotten job, refuse unsafe work, or say no to an employer who knows the bills are due. Remove that floor and flexibility can become private blackmail: take what is offered or carry the sanction.

I grew up with the Swedish version of this. My cousins never once had to do the arithmetic of whether a sick kid was affordable that week.

The alternative already has a name: a youth guarantee. The government is discussing one. Work and Pensions Secretary Pat McFadden visited a Boots warehouse in Nottingham to discuss subsidized jobs for young people. The embryo exists. Make it a floor.

Every young person not in education, employment or training should receive a paid placement within a defined period. The placement should include a wage floor, a training plan, a named adviser and support for disability, health, caring and transport. There should be a clear route to a permanent contract. The young person should have obligations too: turn up, participate and use the support offered. A bargain worth the name has two sides.

Public money should buy obligations, not applause.

Tough rules without that route are not discipline. They are bookkeeping.

Andy Burnham has called the surge in young people not in education, employment or training “an economic catastrophe and a moral failure.” He is also backing tougher benefit rules. Both things are true. Together they can shame people who did not build the institutions that failed them. Unless a funded route comes first.

The Jobs Foundation campaign is a useful hand on the door. Focaldata polling for the charity found that most Britons rank the youth-jobs crisis second only to the cost of living, and above NHS waiting lists, housing, crime and immigration. The foundation, co-founded by Conservative peer Matthew Elliott, is backed by Amazon, Pret a Manger, NatWest, Marks & Spencer and Shell. This is not a party project. It has been a coalition waiting for a mechanism, and a guarantee can turn sponsor logos into enforceable commitments.

A roster of employers signing a pledge is not yet an institution. Each sector should sign an agreement with wages, training hours, retention and union involvement written down.

Make the sectoral floor part of the deal. One standard negotiated for an industry is rather different from asking every inexperienced young person to negotiate alone with an employer who knows they have less savings, fewer references and fewer places to go if the job goes wrong. The subsidy should purchase a floor, not a discount.

If an employer receives public money for hiring a young worker, that worker should receive the negotiated wage, training and sector conditions. The 200,000-jobs figure should not count internships that end. It should count jobs that continue.

If ministers want to say benefit cuts will help pay for a jobs guarantee, show the job first: a named employer, wage, training plan, support and the period during which the worker is expected to remain. Without that, the promise is another way to make hardship do the recruiting.

Now the mechanism, which is where most youth guarantees go to die. Let the guarantee handle the financing and administration. A public budget pays for the placement, the training and the wage floor. Let local-authority and college employment partnerships do the placing. They already know these young people and can tell the difference between someone who needs a bus fare and someone who needs a support worker in the room. Let sectoral bargaining set the terms, so no single firm can undercut the guarantee by paying less for the same work.

The harder part is institutional, and it is the part the money is supposed to buy. A guarantee pays for placements. It does not produce organized employers. In the United States, barely one worker in nine is covered by a collective agreement; Denmark covers more than four in five and got there with organized employers on the other side of the table and a century of patient institution-building. Britain needs that machinery too. It will not arrive because ministers discover a new word for it.

Denmark makes it easy to fire you. It spends roughly two percent of GDP moving laid-off workers into the next job, keeps income flowing while it happens and lets unions run the unemployment insurance, so joining one pays for itself. Protect the worker, not the job.

Britain can borrow that mechanism. Not the smugness. The Danes have their own housing shortages and their own ugly fights over who deserves what. None of that is a case for leaving almost a million young people outside work here.

Britain cannot announce Denmark on a whiteboard. It has to build the machine with employers, unions, colleges, local government and public money in the same room.

Transparency has its place. [Burnham’s proposed salary-publication rules](/articles/2026-11-06-burnham-to-require-employers-to-publish-salaries-on-job-adverts/) can make an offer legible, while [a national survey reporting record-low job satisfaction](/articles/2026-11-03-british-social-attitudes-survey-reports-record-low-job-satisfaction/) shows why people should not be told to feel lucky for any vacancy. But transparency without bargaining power is a sign in a window. It does not raise the wage or create the training place.

Where an employer needs patient money—a small firm that cannot carry a training budget on a thin margin—use public or cooperative finance. Britain has part of the chassis. The British Business Bank could carry patient finance, with repayment terms tied to an employer’s retention commitments. Community-share finance can serve firms not built to take ordinary bank debt. Specify who is responsible for what, because that is where guarantees die.

The public guarantee funds the placement and the wage floor. The development bank carries the finance. The local partnership places the young person. If the employer folds or walks, the young person returns to the guarantee with another placement, not onto the street.

The assumption that no alternative owner exists is not a fact. Cooperative Home Care Associates in the Bronx is a member-owned home-care business. It is not proof that cooperatives solve youth unemployment. It is proof that a member-owned firm can keep difficult work staffed. A British pilot could test whether that ownership and training structure helps young people rejected elsewhere.

Mondragon built a related kit in the Basque Country. When its flagship cooperative went bankrupt in 2013, about 1,700 of its 1,800 Spanish worker-members were relocated to other cooperatives through the federation’s own social-security fund. That is what a floor built out of solidarity looks like.

The cooperative is not magic. A firm can still fail, and a global co-op can still compromise its principles. But when the money runs out, worker ownership changes what happens to the people. That is the whole point.

It is harder here because those institutions have to be built rather than imported in a policy paragraph. But that difficulty is the work. It is not an excuse to send the bill to families.

Unemployment is not weather. It is a budget choice, and so is the decision about who pays to shorten it.

Ministers can set tougher conditions on benefits after they have shown the funded route. Until then, a cut is not a youth policy. It is a tax on a young household for a labor-market failure the government has not fully funded.

The alternative is already within reach: a funded youth guarantee reaching every young person not in education, employment or training; sector-wide pay floors; union-negotiated standards; patient public and cooperative finance; and a benefits system that supports the route instead of substituting for it.

Paid work. Training. Support. A future. That is what gets built.
