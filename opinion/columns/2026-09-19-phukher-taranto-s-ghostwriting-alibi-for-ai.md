In a piece published September 18 in the *Wall Street Journal*'s opinion section, the paper's editorial features editor argues that disclosure of AI assistance is unnecessary, that detection tools are unreliable, and that anyone who thinks otherwise is peddling "bad advice." The piece deploys at least six distinct rhetorical operations across its roughly 1,500 words; this column walks through them in the order Taranto uses them.

I worked that beat. I wrote talking points like his. I sat in rooms where the disclosure question came up and the answer was "don't," then watched the answer become the policy.

> "I use artificial intelligence when I write articles, including this one. I run partial or complete drafts through a chatbot and ask what it thinks. It spits out copious suggestions, some of which inspire me to make revisions and improvements. AI is also an immensely powerful research tool, able to find useful information via queries too vague or abstract to be effective in an ordinary Google search. For this essay, I used it to track down a magazine article I dimly remembered reading almost 40 years ago. As you'll see, that old piece turned out to contain a killer quote."
> *— Taranto, opening graf*

> "I ensure the integrity of my own work with a simple rule: I never paste AI output into a piece I'm writing (except when AI transcribes text from an image or recording, and then I check the quote against the source). Several months ago, Mr. Gigot and I discussed whether to adopt a policy on AI use by outside writers, as other newspapers have done. The New York Times op-ed contract provides: 'As a freelance contributor to The Times, you may not submit any material for publication that contains content generated, modified or enhanced by gen-A.I. tools.' I argued that attempting to police outsiders would be too cumbersome and that we don't yet know enough about AI to make sensible uniform rules governing its use. And although I believe professional writers have an ethical obligation to write our own words, I don't think that's true of every op-ed contributor."
> *— Taranto, second section*

**Narrow rule, broad practice** — operates here through a rule drawn narrowly enough that it cannot be falsified, deployed as cover for a practice the column never names. Taranto does not paste AI output into his pieces. Taranto does run partial or complete drafts through a chatbot and incorporates the "suggestions." The narrow rule is defensible in a way the broad practice is not, and the column's defense rests entirely on the narrow rule staying narrow while the broad practice continues.

The outside-contributor carve-out is the second half of the same structure: WSJ has a rule for staff; WSJ has no rule for the building's other tenants; the no-rule zone is where the bylines that drive the page's traffic actually live. > "A related and similarly candid acknowledgment came from Kyle Crichton, deputy op-ed editor of the New York Times: 'If we insisted that every politician write his own piece, we'd either get terrible pieces or none at all.' **Advantageous comparison** — Bandura mechanism 3; the operators call it false analogy — operates across the column's longest stretch. Taranto retrieves a 1988 *New Republic* essay on plagiarism to argue AI disclosure is unnecessary because human ghostwriting has never required it. Ari Posner's "spectrum of respectability" provides the gradient. The conclusion follows: "There's no obvious reason the same distinctions shouldn't apply when the ghostwriter is a machine rather than a human."

The advantage Taranto is taking is the conflation. A human speechwriter working from notes — who can be sued for libel if the work product is defamatory, who collects a paycheck, has a name, a reputation, an editor, a point of view — is not the same operation as an opaque large language model trained on the entire copyright-bearing corpus of human writing, generating text the user then evaluates, accepts, rejects, and modifies. One ghost is a person. The other is a statistical machine that synthesizes from millions of people's work without their consent. The cases are not analogous — but the analogy's advantages for Taranto's argument are real. If 1988 ghostwriting was fine, 2026 AI assistance is fine.
