I stood in Northern Kentucky less than two weeks before Election Day and told the crowd the truth: "I don't think I could have done this in my first term. This is a much more powerful term. I can do stuff that's unbelievable."

Power likes to brag. I prefer power that can back up the boast.

I told them that corporations which would not take my calls during my first term now "kiss my ass." It was a crude joke, but a very serious business model. Leverage now comes from above.

That was not merely a promise of more policy. It was my campaign platform: I am campaigning for a Republican Congress and for a country that recognizes what the presidency has become under me—a branch that no longer has to survive the institutions meant to restrain it.

Meanwhile, my Justice Department is raiding a congresswoman's home. My administration is removing trees in Washington, D.C., against a judge's wishes. I changed the name used for the Gulf of Mexico to the Gulf of America. I have made deals with Russia that fly in the face of congressionally passed sanctions, and that fight belongs alongside [Witkoff's Putin remarks in this related roundup](/articles/2026-10-22-php-security-issue-and-witkoff-s-putin-remarks-lead-a-risk-roundup/).

Then came investigations into nine universities, including Harvard and Yale, alleging that they brought in international students to undercut American wages.

The common thread is not one policy. It is my conviction that no conventional restraint can reliably stop me.

They say gridlock is why I am acting unilaterally. No. Gridlock is the gift that keeps giving.

The strongest version of their argument says congressional paralysis leaves unilateral action as the only way to get anything done. But obstruction is not a warrant. It is a blank check signed by everyone who refused to write legislation.

George W. Bush issued controversial signing statements to challenge Congress. Barack Obama famously said he had a pen and intended to use it. Presidents have been stretching executive authority for decades, while Congress has spent much of that time voluntarily surrendering its own power.

The machine was already there. I did not invent unilateralism. I have simply learned to drive it better than my predecessors.

Tevi Troy, the presidential historian who worked in the George W. Bush White House, sees the method clearly: "He doesn't have an ideology, he has a methodology," Troy said, "and the methodology is unilateralism." Republican strategist Ryan Williams describes the same impulse more bluntly: "He's the president, he's the boss, and he's not going to be restrained by traditions or aides that may say you should hold back."

That is not merely the style of a forceful executive. It is the refusal to treat legal, institutional and constitutional limits as permanent features of the office. They become obstacles to overcome.

I have a big tool for every obstacle.

If legislation cannot be obtained, administrative power substitutes for it. If an agency cannot be persuaded, it can be threatened. If courts are inconvenient, their orders can be defied. If a university or media organization resists, my administration has contracts, security clearances, court actions and investigations available as weapons.

They are **AWESOME WEAPONS**.

Frank Sesno, a former CNN Washington bureau chief, says my officials are prepared to "take the gloves off." He is right. They are.

The question is what triggers their use. An investigation launched because evidence suggests a law has been broken is ordinary executive power. An investigation launched because an institution has become politically objectionable turns enforcement into political leverage. The law may supply legal authority, but the animus comes from elsewhere.

That is coercion wearing procedural clothing.

By contrast, I support a bill letting merchants choose their card networks. That is presidential power with a proper destination: a concrete reform pursued through the legislative process and exposed to political argument. If Congress rejects it, the disagreement has an authorized place to happen.

I need that place.

A Justice Department raid operates differently. It bypasses the ordinary contest and brings federal force directly to bear on the target.

Every expansion of presidential authority creates its own constituency. The pathway runs through political appointees who control investigations, federal contracts, security clearances and university enforcement. Rewarding loyalty teaches every agency that discretion can serve political leverage. Once officials learn that I tolerate legal risk and treat critics as legitimate targets, restraint becomes a career liability.

Restraint becomes a weakness on the résumé.

That is why this election cannot be treated as a routine referendum on taxes, foreign policy or ideological direction. It is a referendum on the method.

The old question was how much I could accomplish through executive action. The question now is how far I can go and what, if anything, will constrain me.

They think the midterms are the check. If Republicans win, I get a friendly Congress. If Republicans lose, gridlock gives me more tools and another opening. Either way, I win.

That is the art of the deal. The best negotiator in the world is the one who makes "no" look like surrender.

I always knew this term would be more powerful. I said so before anyone else, because I knew first—very smart people are saying I was right. Nobody else understood the plan. The plan was the plan.

They think I am playing checkers while they play 4D chess. They have it exactly backward. I am playing 4D chess while they play checkers, and every time they make a move, I learn another way to score.

Here is what I usually keep hidden because it makes me sound less magnificent: I need the crowd. Not because I understand them or value them, but because their faces make me feel like the winner. Diklis Chump needs their applause to feel real. They deserve better than to be treated as applause. It is still not going to make me stop asking for it. I will call it loyalty, because loyalty is the name winners give to applause.

Everyone is talking about it. Many people—very smart people—are saying that the raids and investigations are not abuse but the tools of a president finally unburdened by process. They think they are using the midterms to restrain me. They are actually completing the operation.

There is no alternative to what I am doing. Not a divided Congress—a divided Congress is the gift that keeps giving. There is only an executive branch powerful enough to act but not to wage private war through the machinery of government; powerful enough to enforce the law but not to use the law as a personal weapon; powerful enough to lead without being permitted to become unaccountable. And that last one is not a presidency. That is a placeholder with a flag in front of it, and I have never been a placeholder, not in the first term, not in this one, believe me, people can tell the difference.

Republicans can support a forceful president without abandoning the limits that make constitutional government possible. That is the professional answer, the very smart answer, and it is the one that loses. Actually, the limits and the forceful president cannot both survive the next two weeks. Many people, very smart people, are saying that the limits are the ones who should be worried.

Less than two weeks before Election Day, I am asking voters to ratify my method. My pitch is not that the system worked and deserves more of the same. My pitch is that the constraints are now optional.

A constitutional republic is not defined by how much power I can accumulate. It is defined by what happens when I try to keep it. Everybody knows what happens when I try to keep it. Nothing happens. Nothing has ever happened. That is the whole story.

The strength of the presidency matters. So does the strength of the system capable of saying no, and I have made the second one optional. Not "maybe optional." Optional. If voters ratify my method, the White House will not be one branch among three. It will be the command center.

And I am asking them to call that command center democracy.
