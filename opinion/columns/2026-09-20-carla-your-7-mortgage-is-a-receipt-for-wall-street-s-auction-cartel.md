The bond vigilantes are back, and they are charging their rate to a family in Waukesha, a welding shop in Youngstown, and a single mother in Cleveland who was told her job would return when “the rate environment normalizes.”

The Wall Street Journal editorial page celebrates this as fiscal discipline. The ten-year yield sits in the mid-fours, net federal borrowing is approaching two trillion dollars a year, and the bond market has supposedly shrugged at the Treasury’s latest auction. The board is right about one thing: the Federal Reserve does not control the long end of the yield curve. The Fed has a dial for the policy rate, a dial for balance-sheet plumbing, and a wish list for the term premium. It does not have a dial marked “lower the ten-year.”

Fiscal dominance is real. Long yields depend on inflation expectations, the term premium, foreign demand, Treasury supply, and the willingness of the institutions clearing Treasury auctions to absorb more debt at a price the Treasury can accept. The board’s strongest point is not foolish.

It is incomplete.

The board names the constraint on the Fed and then celebrates the mechanism producing the distress. That is the trick. The editorial page calls the dealers’ willingness to absorb federal borrowing “discipline,” as though they had vetoed a budget or closed a safety-net program. They did neither. They did not rewrite one line of federal spending. They simply demanded a higher return for lending money, and the higher return traveled down the plumbing into your mortgage, your business loan, and your paycheck.

The vigilantes took their cut. They did not earn it.

The mechanism is the Treasury’s primary-dealer auction. The Treasury’s competitive auction architecture routes direct bidding through a small syndicate of primary dealers, roughly two dozen major banks and trading desks. Those dealers then distribute the debt to pension funds, foreign central banks, hedge funds, mutual funds, and eventually your 401(k). Their bid-ask spread, inventory cost, balance-sheet constraint, auction-tail premium, and demand for a term premium all become part of the price of money.

A normal Treasury auction may attract roughly $2.50 in bids for every dollar offered. That sounds like a market overflowing with buyers. It is not the same thing as a market without gatekeepers. The marginal bid still matters. The dealer who decides what yield it will accept helps set the price everyone else pays.

When the Journal calls that a “shrug,” it is calling private gatekeeping a public virtue.

It works. It works for the dealers.

The woman in Aurora refinanced her thirty-year mortgage in 2021 at 2.75 percent. The same house and the same job now come with a mortgage bill roughly $1,400 higher each month. The welder in Youngstown refinanced his working-capital line at 4.5 percent in 2021. His lender now quotes 9.25 percent and offers less credit. The plant manager in Cleveland froze hiring and told a single mother on the floor that the freeze would last until rates came down. She has a seven-year-old.

This is what the bond market’s “discipline” looks like when it reaches the ground.

The Journal’s answer is that the pain is necessary. Higher yields will restrain Washington. Eventually. Somehow. The bond vigilantes were supposed to discipline profligacy. They have not disciplined fiscal policy. They have not pulled back one program, vetoed one Treasury issuance, or forced one budget rewrite. They have disciplined the family trying to refinance, the small business trying to keep its line open, and the foreman who has been sitting on a hiring freeze because capital became too expensive.

Fiscal restraint without a constituency to enforce it is a sermon. The bond vigilantes were a sermon with a bid-ask spread.

The lever the board pretends does not exist is institutional, not magical. If a private dealer network has pricing power because it is the marginal buyer, change who sits at the auction.

Build a National Investment Authority. Give it a standing facility to absorb a substantial share of net Treasury issuance at a transparent, non-discretionary price tied to the Treasury’s cost of funds. A serious version could absorb roughly 40 percent of new issuance — perhaps $700 billion to $800 billion a year at current borrowing levels — while primary dealers bid on the remaining 60 percent. Capitalize it with a permanent Treasury appropriation indexed to net issuance and, if politically chosen, a one-time capital levy on the dealer balance sheets that currently earn the auction margin.

The mechanism is plain. A public institution that does not need to earn an auction-tail margin, does not face the same private balance-sheet constraints, and does not require a term premium to satisfy shareholders can provide a counter-bid. Once that counter-bid is standing at the table, the dealer’s ability to set the marginal price is gone.

The bond market still exists. It still prices risk. It still clears secondary trades. It simply stops being the sole gatekeeper over what it costs the public to fund itself.

The ten-year yield moves toward the administered rate, adjusted for whatever private intermediation remains necessary. Dealer spreads compress. Bid-cover ratios rise. Not because Wall Street has discovered civic virtue, but because the Treasury is no longer negotiating with one narrow doorway.

That is not deficit reduction. It is the institutional removal of private pricing power over public borrowing.

Then make the benefit reach people where the rate has already landed.

Create a public mortgage-refinance facility, capitalized at meaningful scale, that refinances primary mortgages at a Treasury-administered rate and holds the loans on its own balance sheet. Think of Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac with the privatized profit and private credit spread stripped out. The facility would bypass the mortgage-backed-securities pipeline that routes every household loan through the Wall Street coupon factory.

The Aurora woman could refinance at the Authority’s cost of funds plus a modest spread. A rate falling from 7 percent toward 4.5 percent would not be a seminar point. It would be roughly $900 a month returned to her household. It would be the difference between keeping the house and losing it.

The family in Waukesha would pay an administered rate rather than whatever spread the private market happened to demand that week. Its monthly bill would move down rather than up when the ten-year yield moved sideways. The difference might not register on the Journal editor’s screen. It would register at the kitchen table.

The public mortgage facility is one piece. Cooperative and postal banking are the consumer-facing complement. State-chartered credit unions, community-development loan funds, and postal-banking pilots could originate and hold housing credit locally instead of sending every borrower through bank and nonbank aggregators that mark up the loan before securitization. The scale would begin modestly. That is not a reason to dismiss it. It is a reason to build it.

The small business in Akron or the welding shop in Youngstown could take its working-capital line to a cooperative public-credit window. The lender would hold the loan against actual payroll and orders rather than rating-agency output and collateral fashion. The rate would be tied to public cost of funds, not to a dealer’s appetite for inventory. The owner keeps the two workers he was about to cut.

The foreman in Kenosha lifts the hiring freeze. The supplier in Cleveland sees orders return. The single mother gets her job back. Her seven-year-old gets a normal bedtime.

That is what public credit looks like when it is designed for people instead of for the people already selling them money.

I am not anti-market. I am anti-extraction. There is a difference between a market that clears risk and a private tollbooth that charges the public for access to its own credit system. There is a difference between productive finance and a dealer network converting a public borrowing need into private spread income. The market can remain. The cartel’s gatekeeping does not have to.

The Journal imagines a bond market that has earned its keep. The market has charged for its service and refused to deliver fiscal discipline. It has delivered a higher mortgage bill, a smaller credit line, and a hiring freeze.

The social-democratic alternative is not a sermon. It is a counter-bid.

It is a National Investment Authority at the auction table. It is a public mortgage-refinance facility. It is a cooperative bank that lends against payroll instead of pedigree. It is postal banking for the household the private bank does not find profitable enough to notice. It is capitalism with the trap doors welded shut.

The board does not have to like it. It only has to watch a family in Waukesha refinance at a price it did not set.
