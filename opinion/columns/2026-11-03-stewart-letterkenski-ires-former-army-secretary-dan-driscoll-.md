OpenAI hired former Army Secretary Dan Driscoll to market military AI to governments.

On Monday, OpenAI announced that Dan Driscoll, who served as the Army’s civilian head for eighteen months, would advise the company on ways artificial intelligence could strengthen US security capabilities and cyber defences and encourage US allies to adopt advanced AI systems. The [Wall Street Journal reported](https://www.wsj.com/politics/national-security/openai-hires-former-army-secretary-dan-driscoll-to-cybersecurity-role-0e5cc0a1) that the appointment landed amid OpenAI’s push to deepen its integration with the defence sector, secure government contracts, and shape policy around military and intelligence uses of AI.

The documented remit is advisory and promotional. The commercial ambition is larger.

“Modernization” is the word that makes a sales job sound like public service.

Sam Altman placed the commercial objective inside a democratic frame. The United States, he said, has “an extraordinary opportunity to work with other countries to spread the adoption of democratic AI.” There are two claims in that sentence. One concerns technical capability. The other concerns who writes the requirements, owns the infrastructure, and collects the resulting demand. OpenAI hired Driscoll for the second claim.

It is true that the appointment is intelligible in the narrow sense in which appointments are intelligible. Driscoll is an Iraq War veteran. He pushed soldiers to adopt new battlefield technology, championed wider access to drones and counter-drone systems among American allies, and helped spearhead multibillion-dollar partnerships involving base infrastructure and domestic logistics. He also travelled to Kyiv to discuss a Washington-backed proposal concerning the war in Ukraine.

These are not decorative qualifications. They are precisely the qualifications OpenAI wants.

Earlier MSI reporting on [Driscoll’s resignation amid his clash with Defence Secretary Pete Hegseth over the pace of reform](/articles/2026-09-08-army-secretary-driscoll-resigns-amid-clash-with-hegseth-over-reform-pace/) reinforces the point. He is not a ceremonial retired general. He is a former government buyer with a record of reshaping military priorities and turning them into institutional programmes. A former Army secretary who can speak in the language of battlefield need, logistics, drones, base infrastructure, and allied interoperability is not merely a prestige hire. He is capable of translating operational pain into procurement demand.

Nothing in the public record proves a quid pro quo, and none is alleged here.

Driscoll is also Vice President JD Vance’s law-school classmate. That is a real personal connection. It is not evidence that Vance grants access, transmits policy, or affects a procurement decision. No such chain appears in the public record.

Distinguish what OpenAI announced from what the appointment makes possible. OpenAI has not said Driscoll will help draft live requirements, take part in source selection—the government’s formal comparison of competing bids—or sell directly to a government acquisition office. No public evidence establishes that he will. Nor has OpenAI announced that he will receive classified access or delegated procurement authority. Those are prospective conflicts, not established acts.

The present alignment of interests is still substantial. OpenAI pays a former government buyer to help it understand security needs and promote allied adoption of its products. That is a present conflict of interest, though not proof of a corrupt procurement process. Whether it becomes corruption depends on whether anyone establishes a firewall between commercial advice and public purchasing.

In the risk case, the procurement hinge is simple. If a vendor’s former insider helps identify which problems matter most to buyers, how those buyers express their requirements, and where bureaucratic resistance lies, the vendor can design both its product and its sales approach around the preferred specification. The government then measures competing bids against a requirement that already resembles the incumbent’s architecture. The formal cost estimate and source-selection comparison can become a route for confirming a preference already planted.

That is how procurement influence can migrate without a formal grant of authority. A former official’s institutional judgment can shape what a vendor understands the customer to require. Nothing more nefarious than ordinary alignment of interest is required.

On paper, the former head of the government customer is now the seller’s national-security salesman. What he has not been given is authority over a live procurement. What OpenAI has is his understanding of the customer.

The announcement does not say Driscoll will receive classified access, and the role as described does not require it. It does not need it. The commercially valuable part is what he already knows: which military problems are urgent, which offices determine requirements, which bureaucratic obstacles delay adoption, which allied governments have influence, and what language sounds urgent to each of them. That is not stolen intelligence. It is the working knowledge of a large public organisation now employed by a private company competing to sell that organisation new systems.

Here the marketing language needs to be made boring. An AI model is a trained statistical system that predicts or transforms text, code, audio, or images. It is not, by itself, a cyber-defence establishment. The deployed product is considerably larger than the model: the model, the data fed into it, links to other systems, identity controls, permissions, logs, software updates, monitoring, and the people authorised to act on its outputs.

The target is not the model. It is the supplier’s architecture concealed beneath the word “model.”

Procurement language that names only the model leaves every one of those architectural choices unexamined. A model can perform an impressive malware analysis and still sit inside an insecure system. Malicious instructions hidden in retrieved documents can manipulate a model that processes them—a technical failure called prompt injection. Stolen credentials can permit changes nobody traces. Excessive permissions can turn one mistaken output into one consequential action. These are not decorative qualifications; they are the system boundary.

AI does not float free of labour, material infrastructure, energy, data, and institutions. A claim that a model will strengthen cyber defence is a claim about that whole arrangement. A safety property cannot be verified until somebody writes the specification: which attacks the system must resist, which failures are tolerable, who may use its outputs, and what happens when the underlying model or connector changes.

Because Driscoll’s remit includes cyber defence, these omissions are not abstract pedantry. They are the difference between buying a capability and buying a dependency with the capability.

Earlier MSI coverage of [Australia’s order that agencies audit legacy systems after an OpenAI breach](/articles/2026-10-02-australia-orders-agencies-to-audit-legacy-systems-after-openai-breach/) is the necessary warning. A modern model interface does not cleanse old identity, access, and data-handling paths. New intelligence is frequently bolted onto legacy systems whose permissions, logs, and failure modes remain poorly understood. The audit requirement is where responsible deployment begins.

These systems can do useful work. That is exactly why sound procurement rules cannot wait. Driscoll says national-security requirements are “changing faster than at any time in the past 50 years,” and AI can ease administrative workloads, accelerate the analysis of information, and help officials obtain critical answers faster. The trouble is that “AI” specifies neither what is being secured nor who can inspect the system doing the securing.

A government that needs faster answers cannot justify making those answers depend indefinitely on one supplier’s roadmap.

Once an agency builds its incident procedures, evidence formats, evaluation tests, access controls, and training around one vendor’s model, switching is no longer a matter of changing service providers. A rival system must reproduce the connectors, pass the accumulated tests, satisfy security approvals, and retrain the people who learned to work with the first one. The dependency is technical, contractual, and institutional, which means it will not appear in the procurement announcement.

Tim Wu’s *The Curse of Bigness* offers a useful analogy: private scale can harden into private government when public institutions cannot effectively answer it. Driscoll’s appointment offers a smaller and more literal version of that danger. OpenAI is not merely asking what the Army wants. It has hired the former official who helped set the Army’s priorities, and whose judgment now helps a private company decide how to market military AI back into public defence. The danger is not that OpenAI has become the government. It is that the boundary between vendor and requirement-writer has become convenient to blur.

Lina Khan’s *Amazon’s Antitrust Paradox* supplies another precise tool: concentrated purchasing power can shape supplier incentives and certify a preferred architecture. The danger here is the combination of that purchasing power with architectural certification. When the government selects one vendor, writes deployment policy around that vendor, and then treats the resulting integration as irreversible, it can suppress alternative suppliers while making itself dependent on the one it favoured.

The government becomes the customer whose specifications certify the supplier. The contract then performs market-making that an ordinary sales campaign could not.

Cory Doctorow’s four forces—competition, regulation, self-help through interoperability, and labour leverage—keep a service from degrading. A defence contract can weaken every one. Competition becomes a single awarded vendor, with every rival forced to win requalification against the incumbent. Regulation becomes certification of the winner’s own design rather than an independent check. Self-help disappears when data, workflows, and evaluations are proprietary. Labour leverage disappears when military and civilian users become dependent on the supplier’s roadmap. This is not ordinary vendor preference. It is a chokepoint assembled through procurement.

There is an old local version of this arrangement. My father kept his job at Manitoba Rolling Mills when Gerdau bought the plant in 1995; several of my uncles did not. The production line did not become more competitive because the letterhead changed. What changed was who understood the buyer, who decided what the plant would make, and which memories travelled with the acquisition.

OpenAI does not have to wait for personnel to rotate through the bureaucracy. It can hire the institutional memory outright. The familiar pipeline runs public money through risky research into private firms carrying profitable scale. In this appointment, OpenAI has reversed the direction of travel: public judgement becomes private sales intelligence.

The machinery can now generate its own requirements.

In Harold Innis’s staples analysis, control of communications infrastructure distributes power beyond the transaction in which the equipment is sold. An allied government may remain formally sovereign while becoming operationally dependent on an American vendor’s model, update regime, telemetry, and technical support. The Canadian parallel is not that Ottawa should nationalise a chatbot. It is that durable access, interoperability, and enforceable public duties must be written before communications infrastructure becomes indispensable.

That is why “spread the adoption of democratic AI” is a policy problem rather than merely a phrase from a press release. Washington is also [reportedly preparing to centralise AI policy at the centre](/articles/2026-10-02-trump-expected-to-name-dni-jay-clayton-as-white-house-ai-czar/). Centralised policy without a procurement firewall will simply make the conflict of interest easier to industrialise.

Democracy is not a model nationality, an adjective attached to a vendor’s preferred architecture, or an assurance supplied by the seller. Can the purchaser audit the deployed system? Can it determine where information goes and how long it is retained? Can it obtain the model’s update history, security testing, and failure reports? Can it challenge an output without becoming dependent on the vendor for the machinery of challenge? Can it leave? Can a rival replace the system without rebuilding the government’s work? Can elected authority change the requirement after the vendor has made the requirement expensive?

The answer cannot depend on OpenAI’s goodwill, because the company’s commercial interest is aligned with making its own technology the default. The allied-adoption part of Driscoll’s remit makes that preference structural. If defence ministries adopt the same proprietary stack, a future decision to choose a rival must overcome more than a price difference and a benchmark. It must overcome inherited interfaces, test suites, training, security approvals, and operational habits. OpenAI need not own every component if it owns the interfaces through which the rest of the system must be reached. The sales channel becomes a chokepoint by accumulation.

Government need for speed does not justify surrendering the market to one supplier. The governing rule must be replaceability: no defence deployment without a tested exit path, portable evidence, and enforceable termination rights. Open interfaces, exportable logs and evaluation results, data minimisation, independently run security testing, and human authority over lethal uses are conditions of that rule, not a decorative list of best practices. Termination rights must be backed by portable system settings and data. Interoperability is what keeps public procurement from becoming a permanent private chokepoint.

OpenAI may sell systems to the Pentagon. It must not be permitted to buy the official who writes the requirements and sell the requirements back. Before the next defence-AI task order, Congress should require conflict disclosures, a cooling-off period for former senior officials, and a firewall between their private work and any live defence requirement. Independent government cost estimates and source-selection reviews should test—not ratify—the specification. Every contract should carry independent security audits, incident reporting, open interfaces, and enforceable exit rights.

Those lines attack the hinge. They keep the person who knows the customer’s map from writing the benchmark, pricing the requirement, and winning the order.

Until those lines are in the contract, Driscoll is a salesman travelling with the customer’s map and meeting calendar. A democratic defence system should require him to leave a copy of the keys before he carries away the map.
