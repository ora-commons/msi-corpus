The Reserve Bank of Australia is robbing Australian families with five interest-rate hikes.

At eleven at night, my kitchen-table spreadsheet asks one question: what can this household pay without breaking the next month? Australia has an answer this week: $107 more a month on Canstar’s $700,000 home-loan example. Canstar estimates the latest RBA increase adds $107 a month; for that same $700,000 loan, the five increases this year have raised the monthly repayment by $532—$6,384 over a year. Those are the example loan’s numbers, not a flat charge on every borrower, but they show the squeeze the policy is producing before a household can put a dollar toward food, care, utilities, or savings.

The arithmetic is not identical for every borrower. A fixed-rate mortgage does not reset when the central bank moves; a variable-rate borrower meets the increase when the loan resets or is refinanced. Savings, fixed rates, and family help can cushion the shock, but they are cushions, not the rule. That is not mercy. It is the extraction mechanism: the RBA can reprice the variable-rate slice of the market while leaving families with existing fixed debt outside the immediate increase.

The fifth rise this year lifted the cash rate from 4.6% to 4.85%, its highest level since 2008. The monetary policy board split 6-3: six for another hike, three for a hold. By August, [Australian inflation had already risen to 4%](https://mainstreetindependent.com/articles/2026-09-30-australian-inflation-rises-to-4-in-august-abs-says/); by September, it reached 4.3%. The first increase to 4.6% had been [widely expected](https://mainstreetindependent.com/articles/2026-09-28-rba-widely-expected-to-raise-cash-rate-to-4-6-on-tuesday/), and recent comments by senior central-bank officials pointed the same way. The market understood the direction. It underestimated the accumulation.

The RBA says it is doing what it considers necessary to bring inflation sustainably back to target. Its defense is true where it can be true. Rates can restrain demand and the second-round inflation that follows an oil shock. They cannot produce oil or make it cheaper.

The shock is imported. The global oil surge produced by the war in the Middle East is expected to ease over 18 months, but prices are expected to remain elevated throughout that period. The RBA can raise a benchmark; banks can reprice variable loans. That converts an imported energy shock into a domestic repayment shock for borrowers at reset or refinancing. More than 3.3 million households with home loans sit inside the decision’s reach, even though the immediate cash-flow change falls on the variable-rate subset when a loan resets or is refinanced.

The RBA’s own *Statement on Monetary Policy* makes the transfer harder to defend. Public demand was weaker than expected through the first six months of 2026 as cost-of-living measures rolled off and spending in defense, disability, and aged care moderated. The RBA forecasts growth of 1.6% this year and 1.3% next year against potential growth near 2%. Unemployment is expected to rise from 4.7% now to around 4.9% next year and above 5% in 2028.

This is a central bank tightening into weakening demand. The line item that does not show up in the forecast is what each family gives up after the mortgage payment.

The RBA now expects inflation to fall below 3% only by the end of 2027, nine months later than previously expected. The lag arrives on the repayment schedule. The statement did not explicitly point to another hike, but five back-to-back increases have already done the work. The bank raises rates again, choosing mortgage households to finance the demand restraint. The RBA says inflation will fall later; families make the sacrifice now.

The legal label is monetary policy. The household label is extraction. The RBA has chosen which families finance the inflation fight: the ones carrying mortgage debt. Every extra dollar of repayment comes out of cash for everything else while the household carries a shrinking share of its own income. That is not a failure of household planning. It is a policy extracting money from households to purchase an inflation target.

What looks like economic inevitability is a policy decision. The RBA’s forecast makes that decision visible in monetary language: weaker demand now, slower growth, a higher jobless rate, softer housing, and lower inflation later. The immediate burden has been assigned in advance to Australian mortgage holders.

The bank also identifies a possible housing downturn as one of the steepest over the past 50 years, while saying it sees no evidence Australia is headed into recession over the coming two years. So no recession slogan. The forecast is more ordinary and more brutal: weak growth through 2028, rising unemployment, softer housing, and a 4.85% benchmark carried by borrowers.

Who really pays? Variable-rate households when their loans reset or are refinanced; across the wider borrower population, higher rates reduce the room for everything else. On every reset or refinancing, a larger share of a variable borrower’s payment goes to interest rather than consumption. That is the transfer: the household loses spending power to the financial system while the original energy bill keeps rising.

The six-vote majority sets the price; households absorb the interest, cut purchases, and carry the oil shock’s domestic bill. That is the suppressed variable: a concentrated decision produces a diffuse cost, and the forecast prints it as a number on the first page while the household meets it as $107 every month.

Anne Helen Petersen wrote that millennials learned to regard themselves as human capital. The RBA’s version of the human-capital bargain is more literal: the variable-rate household is marked up whenever inflation becomes inconvenient. It must keep earning returns whether or not anyone in the house has received a raise. Three board members voted to hold. The six who voted up did not have to carry a dollar of the repayment increase, and borrowers will. No amount of resilience talk changes who signed the mortgage and whose wages absorb the interest.

Taylor Swift’s “Anti-Hero” catches the next step in the mechanism: depression works the graveyard shift, and then the exhausted speaker treats the damage as a personal defect. That is how structural pressure becomes household self-blame. Here, the macro decision arrives as $107 missing from the month. Groceries shrink, fuel waits, school costs get squeezed, and a job search becomes another line of defense. The household becomes the shock absorber.

No spreadsheet, productivity ritual, or individual fix cures a policy that adds $6,384 a year to Canstar’s $700,000-loan example. The household is not failing to optimize. It is being charged.

A collective safety net cannot be converted into a private improvisation problem without treating households as shock absorbers. The RBA’s five hikes turn a public inflation problem into a private mortgage problem. That is why the title belongs beside the Canstar estimate: the institution sets the terms of the shock, and the household is left to perform resilience.

A genuinely pro-family policy can pause before another hike, protect variable-rate borrowers from further resets, use direct energy and fiscal measures rather than place a lien on every mortgage, and provide childcare, paid leave, and targeted support for borrowers hit by the oil shock. Those measures are not substitutes for inflation control; they are how a government stops inflation control from becoming family insolvency.

Leo XIII’s *Rerum Novarum* asks whether wages support a family; the RBA is shrinking the income left after debt service even when nominal wages do not move. Shelter and feeding people belong in the same moral sentence as responsibility. The RBA’s schedule does the reverse: it makes households finance the repair, then calls the strained payment responsible budgeting.

At the kitchen table, the spreadsheet has three columns: money in, money out, money left. The latest increase raises repayments for more than 3.3 million Australian home-loan households. For Canstar’s $700,000-home-loan example, the third column now carries another $532 each month across this year’s five increases—$6,384 each year.

The RBA is robbing Australian families to fight an inflation shock its interest-rate tool cannot end. The legal mechanism is monetary policy. The household calls it the mortgage payment—the cost of keeping a roof while the central bank decides who pays for oil. The RBA has its forecast. A household has its spreadsheet. They land on the same kitchen table, and somebody has to make the numbers work alone.
