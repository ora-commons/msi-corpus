Here is the irony nobody at the victory party will name: the movements that won the midterms won them by rebuilding the neighborhood — the packed hearing, the county ordinance, the parish-hall teach-in, the mutual-aid roster — and their first act as victors is to wire every volt of it back to Washington. That is how mediating institutions die. Not by storm; by deposit. Writing in The Guardian, [Fabiola Cineas reports](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2026/nov/13/us-midterms-democrats-accountability) that Democrats flipped five governors' seats and took the House and the Senate by wide margins on anger at prices, at war, at masked agents in the streets and datacenters in the back pastures — and that the organizers who built the wave now mean to press the newly elected to [deliver on the priorities they have already set out](/articles/2026-11-04-democrats-set-post-election-priorities-on-affordability-and-accountability/). Every organizer, says the Princeton professor Keeanga-Yamahtta Taylor, must arrive with a Wednesday agenda. The report they have handed us is an argument against the frame they have built around it.

Give these people their due before anything else, because they have earned it. When forty-one datacenter measures reached the ballot in ten states this fall and went against the industry in all but one, that was not a hashtag. That was retirees who learned to read a county code, school parents who sat through the third hour of a public hearing, farmers who could name the aquifer, and — the detail that should embarrass both national parties — mothers who disagree on everything else but stood in the same meeting room, what one organizer called "Maga moms." The demands fit on a petition and pointed at a thing: pause construction until the impacts are studied; make the developers pay the full cost of the grid upgrades instead of billing them to the taxpayers; end the tax breaks; let the town vote before anything is built. People felt, some for the first time in years, that they could stop something — *I can stop that datacenter* — which is the oldest sensation in democratic life and the one the capital is least able to simulate. Nobody in Washington did that. Nobody in Washington could have. And Taylor is right about the thing most people get wrong: "Elections themselves don't achieve what it is that people want… They set the table for the possibility of change." Jamila Raqib, who studies nonviolent action, is righter still — people are "more engaged than ever before… but they're less often achieving concrete objectives." An election is a permission slip, not a deliverable, and anyone who treats Tuesday as the finish line deserves to lose on Thursday. The Minnesota general strike — tens of thousands in subzero cold under "No Work, No School, No Shopping" — did not fall from the sky; Cineas traces it to years of labor, faith and community organizing, legal observers trained through immigrant-rights groups, rapid-response networks that documented the agents, congregations that opened their doors. That is the only thing that has ever been anything. Institutions live only as long as someone shows up to maintain them, which happens to be the first thing Edmund Burke taught — society is "a partnership between those who are living, those who are dead, and those who are to be born," and partnerships do not maintain themselves.

But look at where the victories live, and then look at where the ambitions live. Every deliverable in this piece has a zip code: the moratorium petition, the ordinance, the teach-in held with faith leaders, the workplace safety plan and the sign in the shop window. The demands with no address are the national ones. Read the slate the Democratic Socialists are sending to Congress — Medicare for All, the abolition of ICE, an arms embargo, an end to wars and sanctions — and note the address on every instrument: federal. Not one can be wielded from a county commission, a city ordinance, a co-op board, or a kitchen table. [The affordability agenda already being drafted](/articles/2026-11-09-democrats-prepare-affordability-agenda-after-the-midterm-win/) is another list written for Washington. The whole program instructs the neighborhoods that just proved their competence to form a line, hold out their hands, and wait for a distant class of administrators to do their governing for them. Nobody in this article explains how the local becomes the national, because it doesn't.

My tradition put a number on this error ninety-five years ago and has never retracted it. *Quadragesimo Anno*, 1931: it is "a grave evil" to assign to a higher authority what lesser communities can do for themselves. Pius XI was not scoring efficiency; he was describing how souls get lost, because when a town hands its functions up the ladder, the people who composed the town do not follow the function — they are left standing alone before the state, and the state obligingly grows to occupy the space the town vacated. Robert Nisbet drew the map: weaken the mediating institutions and the atomized citizen turns to the central power, which expands to fill the void. Raqib tells her activists to map the "pillars of support" any government depends on — courts, businesses, media, religious institutions — and to organize where an opponent's support actually lives: workplaces, agencies, businesses, congregations. Read that list again without the movement vocabulary on it. It is the mediating institutions. It is the parish, the union hall, the town meeting. The organizers in this piece are practising subsidiarity with the word excised. Fine by me — I would rather they practised it than named it. What I cannot abide is practising it in the morning and theorising against it in the afternoon.

And then there is the design principle. The No Kings coalition, Cineas writes, emerged in mid-2025 "intentionally leaderless and decentralized," a container for grievances — gas prices, federal workforce cuts, war. A container is a useful thing. It is not an institution. There is no one to answer for it, no one to inherit it, no one who can be wrong in public and made to fix it. The Minneapolis victories held because there were names on them: Unidos, the Immigrant Defense Network, Defend the 612, the Organizing Lab — named, answerable, mortal, which is to say real. Eighteen months of marches and the coalition is regrouping.

The correction cuts both ways, and I have spent my columns cutting it toward my own side: you do not fight concentrated power by reconcentrating it under better management. A national machine for one collective purpose is the same machine no matter whose purpose fills the hopper. The men who would replace this administration's enforcement apparatus with a reorganized federal police have found the tumor and prescribed a different tumor. Hilaire Belloc gave the arrangement its name more than a century ago — the servile state, where a people trades its standing for its security and wakes up administered. The trouble with the new majority's program was never that it promised Americans too much power over their own lives. It is that almost none of them would own any. I spent years on a Chicago desk trading the crops my county grows, and I can tell you what a crowd's pooled power looks like from inside that building. It looks like liquidity. Something to be borrowed against, sold short, settled in someone else's currency. A movement that deposits itself at the capital has done the pooling for them. The knowledge a neighborhood has of itself — what a datacenter will do to the water table, which deputy walks which beat, what a family actually needs in December — does not survive the trip to Washington, and no bureau can reconstruct it. That was the whole of Friedrich Hayek's case against central planning, and it indicts a giant government with exactly the force it indicts a giant corporation.

One more thing belongs on the record, because the record deserves it. The executive order Houston's mayor finally signed — barring police from handing stopped drivers to federal agents, with a council measure now filed to write it into law — arrived only after the machinery had killed Lorenzo Salgado Araujo at a stop, after nearly thirty-five years of an immigrant's life in that city, and the case the Harris county district attorney is taking to a grand jury should be weighed without mercy. Welcome the stranger is not a slogan in my church; it is a duty with names attached. The fire on this one belongs exactly where these organizers aimed it: on the machinery that turned a man's commute into a deportation pipeline, and on the employers who kept that workforce cheap and frightened for decades. Never on the stranger. On that point the movements and the old social teaching stand shoulder to shoulder, and I am glad of the company.

I owe my own side the same sentence. The coalitions that stopped those datacenters included farmers, retirees, school parents and mothers who agreed on nothing else. I have spent enough of my life listening to my own side preach local control to know how little of it survives contact with a county board agenda. Conserve what, exactly? And I have watched this particular play before, in a county small enough to see the whole stage. The little frame parish founded in 1884, where the Bohemians and the Poles and the Germans were still singing in their own tongues when my grandmother was married, was consolidated, and the children's names left for a diocesan office that knew the enrollment figures but had never heard them sing. The lodge hall emptied the way halls do, one funeral at a time. The mill upriver at Wisconsin Rapids idled in 2020 with roughly a thousand paychecks folded into the announcement. Every time, the offered solution was bigger: a bigger district, a bigger agency, a bigger market. We got the bigger, all right. It was never ours, and it has not once asked us what we needed.

So here is the correction, offered the way Christopher Lasch offered his — from inside the argument, to people whose seriousness he never doubted. The Wednesday agenda is right about the instinct and wrong about the address. The four demands these organizers put on paper — study the impacts first, make the developer pay for the grid he needs instead of passing it to the taxpayers, take back the tax breaks, give the community a vote before anything is built — are the universal destination of goods written in ordinance form. The earth was given for all; nobody gets to write his costs onto a public ledger and call the difference a market. [The scrutiny now gathering around the tech fortunes behind them](/articles/2026-11-05-tech-billionaires-face-scrutiny-after-democratic-congressional-gains/) is welcome for exactly that reason. Those four lines are not a left demand or a right demand. They are the demand of a place that intends to still be here.

Wednesday morning is when the co-op board meets. It is when a credit union's members gather under the one-member-one-vote rule their grandparents wrote. It is when a mutual-aid network that has proven it can field legal observers and open doors on a Sunday stops being a group chat and signs a charter, elects officers, keeps a treasury — becomes an institution, in other words, that will still be there when the news cycle is not. It is when the datacenter moratorium won in November is rewritten as a standing ordinance that survives the councilmen who passed it. None of that centralizes anything. This is slower than a march and it will not trend. In a county like mine, where nearly half the houses sit dark until the lake season opens and the hands for any given Tuesday night are thinner every year, building the thing is harder than mourning it — which is exactly why the charter has to be written anyway. Bylaws are written in rooms with bad coffee.

None of this is a theory. Farmers have pooled to bargain with the grain trade since Capper-Volstead made it lawful in 1922. When electricity passed the farms by, it was not the market that lit them; it was member-owned cooperatives, financed by the Rural Electrification Act — one farm in ten with power in the mid-1930s, four farms in five a generation later — built by people who signed for the poles their own knees climbed. The question was never whether a people should insure its sick or light its farms. It is who owns the pool. The electric cooperative headquartered in this village serves some thirty-one thousand member-owners across twelve counties, one member, one vote, no absentee shareholder, and its annual meeting is not a rally; it is a fiduciary proceeding, with a budget, elections, and dissent that changes votes. It carried current onto ground the investor-owned utilities had already priced out of existence. Nobody waits on a senator to have the meter read.

Taylor is right that the blue wave is only the beginning. Keep the pressure on the congressman if pressure steadies you — a county that answers to its own commission has nothing to fear from him, and a town that owns its institutions does not beg. But a wave goes where the ground sends it, and the ground here is a county board agenda, a church basement, a counter with a ledger on it. In Friendship the frame parish this county was built around was closed with the rest of the diocese's rural churches, and the Main Street storefronts still wear paper in the windows, and I manage a co-op where people solve things at the counter because nobody is coming to solve them from a capital. The demand that matters Wednesday morning is not addressed to a Capitol. It is addressed to your own street, and it has a form: the charter, the bylaws, the treasury, the annual meeting, the ordinance made permanent, the ownership filed under local names. The movements spent two years proving the town can govern; the whole question now is whether they will believe their own proof. Leave the town its life.
