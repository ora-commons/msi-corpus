PIP’s problem is not a bill that is too big. It is a broken assessment and an average payment that is £630 a month too small. A year-long review has collapsed into an argument about which direction to move the number. The [Guardian reports](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/nov/26/timms-review-pip-disability-benefits-claimants) that social security minister Stephen Timms told the Commons that PIP needs a “fundamental overhaul”: make the assessments accurate rather than shrink the bill. [Main Street Independent’s account](https://mainstreetindependent.com/articles/2026-11-26-timms-calls-for-pip-overhaul-focused-on-claimant-needs/) sets out the collision with Alan Milburn’s proposal to take £3.6 billion out of benefits for under-25s. One review says the process should fit the person. The other says the person should fit the budget. Downing Street is expected to say the reviews are not competing. The white paper due in the new year will reveal which one was the actual policy.

Give Timms his due first, because he has earned some. His review consulted more than 38,000 people and organizations and came back saying the machinery should stop grinding claimants down. The government has already accepted its two most sensible changes: a fast track that spares people with severe, easily evidenced conditions an interview, and fewer reviews for conditions that are lifelong or unlikely to improve. As [MSI reported when the faster-claims commitment first emerged](https://mainstreetindependent.com/articles/2026-11-24-ministers-pledge-faster-pip-claims-for-severe-conditions-in-england-and-wales/), this is not a radical transformation. It is an acknowledgment that the existing process treats administrative neatness as more important than human sanity.

Thirteen years ago, the coalition created PIP and phased out lifetime awards in the same motion. Since then, people have had to re-prove, on schedule, conditions that cannot improve. Disability advocates have called those reassessments pointless, and the review’s proposed “rehumanising” language is an admission that the process has done more than inconvenience some claimants. It has damaged mental and physical health. Tailor the payment. Stop wasting people’s energy. That is reform, not sentiment.

Now the part the budget conversation refuses to hear. Here is where the £630 goes.

Scope research found that the average PIP award runs £630 a month below average disability costs. That gap does not vanish. It gets absorbed somewhere: a credit-card balance that bought the wheelchair, a parent’s unpaid hours standing in for care nobody bills for, treatment quietly deferred, the extra heating switched off for one more winter. The bill does not shrink. It migrates, off the Treasury’s ledger and onto a kitchen table.

Follow the cost down, and a benefit cut begins to look less like a saving. When ministers go hunting for savings in PIP, they may be hunting money that has already been saved. Someone eats the difference every month. The savings are real. So is the person paying them.

Walk me through the cutting slowly, because I would like to see the mechanism. The average payment is £630 short. Cut the payment. Where does the £630 go? Disability does not get cheaper because the cheque did. It moves into overdraft fees, unpaid hours and care that stops happening. A budget that balances because a household off the ledger absorbs the gap is not lean. It is a cost that has been moved onto people who never agreed to carry it.

The review’s sharpest finding cuts the other way, and it deserves better than the framing it is getting. The Resolution Foundation reports that one in six working-age PIP claimants say their conditions add no extra costs. Timms’s report says PIP is “not appropriate” for people who incur no additional costs. So concede it plainly: some claimants incur no additional costs, and a flat weekly payment fits them badly. Take the finding seriously, which means taking it literally. A benefit intended to cover extra costs should be priced to actual costs. That is what Timms’s proposed impact assessment is for.

Now run the arithmetic honestly. An average payment can conceal both overpayment for people with little extra cost and severe underpayment for people with a great deal. That is precisely the case for better assessment. It is not a case for age.

A uniform payment that underprices disability on average is a measurement failure. The remedy is a better ruler. Not amputation. The £630 figure does not prove that accurate targeting could not produce savings. It proves that ministers cannot treat the present flat rate as an efficient instrument while ignoring the average gap. If the bill is the problem, show the overpayment and show why correcting the assessment would not release money. Until then, “the bill is too big” is not a finding. It is a mood.

And then there is Milburn, who says most young people should lose the right to monthly cash. Sit with the phrasing. Not the money. The right to it. Cash is the only payment that does not make you justify your groceries to anyone. A benefit that can be spent only on pre-approved things is not support. It is a ration card with a mission statement.

Turning age into a proxy for need is not a needs assessment. It is a birth certificate with a budget attached. One survey finding cannot carry that load. It may show that cash is the wrong form of help for some people. It does not show that they need less total support, much less that a 24-year-old deserves less because of the calendar. The £630 average gap points the other way.

[The Treasury is already picking at the cost of Milburn’s wider youth plans](https://mainstreetindependent.com/articles/2026-11-25-treasury-questions-cost-of-milburn-youth-support-plan/), which is the closest this government has come to auditing the policy it may inherit. Anyway. Downing Street says the two reviews are not competing. They are competing, whatever the press office calls them, and a prime minister who promised no “crude cuts” now has one on his desk with £3.6 billion on the tag.

Timms’s impact assessment could repair this, but only if it measures actual costs instead of treating today’s flat payment as the right answer. The danger is already visible. Years of faulty testing make it harder to establish need. A state with that record could use its own bad paperwork to narrow eligibility. Disability campaigners are right to fear that outcome. The ministry that spent years breaking the instrument now proposes to sharpen it. Fix the assessment before using it to narrow the benefit. The burden belongs on the assessment, not the claimant. Ministers also have to decide whether these changes apply only to new claimants or to the millions who already receive PIP.

I am not against cash. I am against turning cash into a moral verdict, or replacing flexible money with a service package whose menu was written before the claimant got a vote.

The Motability scheme shows that swapping part of a payment for a service can be useful. It does not establish that every claimant has a usable menu or comparable value. Voluntary means something only if cash remains real, the value is comparable, the services are usable, and a claimant can hire their own care staff. Otherwise the swap is a discount with better stationery. The claimant has not chosen services. The budget has chosen for them and calls the result choice.

So what do we build? PIP as a public floor with a cash base and a service menu.

Keep cash available for the extra costs disability adds. Use the impact assessment to set the amount. Fast-track the clearest severe cases. Reduce repeat reviews for lifelong or unlikely-to-improve conditions. Train assessors who understand a condition that changes by the week. Use “Tell Us Once” so no claimant has to give another office the same information again. Provide a clear route to challenge an error. Put education, health, social-care and mobility supports beside the cash. If someone prefers a service to money, let them swap. If they need cash for a cost the catalogue missed, keep the cash.

Every summer at my aunt’s table outside Gothenburg, public help arrived like the water bill, and sometimes like the mail: on time, unremarked, nobody performing gratitude or treating support as a confession. That was the principle my Swedish cousins took for granted. Public help should be ordinary plumbing, not a reward for deservingness.

Sweden’s model rests on high-capacity administration and universal public services built over decades, so Britain cannot import the machinery without installing the pipes. The harder part here is joining health, education, employment and social care without making the claimant coordinate them unpaid. What Britain can do now is make the existing benefit accurate, keep cash as the base, and let services add choice rather than subtract dignity.

America ran the controlled experiment, for anyone who thinks cash cannot do this. In 2021, the Census Bureau measured child poverty falling 46 percent in a single year, from 9.7 percent to 5.2 percent, after the monthly child-tax-credit expansion. It lifted 2.9 million children out of poverty. Congress let the expansion lapse, and the poverty came back right on schedule. The money did what money does. Remind me what the obstacle is again.

Why is it harder here? Britain is arguing about the trim on a coat America never sewed as one garment. American disability support is scattered across programs that do not talk to each other: Social Security Disability Insurance for people with the necessary work record; Supplemental Security Income, means-tested so tightly that money in a savings account can cost somebody the benefit; Medicaid and Medicare carrying the health half through separate doors with separate rules. Britain at least has one extra-cost benefit on which to argue about adequacy. America cannot even hold that argument because there is no single thing to argue about. So watch the white paper with envy, not scorn.

Because the build, plainly stated, is not mysterious. A disability benefit that works is boring. You qualify. It pays. It does not make you perform your worst week for a stranger with a clipboard, over and over. Price the actual cost and pay it. For the average claimant, the first honest correction widens the cheque, not the eligibility maze. Trust the claimant to narrate their own life; audit the assessment, not the claimant. That is not utopia. That is plumbing.

The economy is a set of choices, not the weather. A £630-a-month gap between what disability costs and what Britain pays is a choice—made by people, in rooms, on schedules. It can be unmade the same way. Britain gets its turn at the white paper. America should take notes, and then go sew the coat. Build the floor first. Then add the menu.
