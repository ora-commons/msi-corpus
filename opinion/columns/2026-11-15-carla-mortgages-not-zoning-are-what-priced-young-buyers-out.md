A house sells for what a family can finance over thirty years. It gets bid up by whoever can make the payment — an investor with cash, a household with two incomes and parental help, a buyer stretching credit. The permit fee is a bolt on that machine, not the machine. Mehek Cooke argues the opposite road in [Young voters are not buying socialism. They want a made-in-America future](https://www.foxnews.com/opinion/young-voters-not-buying-socialism-want-made-in-america-future) at Fox News: young voters bolted left because rent and groceries stopped fitting in a paycheck, and the cure is a competence revolution — faster permits, thinner zoning fees, factory-built homes, a paycheck turned into a down payment. That is how you beat socialism, she writes.

Supply is a real cost, and she's right about it. Permit fees, impact charges, zoning that forbids the cheap thing — those land on the buyer's bill, every one. I have no objection to a faster permit. She's also right about the label. Voters under thirty backed Democratic House candidates 73 to 25, yet 57 percent still viewed capitalism favorably against 33 percent for socialism — and 69 percent said the economy favors the wealthy. In the Harvard Youth Poll, 45 percent of young voters said "democratic socialist" made no difference to their support, and 31 percent said it made them less likely. This generation didn't buy socialism. It couldn't buy a house. That 69 percent is not a conversion. It's a service complaint. Both halves of her point are true. Grant them. Then look at what follows.

Because the piece answers its own question and walks past it. "Existing homeowners gain equity as restricted supply drives property values higher" — one sentence, then on to the permit office. Read that line again. Restricted supply makes the people who already own richer, and those people are the ones who show up to the zoning hearing. So the plan is to ask homeowners to approve the end of their own windfall, armed with a federal report card tracking how fast the county stamps forms. Walk me through it slowly: what pressure does a dashboard in Washington put on a vote at a county board?

Here is the mechanism. A builder prices a house against what a family can pay each month. That monthly number is the price spread over thirty years plus interest. So the price rises to meet the payment. Lower the rate, loosen the credit, add an investor bidding cash — the payment goes up, the price follows, and the fee was never the lever. Debt is the machine. The fee is a bolt on it. If the fee is the culprit, show the fee's share of a monthly payment. Until then, the debt stays the suspect.

And who carries it meanwhile? The piece says it: 49 percent of adults under thirty live with a parent, up twelve points since 2019, and 47 percent got outside help covering an expense. The parents are the subsidy. They are absorbing the gap between a paycheck and independence — the gap the column wants to close with a faster permit. The young worker who went looking for roommates already found one. It's his mother.

The other half the column never reaches is the paycheck. Productivity has roughly doubled since the 1970s; the typical paycheck has barely moved. Union coverage ran about a third of the workforce in the 1950s; it is around 11 percent now, against better than 80 percent in Denmark. That didn't evaporate. It was legislated thin — right-to-work laws that let workers collect union wages without paying dues, and a government that fired the striking air-traffic controllers in 1981 and told every employer in the country which side it was on. Union members still earn roughly $230 a week more than their non-union peers. A permit clock touches none of that. You can't tell a young man to man up and then leave his wage where four decades of policy put it.

The litmus test in the column is fair: does a proposal produce more homes, or just throw handouts at the same short supply? Good. Apply it twice. First to the handout nobody names — the mortgage-interest deduction and the capital-gains exclusion on a primary residence, both written to reward a house for rising in price. Every dollar of that reward is a dollar added to the price of the next one. A policy that treats the home as a portfolio will always outbid the family that wants to live in it. Then to the actual experiment: in 2021 Congress mailed families with kids a monthly check, the expanded Child Tax Credit, near-universal and dead simple. Child poverty fell 46 percent in a single year, from 9.7 percent to 5.2 percent — the lowest ever recorded, about 2.9 million children lifted out by the Census Bureau's own measure. When the checks stopped, the poverty came back on schedule. That is not Scandinavia. That is us: recent, measured, reversible. A country that can halve child poverty with a monthly deposit and then let the policy lapse does not get to lecture the under-thirties about values.

Then there is the advice to young women — work, career, marriage, motherhood, all of it — arriving without pricing the one line item that makes "all of it" impossible. Quality childcare takes well-paid adults per handful of kids. Make it cheap for parents, decent for the workers, and profitable for the owner: pick two. The arithmetic refuses, which is why every other rich country subsidizes care and we ration it through grandmothers. The parents absorbing the gap in Cooke's own data are the grandmothers, economically speaking.

"A nation of owners" is the strongest line in the column, and the plan behind it is one asset: a house, bought at today's prices, on a thirty-year payment plan. That is not a nation of owners. That is a nation of debtors with yards. Real ownership means owning a piece of the place you work, and America already has the machinery. About 145 million Americans belong to credit unions — member-owned, not-for-profit, quietly better rates than the banks. Roughly 6,500 companies run employee-ownership plans covering 15 million workers, with more than $2 trillion in those accounts, and the idea has had Republican champions for decades. Nothing in that paragraph required a revolution, because it already happened. And if the market won't build starter homes at starter prices, that is not a mystery either — that is a market answering exactly as designed.

We already know what to do with that answer. 1936: the Rural Electrification Act, low-cost loans to member-owned cooperatives, because investor-owned utilities wouldn't wire a countryside that didn't return enough. Roughly 900 of those co-ops still run, serving 42 million Americans across 56 percent of the land. My grandfather sat on the board of one. Nobody ever called Bismarck the Kremlin. Start with the land itself: a community land trust owns the ground permanently and sells only what sits on it, so the family builds equity in the house and the speculation has nowhere to sit. Then build the building the way we build the boring things that work — limited-equity co-ops, one member one vote, the same structure as the credit-union card in your wallet.

Why it's harder here, and I won't pretend otherwise: the median American household's wealth is the house. Every owner has a reason to vote against the next one being built. The decision lives in fifty states and thousands of counties, and nobody can opt out of their own zoning. So it gets built parcel by parcel — with the permitting reform taken whole, because I'll help carry the boxes; the child allowance turned back on, because we wrote the recipe in 2021 and watched it work; wage floors set industry by industry the way several states already do with wage boards in fast food and home care, one pay floor for an entire occupation so no employer gains by undercutting the rest, legal under existing law. That is the honest-sized version of the Danish bargain, where losing a job still replaces most of a paycheck for up to two years while the state spends about two percent of its economy moving people into the next one. This country spent forty years dismantling that machinery and will not rebuild it by lunchtime.

So give them a wage that reaches, a mortgage that doesn't eat the paycheck, and a house that isn't somebody's portfolio — built, owned, and voted on by the people who live there. That's not socialism. That's a co-op, and this country has already built a few thousand of them. They always wanted to. That was never the problem. Anyway — build it.
