The Pentagon and Congress are keeping American troops under missiles they cannot stop. Along the Bahrain corniche, the American military compounds are dotted with the charred hulks of destroyed radars and communications installations; Iran did that this year, and the damage forced the abandonment of key American installations across the Persian Gulf. Yemen's Houthis, fighting out of one of the poorest corners of the earth, have spent months continuously striking Saudi airports and energy facilities, putting economic pressure on their much wealthier adversary with the same class of weapon. One Iranian attack on Jordan in September burned through [60 to 70 Patriot interceptors](/articles/2026-09-16-u-s-fires-60-to-70-patriot-interceptors-in-iranian-missile-attack-on-jordan/), and the drain has already [fueled allied doubts over the reach of the American deterrent](/articles/2026-08-21-iran-war-stockpile-drain-fuels-allied-doubts-over-us-deterrent-reach/). U.S. and allied officials acknowledge the American military-industrial base will be unable to rectify the interceptor shortage for several years, and no decision on how to adapt has been announced. The Pentagon and Lockheed are leaving troops in bases they know are indefensible. The troops are still at the bases.

Since 1945 the American way of war has run through concrete. The republic has fought from a lattice of forward bases built on an assumption that held for three generations: American airpower owns the sky quickly, and the rear, where the fuel farms and the hangars and the command tents sit, is safe. A ballistic missile is a rocket that arcs out of sight and comes down on a fixed point faster than anything you can park on a ramp can answer, and cheap precision has put it in the hands of governments and militias alike. "Everything that is fixed is a target now," said retired British Air Marshal Martin Sampson, who heads the Middle East office of the International Institute for Strategic Studies; he means the deep battle, the space behind the line where headquarters and spare parts used to sit safe, is now inside a nonstate actor's range. Fabian Hinz of Conflict Armament Research notes that the Western way of war was built on seizing air superiority fast, and that an adversary with no air force at all can now strike your rear. Jennifer Kavanagh of Defense Priorities names the change: "Military power has been democratized," and the forward bases, "the way they have been constructed, are simply not defensible, even against a weak adversary."

Now, I'm just a simple man, but the arithmetic here is brake-job simple. The defense against these weapons is the interceptor, a defensive rocket fired up to knock the incoming one out of the air before it lands. The record calls interceptors some of the costliest and most advanced military products in existence. THAAD, the high-altitude shield that sits above Patriot, costs more still. You do not fire one and go home; you fire several, because the incoming missile is falling too fast to forgive a miss. The missile coming down at you costs a fraction of the round you fired at it. So the other side buys cheap and shoots often, and we buy dear and run dry. The attacker chooses the night and the volume; the defender pays full price for every round of the answer, and waits years for delivery. Lockheed Martin, the biggest Western interceptor maker, cannot keep up with demand, and its order books for Patriot PAC-3 and Thaad run well into the 2030s. Fabian Hoffmann, a missile researcher at the Norwegian Institute for Defense Studies, put the quiet part on the record: "Even the United States, with its industrial capacity, is not prepared to defend against an attacker that builds ballistic missiles at industrial scale." In the shop we keep parts on the shelf because the customer's truck will not run without them. A supply line that delivers in 2034 for a fight happening now does not pencil out for the soldiers standing under it.

There is a name for a machine that answers every failure of the posture with a bigger order book. Eisenhower named it. In his farewell address of January 17, 1961, paragraph 24, he warned the country to guard "against the acquisition of unwarranted influence, whether sought or unsought, by the military-industrial complex." An early draft of the address read military-industrial-congressional complex, and the word congressional was cut before he spoke; somebody votes the money, and putting the word back restores what the machine actually requires. Congress writes the checks, the primes book the revenue, the fixed bases stand, and when the bases burn, the remedy on the books is more interceptors. An order book running to the 2030s while the positions it is supposed to protect sit exposed is that influence, sought or unsought. Sixty-five years later the mechanism Eisenhower warned of is not rising over some horizon; it is the operating system. In the paragraph right after the warning he named the check on it: "Only an alert and knowledgeable citizenry can compel the proper meshing of the huge industrial and military machinery of defense with our peaceful methods and goals." That citizenry is supposed to be us. It is reading photographs of burned-out radars on a waterfront, and the showing up is sixty-five years overdue.

Barbara Tuchman gave the historians' definition of folly: policy persisted in against a nation's own interest, by groups rather than lone men, with the alternatives named at the time, long after the evidence had spoken. Run the checklist on the fixed base. February 1991: one Iraqi Scud came down on an American barracks in Dhahran, Saudi Arabia, and killed twenty-eight soldiers in their bunks. January 2020: Iranian ballistic salvos walked across Ain al-Asad air base in Iraq; nobody died that night, and the Pentagon's own count of the brains rattled in that one strike eventually passed one hundred. Each time, dispersal and hardening were named. Each time, Washington bought more fixed base and more interceptors instead. The treaty that banned American and Russian land-based medium-range missiles lasted until 2019, and under its shadow Europe stayed out of the ground-launched missile business; the treaty is gone, the missiles spread anyway, and the barrier to becoming a missile power now sits low enough that a militia cleared it. That is not surprise. That is the checklist, filled out by hand, over thirty-five years.

None of this belongs to one administration. The forward-basing posture, the assumption of air supremacy behind it, and the failure to buy interceptors at the rate the threat grows span presidencies of both parties, and the analysis is the same whichever party held the keys.

The strongest case for keeping the posture came from retired Lt. Gen. Ben Hodges, who commanded American Army forces in Europe: "You cannot defend the United States from Fort Hood, Texas, or Fort Bragg, North Carolina." Capabilities, logistics, command and control have to be positioned out where the commitments are, so harden the air bases, disperse the forces, keep the positions, and lean on deterrence. Take that at full strength; geography alone does not defend a republic, and some commitments are worth their cost. He also conceded the other half: the bases in the Middle East turned out to be "so vulnerable" that there will have to be a cost-benefit analysis. Then hold the argument against the magazines. The rounds over Jordan came out of the same pool meant to back every other promise on the books; [the drain has already thinned South Korea's air-defense math](/articles/2026-08-09-iran-conflict-depletes-u-s-interceptors-raising-south-korea-air-defense-concerns/), and the same global shortfall has left Ukraine exposed to salvos of Russian missiles aimed at its power grid, its bridges, its factories. A commitment without a magazine is not a commitment; it is a promissory note written against a shelf that is already bare. Retired French Lt. Gen. Michel Yakovleff's answer, that the largest stakes return to deterrence between states, the old idea that you answer being hit by making the other side fear what happens next, is honest at the top of the ladder. It is not an answer for the airmen sleeping under hangar roofs at installations the record now calls indefensible. The honest criterion sits between keeping everything and keeping nothing: hold the presence the magazines can actually back, and bring home the rest.

Name who lives at the sharp end of the arithmetic. The men and women under those roofs enlisted the way the cohorts before them always enlisted, from the towns where the recruiter's office in the strip mall is the only door to the GI Bill. From where I sat in the gunner's seat in Iraq in 2003, the threat I planned against was in front of the tank, and the fixed base was simply the shape of life: you ate where the concrete was, you slept where the concrete was, and you understood, in the wordless way soldiers understand things, that somewhere a man with a map already held your grid square. Twenty-three years on, the arrangement is unchanged, except the missiles now arc farther, land truer, and cost less than the rounds we fire back. The people working those compounds are not a line item in a force-posture study. They have a flight line, a mail room, a family waiting on a housing list. Nobody who signed those enlistment papers volunteered to be the live-in proof that a posture failed. Nobody whose name is on the interceptor order book sleeps under any of those roofs.

So here is the position, stated plainly. A country that keeps troops forward owes them one of two things, decided in the open and paid for: the defense of those positions at the scale the threat actually requires, or the movement of the troops to ground they can hold. What it may not do is keep the posture running on a 1990s assumption while the stocks run dry and call the gap a contingency. The honest version looks like this: bring the troops home from the installations that cannot be defended, disperse and harden what genuinely must stay, buy interceptors at industrial scale only where the commitment justifies the magazine, and make Congress vote the choice in the open, where the purse and the accountability live. It means smaller signatures in the Gulf and a straight answer about which promises this country can actually keep. Providing for the common defense is not an order book that delivers in 2034. That is the position the evidence supports, and I take it.

The radars on the Bahrain corniche still sit where they burned. Lockheed's order book runs well into the 2030s. Those are two facts about one arrangement, and the people stationed between them are paying for it.
