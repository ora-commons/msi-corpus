France’s unrest began with high-schoolers angry about crowded classrooms, dilapidated schools, and access to college; retirees who said pensions were not enough to live on; and then looting, arson, and a public-safety system too thin to contain what followed. That is not a “socialist bill coming due.” In [“France is learning the hard way what happens when the socialist bill comes due,”](https://www.foxnews.com/opinion/france-learning-hard-way-what-happens-when-socialist-bill-comes-due) Fox News opinion columnist Simon Hankinson argues something else: generous benefits, mass immigration, and fifty years of red ink built the pressure; benefit cuts triggered mass protests; looters and some rioters with immigrant backgrounds made the protests violent; and France’s deficits, immigration, and socialist-led local governments are driving the country toward breakdown. He also points to New York and Seattle as the next example. The column gives us a trigger, a crowd, and a scare word. It never shows who is paying it in matches. “The bill came due” is a good title. It is not a mechanism.

France’s unrest is not a verdict on the word “socialist.” It is a security failure, and blaming immigrants is a diversion. The deeper problem is unmet public promises, as Hankinson’s own examples show.

The fiscal squeeze is real. Hankinson reports a deficit above 5 percent of GDP, national debt at 120 percent of GDP, growth under 1 percent, and no balanced budget in half a century. He also notes that diesel costs about $10 a gallon. The government is cutting benefits while people are angry. That is the strongest part of his case.

It establishes pressure. It does not tell us who pays, who loots, or why police capacity failed. A deficit tells you what a government spent. It does not tell you who set a town hall on fire.

Now read the grievances side by side. A student wants smaller classes and sound schools. A retiree wants a pension large enough to live on. Households complain about the cost of living. These are not one demand for more spending. School budgets, pension checks, wages, and prices pull in different directions. More money for schools does not raise a pension. More welfare spending can raise taxes without fixing a shortage of workers.

The source compresses all of this into one sentence: “They all want more state spending to cure their ills.” But the actual choice is concrete. Which service gets protected? Which tax rises? Which household absorbs the shortfall? If the state promised working schools and delivered half-built ones, the broken promise belongs in the explanation of the protest.

Make “broke” plain, too. A deficit is one year’s gap between what the state spends and what it collects. Debt is the stack of earlier borrowing after repayments. Neither means France has run out of euros. Both mean the budget is under pressure and choices are due. Neither says who pays.

Hankinson identifies a deficit and names benefit recipients, but he never follows the euros to the people who lose purchasing power. The payee is missing. The adjustment can come from high incomes, capital gains, shareholders, or from schoolchildren, pensioners, and renters. The article points at “socialism,” immigrants, and city government, but it never shows who bears the cost.

One event coming after another does not prove that one produced the other. Benefit reforms came first. Then came anger, theft, arson, and street fighting. The column treats that order as proof.

Looting and arson are crimes. Prosecute them. But a violent crowd is not a legislative chamber, and a looter is not the legal representative of every student, retiree, immigrant, and union member in France.

The article says many violent participants “seem to be of migrant background.” Even if that description is accurate, background is not a cause. It does not explain crowded classrooms or stretched household budgets. Ancestry is not a substitute for evidence.

The column’s immigration accounting is worse. It says immigrants and their children have a lower employment rate than native French while receiving the same social services, and that “those numbers don’t work.” It gives no numbers. No employment rates. No earnings. No taxes paid. No adjustment for age. No cost of those services. It counts what France spends and skips what immigrants pay.

It also folds children into the employment comparison. Employment rates are calculated for working-age adults. Children sit outside that count, so including them drags down an average without measuring one immigrant’s earnings. A low employment rate tells you how many people have jobs. It does not tell you what a group costs the state.

Equal access to public services is not an accounting anomaly. It is the point. A country that makes benefits available only to people with the right pedigree teaches citizens that citizenship is rationed by ancestry. If the budget does not balance, change the taxes or the services. Do not make a person’s birthplace the unit of account.

The claim of foreign influence is worse still. The column says the left is “well organized, galvanized, and funded — including with foreign money,” and that foreign influence is what is new this time. It offers no named donor, no money trail, no organizer, no decision chain, and no proof that the people setting cars on fire took orders.

A Palestinian flag tells you somebody’s politics. It does not tell you who commands a mob. A flag is not a funding document.

Now take the security gap seriously. Hankinson himself says France has too few bodies to handle multiple riots at once. National police handle the cities and gendarmes cover the countryside; both are centrally funded. Towns have municipal police who are not equipped or trained for law enforcement, let alone riot control. France has specialized riot-control units, but only so many. The military is not used for routine internal operations.

This is the sharpest thing in the column. The first line can be local while the tools for containing riots are scarce and remote. It explains how disorder beginning in one district can spread while the city watches. It does not explain the first fire. And it does not need the socialist bill to work. A state can run short of money under any label on the ballot.

Police who cannot protect a perimeter or coordinate a handoff to national units leave people and stores exposed. Better training, equipment, command, and local capacity can change the outcome. A label on the people in the street cannot.

Take the American experience the column waves at. When federal child payments were expanded to arrive every month in 2021, the Census Bureau’s first assessment found child poverty roughly halved—from just under 10 percent of children to about 5 percent—and roughly 3 million children moved above the poverty line. When the expanded monthly payments ended, later Census estimates showed child poverty climbing again.

That does not prove benefits prevent riots. It proves something the column needs and never supplies: money in a family’s hands can reduce hardship. The column cannot reverse the sign and claim that the spending built the unrest.

The forecast at the end does not follow either. France’s present, it says, could be America’s future because New York and Seattle are run by avowed socialists, while Boston and Los Angeles are, in the column’s words, “arguably” closely aligned with the DSA. As the column lists it, the DSA platform is a set of votes: open borders, nationalized health care, curtailed law enforcement, and higher taxes. Votes are not a forecast of fire.

The column’s own election summary undercuts the alarm: the leading candidate on the right has never campaigned on cutting the benefits bill. If the socialist bill were what sets cities alight, that election would be a referendum on the bill. It is not.

And a municipal election is not a national welfare state. A mayor’s label is not a labor contract. New York, Seattle, Boston, and Los Angeles can set different taxes, services, and policing rules from France. Calling them all socialist erases the actual choices before anyone has examined them.

So build for the two things the evidence shows.

If the trigger is unmet promises, fund the promises and make them universal. Give cities predictable, multi-year central grants so a school budget does not depend on a negotiation that ends with a burned town hall. Protect a floor under schools and pensions. Fund essential services—schools, transit, and clinics—that every resident uses, so every resident has a stake in keeping them open. Tax high incomes and owners of capital enough that service users do not absorb every shock. Make elected officials publish the trade-offs: this floor, that tax, this grant. No slogan can replace a budget.

If the escalation is a capacity failure, buy the capacity and say who is in charge of it. Equip and train municipal police for riots. Put enough specialized units in place to answer more than one city at once. Draw a written rule for who commands a crowd and who pays the bill. Publish the accounts so the national government cannot quietly leave one city holding the tab. Then set a clear handoff between local police and national units. A mayor should not have to improvise during the next riot.

Denmark’s protection during retraining is a design idea, not a plug-in answer. France can build that around its own unions and industries. Make unemployment benefits follow workers across jobs. Let unions help run retraining and job placement. Put wage floors and training plans around entire industries instead of leaving every worker to bargain alone. An agreement covering an entire industry has to be rebuilt or widened, not announced.

Put looters in court. Put public services in budgets. Make the budget match the duty, then show the receipts: who paid, who decided, who can be replaced.

Start where the television cameras were: protect the services, fund the police, give workers a floor and a bargaining table. Then a bad night can end in an arrest, a repaired storefront, and a route back to work. That is how you build a city harder to burn—and how a fiscal crisis stops becoming a riot.
