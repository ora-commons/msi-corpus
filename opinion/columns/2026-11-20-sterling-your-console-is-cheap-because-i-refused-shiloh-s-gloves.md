Dear Export-Control Authority: I keep the dividend in the parent and Shiloh’s burn in the acid subsidiary, which is how I make the graphics processor in your gaming console cheap, and I do it by refusing his chemical gauntlets. Holman W. Jenkins Jr.’s [Wall Street Journal column “Chips Aren’t a Magic Bullet for China Risks”](https://www.wsj.com/opinion/chips-arent-a-magic-bullet-for-china-risks-772e6d4a?mod=rss_opinion) says advanced chips constrain Chinese AI companies but are no magic bullet for cyber or loss-of-control risks, and he expects commodity AI to permeate the economy and boost productivity. Correct. If AI permeates the economy, the chip is only an input. The hand exposed to the acid doesn’t cross a border. I price that one by what the claim will cost. Shiloh is a 31-year-old fluoride drudge in the acid bay I own outside Phoenix. His left arm is red to the elbow. You buy the console under your television. I keep the claim benchmark Bhopal left us: under $1,000 before legal and administrative costs. God bless your purchase and the orderly hierarchy that keeps it cheap.

Let me make the filing easy for you, because I’m telling you this for your own good. Jenkins is right that Silicon Valley’s lead includes a venture-capital industry willing to fund many failures to find one success. He also expects superintelligent AI to be tightly regulated and confined to national security and high-value science. I agree. AI is national-security policy. Venture capital needs failures. So do chip bids. Your authority can stop a chip from crossing a border. It can’t stop me from moving the parent’s dividend into the parent’s account and the acid subsidiary’s liability into a thinner one. I own both. That’s not a loophole. That’s the design. I didn’t buy the chemical gauntlets required for [hydrofluoric acid](https://www.osha.gov/hydrogen-fluoride). The burn becomes a write-down in the subsidiary’s books. Your examiners can verify every noun, which is why they’ll arrive after the order is filled.

The principle is older than your export schedule. It separates the yield from the person who bears the cost. Scripture puts the labor bargain this way. Luke 10:7 places payment and testimony in the same sentence:

> “And when he cometh to the house, he shall say to the workmen, Come ye into my house, and take your meat. And testify unto me that the Lord hath sent you. And put that which is due unto a labourer, and go thy way. Make not your whole armour shine before him, that he should return and go again to him.”
>
> — Luke 10:7 (KJV)

I’ve never denied the verse. It requires the labourer to be paid. I pay the wage. The injured claim belongs to the subsidiary. Theology has categories, and so do I. Keep the yield in the parent. Put the claim in a shell with fewer assets than the injury. Let the shell answer.

Take the number slowly. At Bhopal, 500,000+ people were registered for compensation. Union Carbide’s 1989 settlement was $470 million.

Before legal and administrative costs, that arithmetic is under $1,000 per registered person.

That benchmark tells me what a claim looks like when the profit sits in the parent and the claim sits in an undercapitalized subsidiary. Shiloh’s forearm is red to the elbow. You may think that should move me. That would be sentimental accounting. The gauntlets are what I refused. I discount the claim to that benchmark. The injury is expensive. The claim is cheap. I keep the claim. The clean arithmetic is what I enjoy.

Selma is the same law in concrete. She is a slab line-filler at the chip-fab concrete company I own outside Madras, Oregon, and I make advanced chips affordable by running the saw dry through her lungs while I value her claim below $1,000. Selma is the entry on my cost sheet. I run the saw dry because wet cutting and respirators are burdens I decline. By hour 2, the air at her cut is thick with respirable silica. Years of that exposure can scar her lungs into silicosis. I call that maintenance. Her breathing is an externality. The pour schedule is not. The silica is not wages. It is the cost of breathing. I keep the difference.

Yusuf is Selma’s inverse: a teardown drone at the old-fab demolition yard I own outside Phoenix, Arizona. His hose lifts silica from the old slab. His lungs collect it. Same law, different invoice. His claim is under the same $1,000 ceiling.

Widen the frame. This is not a silica rule or an export schedule. It is Jurisdictional Risk Containment: put the dangerous work under a thin company, let somebody else carry the consequence, and value the body below the cost of protecting it. Bhopal is the old proof. The parent held the profit. The subsidiary held the release. The settlement bought closure. It did not repair the lungs. I turn human lungs into venture capital. Your payment keeps the line moving. I keep the liability discount.

The forecast is merely inventory. Every advanced chip needs a fab. Every fab needs a building. Every building needs concrete. I have sold the labor-saving dry cut into the AI buildout. Commodity AI makes the console cheaper and automates more of the acid bay. Automation removes the worker whose output it can reproduce; personalized pricing resets the wage of the worker it cannot. The first makes labor optional. The second makes refusal expensive.

My acid bay is scheduled for the first. Every worker I displace removes some of the payroll that would have bought the console. Demand goes somewhere else. Shiloh’s hand cannot close.
