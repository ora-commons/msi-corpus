They built something rare — fifty workers, one union branch, eleven years of a paper that answered to its readers instead of its advertisers — and watched it come apart not from a shortage of readers, but from the hands of the people entrusted to keep it. *The Guardian* reports that [The Canary, the leftwing news site, has announced it is closing "for the foreseeable future"](https://www.theguardian.com/media/2026/oct/19/the-canary-leftwing-news-website-closure), blaming "malicious lies" from former employees and a "vicious campaign" from hostile forces, "the state and those who'd see us destroyed." I do not doubt that some of the campaign was vicious. That is not why it died.

Start with what it was, because the obituaries are already skipping past it. Founded in 2015, The Canary set out to do journalism for an audience the mainstream press had spent a decade assuring did not exist — people who wanted anti-austerity reporting that took their side of the recession seriously, and coverage of the Middle East that did not begin from the premises of the Foreign Office. That audience was real. It funded, shared, and read a newsroom of roughly fifty full- and part-time workers, unionised into a branch of the National Union of Journalists. That is not a meme. That is a mediating institution in embryo: workers in possession of their own means of publication, holding each other's backs, building a thing their readers had a stake in. Whatever one thinks of the line it took, there is almost nothing like it left in the English-speaking press, and its disappearance is a loss I grieve on grounds that have nothing to do with its politics.

And that is exactly why the record — as *The Guardian* has it, and as the staff themselves have told it on the record and in allegation — deserves to be looked at squarely, rather than either sanctified or danced on. When Lloyds froze access to "a substantial amount" of the company's money, staff said they had already been paid late through the summer. They had been told the operation had roughly £400,000 to carry it to year's end. They were asked to sign "non-negotiable" non-disclosure agreements or have their contracts unilaterally altered. Nest, the government-backed pension scheme, flagged late contributions — an outfit that flags employers for arrears and not for opinions. And the additional £2 million-plus that management said would steady the ship was, within months, almost entirely gone, much of it, staff alleged, spent on a short-lived print newspaper and the purchase of other websites. The Canary says it will publish its own account. Let it. The staff are owed the last word on the wage queue they organised to defend.

Here is where a conservative has to say what a conservative is supposed to say, and mean it: those managers were stewards, not owners. That is the whole content of the word. A newsroom built by its workers holds its property in trust — for the readers who fund it, for the journalists whose names are on the stories, for the idea that brought the thing into being. The universal destination of goods is not a left principle; it is the oldest one in Christendom, and it means that a man's labor and a community's livelihood were never the private patrimony of whoever happened to hold the title. To take two million pounds raised in good faith and burn it on vanity expansion, and then to gag the workers who asked where it went, is not a political failure. It is the corruption of stewardship — the office turned against the people it existed to serve. Belloc had a name for the endpoint: the servile condition, in which the worker keeps his bread and loses his voice. A unionised newsroom arrived there through its own management. That is a sorrow, not a victory.

But the sorrow is not the whole of it, because the single most important fact in this story is not about The Canary at all. It is that one bank, at one compliance desk, was able to switch off an eleven-year newsroom's access to its own money. Whatever that desk's reasons, and whatever The Canary's bookkeeping, a country whose independent press can be unplugged by a single counterparty does not have a press. It has tenants. Subsidiarity is the principle that decisions ought to be made at the lowest level competent to make them; here the decision was made at a level answerable to no reader, no member, no community — a level with no obligation to explain itself to anyone it affects. The state-adjacent pressure being brought to bear on media-adjacent entities elsewhere in the news this autumn is the same lever with a different handle. I have no interest in the establishment-plot narrative. I have a great deal of interest in the fact that dependency on infrastructure you do not own is a standing hazard to every small outlet in the country, including the ones I cannot stand.

So build the counter-model, and build it the way this county builds things — member by member, on ground the members hold. The first layer is banking that cannot be pulled: credit unions and mutual institutions where the board is elected by the people whose money is on the line, the way our own rural electric cooperative here in Friendship is owned by thirty-one thousand member-owners across twelve counties and governed by a board the members can throw out. A newsroom whose payroll, deposits, and credit line ran through institutions like that would not have woken up to a frozen account. The second layer is the parish-press tradition applied honestly: local reporting funded by membership rather than scale — subscribers who are members, one member one vote, the model every farm co-op in Wisconsin runs on and the reason our own county papers outlasted every chain that bought and stripped them. The third layer is the labour floor made permanent: a journalists' union with a shared legal and payroll apparatus behind it, so that asking where the money went is not an act of individual courage. Capper-Volstead gave farmers a recognised right to bargain against concentrated buyers because the republic understood that atomised producers get ground up. Publishing needs its own recognised right to organise.

None of this is easy, and I will not pretend otherwise. Cooperatives struggle to raise growth capital, which is precisely why the movement that funded The Canary should have funded its infrastructure and not its ambitions. But the difficulty is no argument for the alternative on offer, which is a press of petitioners waiting on switches they do not own.

The readers do not vanish with the URL. They are still out there, wanting the journalism they wanted in 2015, and some of them are already building the next thing — smaller, slower, and owned. The only question worth asking about this closure is not whether The Canary deserved to survive. It is whether what comes after is built on ground its builders can stand on. That is the ledger that opens when the masthead goes dark, and it is the only one that matters.
