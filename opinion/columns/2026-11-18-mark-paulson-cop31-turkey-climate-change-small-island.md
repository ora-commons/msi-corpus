Trump is abandoning thirty-nine island nations to the rising sea. America keeps burning the fuel. The United States abandoned small island nations at COP31.

Rain was standing in the low place along my section-line road Wednesday morning. By noon, the sandy ground had taken it. I have kept a notebook at the bench for twelve years. The ice on Lake Petenwell went out eleven days earlier than the first winter I wrote the date down. The rut behind the shop ran about two weeks later than when I started keeping track. First-mosquito week has crept from May into March some years. Some winters the lake barely freezes hard enough to set an auger on before it starts letting go again. That is my own count on my own water, not the record itself.

The Wisconsin Initiative on Climate Change Impacts’ 2021 assessment puts statewide warming at about three degrees Fahrenheit since 1950, and the 2010–2019 stretch was the wettest decade since records began around 1900. The continuous Lake Mendota ice record, kept since the 1850s, shows about twenty-nine fewer days of ice cover than in the mid-nineteenth century. The water around Tonga does not have that easy way out. The water coming for them is the same water coming for my lake, on a longer schedule and a bigger scale.

The talks are COP31, in their second and final week in Antalya, Turkey. The Alliance of Small Island States—39 states from the Pacific, Caribbean, and African regions—wants $500 million for climate-resiliency programs and $200 million for natural-disaster early-warning systems. Seven hundred million dollars in all. Tonga’s maritime territory dwarfs its landmass. Record ocean temperatures threaten the marine life Tongans depend on, while melting glaciers raise the sea and increase erosion and flooding. The world’s oceans reached their hottest temperatures on record in August, and an El Niño is still intensifying.

Prime Minister Fatafehi Fakafānua put the demand without ceremony: “We don’t need more studies. We don’t need more negotiations. We just need more action.”

This is not a bid for luxury. Early warning buys time. Resiliency decides whether that time becomes survival or merely a longer wait for the next bad season. Time is the one resource a coastal community cannot manufacture after the sea has crossed the road. A warning gives families a chance to act before the water decides for them; resiliency turns that warning into a community able to keep essential systems working and rebuild what a storm breaks.

Fakafānua’s second sentence was the moral claim: “It’s only fair that those who are suffering from the effects of climate change are assisted by those who were at fault.” Against the resources of the world’s largest economy, the alliance’s entire request is modest. It is money for warning systems and for communities that must keep functioning after disaster strikes. The countries responsible for the carbon that makes those disasters worse are being asked to pay part of the bill.

The United Nations says the world is unlikely to cap warming at 1.5 degrees Celsius above preindustrial levels. Adelle Thomas, a Bahamian senior director of the Natural Resources Defense Council and vice chair of an Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change working group, called the line more than a target: “It’s really not a temperature threshold for us, it’s a survival threshold.” The target remains necessary. What is missing is enforcement powerful enough to reach it.

The United States was not in Antalya. For the second year in a row, the world’s largest economy and a major emitter kept its chair empty. On January 20, 2025, President Trump signed the order pulling the country out of the Paris Agreement for the second time, out of the framework in which the islands’ survival gets negotiated. There was no American demand for binding emissions cuts, no American commitment at the table to the alliance’s two demands, and no American vote for ending fossil fuels. A country can be absent from the room. It does not erase the bill.

Oil data tell the other half of the story. American crude averaged 12.9 million barrels a day in 2023, 13.2 million in 2024, and set an all-time monthly record of 13.6 million in July 2025. Those production records were set under the previous administration and extended under this one. The barrels did not stop on their own under either flag. What changed is that the largest economy in the column marked “at fault” stopped showing up where the bill gets discussed. The burning continued. The country left the room.

We Too, Chapter 16, calls that maneuver the Nationalist Shell Game: the flag does the talking while the bill slides under the shell. “Energy dominance” reads well on a podium in Washington. In Antalya, it reads as a country whose oil operations just set production records, absent from the table where the smallest members of the neighborhood are asking what they owe.

The same conference supplied the shell’s other hand. Even as [a fossil-fuel coalition pressed its case after Colombia’s withdrawal from the process](/articles/2026-11-15-fossil-fuel-coalition-presses-on-at-cop31-after-colombia-s-withdrawal/), production kept climbing at home and producers worked the room abroad. Production records at home, a producers’ coalition abroad, and the bill slides under both.

Ilana Seid, the Palauan ambassador who chairs the alliance, described the bloc’s mechanics the way a small operator facing a consolidated buyer would: “On our own, individual SIDS are small economies. We have little leverage, but when we put us together and we speak with one voice, we can speak pretty loudly and pretty strongly.” Thirty-nine governments are not thirty-nine footnotes. A major emitter may keep its seat empty, but it cannot pretend that the governments addressing the room are marginal.

Negotiated commitments are made in rooms where states decide who must cut, who must pay, and which promises bind them after the cameras leave. The United States’ empty chair removes a major emitter from each of those decisions. No other government can put the necessary emissions cuts in the United States’ name. The absence does not merely reduce ceremony. It shifts practical power toward states with less money and less historical responsibility.

Adams-Columbia Electric Cooperative is still headquartered in Friendship. Rural electrification was treated as a public duty, with long-term federal loans from the 1936 Rural Electrification Act running through member-owned utilities rather than leaving service to whoever could profit by reaching isolated farms. The important part was ownership as well as electricity. At Adams-Columbia, the members own the cooperative and hold the vote. An investor-owned utility could judge a line to a far-off farm too thin a margin. The cooperative had a different reason to keep service alive across the same membership.

That is why the 1936 program belongs beside the islands’ demand. Their survival systems need owners and operators accountable to the communities that will use them when the next storm arrives. COP31 has the duty backward. The islands must chase adaptation money from the largest emitters while the systems that might save lives remain voluntary.

That arrangement protects the machinery that creates the harm. Governments can announce shared concern while writing no obligation their own industries must obey. A [report that global climate action has stalled for five years](/articles/2026-11-12-global-climate-action-has-stalled-for-five-years-report-finds/) is not separate from Antalya; it is the condition the conference is trying to reverse. Many agreements coming out of the talks are voluntary and nonbinding. The world has yet to agree how to transition away from fossil fuels.

Voluntary language does something worse than fail to solve the problem: it lets each government present its own pledge as if it were a common obligation. The islands cannot inspect another government’s energy system or compel it to keep a promise, and they cannot finance the gap between an announced target and an enforced one. The process becomes a place where responsibility is discussed and payment is deferred. Every missing signature becomes another adaptation request. The islands receive the loss.

Wendell Berry’s *The Unsettling of America* described communities being turned into instruments of production. Berry’s point was not that people are sentimental about factories. A community’s economy is its membership: who owns the land and machinery, who does the work, who receives the return, and whether people can remain there while making a life. A climate-finance system that makes Tonga an adaptation line item repeats that bargain. The carbon economy keeps its revenue while the country that did not create the debt competes for a smaller check. The absence counts thirty-nine members out of the book and calls the counting patriotism. The only difference is that the factory is now the atmosphere.

Aldo Leopold’s “Thinking Like a Mountain” tells how men shot out the wolves because the ledger in their heads held only deer, while the mountain’s ledger carried deer, wolves, soil, and decades. Quarter-thinking against decade-thinking is the whole problem in Antalya. The talks move at committee speed while the water moves at water speed. In Tonga, the reef, shoreline, and fishing families are one account. Damage to one travels straight through the others. A government cannot call itself a friend of the people while treating their water and food supply as an expense to be pushed onto the next budget.

My 2014 Silverado with 187,000 miles on it, the Briggs & Stratton engines on my bench, and the LP I burn all winter belong to the same carbon economy I am judging in Antalya. The Silverado gets me to the co-op, the grocery, and the jobs that pay for fuel. My household is not outside this economy; I use its products and carry its debt. That does not make the bill less owed. It keeps me from turning the bill into an abstraction and handing it to somebody else.

The difference between me and the man who signed the withdrawal is not virtue. It is authority. He had the authority to decide whether the largest economy on earth would help pay for the water, and he decided.

Adelle Thomas says the climate talks kept warming from climbing even higher and should not be abandoned. She is right; COP has bent the curve—bent it, not broken it. “The real question is going to be whether political ambition is catching up with the lived reality that people are facing,” she said.

For all that, the islands themselves will not scrap the process. Fakafānua said, “we’ve definitely made progress and we’re better with COP than without.” Thomas said, “I don’t think that we should give up on COP.” Atiterentaai Rinimarawa, a 24-year-old youth representative from Kiribati, put the stake plainly: “I feel that my future and the future of generations after me are on the line.” Then came the demand: “We are not asking for sympathy, but we are calling for climate justice, stronger commitment and real action.” The people asking have kept more faith with the table than the largest economy refusing to sit at it.

The failure of voluntary pledges makes the next move plain. The major emitters should put the $700 million into adaptation and early warning now, make the money usable without a maze of conditions, and put enforceable emissions cuts into the agreement instead of another chorus of voluntary pledges. Run that money the way the 1936 act ran wire—long-term federal support delivered through institutions the recipients control—so the warning system is still standing when the storm comes. The islands’ first demand was emissions cuts; the money is the second demand. Adaptation without cuts is a subscription to recurring disaster; cuts without adaptation make the islands pay twice.

I do not need to imagine the water at my children’s door to know what comes next. Around Tonga, the threat of the sea coming ashore is already there. Next April I will walk down the bank below the shop and write the ice-out date in my notebook. It will likely be early again—one more line in a ledger the water keeps for everyone. Mike and Quinn are eight and five, the same ages as kids on those islands. My county takes the change one spring at a time: earlier ice-out, wetter weeks, mosquitoes in March. Theirs takes it in the ocean itself: hotter water threatening the reefs and fish their families live on, higher seas chewing at the shoreline, flooding where the water used to stay out. Same water, two shores, and only one of them has an ocean for a doorstep.

Trump left the room where the smallest members of the neighborhood were asking for help staying in it. The United States can stay out of the room, but it cannot stay out of the bill. The rest of us can still name what we see, put it on the record, and refuse to let the ledger be closed by absence.
