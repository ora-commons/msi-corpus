Biden and Trump armed the killing of Gaza’s civilians.

Josh Shapiro puts the matter correctly. In a Wall Street Journal interview this week, the Pennsylvania governor said a version of it out loud: “I don’t think that either the current president or President Biden used the leverage of the United States of America effectively to make sure that the weapons were being used in ways that comport with American values.” The remarkable thing is not that a governor said it. It is that the shipping records have been saying it since the fall of 2023 while the trucks kept rolling.

Fine words. But accountability in this business has an address, and the address is not Jerusalem alone. It is the warehouse in the United States where the crates get loaded. Biden and Trump had leverage. They chose not to use it.

Shapiro defended the U.S.-Israel alliance while saying Israel needs accountability for how the war has been conducted. He did something Washington avoided: separate Israel’s right to defend itself from Washington’s duty to control what American weapons do. Israel had that right after the Oct. 7 attacks, which killed about 1,200 people and took about 250 hostage. That right is real. A right of self-defense is not a permit without a ceiling on the dead.

Michael Walzer’s *Just and Unjust Wars* separates the justice of going to war from the justice of fighting it. Discrimination means directing force at combatants and military objectives, not at whoever lives nearest the target. Proportionality means foreseeable civilian harm cannot be excessive compared with the concrete military advantage sought. Those obligations bind a nation defending itself exactly as they bind an invader. That does not pencil out as pacifism. It is the principle that an ally does not convert American money, weapons, and political exposure into an unlimited instrument.

Weapons are not groceries. They change what an ally can do and what the supplier can be held responsible for. A weapon and the rule governing its use belong together. Without the rule, “defensive” becomes a word the supplier repeats after the dead have been counted. Shapiro’s prescription is plain: Israel should be able to purchase defensive weapons, but those weapons should come “with very clear guidance on how and when they can be used.”

Under the ten-year, $38 billion memorandum the two governments signed in 2016, Foreign Military Financing was set to provide about $3.8 billion a year for Israel to purchase American weapons. Gift. Not a loan, not a credit line; an annual grant. In the shop, when a part I sell fails downstream, the customer comes back to me, and no amount of paperwork between us changes that. The supplier whose product does harm downstream carries the same duty: ask how the parts are being used, and stop the trucks when the answer troubles him. That is leverage. It sat in plain sight for three years.

In spring 2024, the Biden administration held up one shipment of 2,000-pound bombs. The confirmation was the administration’s own. The same month, its report to Congress found it reasonable to assess that some Israeli use of U.S.-supplied weapons had not been consistent with U.S. law and policy. One shipment was the size of the answer. The rest of the pipeline never stopped.

Render the ledger slowly, from the documentary record rather than my memory, because it is what the unspent leverage purchased. By spring 2024, Gaza’s Health Ministry had published a count of the dead beyond 30,000. The judicial record was public too. In January 2024, the International Court of Justice issued provisional measures, the court’s emergency orders, including an order that Israel take all measures to prevent acts within the scope of Article II of the Genocide Convention and facilitate humanitarian assistance. Those were not a final genocide judgment. In November 2024, the International Criminal Court issued criminal arrest warrants for Benjamin Netanyahu, Yoav Gallant, and Hamas commander Mohammed Deif. Those warrants were not convictions, but they were formal judicial action. Biden was president when both filings were issued, and his administration kept the pipeline fed. Trump inherited the war after those filings and did not stop the trucks either. The money’s route was never secret either.

Congress has already touched the lever. In July, more than 100 House Democrats voted for an amendment seeking to cut off military financing for Israel. The vote failed. It still matters, because it puts the central issue plainly: Congress can condition money and refuse support, but the blank check has remained largely intact. Military financing is not a ceremonial expression of friendship. It is leverage, and leverage that is never used is not leverage.

The standing excuse has always been that the lever cannot be pulled without breaking the alliance. That excuse was falsified in 1982. Ronald Reagan suspended cluster-bomb shipments to Israel until he had assurances about how American cluster munitions were being used in Lebanon. He used the leverage, and the alliance did not collapse. It is alive forty-four years later, which is more than can be said for the people the leverage was not used for. The men who declined the tool were declining the job.

Then the file passed to Donald Trump. There was no hold left for him to lift; the last administration had already let the big bombs move again before it left office, and what crossed his desk was not a pause but a green light. This fall, a cutoff in American support tested the alliance itself, as [the midterm results and Trump’s aid cutoff](/articles/2026-11-06-midterm-election-results-and-trump-s-aid-cutoff-test-the-u-s--israel-alliance/) showed. The lesson is the same. The relationship gets governed by weather, not policy. When the political wind shifts, a shipment pauses; when it shifts back, the crates load. A supplier who conditions weapons on the news cycle rather than on the law is not exercising leverage. He is being leveraged.

Eisenhower saw the machinery coming, and he named it while he was still inside the White House. In his farewell address of January 17, 1961, paragraph 24, he warned: “In the councils of government, we must guard against the acquisition of unwarranted influence, whether sought or unsought, by the military-industrial complex.” His speechwriters’ early draft had gone further and called it a “military-industrial-congressional complex.” The word “congressional” was cut before the address aired. This summer’s failed amendment shows what the cut cost. A weapons relationship built across four decades is not a policy a president can dial back with a phone call. It is infrastructure wired through appropriations committees, arms-sales notifications, and industry production plans. Two administrations of opposite temperament produced the same freight schedule because the machinery, not the man, sets the tempo.

Andrew Bacevich’s *Washington Rules* identifies the rules that took hold in the early Cold War and outlived it: maintain global military preeminence, lead, be present, and intervene. Shapiro is challenging the rule that turns those commitments into blank checks. When an ally can use American power without conditions, Washington supplies the means and surrenders the responsibility.

Shapiro calls Netanyahu “a failed leader” who “led Israel down a dangerous, dark, isolated path.” That is a strategic diagnosis, not etiquette. A leader who spends his allies’ patience, treats American weapons as an unlimited resource, and makes his own country’s isolation a policy has not strengthened his state. He has consumed the reserve of friends. Civilians in Gaza pay the bill in blood while Washington and Jerusalem preserve the language of partnership.

From where I sat in the gunner’s seat in Iraq in 2003–2005, the point was plain: the round did not sort out who was a combatant before it reached a man standing in his own street. The weapons around us carried consequences into that country, and the men around the vehicles had to live inside those consequences. The round does not sort that out once it is fired, and somebody has to answer for it. Supplier responsibility is not an abstraction imposed after the fact.

That discipline runs through the rest of Shapiro’s [foreign-policy priorities](/articles/2026-11-20-pennsylvania-gov-josh-shapiro-lays-out-foreign-policy-priorities/). He wants the United States to rapidly increase support for Ukraine, share intelligence and weaponry, rebuild trust with allies, and help negotiate an end to the war on terms that do not give Russia what it wants. He says Trump undermined Zelensky and coddled Putin, even after posting a picture of himself embracing the Russian president with the caption “My Friend.” On Iran, Shapiro says, “Trump decided to go it alone. Didn’t explain to the American people why he was doing it,” and that he, Pete Hegseth, and JD Vance “have no idea how to get out.” Support an ally toward a defensible end, use American power to shape conduct, and demand honesty about the cost. That is disciplined power.

Now, I’m just a simple man, but a supplier who can read what his ordnance is doing to a city and keeps the trucks rolling is not conducting diplomacy. He is supplying the means of a campaign that is killing civilians. The remedy does not require heroics, because the tools are already on the books. The Arms Export Control Act requires the administration to notify Congress before major arms sales take effect, with a waiting period in which Congress can object. An administration willing to spend the leverage would attach a certification to the next shipment: show the accounting of how American weapons are being used under the laws of war, or the next crate stays on the dock. Shapiro’s own prescription points the same direction. Israel should be able to buy defensive weapons with clear guidance on how and when they may be used. Guidance for defensive systems is the floor. The offensive pipeline needs the identical rule, and the record shows two administrations that would not write it down.

Congress should make the condition law. Because military financing and major arms transfers depend on congressional appropriations and notification, Congress should tie support to monitoring of how weapons are actually used, a right to inspect, and compliance with the rules separating military targets from civilians and prohibiting disproportionate civilian harm; it should require a public report when an ally seeks to use American weapons beyond those limits and suspend support when the limits are ignored. The executive can conduct diplomacy and direct military operations, but Congress holds the purse and the authority to transfer arms. A president who wants war without a limit should have to ask Congress for war without a limit.

That is what leverage means. It is not a quarrel after the funeral or a condition announced after the next strike. It is a line drawn before the weapon leaves the warehouse.

Biden and Trump left the weapons moving and the civilians dying. The next president can defend an ally without defending every act that ally commits. Put the conditions in law, or call the blank check what it has been: complicity. The ledger for the charge is kept in the graves of Gaza’s civilians.
