The Pentagon is leaving Ukrainian civilians to die without enough Patriot missiles.

The receipts sit in the open. By the end of July, the Center for Strategic and International Studies estimated that U.S. PAC-3 inventories were roughly 65 percent smaller than before the Iran war began in February. The PAC-3 MSE, the latest Patriot interceptor, is a hit-to-kill weapon. It slams into an incoming ballistic missile instead of exploding beside it. Each MSE costs about $4 million, takes roughly two years to make, and contains hundreds of components. After Russia invaded Ukraine in 2022, Kyiv asked for a Patriot battery. It proved effective against Russian ballistic missiles and more-maneuverable hypersonic projectiles. The threat did not disappear. The Pentagon’s capacity to meet it did.

The response is procurement on an industrial timeline the war has already overtaken. Washington has signed seven-year agreements with Lockheed Martin, which builds the PAC-3 MSE, and L3Harris, which supplies the motor that gives the interceptor greater range. Lockheed’s agreement calls for more than tripling PAC-3 MSE production to about 2,000 a year by the end of 2030. Those contracts give contractors time to build capacity. They also tell the government to wait.

Two decades after Snecma’s C2 rayon was qualified for Patriot nozzles in 2005, the new contracts are paying for capacity the qualified supply chain cannot yet feed. ArianeGroup’s new French line is expected to begin production in 2028, and its output will fall far short of U.S. demand. A preliminary U.S. factory financed in part by Park Aerospace could open around 2030. Park and L3Harris are testing other rayon sources, but none has completed qualification.

At a parts counter, a delivery date is not a part. Rayon is the stuff of blouses and suit linings. Its brushed form burns fiercely, which caused panic over knockoff “torch sweaters” and a Federal Trade Commission investigation in the 1950s. Researchers at Union Carbide discovered in 1958 that heating rayon slowly in a vacuum transforms it into heat-resistant carbon fiber. Woven into a rocket nozzle, it forms an ablative barrier, a lining designed to burn away above 5,000 degrees Fahrenheit so the motor can survive its own fuel long enough to fire.

The newest Patriot cannot simply use whatever cloth is available. Only one carbonized rayon product is approved for the PAC-3 MSE. Qualifying another can take two years, and a candidate can fail its final test after everything else has gone right. One small structural flaw can send the interceptor off course. The qualification clock and the production clock now meet on the same strained supply chain.

Park Aerospace is the sole approved U.S. supplier. Its qualifying carbonized rayon comes from ArianeGroup, the Airbus-Safran joint venture that carbonizes fabric near Bordeaux and sells the blackened rolls as Raycarb. The fiber begins at a Czech factory owned by a Thailand-based chemicals conglomerate. The yarn moves to France to be woven and carbonized. Park then coats the material with custom resin in Newton, Kan., where sixty air-conditioning units hold the temperature below 80 degrees and humidity at 20 percent during summer heat that can reach triple digits. Park ships it to L3Harris, which layers it inside a dual-pulse solid rocket motor, packed fuel that fires in two pulses. Lockheed assembles the missile, with a Boeing seeker in its nose to guide the interceptor home.

The chain has many corporate names and one qualified material source. A fire, strike, natural disaster, bankruptcy, or shutdown can cascade through several weapons at once. The system has redundancy in logos and vulnerability in material.

That danger is not confined to Patriot. In March, Air, a defense software company, reported that more than 70 percent of second-tier PAC-3 suppliers also supply the THAAD missile system, with even greater overlap among third-tier suppliers. The second-tier suppliers are the smaller companies beneath Lockheed and L3Harris that machine castings, make wiring, process chemicals, and build other parts. One interruption can therefore touch several weapons at once.

That chain was made, not found. For most of the Cold War, the sole producer of aerospace-grade rayon in North America was a textile plant on the Shenandoah River in Front Royal, Va. When the factory fell on hard times in the 1980s, NASA and the Department of Defense pooled $38 million to keep it running. State authorities shut it down in 1989 for discharging dangerous chemicals into the river. North American Rayon Corp. filled the void in Elizabethton, Tenn., but wound down production in 1998. The Pentagon stockpiled what remained until that stockpile dwindled in the mid-2000s.

Washington then looked abroad. The French engine maker Snecma Propulsion Solide had experimented with ablative materials made from high-tenency rayon fibers used in car tires. NASA had previously found tire-cord rayon too weak for rockets, but Snecma developed a treatment that solved the problem. Its C2 product matched or beat the old North American rayon in tests, and Snecma partnered with the company that later became Park Aerospace to qualify a resin-coated version for Patriot nozzles in 2005. Snecma is now inside ArianeGroup.

The consolidation of the rest of the arsenal was not an accident either. After the Soviet Union collapsed, Clinton administration officials told defense-industry leaders at the 1993 “Last Supper” that military spending would be slashed. Over the following quarter-century, the number of primary defense contractors fell from 51 to five. Specialized manufacturers and machine shops left defense work or closed, while manufacturing shifted overseas. Workers with niche skills lost jobs and decades of know-how. In the decade before a 2022 Pentagon report, the number of small businesses in the defense industrial base fell by 40 percent. The post-9/11 wars produced surges followed by cuts. The missing suppliers did not return when Russia invaded Ukraine or when the Iran war began. One defense executive said the new munitions plans needed to have started five or more years earlier. Another compared the effort to pushing a basketball through a garden hose. The town booked the closures as savings.

Eisenhower, in his farewell address of January 17, 1961, paragraph 24, warned: “In the councils of government, we must guard against the acquisition of unwarranted influence, whether sought or unsought, by the military-industrial complex.” His early drafts at his presidential library used “military-industrial-congressional complex,” and the word “congressional” was cut before delivery; he also told the country, “Only an alert and knowledgeable citizenry can compel the proper meshing of the huge industrial and military machinery of defense with our peaceful methods and goals.” The general who commanded the Normandy invasion was nobody’s idea of soft on defense. The consolidation record above is what the warning looked like when the country filed the speech and kept shopping.

Andrew Bacevich, the retired Army colonel whose son was killed in Iraq, gave the warning its working sequel in *Washington Rules*. He named the settled conviction held in Washington across the administrations that cut the industry and the administrations that spent what remained of it: America must maintain military preeminence everywhere and be ready to fight anywhere. The Pentagon’s own buying formulas rested on post-Cold War assumptions about short, limited wars. The future the planners feared, one long war or several at once against well-equipped enemies, arrived in Ukraine and the Iran war while the factories stayed where they were.

A longer contract can preserve a contractor’s production plan and a government budget line. It cannot restore a qualified supplier or a machine shop full of people who know how to run it.

Ukraine has already shown what that means at the sharp end. Its battery worked exactly as designed while the interceptors ran short, leaving cities under barrage with less protection than the war demanded. Washington approved [a $2.1 billion sale of 500 Patriot missiles to Ukraine](/articles/2026-10-31-us-approves-2-1bn-sale-of-500-patriot-missiles-to-ukraine/), and those missiles were [understood to be new builds](/articles/2026-10-30-us-approved-patriot-missiles-for-ukraine-are-understood-to-be-new-builds/) rolling off the same constrained line intended to refill American stocks. New-build interceptors can bring relief. A government cannot call an emergency sale a replenishment plan.

Increasing ArianeGroup’s output would also mean taking allocations from top-priority customers in France, including the French military. Park’s Brian Shore brokered a call between the Pentagon and ArianeGroup executives, who reluctantly agreed to speak after several requests and said they could not do much more, according to Shore. He put the dependency plainly: “They’re a French company. They can’t be bullied. They’ll just shut down on you.” France is not a supplier the Pentagon can order around. Dependence turns allies into competitors for the same scarce material.

Germany’s Enka is the defense industry’s other major rayon source, but its product is not qualified for the PAC-3 MSE. Enka’s main business supplies rayon for men’s suits and women’s high fashion. In January 2025, its executives told ICF Mercantile, the company’s sole U.S. importer, that they planned to shut down production. The Pentagon and defense contractors eventually committed to buying enough fabric to keep Enka operating, including a promise to purchase more than 470,000 pounds over two years. ICF Chief Executive Dave Ronner said that merely postponed the closing and that the arrangement still has an ending sometime in 2028. The Pentagon is buying suit cloth to keep a missile material alive. That does not pencil out as readiness.

L3Harris says it can maintain the materials needed through at least the end of its seven-year agreement. I discount contractor confidence in a procurement surge the same way I discount a brake-job estimate made before anybody has pulled a wheel.

Park Aerospace is trying to build capacity ahead of the contracts. It has provided a $5 million advance for ArianeGroup’s French line, promised $25 million for a proposed American factory, and is building a $65 million plant in Oklahoma while using spare capacity in Kansas. Shore said, “The big problem is that nobody wants to wait 3½ years.” Capital is arriving. A qualified U.S. rayon source is not.

From where I sat in the gunner’s seat in Iraq in 2003 and 2004, I learned the distance between what a war plan promises and what an arsenal was built to carry. We were told it would be over quick. It was not quick, and the men I served alongside paid the difference. When the next long war comes, it will draw from the same kind of men and women, the ones who walked into a strip-mall recruiting office because the GI Bill was the only scholarship on the table. Their protection now runs through one fabric plant in France, one resin line in Kansas, and a procurement calendar measured in half-decades.

A physician recently asked Shore, “How many do they have left, Brian? Because I’ve heard a lot of stuff.” That question, asked in a doctor’s office, is the supply chain as it actually exists.

A posture worthy of the Constitution’s war powers starts with Congress, which declares wars and raises armies, and the arsenal is part of what it raises. Congress should not authorize or sustain a deployment without a certified plan for what factories can actually produce, without funded effort to qualify a second source for every input resting on a single supplier, and without a public account of what the inventory holds. The President can accelerate emergency buying, but Congress must sustain the capacity that makes buying possible; no executive order substitutes for a test stand.

Congress controls the purse and should make readiness, not contract length, the condition of missile-defense spending: at least two independently qualified U.S. rayon sources, protected test capacity, and a strategic stockpile that survives a shutdown. The Pentagon should publish the supplier map from first tier through fourth tier, fund qualification before the crisis, and make contractors bear the cost of failed materials. A multiyear purchase order is not a factory.

The carbonized rayon burns slowly inside the rocket nozzle at temperatures above 5,000 degrees. It is meant to sacrifice itself so the interceptor can strike down a lethal incoming missile. The arsenal was allowed to burn away so the plans survived. The difference is being paid where the missiles point, by the soldiers at the firing end and the cities behind them. The procurement choices belong to the Pentagon. The consequence belongs to Ukrainians.
