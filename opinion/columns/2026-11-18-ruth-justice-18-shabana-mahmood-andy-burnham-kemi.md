Shabana Mahmood is turning the perception of two-tier policing into official policy, which is how the powerful convert an accusation into a rule.

In 1993, the boy was waiting for a bus when Stephen Lawrence was killed. He was 18. The inquiry that followed found institutional racism in the Metropolitan Police. Thirty-three years later, Mahmood will tell the National Police Chiefs’ Council that the proportion of Black officers remains virtually unchanged and call that “shameful.” She will announce an “impartiality standard,” promise faster promotion, warn officers against engaging with “activist groups,” and tell them to “leave the politics to the politicians.” Fine. The politicians will keep the pen.

Set the record beside the perception and watch which one gets the policy. The advance extracts promise to “tackle head-on” the “corrosive and growing perception of two-tier policing.” A Guardian analysis found that Black people remained more likely than any other ethnic group to face almost every form of force, from handcuffing and dog bites to firearms and Tasers, compared with population or arrest figures. A More in Common poll found that one Briton in three believes police treat ethnic minorities more favourably than white people, and the share has doubled in two years. The public sees two rules. The Home Office sees a message problem. Mahmood says there have been examples of differential treatment, too. Fine. The speech puts the perception first, while the record gives the belief real purchase.

Place the record, the advance account and her sentences side by side and they convict without me. One: the missing Black officers, 33 years on, are “shameful.” Two: the advance account calls it “patently absurd” to say that fair policing does not mean treating everyone the same. Three: “We must also be wary of misguided attempts at course correction, where the pursuit of equality leads instead to differential treatment.” The Police Anti-Racism Commitment says producing equality of policing outcomes “does not mean treating everyone ‘the same’.” Equality of outcomes is the disputed premise. Unequal treatment becomes “differential treatment” for the Home Office to correct. Shameful for the record. Absurd for the remedy. A ministry for the lie—the part of the accusation that outruns the record and then gets a rulebook.

An accusation blessed by the state does not retire. It recruits. The purchase buys her party quiet, and the minister collects a national standard. So she has earned the title—the Perception Secretary. The job description is simple: turn grievance into guidance, give guidance the force of office, and hand the critics a certificate that the government heard them. She wins her fights by agreeing with the people who started them. Disarmament is when the other man puts the gun down. This is picking the gun up and calling the exercise statesmanship.

I have watched this movie since Nixon. It has opened in my country too: a party conceded the fear, consecrated the fear, and learned that fear does not do gratitude.
