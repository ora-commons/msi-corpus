The [Donald J. Trump and Jeffrey Epstein Memorial Reading Room](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2026/nov/08/jeffrey-epstein-survivors-exhibition) is a traveling exhibition of Epstein investigative files. It was first held in a New York City gallery in May. It has stopped in Washington DC, Des Moines, Columbus, Detroit, Dallas and Fort Lauderdale, and a traveling bookmobile made about two dozen more stops. In all, visitors left approximately 12,500 to 13,000 notes, according to David Garrett, founder of the Institute for Primary Facts, the organization that stages the exhibition.

The notes began as a cork board two feet by three feet, standing in the last room of the first installation. Garrett put it up beside information about the national suicide prevention hotline and resources for people experiencing gender-based violence, and left out notecards and pens. "We didn't think that we would need more than that two foot by three foot corkboard," he said. By the end of the first day the board was full and cards were taped up around it, about 300 of them. For ten days the producer bought more boards every day. There were 30 by the end of the initial run. "Every surface on that basement downstairs was full of cork boards and full of cards," Garrett said. Some notes bore drawings. Some bore abject sadness. Some, survivor Danielle Bensky said, were pure rage.

The exhibition was assembled to draw attention to Epstein's crimes and to law enforcement's decades-long failure to act. Epstein was arrested in 2019 for the sexual abuse of teen girls and died by suicide in jail. Ghislaine Maxwell was arrested and found guilty of luring teen girls into Epstein's orbit. Documents show that law enforcement officials knew of the crimes decades earlier. Survivors have called for greater transparency and legal action against the other participants.

The tour began several months before the contentious midterm elections, which went badly for the president's side. The White House has rejected criticism of the administration's handling of the files. By late October, [the files had slipped from the midterm focus as advocates sought renewed scrutiny](/articles/2026-10-24-epstein-files-slip-from-midterm-focus-as-advocates-seek-renewed-scrutiny/).

A Department of Justice spokesperson called the exhibition "a sham tour" traveling to "politically competitive areas" and "shamefully using victims as political pawns," called the nonprofit's fundraising "a disgrace," and said the files were released in compliance with the Epstein Files Transparency Act "under the most transparent administration to date." White House spokeswoman Lauren Bis said President Trump had been "totally exonerated on anything relating to Epstein" and had "done more for Epstein's victims than anyone before him," by releasing thousands of pages of documents, cooperating with the House oversight committee's subpoena request, and signing the Epstein Files Transparency Act.

Danielle Bensky did not walk into the gallery to become a political fact. She walked through her trauma. The room did not take the wound away. It made the wound visible. She had worried that the reading room would be "a form of exploitation for survivors." Instead, she said, "it was just so beautifully done." A week later she came back and found six or seven corkboards full and overflowing. Liz Stein says she will never forget seeing that first bulletin board and feeling, "Oh my goodness, people are really reacting to that." Families left notes too. Stein says the love people show them is overwhelming, and that the wall of notes fuels them in a way people do not understand. Bensky: "Being seen in this process has been pretty remarkable." One note said, "Money and power should not dictate justice." The one Bensky kept was the shortest. It said, "It wasn't your fault."
