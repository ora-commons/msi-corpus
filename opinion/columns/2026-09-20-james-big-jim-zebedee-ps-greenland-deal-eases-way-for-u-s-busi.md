Trump threatened to take Greenland, then cut a deal that lets American companies mine its minerals. The people who live there get the subcontracts.

For two years the machinery of American coercion pointed at an island of 56,000 people who fish for a living and depend on more than a billion dollars a year from Copenhagen just to keep the government running. Annexation threats, tariff warnings against allied nations, the worst diplomatic crisis with Denmark since the Second World War — all of it aimed at the world's largest island and the minerals under its ice. Now the security deal is announced, built on a 1951 treaty that already gives Washington the right to put troops and build bases, and the press reports the mood shifting from fear to opportunity. The boogeyman walks offstage.

The man who made the threat is the man claiming credit for the resolution. That is a racket I recognize.

The sovereignty fight was real, and [Greenland's leaders were right to hold that line](/articles/2026-09-19-greenland-denmark-leaders-say-us-security-accord-preserves-sovereignty/). But look at what the deal actually does. It builds on a treaty that already lets the U.S. deploy troops and build bases on the island. The new piece is a clause that says the agreement survives even if Greenland becomes independent from Denmark or NATO collapses. The architects locked in the American position at the moment Western institutions look least durable. Whatever happens to the alliance, whatever happens to Denmark's hold on its territory, the U.S. footprint on the island is now anchored in a bilateral instrument that does not need either institution to function. That is not a safety net for Greenlanders. That is a claim staked in advance.

The press treats this as a security deal that clears the way for investment. I would read it the other way around. The minerals under the ice — rare earths, hydrocarbons, the materials the American economy needs and does not want to buy from China — are what is being opened up. The security language is the packaging. Greenland has been sitting on the world's largest undeveloped mineral inventory for a very long time, and the reason it has not been tapped is not that nobody knew the minerals were there. It is that getting them out is brutally hard, the island has restrictions on foreign ownership of infrastructure and energy, and nobody wanted to write the kind of check a 30-year mining project requires without knowing which flag would be flying over the harbor.

Now they know. That is what the deal changed.

I am not writing about this as an investment story because I have seen this pattern before. Bacevich wrote about the permanent architecture of American forward presence dressed up as local benefit — the same machinery running from Okinawa to the Gulf, the same promise that the base brings jobs, the same result where the locals get the subcontracts and the outsiders get the ore. The quote from Christian Keldsen at the Greenland Business Association tells you everything: American base spending benefits Greenlandic businesses "just like it does in other parts of the world where the Americans operate bases." It does work. It works for the subcontractors who land the catering contracts and the cleaning jobs and the construction work. It does not work the same way for the people who do not.

A Greenlandic fisherman landing halibut at the docks in Nuuk does not get a mining concession. He gets the same fish price he got last year, except now the harbor may have American military traffic and the cost of living ticks up because more people with more money are buying the same small supply of housing and food. A dock worker loading supplies onto a construction crew rebuilding a Cold War runway gets a job, and a job on that island matters, but he does not get a share of what comes out of the ground when the rare-earth extraction begins. A small contractor in Sisimiut who lands a catering gig at a reopened American base gets steady work for a few years, and steady work is nothing to turn down. But the contract is written in Washington, the profit margin is set somewhere far from Sisimiut, and when the base closes again — and they close — the contractor is right back where he started.

Naaja Nathanielsen, Greenland's former business and mineral resources minister, said the deal lets them put the insecurity behind them and build on American investment interest. She is not wrong that the insecurity was real. But the insecurity was manufactured. Trump created it with annexation threats, and now the deal that resolves it opens the door to exactly the kind of access that annexation would have delivered, just with better branding. The Greenlandic political class gets stability and a seat at the table. Ordinary Greenlanders get told the table benefits them.

Two empires are courting this island. The United States offers a security deal. The European Union has been making its own bid through investment pledges. Both offer money. Both want something. The revolving door is already turning: Drew Horn, a former Trump administration official developing a rare-earth venture on the island, told the Journal the deal removes the "boogeyman" from the equation. He also said he helped shape it behind the scenes. A former administration official who stands to profit from the deal helped write the terms of the deal. On a 56,000-person island where the economy runs on fishing and public subsidies, that is not an abstract concern.

Roughly $24 million in U.S. development aid has moved to Greenland since 2020, most of it paid to American consultants to map what lies under the ice and advise the Greenlandic government on how to price its own minerals. That was the reconnaissance phase. The deal is the transition to extraction. The Greenlandic people were not consulted on the reconnaissance, and they will not own the extraction.

The question Greenlanders should be asking is the question working people everywhere should ask when two powerful parties start talking about what they are going to do for you: what do you get that you can keep?

On this island, the answer is the subcontracts. The ore goes to the people who wrote the deal. That is not partnership. That is someone else's deal, and you are told to be grateful for the work.
