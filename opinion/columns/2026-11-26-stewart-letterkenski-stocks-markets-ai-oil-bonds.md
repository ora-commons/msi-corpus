Nvidia sells unverifiable computing promises as markets launder its governance warning.

The immediate beneficiaries of Thursday were the people who sold into renewed bids; the governance warning remained where S&P Global Ratings put it. It is true that Thursday’s rebound carried information, and that U.S. markets were trading ahead of Thanksgiving, when positioning can magnify moves. A market that absorbed Nvidia’s 3.9 per cent fall, a possible credit downgrade and the Kospi’s 6.2 per cent drop without breaking was not panicking. The trouble is that a rebound is good evidence that traders bought risk on Thursday and rather poor evidence that the risk itself shrank. People who needed an exit on Thursday had one they did not have on Wednesday. That is valuable. It is not an answer to S&P.

On Tuesday, [Asian chip stocks surged as Jensen Huang returned to Nvidia CEO](/articles/2026-11-24-asian-chip-stocks-surge-as-jensen-huang-returns-to-nvidia-ceo/). On Wednesday, [AI-linked losses pulled most Asian stock markets lower](/articles/2026-11-25-ai-linked-losses-pull-most-asian-stock-markets-lower/), and Nvidia fell 3.9 per cent as AI shares lost steam for a second day. S&P warned that it could downgrade Nvidia’s credit rating over management and governance concerns. Wall Street then produced a mixed Wednesday close: the S&P 500 fell 0.3 per cent, the Nasdaq lost 0.6 per cent after falling as much as 4.6 per cent intraday, and the Dow rose 0.3 per cent.

Thursday produced the partial recovery. The Nikkei rose 1.3 per cent to 57,768.92, the Kospi added 1.2 per cent, the Taiex gained 2.6 per cent, the Hang Seng rose 1.9 per cent, and Australia’s ASX 200 added 0.9 per cent. OpenAI investor SoftBank Group jumped 9 per cent, Kioxia Holdings surged 12.4 per cent, SK Hynix gained 3.7 per cent, and TSMC added 0.2 per cent. Broadcom ended 0.1 per cent lower. Anthropic, which had made “safety” part of its public identity, recovered 1.1 per cent but remained below its $83 initial public offering price. This was selective buying after indiscriminate liquidation, not a broad verdict on the physical build-out.

It is true that a warning is not a default, and a default is not required before a warning has force. The trouble is that the market can trade the consequence of a warning while leaving the warning unanswered. The available reporting does not set out S&P’s precise concern, and inventing one would be false precision. What is established is enough: a major credit assessor identified management and governance as issues capable of changing Nvidia’s credit rating. Wall Street may price uncertainty. It may not honestly price the warning as though no assessor had raised it. No governance record changed after the warning. The price did.

“AI” is a category label, not a specification. An accelerator chip is not an AI system by itself. An AI build-out is a coordinated machine: fabrication, advanced packaging that connects processors to high-bandwidth memory, networking, storage, cooling, power contracts, buildings, grid connections, and useful workloads. The workloads must cover depreciation, financing, power, and operating costs. Utilization—the share of purchased computing capacity actually doing paid work—must justify the rest.

The chip can perform exactly as promised while the project around it earns a poor return. Performance and project economics are different proofs.

Thursday’s rally bought more than a rebound in one chip vendor. Kioxia, SoftBank, SK Hynix, and TSMC are different links in, or around, the same build-out: memory, investment, and fabrication. Their rise says capital expected constrained capacity to remain constrained long enough for orders to become revenue. It does not say final customers can use the output at prices that repay the capital committed to it.

It is true that constrained supply can be legitimate, that the tools have real value, and that a rebound can reflect positioning rather than doctrine. The trouble is that the market often lets a category label stand in for the cash-flow mechanism. The ticker says AI; the physical system says power, capital, utilization, and who bears the loss when utilization disappoints.

The warning and the rebound were priced by different machines. An equity is a claim on expected future profit; a bond is a claim on promised cash flow, collateral, refinancing, and governance. A share can rebound while the debt remains impaired. A lender cannot mark a risk down by arguing with an index.

The bond market supplied the less theatrical answer. The 10-year Treasury yield, the benchmark used to discount long-lived cash flows, was little changed at around 4.86 per cent. FRED—the St. Louis Fed’s public data service—put it at 4.91 per cent on November 26. Equity buyers got a rebound; long-term capital did not become materially cheaper. At those yields, a long-lived compute build-out must cover power, construction, depreciation, and financing before reaching the returns that made the expenditure appear inevitable. That is not an argument against useful infrastructure. It is an argument against treating governance as a footnote to the discount rate.

Nvidia was already one of Wall Street’s biggest drivers, but index weight is not market definition. Huang’s return as CEO and S&P’s warning belong in the same file. One event changes the story traders tell about demand; the other changes the margin of safety around cash flows. The market spent Thursday answering the easier question.

Investors should demand the records: customer-concentration figures; the size of inventory and purchase obligations; which supply commitments survive a downturn; which capital-allocation decisions require independent board approval; and what happens to revenue if customers build less than expected. None of those records requires a theory of human nature. They require documents.

People who work with machinery learn to ask who is left holding it when the promised output fails to arrive. My father kept his job when Gerdau bought MRM in 1995 while several uncles did not. The difference was not suddenly useless skill; it was which jobs the new owner preserved. Credit analysis asks the same question without anyone standing beside the machine: which promises survive a bad year, and who absorbs the shortfall?

There is a name for the interval in which this trade now lives. John Kenneth Galbraith called it the bezzle: the stretch between the setting-in-motion of a loss and the victim’s discovery of it, when the offender’s books balance and the victim’s pocket does not yet feel lighter. Nobody in the AI trade has been charged with anything, and fraud is not the accusation here. The bezzle requires only a gap between what is being sold and what can be checked, combined with a market content to leave the gap unmeasured. The gap here is the distance between what the models demonstrably do and what the multiples assume they will shortly do. Every quarter the gap is carried without measurement, it widens.

It is worth being precise about what is being sold, because the public discussion has the misleading habit of treating the current generation of large language models as minds. A large language model is a trained parameter set—billions of weights—executed inside an architecture and an inference stack, meaning the code and machinery that turn those weights into output. The weights encode learned patterns; what comes out also depends on how text is divided into units the model reads, how the model code performs the arithmetic, what alignment training has layered on top, and the compute underneath. Nothing in that description is mystical.

Capabilities are measured on benchmarks, which are examinations the system has effectively studied for and cannot be assumed not to have memorised its way through. Benchmarks are not tests against a written specification of everything the system may never do. In the verification trade—the one where you prove with mathematics that a protocol does what it says and not what an adversary wishes it did—the first rule is that you cannot verify a system whose specification you cannot write down. “Artificial general intelligence” is not a specification. It is a fundraising document, and this season it is also a stock price.

It is true that the chips are real. Nvidia sells general-purpose graphics processors used extensively for model training and inference, TSMC fabricates many of its advanced chips, and the models running on that hardware do work in well-defined domains, at costs the customer can read on an invoice, with failure modes researchers catalogue in public. Grant the industry its strongest case in full. The trouble is the second half of the sentence the market is buying: not “the models work,” but “the models work, therefore they replace knowledge work at scale, therefore the payroll of the developed world is a revenue line.” The first half is engineering. The second is a promise, and the capital has been poured into the promise.

On Tuesday, the equity market treated Huang’s return to the job as a catalyst; on Wednesday, the credit market put a flag on Nvidia’s management and governance. The two instruments were pricing two different questions. The equity was a claim on expected AI cash flows. The rating was a judgment about whether the people and processes behind those cash flows could be supervised well enough for the debt to be safe.

The specific governance triggers remained unknown. The question the warning opened was not whether Huang attended the office. It was whether a board whose central asset was one man’s presence at a desk was an audience or a supervisor. The equity market bought the demand story on Tuesday; S&P declined to treat the governance question as settled on Wednesday. It is worth remembering, before the flag is dismissed as bureaucratic timidity, that the last time rating agencies were asked to grade complexity they could not fully model—American mortgage pools—the AAA stamps accumulated until the housing market delivered the correction. The flag may be late. It is not nothing.

The strangest item in the ledger is that the trade’s profitability has come into question partly because the industry asked to be slowed down. The sector’s loudest public argument has been the existential-risk pivot: the rhetorical operation that makes present electricity, water, books, and payroll subordinate to a hypothetical future catastrophe. Adam Becker describes the wider ideology as technological salvation, because it channels attention into abstract future possibilities while postponing present questions of power and distribution.

The pivot’s function is not primarily to warn. It is to license. If the technology is too dangerous to be built by anyone except the builders, the builders inherit the field, and the regulation that follows gets drafted by their own policy departments. To be fair to the people who signed the warnings, the failure modes are real, and the field says so itself. The objection is not to the worry but to the remedy, much of which has been written by the same companies whose models created the danger. The pivot has a market consequence its authors did not put in the filing: what value should an investor assign to a company if the forecast catastrophe occurs?

Meanwhile, the build-out keeps getting more physical, because it always was. Kate Crawford’s *Atlas of AI* makes the relevant point: these systems are not disembodied cognition but assemblies of land, water, fuel, and labour. The ledger of that assembly is where the promises come due.

Microsoft has signed a twenty-year power-purchase agreement—a contract to buy a power plant’s output for twenty years—to restart Three Mile Island Unit 1, an 835-megawatt reactor, with the project accelerated to 2027 through a $1-billion federal loan. Meta has contracted the full 1,100 megawatts of the Clinton nuclear plant in Illinois on the same twenty-year term. An Australian Strategic Policy Institute analysis of TeleGeography’s cable data puts the four hyperscalers at 71 per cent of international submarine-cable capacity, up from roughly one-tenth a decade earlier. The midterms have raised fresh doubts about the pace of further construction. These are not forces of nature. They are signed contracts, which means they are decisions somebody could have declined to make.

The tariff increases planned for China, Japan, the European Union, and other trading partners can add to inflation and consumer prices. Brent crude rose 0.5 per cent to $91.11 a barrel, about $19 above its approximately $72 level before the war began in late February, even as global supply continued to recover. Oil is not the main electricity source for most data centres, but it feeds inflation, interest rates, and transport costs; tariffs can tax cross-border inputs, whoever ultimately pays them. These facts do not disprove demand for large-scale computing. They raise the hurdle. The build-out needs paid utilization, not merely a higher bid for chips.

Cory Doctorow argues that this is a bubble of the WorldCom kind rather than the tulip kind: the infrastructure gets built either way, and the residue after a correction—data centres, chips, open models, and a workforce that learned to label data—is real. His accompanying measure is sharper. The early web grew more profitable every day, and workers had to force it on their bosses. This build-out grows less profitable every day, and bosses force it on workers, on the theory, as Doctorow puts it, that “An AI can't do your job, but an AI salesman can convince your boss to fire you and replace you with one that can't.” The multiple prices the payroll’s departure.

The Thursday-morning buyers in Tokyo and Seoul were not, strictly speaking, buying chips. They were buying the payroll. When a bezzle resolves, the write-down lands on the people who lacked the option to sell first: the workers, the pension funds that own the index, and the ratepayers whose utilities signed the long-term power contracts.

None of this requires a verdict on Nvidia before the evidence is assembled. It requires reading the architecture. Tim Wu’s *The Curse of Bigness* supplies the political question: when a private firm becomes a gatekeeper for an entire field, the issue is not merely its share price but the rules it can impose around it.

Nvidia’s position is partly hardware and partly software. CUDA is its proprietary programming platform and library stack. The lock-in does not mean code cannot be moved at all, but moving it can mean porting, retesting, and rebuilding performance-tuned software. That friction is a chokepoint. A hardware market can look competitive while the software required to exploit it is not portable. Whether particular contracts or refusals amount to exclusionary conduct is a separate evidentiary question; the technical switching cost is not a slogan.

Lina Khan’s “Amazon’s Antitrust Paradox” made competition about governance rather than price alone. Her point was not that every large firm is unlawful; it was that competition law governs private rulemaking. A firm whose hardware, software, and supplier position is indispensable can set terms for businesses and users that cannot easily leave, even when its own price is not extravagant. For Nvidia, the question is not whether chips are cheap. It is whether the firm can write the rules on which the market for current-generation large language models and related infrastructure operates. A low price can still be a high toll.

Cory Doctorow’s four forces provide a cleaner test: competition, regulation, self-help through interoperability, and labour power. Competition can discipline price; regulation can police exclusionary contracts; interoperability gives buyers a way to switch without surrendering their software; labour power lets engineers refuse instructions that degrade the product. A chokepoint that persists while the relevant constraints are absent is not a law of nature. It is architecture waiting to be changed.

Before another rebound is treated as vindication, Nvidia should disclose the customer-concentration, inventory, supply-commitment, capital-allocation, and governance facts behind S&P’s warning, while S&P should state what evidence would resolve its concern. Regulators should examine whether proprietary software and hardware supply make the accelerator layer a chokepoint, with structural remedies following only if the examination establishes exclusionary conduct rather than merely a large and useful business. The faster lever is portability: customers should be able to move workloads across accelerators through open interfaces without surrendering their software stack, supported by audit rights over deployed models and bright-line rules for the companies conducting this scale of extraction.

That is where the laundering occurs. A management and governance warning enters the market; within a trading session it becomes volatility; by the next session it becomes a reason to buy the components. No governance record changed after the warning. The price did. The market has a rare talent: it can convert a warning about the machinery into weather.

Short-horizon traders can sell into renewed bids, and existing shareholders receive a higher price without any governance record changing. The unresolved risk belongs to lenders and customers whose capital is already committed. The market has moved the price of unanswered governance off the screen and onto the balance sheets least able to diversify it.

A bezzle ends one of two ways: somebody measures the gap, or somebody finances it. This week managed a little of both. S&P put a governance flag on the trade’s central asset; Tokyo put 9 per cent on SoftBank and 12.4 per cent on the memory maker. The next measurements sit on other people’s calendars: the twenty-year terms on contracts with two nuclear plants, and the 10-year Treasury yield near 4.9 per cent, which is the benchmark against which every long-dated promise in the file is discounted. The industry asked Washington for a slowdown in the name of safety. Thursday’s tape slowed nothing down; it bought. The next relevant document is the rating action, not tomorrow’s Asian close.
