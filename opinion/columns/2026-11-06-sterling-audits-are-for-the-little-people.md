Verla is a bobbin on the weave floor of the blanket mill I own outside Greeneville, Tennessee, and I've never broken a labor law in 22 years of running it. Not once. James Freeman writes in [the *Wall Street Journal*](https://www.wsj.com/opinion/bernie-bros-plan-a-new-war-on-business-2c6f70fb?mod=rss_opinion) that the incoming committee plans to examine the companies doing business with the government, and that the plan reaches firms "even if the businesses have operated lawfully," and he asks whether the investigators even care whether a company has done anything wrong. He files that as a scandal. I file it as a résumé. Lawful was never the fence I stood behind. Lawful is the fence I built everything up against, to the inch, on purpose, and your taxes pay $2.14 a yard for the olive-drab my looms weave. The surplus blanket folded in your trunk came off Verla's loom. Thank you, by the way. The blankets are good and the invoice has never once been late.

A bobbin, by which I mean a woman of 51 whose chest locks tight every Monday and lets go by Wednesday, and who has never cost me a citation. The crew that would vacuum the lint off my rafters quoted me $310 a week in 2019. I spread it across the year's yardage and it came to $0.002 a yard.

$0.002 a yard. That's the margin, and it fits inside a rounding error. The Lord said the poor would always be with you. He did not say who would do the organizing.

Since the investigators are being promised to you like a change in the weather, let me save them the mileage. The questions always come in the same order, and every answer that lands in a file is the same word. Did the air in the weave room violate the cotton-dust standard? No. The standard sets a permissible ceiling, and my air sits under it, quarter after quarter, because the sampling is scheduled for the morning after a holiday shutdown. The looms stand silent for 3 days. The lint settles where the sampler can't reach. That's the entire protocol, and it's beautiful. Three words on a calendar buy more safety than any filter, because they buy the measurement itself.

Is the lint dangerous? It has a name older than the standard. Byssinosis. The signature is a chest that goes tight on the first day back after time off and loosens as the week wears on, and Verla's chest has kept that calendar for 6 years. 312 working days. Her body tells her what the air is doing every Monday morning, in the one language the sampler isn't built to hear.

Will her lungs fail? On the current curve, yes. That one has never been asked in a file. Files keep only the other word. And here is the part the paperwork was never drawn to hold. Byssinosis takes years to declare itself, and it does not care whose name is on the door when it does. I've sold the mill twice since Verla started and bought it back both times, the way other men trade trucks. She signs through a staffing agency, and the agencies cycle hands through my floor every 14 months, so the exposure log is always somebody else's paper in a room where the air has never tested over the line.

My air samples pass. Verla's lungs do not.

In 2031, on the current curve, Verla becomes a write-down.

The gate never empties. There's a woman named Lorna in the Greeneville employment office this morning who has never met Verla and fits her station exactly.

He is right to be alarmed, and I'd like to shake his hand, because nothing wrong is ever found on my floors. I keep it that way the way I keep everything else. A columnist who guards my empty visitor log for free is my favorite kind of overhead. He isn't asking to be found innocent. He's asking for an empty visitor log. So am I. We are the same request, filed 40 years apart.

Now a second floor with the same customer, because the government eats too. Gwen is a kettle hand on the noon line of the school kitchen I run under contract in Danville. 220 schools in the district. 4,100 trays at the bell, and your child eats off this line, and your taxes pay my contract, so you and I have been partners since kindergarten. Nobody has inspected a kitchen of mine in 11 years, and I built it that way the way I build everything else. The district spec calls the kettle pour a 2-person job, on account of scalds. The 2nd pair of hands costs about $2,200 a year. Gwen pours alone. Spread across her 738,000 trays, the missing hands come to $0.003 a meal. I keep the $0.003. I round it down. Thank God, nobody comes.

In February the steam took the web of her left thumb. The file's word for it was an unscheduled steam release. That's the polite file term, because the file gets read by people who set down their forks, and the fork crowd funds the school board. An unscheduled steam release, by which I mean the steam took the web of her left thumb and she wrapped it herself at the pot sink, because wrapping it herself is faster than reporting it. She's rostered through a staffing house I also hold, so the burn isn't on my insurance. It's on the emergency room. Which is to say it's on you. You bought the lunch and you bought the bandage, and I kept the difference between the two receipts. The children come to the line at 12:15 regardless. Suffer the little children; mine arrive on schedule.

They tell me the honorable course is legislation: if the committee believes the government's vendors should be scrutinized, let it write a law instead of a subpoena. I'd welcome the law. I am lawful in every county I operate in. My attorneys speak statute the way farm families speak weather. Legality has never been my worry. Throughput is. My floor loses about 4% of its output on any day it has an audience, because hands slow down when they're watched. Everyone's do. It is the entire theory of the cameras I run on my own people. The scanner doesn't find crime. It finds the idle second, and the idle second is mine. The same physics runs in reverse when the watcher is somebody else's.

Take the same contract paper 200 miles north, because the law travels. Bennie runs the steam table in the federal courthouse cafeteria in Richmond, and his kitchen is Gwen's kitchen wearing a better seal. If you've ever served on a jury in that building, you ate off Bennie's table, and I appreciate the business. A federal inspector did come once, in 2024. She sat on the lobby couch for 40 minutes with the compliance binder my office prepares, initialed the checklist, and left.

She saw the binder. The kitchen is 11,000 square feet. The binder is 190 pages. Read the next sentence twice: she was on the premises for 40 minutes and never crossed the kitchen line.

Now the part I'm genuinely proud of, because it's the part no columnist can see from his desk. My office wrote that binder. The specific binder. The man who drafted it sat in my seminar at Warden 9 years ago, second row, and he took the subtraction faster than I grade it now. The course was called Compliance as Appearance, Tuesday evenings, and it is full every fall. I have taught at Warden for 30 years. Two of my graduates staff the regional inspector general's office. Step back until the floor is small, because Verla and Gwen are instances and the shape is the lesson. The right to look has run one way for as long as there have been floors. Cato's farming manual is 2,200 years old, and its 2nd chapter orders the owner, on the day he arrives at the farm, to go around it and see what work is done and what is wanting. In 2,200 years the manual has never once asked the field to go around the man. The field Cato's owner walks is never walked back. I have built 40 years of floors on that asymmetry, and I am pleased to report that it is still not a crime. The plantation ledger had one reader: the man who wrote it. The company town's books were the company's. For 4,000 years the mudsill has been measured at machine speed, and the measuring of the measurer is a young invention, barely a century old, already ending. Here is the principle the case is only an example of. Freeman thinks the law is a wall I might someday be caught crossing. The law is not a wall. The law is a floor plan, and I built to it. Every ceiling on my floor was priced before it was passed, and my margin lives exactly one step beneath each one, and every inspector arrives to find a room that performs compliance the way Verla's chest performs health: one day a week, on schedule. I have never been charged, cited, or fined. Being lawful was never my defense. It's my product. I manufacture compliance the way the looms manufacture olive-drab, and your government buys both.

The arrangement is older than the loom, older than the mill, older than the country the blankets ship to.
