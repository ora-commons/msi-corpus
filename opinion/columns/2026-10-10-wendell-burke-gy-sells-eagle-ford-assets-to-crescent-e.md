Ninety thousand net acres across Karnes, DeWitt, and Gonzales Counties are being converted into share buybacks, and not one dollar of the $4.2 billion will pass back through those three counties. Robb M. Stewart [reports it in the Wall Street Journal](https://www.wsj.com/business/energy-oil/devon-energy-sells-eagle-ford-assets-to-crescent-energy-for-4-2-billion-33e48b1c): Devon Energy has signed a definitive agreement to sell its Eagle Ford assets — roughly 68,000 oil-equivalent barrels a day of net production — to Crescent Energy, effective July 1, closing around year-end. The proceeds, the company says, will accelerate share buybacks and cut debt.

Devon's case, stated fairly, is not nothing. This is a mature asset accounting for about 4% of total production, sold into a strong commodity window — the kind of disciplined exit a farmer admires when a neighbor sells a marginal forty to keep the home place debt-free. Clay Gaspar, the president and CEO, said selling into strength "improves our go-forward capital efficiency," and the company calls the deal accretive per share to free cash flow and net asset value. Crescent, meanwhile, is no dilettante: David Rockecharlie says his company has operated next door for years and knows the rock, and it pays a net purchase price near $3.85 billion after adjustments for adjacency it can monetize on day one. Devon has just absorbed some $21.5 billion of Coterra Energy to build one of the world's largest shale operators around the Delaware Basin's core. Concentrate on the asset that earns premium capital; sell the rest into a good market. That is a coherent strategy, and I've watched enough commodity boards to know how rarely coherent strategy survives contact with a quarter.

But look at what the coherent strategy does. The Eagle Ford is not an abstraction on a slide. It is pads and roads and water hauling and a supply chain, and beneath it, mineral interests owned by people whose royalty checks now pass through a Houston consolidator rather than a company with a yard in the country. Those are the silent counterpart to this deal: the royalty holders, the lease-fee owners, the school district that budgets on oil and gas property tax, the vacuum-truck operator, the diner that feeds the night crew. The acreage becomes "inventory" — which is what land is called once the people who live on it stop mattering to the accounting.

And the buyback is the tell. A buyback builds nothing. It produces no barrel, hires no one, paves no county road. What it does is shrink the share count so that every per-share figure looks better, and it moves cash out of a place and into the accounts of shareholders — most of whom will never drive that road, and none of whom will be there when the rig count turns down and the service yards go dark. Gaspar called it value for shareholders. I'd call it value lifted off a rock. A community cannot re-form on a quarterly timetable, and it cannot bargain with an owner who is constitutionally free to sell it again at the top of the next cycle.

This is bigness in the sense Louis Brandeis meant — not that scale is inefficient, but that it is dangerous to liberty. The independent operator with a name and a shop gives way to a holder of inventory answering to a board that meets elsewhere, and the pattern repeats wherever the shale patch consolidates. I don't want a federal reviewer to bless this either; concentrated state power watching over concentrated capital is the same disease in a different coat, and Kierkegaard was right that arrangements made automatic from above tend to kill the thing they mean to protect. The earth was given for all, and property carries a social mortgage; a business that severs itself from the place that made its cash possible has broken a partnership between the living, the dead, and those not yet born.

There is an answer, and it is distributed. Mature basins throw off real cash — the question is whether that cash stays near the rock. It can: royalty-owner associations bargaining collectively, the way Capper-Volstead let farmers bargain against concentrated buyers; community trusts taking a carried interest in mature acreage so a county holds a claim on production for as long as the wells flow; steward ownership, in which shares carry obligations and cannot be sold to the highest bidder in a price environment. Land O'Lakes and the credit unions and the rural electric co-op headquartered in my own county are proof that this form scales and endures. None of it centralizes anything. It just refuses to accept that the highest use of ninety thousand acres is a per-share figure on a screen in a city that has never seen it.

Devon will close this deal around year-end, and the analysts will call it disciplined, and they will not be lying. The rock is still there. The only question is whether anything the rock earns will stay anywhere near the people who live on top of it — and a movement that once claimed to conserve the rooted things now answers that question with a shrug and a ticker symbol.
