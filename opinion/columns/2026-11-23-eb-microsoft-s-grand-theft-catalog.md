Remember how Microsoft’s $75 billion acquisition of video game publisher Activision Blizzard was supposed to make it an unstoppable monopoly? Biden antitrust regulators said exactly that a few years ago, and they were right to fight it. Their lawsuit to block the deal died in a court that saw a purchase and missed a power grab, and Grand Theft Auto VI’s mega-launch last week is not evidence that the regulators were wrong.

Take-Two Interactive Software announced that its sixth installment of its Grand Theft Auto franchise generated $3.79 billion in revenue worldwide on the first day of its launch, a payday that puts who owns the shelf in front of regulators. That’s more than the total sales of the top-grossing movie of all time, “The Avengers: The Endgame,” which premiered in 2019 and brought in $2.9 billion. One title. One day. A record. That is not proof of a healthy market. That is an industry’s rewards pooling at the top.

Who saw the problem coming? Biden Federal Trade Commission Chair Lina Khan, who argued that Microsoft’s acquisition of Activision would let it dominate the video-game ecosystem and hurt competitors. That was never a fringe theory. It was a warning.

Microsoft makes gaming devices and publishes games like “Call of Duty,” some of which were acquired in the Activision deal three years ago. It is the company that builds the shelf and fills it. That is not competition. It is a toll road. Microsoft also licenses games from publishers like Take-Two, taking a cut of sales and in-game purchases. The company that builds the shelf now operates the tollbooth on other people’s work. The FTC argued that its Activision acquisition would hurt video game competition and innovation, and that argument deserved to win.

A federal judge ruled against the FTC in 2023, and that is how one company ended up owning a catalog and the shelf at the same time. Microsoft has since struggled to capitalize on the acquisition. $75 billion bought a catalog instead of a market. Its Xbox made up roughly 25% of Grand Theft’s early sales, versus 75% for Sony’s PlayStation. That is the competitive field this deal was cleared into. The shelf decides who plays, and a company that owns both a catalog and the shelf is not merely a competitor. It is the referee.

Microsoft announced this summer it would cut 3,200 jobs from its video-game division and divest some game studios. Three years after the $75 billion merger, the news out of Xbox is a thinner payroll and studios on the block. Executives place the bets; workers cover the losses. “Our business today is not healthy. We are operating at margins that are 3-10x lower than comparable platform and publishing businesses,” Xbox CEO Asha Sharma said. “To grow, we bet on Game Pass, multi-platform, and a broader portfolio of content.” That is the growth plan of a company that paid $75 billion to acquire a catalog and now has to put games on other people’s shelves, because its own shelf is not carrying its weight.

The FTC claimed the Activision acquisition would make those Microsoft businesses indomitable. Wrong. The warning stands. What the acquisition delivered was a company cutting 3,200 jobs, selling studios and running on margins 3-10x lower than comparable platform and publishing businesses. They were right to try to stop it. “While those businesses have created meaningful value, they did not grow at the pace we expected,” Ms. Sharma said. “In a typical year, we lost 64 cents for every dollar we invested.” Read that again: sixty-four cents lost on every dollar invested in the very businesses the FTC said would become indomitable, after a judge waved the merger through. That is the acquirer saying in its own words what $75 billion bought: meaningful value, and then not much growth on top of it. Yikes.

It’s hard to predict how markets will evolve. That is the case for stopping a merger while it stands in the dock, not for clearing it and waiting to find out. A company big enough to buy a catalog and the shelf is exactly the concentration that matters, and in a typical year this one loses 64 cents on every dollar it invests. Antitrust enforcers don’t blunder when they challenge businesses because they are too big. That is the job. The blunder was the green light.
