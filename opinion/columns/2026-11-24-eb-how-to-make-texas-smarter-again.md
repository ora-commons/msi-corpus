A relief and triumph of politics these days is the Democratic Party’s return to public education and abandonment of test-driven education reform. The midterm blue wave is already proving it across the country, and no more so than in Texas.

Democratic Governor-elect Gina Hinojosa is threatening to end the state’s education savings account program that helps children afford private school tuition or tutoring. She’s also targeting the test-based punishment system masquerading as accountability for learning results.

In an effort to reward test scores and retain compliant teachers, Texas created the Teacher Incentive Allotment program in 2019. It’s optional for school districts, which get state money to pay bonuses to teachers who do well on evaluations created by districts and approved by the state.

The program offers up to $36,000 in pay boosts to the best test scorers, and funding formulas concentrate the prizes in high-poverty-rate and rural schools—lottery tickets issued where the paycheck is owed. Some $750 million was distributed to more than 65,000 teachers in more than 800 districts last school year: money that might have simply raised salaries for everyone, underwriting the testing regime.

For once, Ms. Hinojosa told the Texas Tribune last week she wants to “transition away” from the program, saying she’d prefer “a compensation model that is really applying to all Texas teachers.” She said the program is “about focusing on test results.”

Exactly, because tests are a poor measure of student achievement, and a poorer measure of teacher performance. Examining test data through 2023-24, Texas Tech researcher Jacob Kirksey found that the pay incentive increased student math and reading achievement by about a third of a year of learning each year on average in districts that adopted it. He also found that it lowered teacher turnover and helped retain the teachers the tests flattered. Money moved teachers, and teachers moved children. The lesson is not to rank. It is to pay.

As one Dallas second-grade teacher who earned merit pay said in 2024: “In what other industry can you be average and be paid the same amount of money as somebody that is phenomenal?” That question exposes the testing machine’s deadening logic: educators are taught that some children are worth more than others. The program’s premise is a second-grade classroom scored like a sales floor.

Several other states have a version of merit pay, and another recent study found good results out of South Carolina—the citation every bonus scheme will carry for a decade, measured, as everywhere, by the tests. It makes perfect sense to pay teachers more for doing the whole job, or for earning a graduate degree, as many states and districts do. Advanced training deserves compensation. What makes no sense is auctioning raises to whoever tops a ranking. Teachers unions oppose merit pay because it puts punishment pressure on teachers, pinning each teacher’s paycheck to a test score, and they’d rather get automatic pay increases across the board. Those raises are not a union indulgence. They are what Texas owes every teacher so no child is reduced to a test score.

Ms. Hinojosa has said she’ll replace state education commissioner Mike Morath, the champion of the test-and-grade regime. He was behind the 2023 state takeover of Houston’s public schools—the state seizing a district it had graded into failure—under which test scores have improved on the same tests that did the grading. She’s also said she wants to pause the state’s letter-grade system for branding schools and end the state standardized test and “tests like it.” Those are steps toward ending the ranking machine.

Accountability metrics are instruments of punishment, doing exactly what they were built to do: sorting teachers and branding schools. Ms. Hinojosa wants to get rid of them so teachers can teach children and the state can start funding its schools instead of grading them. That is how Texas gets smarter instead of dumber.
