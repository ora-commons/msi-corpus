New York City is abandoning Flatbush families to homelessness.

On November 11, [the building at 1321 Flatbush Avenue came down and killed eight people](/articles/2026-11-12-apartment-building-collapse-kills-at-least-eight-in-brooklyn-s-flatbush/), with eighteen more injured. [Rescue crews searched the Brooklyn rubble](/articles/2026-11-12-rescue-crews-search-brooklyn-collapse-rubble-as-death-toll-rises-to-eight/) as the death toll rose to eight. The Guardian’s November 26 report, drawing on the New York Times, identifies the mechanism: excavation at a construction site directly beside the apartment had weakened its foundation. The collapse exposed a wall in the building attached to 1323, and evacuation orders covered every resident of 1323 and 1327 Flatbush.

One of the dead was José Luis Caguana Cosquillo, a 26-year-old Ecuadorian construction worker. The survivors went into hotels. Some rooms had roaches and mice. Several families were sent to Manhattan, more than an hour away by public transit, away from the community they had built. Some stays lasted only through November 30.

Today is November 26. That deadline is four days out. “These families will be essentially homeless,” Mutual Aid for Flatbush said. “There is no plan for them to have a home.”

The math, because the math is the indictment. City officials distributed $60,000 in gift cards to affected residents and families; residents said each card held about $3,000. The Flatbush Development Corporation also provided $200 cards, though it was unclear how many went out. Some estimates put average hotel prices across the five boroughs above $300 a night. Divide it out: $3,000 buys roughly ten nights, fewer wherever the rate runs higher.

At my kitchen table in Fishtown, I know what $2,400 in monthly childcare does to a household with $8,800 in combined take-home pay. I know what a 7% mortgage does because that is what David and I still pay on the rowhouse we bought in 2022, partly with help from David’s grandmother’s estate. My household is not the Flatbush emergency, and I am not treating its arithmetic as every family’s arithmetic. But our table has a floor because we have two incomes and help from David’s grandmother. The difference between my table and theirs is that nobody excavated next to my foundation.

One hotel room for 30 nights costs at least $9,000, more than our entire monthly take-home pay. Some families get less than ten nights. Then what?

Mutual Aid for Flatbush raised about $131,000 from neighborhood residents and distributed it to families from all three affected buildings. That is more than twice the amount of the city’s reported gift-card distribution, raised in days by the block itself. The math is the math. A number with an expiration date is not support; it is a lease on panic. Ten nights is not a housing plan. It is a countdown with a receipt attached.

This did not happen in a forgiving housing market. A 2024 Harvard Joint Center for Housing Studies estimate found that a buyer needed $119,800 in income to afford the median mortgage payment of $2,201, and only one in seven renters could afford that payment. A family pushed out by unsafe housing does not merely lose square footage; it loses the financial floor that makes every other bill survivable.

The excavation was not weather. Eight people paid with their lives. A tenant’s [lawsuit over allegedly ignored cracks before the collapse](/articles/2026-11-18-tenant-sues-over-alleged-ignored-cracks-before-flatbush-building-collapse/) adds an allegation of pre-failure warning, not a judicial finding. The Department of Buildings did not respond for comment. Whatever legal liability remains to be determined, the sequence is documented: excavation next door, weakened foundation, occupied apartment, collapse. This is the first chain. The second begins in the rubble: eight people dead, survivors pushed into hotels an hour from home, and a city response that priced them at ten nights and a date.

The housing response was a countdown. The first hotel stays were set for as little as four days, and community leaders had to push for extensions through November 30. The day after the collapse, Mayor Zohran Mamdani announced that the American Red Cross had set up a supply center at the Flatbush YMCA down the street from the wreckage. The YMCA provided temporary housing, social services, and mental-health support while the mayor’s office worked with the Department of Buildings on recovery. The support center operated for less than a week.

The mayor’s office reported that it was covering funeral costs, connecting immigrants with legal aid, working with the health department and the Flatbush Development Corporation, and accepting donations. Those tasks are real. None of them is a housing plan. For the families in those rooms, November 30 was the end of the answer, and nothing replaced it. This is not emergency housing. It is a hotel reservation with an expiration date.

The missing question is who pays. The families pay: with a hotel room, a transit hour, lost income, scrambled childcare, and a deadline. The cost lands exactly where housing and safety should have landed.

Many affected residents were undocumented immigrants, making matters harder for relocation and rehabilitation. “A lot of the resources that these families are being told they will get do not apply to them yet because of their paperwork situation,” Mutual Aid said. For some families, status became the doorway to aid. Paperwork is not neutral when emergency shelter ends on a fixed date. A parent cannot solve a housing crisis by becoming better at navigating the system that displaced them. Legal aid after November 30 is counsel for the homelessness the city has scheduled.

The coalition called the response “a toss-up of responsibility across multiple people.” That is not analysis. It is responsibility dispersal.

The landlord and construction operator had obligations to residents before the collapse. The Department of Buildings and the mayor’s office had different duties to inspect, regulate, and prepare a response. The Red Cross and community groups are repairing the harm. These actors are not interchangeable, and power is not evenly distributed. When every actor is listed as equally responsible, no actor is accountable. It is an invoice, and the address on it is City Hall.

Matthew Desmond’s central finding in *Eviction* is that eviction is a cause of poverty, not merely a condition of it. This was not an eviction: no landlord served notice, no lock changed. But the economic mechanism converges. The home vanished. Income and community were disrupted. The family was pushed into housing that costs more and delivers less. A response that schedules survivors onto the street converts the catastrophe into the slow kind.

Taylor Swift wrote the mission statement of American care infrastructure in five words: “You’re on your own, kid.” The song’s redemptive turn is lateral: when the vertical institutions fail, the friends answer. Here that has become literal. Mutual Aid for Flatbush raised $131,000 from neighbors because the public response stopped at the hotel. Flatbush demonstrated the lateral safety net while the vertical one checked out of its room.

Anne Helen Petersen’s *Can’t Even* treats burnout as a condition produced by work and life design, not a personal failure. Her named concept of “errand paralysis” describes the administrative overload that follows. The coalition describes families relocating with children, staying in rooms with pests, navigating transit and immigration documents, and trying to preserve employment on a hotel-stay clock. They are not failing to cope. The housing system’s output is exhaustion. Any account that calls that failure to cope mistakes the system’s output for the family’s character. There is no resilience badge for surviving a voucher.

My parents raised three children on my father’s postal-supervisor salary through my elementary years, paid off their house by 2007, and sent all three of us through Catholic schools. David and I earn more in real terms than my parents did together, and we cannot afford for two children what they could afford for three. The generational betrayal is not that my parents were careless. It is that the rules changed underneath families while the promise remained the same. The material arrangement was dismantled.

Annie Lowrey’s line is the one for this: “Poverty in the United States is a choice. Stagnant middle-class incomes are a choice.” The excavation that weakened an occupied building’s foundation was a choice. The city response that gave families a deadline and called it a plan was a choice.

The old list still works: feed the hungry, give drink to the thirsty, shelter the homeless, bury the dead. At St. Stanislaus in Lansdale, my mother’s Altar and Rosary Society organized funeral lunches, and the parish conference quietly covered a family’s rent when the breadwinner’s job vanished. These were not vague moral slogans; they were works. On Flatbush Avenue, neighbors raised $131,000 for the displaced. At St. Barbara’s in Bushwick, mourners carried José Luis Caguana Cosquillo’s casket.

Dorothy Day wrote, “The Gospel takes away our right forever, to discriminate between the deserving and the undeserving poor.” The paperwork line this response has drawn is that discrimination updated for forms and status checks. Day’s own tradition presses harder because the works of mercy without the works of justice are inadequate to the commandment. The city’s gift cards offer charity without a home. The neighbor love is beautiful. It is also not housing policy, and it was never supposed to have to be one.

A city that meant a word of it would already be doing the obvious: extend hotel stays until real relocation exists, write relocation checks against Brooklyn rents instead of tourist-season rates, fund the coalition doing the city’s work, put legal aid in place before the deadline, and provide case management that does not make emergency shelter depend on immigration paperwork. That is not a radical list. It is the invoice for a place that means “families” when it says families.

The federal policy shelf is not empty. The AEI-Brookings paid-leave compromise would provide eight weeks at 70% wage replacement, up to $600 a week, and the Hamilton Project’s Cascio proposal would pair a refundable federal child-care tax credit with QRIS investments and universal pre-K. Those are real pro-family investments, not consolation prizes for a failed housing system; they are supports designed to keep an eligible displaced worker from losing work and care in the same month the housing disappears.

At my kitchen table at 11 PM, I would put one hotel room for 30 nights beside the $3,000 card, the transit time, the childcare bill, and the missed shift. The spreadsheet would not show a failure of planning. It would show a failure of housing policy.

Four days. Somewhere in a Manhattan hotel an hour from home, a family is holding a receipt that runs out on November 30, and behind it there is nothing—no plan, no unit, no next address, just the date. A gift card can buy ten nights. It cannot buy a home after November 30. That is the line item that does not show up in the press release: a home is not a service, and a deadline is not a plan.
