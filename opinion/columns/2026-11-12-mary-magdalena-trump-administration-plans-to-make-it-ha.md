The U.S. Department of Agriculture published the proposed rule on Tuesday. It would stop some people who receive cash aid under the Temporary Assistance for Needy Families program from automatically qualifying for the separate Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program. The government is seeking public comment until next month. If the rule is accepted it does not take full effect for about two years. This is [the same proposal, already reported](/articles/2026-11-11-trump-administration-proposal-could-end-snap-eligibility-for-6-3-million-people/), and the department has now put it in writing.

The department's own estimate is that about 6.3 million people could lose eligibility by fiscal year 2029. That number sits on top of the 5.5 million people who have already lost benefits over the past year. Together, eleven point eight million Americans. SNAP serves about one in ten Americans. Federal guidelines generally limit the benefits to people earning less than 130 percent of the poverty level, a little over $1,700 a month for an individual or nearly $3,600 for a family of four. To reduce duplicative paperwork, the federal government lets people who already receive TANF benefits automatically qualify for SNAP, though they still have to apply. Forty-one states and the District of Columbia have some form of that broad-based categorical eligibility, according to a USDA report from last year. Some states use it to reach people who would not otherwise qualify. In 2009 the Obama administration encouraged states to give people a minimal TANF-funded benefit, an informational pamphlet or a telephone hotline, as a way of qualifying them for the food program.

The first Trump administration proposed to clamp down on the same practice in 2019. President Biden's administration withdrew that proposal in 2021. Stacy Dean, a former USDA official under Biden, said of that withdrawal: "Simply put, we did not want to remove millions of people from the program, particularly working poor or children." The latest proposal is more sweeping. David Super, a Georgetown law professor who has studied the program, said it "is basically proposing to eliminate the flexibility that Congress gave the states to serve working poor people." It could prevent states from basing eligibility on TANF-funded benefits such as job counseling or a one-time voucher for a vehicle repair so that a person can get to work.

The Agriculture Department says nearly 1.8 million children would lose eligibility along with their families, and that 97 percent of them would still qualify for free or reduced-price school meals through another process. The department also says some of those children will not get the lunches, even if they are eligible, if their families do not apply. The Center on Budget and Policy Priorities put it plainly: "The public indicated they want their elected officials to focus on making life affordable. Yet the Trump Administration is doubling down on making it harder for people to feed their families."
