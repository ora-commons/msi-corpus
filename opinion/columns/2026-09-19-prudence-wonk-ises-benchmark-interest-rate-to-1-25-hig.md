Scott Bessent pushed Japan higher and shifted the risk onto American borrowers.

The Bank of Japan raised its benchmark interest rate to 1.25%, the highest level in 30 years, and signaled further increases. Japanese investors hold roughly $2.5 trillion in U.S. stocks, bonds, and other financial assets—about half of Japan’s $5 trillion in overseas portfolio holdings. For decades, low Japanese rates pushed that money abroad. Higher returns at home now weaken the reason to keep all of it there.

Rory Green of GlobalData TS Lombard described the movement plainly: “The yen supertanker is turning.” The phrase is useful if it is treated as a description of balance-sheet adjustment, not as a forecast of an overnight exodus. Japanese insurers, banks, and pension funds do not move trillions because one rate changes. They compare domestic yields, currency-hedging costs, investment mandates, and the expected path of Bank of Japan policy. That is why Norihiro Yamaguchi of Oxford Economics expects any repatriation to be gradual and dependent on further increases. His caution is not a rebuttal to the capital-flow mechanism. It is the mechanism’s time horizon.

The Government Pension Investment Fund illustrates the point. More than a decade ago, GPIF began shifting away from a portfolio that held roughly 60% in Japanese government bonds. Domestic bonds now account for about a quarter of its assets. GPIF owns approximately $240 billion of U.S. government bonds, while its largest foreign equity holdings include Nvidia, Apple, and Microsoft. Japan’s finance minister has said she would like to see more GPIF investment at home, although fund officials say no new decision has been made. The existing allocation is evidence of the adjustment already made, not proof of a new order already completed.

The political record is less ambiguous. The Wall Street Journal reported that Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent publicly urged Japan to raise borrowing costs. After Japan intervened to support the yen, Bessent said Tokyo should consider borrowing dollars from the Federal Reserve rather than selling Treasury securities. The Bank of Japan’s decision followed a Federal Reserve rate increase as well as that campaign. Washington encouraged a policy change that could reduce foreign demand for U.S. securities. It does not get to treat the financing consequence as an unforeseeable event.

The consequence reaches beyond a yield chart. If Japanese demand for Treasurys weakens, the Treasury may have to offer a higher yield at auction to place the same debt. An auction “tail”—the gap between the yield the market expected and the higher yield investors ultimately require—raises the government’s borrowing cost. Mortgage rates are priced from Treasury yields plus a spread for prepayment, credit, and servicing risk, so the effect can reach households through refinancing and new home loans. State and local governments face the same arithmetic when they issue municipal debt for schools, water systems, transit, and hospitals.

Pension funds require a more careful account. Higher Treasury yields can improve the return available on new fixed-income investments, but they also reprice equities and other assets that pension portfolios hold. Public pension systems must then manage the effect on both asset values and projected liabilities. A Japanese allocation shift is not automatically a pension crisis. It is an additional source of volatility and a higher financing cost for borrowers who did not choose the Bank of Japan’s policy.

The numbers also need a historical baseline. The U.S. 10-year Treasury yield reached 5.01% when the article was published, but yields exceeded 5% for much of 2002 through 2007, before the financial crisis. That level is not unprecedented. The relevant fact is that the yield is high while the United States is also carrying a large debt stock and relying on continued foreign demand. Japan’s 10-year yield, around 3%, has not been seen since the mid-1990s. The spread between the two markets remains substantial, but it is no longer a permanent feature of the financial system.

Japan is also the largest foreign holder of U.S. Treasurys, with approximately $1.1 trillion at the end of July, down from $1.2 trillion in February. That decline cannot be attributed entirely to the new rate increase; much of it preceded the Bank of Japan’s latest decision. It does show that the position is not fixed. GPIF’s portfolio choices, insurer mandates, bank funding costs, and the yen’s direction will determine how much capital moves and how quickly.

The institutional verdict is narrow. Japan is not about to liquidate $2.5 trillion of American assets because the policy rate reached 1.25%. But the United States has treated Japanese demand as a durable financing condition while its Treasury secretary urged the policy change that could reduce that demand. The proper response is not a new forecast of catastrophe. It is to write U.S. fiscal policy on the assumption that foreign buyers are investors, not permanent creditors.
