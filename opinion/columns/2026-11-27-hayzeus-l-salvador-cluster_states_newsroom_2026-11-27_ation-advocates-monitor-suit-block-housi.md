Donald Trump is about to strip housing aid from mixed-status families. This is not a side effect. It is the design. The Department of Housing and Urban Development has finalized a rule, effective December 14, that removes mixed-status families from federal housing assistance. A mixed-status household contains people with different immigration statuses, including legal residents and U.S. citizens. Today, benefits are prorated. Under the new rule, proration gives way to exclusion. A family would lose assistance because the immigration statuses within its home differ. That is what the rule does.

Twenty-two states announced Monday that they would [sue to block the HUD rule](/articles/2026-11-23-states-sue-hud-over-housing-aid-rule-affecting-mixed-status-families/). Their case is direct: “HUD lacks authority to rewrite federal law to exclude families this Administration disfavors from federal affordable housing programs.” The courts will test that authority. Authority cannot make cruelty right.

Start with the arithmetic, because the arithmetic indicts the thing on its own. In Nevada, 9 percent of households are mixed-status—the highest proportion of any state, according to Pew Research Center. A 2024 Center on Budget and Policy Priorities analysis found that 150 mixed-status families receive housing assistance in Nevada. By recent estimates, more than 12 million U.S. citizens live with someone who is undocumented or holds temporary protection.

One hundred and fifty families.

For a family that cannot carry the full rent, losing the aid is losing the housing the aid was holding up. When the rule was proposed in February, HUD Secretary Scott Turner told affected households to prepare to “pack your bags.” A man whose office is housing looked at families with American citizens under their roofs and told them to pack their bags. The local housing agencies now working to comply with the rule know exactly what that means, because every family the rule touches comes through their offices, and they—not Washington—will have to look those parents in the eye.

The shortage is real. It was not built in a season, and it was not built by the 150 families. Noé Orosco, coordinator of the Nevada Immigrant Coalition, called the rule “a shameful attempt to scapegoat immigrants for the ongoing housing affordability crisis.” He is right. Without providing evidence, the Trump administration has repeatedly blamed the crisis on immigrants.

I must put my own community inside this accounting. We have often made it easy to welcome the stranger at worship while leaving housing, wages, and public policy arranged against that same family. We have wanted hospitality without proximity. That is complicity, not humility.

My hands are in it too. I have taken carpentry work renovating units that were then priced out of the reach of the families who cleaned them. The Church’s hands are in it when parishes sing welcome on Sunday and call the city on Monday about the rental house next door. The country’s hands are in it.

The machine Mr. Trump is running did not arrive with him. For a century, administrations of different parties have used removal, detention, and calculated fear; officials have often recorded a departure as “voluntary” when hunger left the person no real choice. Family separation is older than every official now in office. The machine’s age excuses no one. It convicts a country that keeps rebuilding it every few years and calling it new.

Secretary Turner, you may enforce the law. The courts will test whether this rule is one you may enforce. You may not decide that a family’s roof depends on which relative has which immigration status. That is not border enforcement. It is wicked. This rule uses housing assistance to punish a family for the immigration status within its home. You can stop this before December 14.

This country claims its faith in public. It is printed on the money the rent is paid with. Fine. Then the country must hear the texts it claims.

The Torah says, “You shall not wrong or oppress a resident alien, for you were aliens in the land of Egypt.” Leviticus says, “You shall love the alien as yourself.” Deuteronomy commands Israel to love the stranger because God loves the stranger and executes justice for the orphan and widow. And Jesus says, “I was a stranger and you did not welcome me.” Later in the same judgment: “Truly I tell you, just as you did it to one of the least of these who are members of my family, you did it to me.”

The texts carry no mixed-status exemption. A citizen under one of these roofs is both the citizen this government says it protects and a member of the family this rule strips. The tradition refuses to let a person be sorted out of their own household. The rule makes one relative’s immigration status decide whether everyone else keeps shelter. The Torah calls that an offense against the alien. Jesus calls it a failure to welcome Christ.

The gospel I was raised on takes away the right to sort the poor—deserving and undeserving are categories it does not recognize because every one of us stands in need. Dorothy Day wrote, “The Gospel takes away our right forever to discriminate between the deserving and the undeserving poor.” This rule is nothing but that sort, run through a federal agency: poor families sorted by a parent’s paperwork into who may keep housing help and who may not, the second pile handed a date. It is cruelty: chosen in a rulemaking, announced in a press release, scheduled for December 14. It is the sin the prophets kept naming—the powerful crushing the needy and calling the crushing order. The suffering is not the price of the policy. It is the product.

Nevada’s decision not to join the lawsuit did not mean indifference. Attorney General and governor-elect Aaron Ford is monitoring the case and supports the coalition’s effort to block the rule, spokesman John Sadler said. Nevada’s absence from the case rests on the structure of its housing programs, which it administers through regional organizations and nonprofits. [Local housing agencies are already working to comply with the rule while trying to keep families in their homes](/articles/2026-11-24-local-housing-agencies-work-to-comply-with-hud-rule-while-minimizing-evictions/). Delegating damage is not absolution. Whatever the structure of Nevada’s housing programs argues for staying out of the case, the man who leads its law department—and will soon govern the state—can put Nevada inside it. A government that backs the block from the hallway is watching its own residents from the hallway.

Catholic Social Teaching is not squeamish about borders. It holds that a nation may control them. It holds, in the same breath, that people have the right to migrate to support themselves and their families, that refugees and asylum seekers require protection, and that the human rights and dignity of undocumented migrants must be respected. The bishops of the United States and Mexico set those principles down together in *Strangers No Longer* in 2003. None of them has an expiration date.

A just housing policy would count every eligible member of the household honestly, prorate the assistance, keep children in their beds, and expand affordable housing rather than blame immigrants without evidence for the shortage. If a government wants fewer mixed-status households in subsidized housing, it has exactly one decent path: make legal status easier to reach. This government has chosen the other path.

On March 23, 1980, Archbishop Óscar Romero stood in the cathedral in San Salvador and spoke by radio to the soldiers carrying out their government’s repression: “En nombre de Dios, pues, y en nombre de este sufrido pueblo, cuyos lamentos suben hasta el cielo cada día más tumultuosos, les suplico, les ruego, les ordeno: ¡cese la represión!”—Stop the repression! No soldier is obliged to obey an order against the law of God. An immoral law, no one has to obey.

This rule is carried out at desks instead of checkpoints, but it is still an order, and it is still against the law of God. So to the caseworkers holding the files, the hearing officers, and the staff of the housing authorities: your conscience was not issued to you by HUD, and nobody can order you to surrender it. And to Secretary Turner: you are a person before you are a secretary. This rule makes a family’s housing the price of demonstrating which relatives the state values. That is the moral machinery Romero named. The door of return does not wait on the docket. You can stop.

Stop the rule. Suspend it before December 14. Protect the mixed-status families now facing loss, and make the officials who chose this cruelty answer for it. Romero left a command, not an acquittal.

The hundred and fifty families of this state are not waiting on the docket. December 14 is on the calendar, and their government has told them to pack their bags. I translate Romero’s command for this December: stop the evictions. The door of return remains open to every person with the power to stop, repair, or answer for this choice, and to every reader who accepted it. That is not acquittal. It is the beginning of repair. It has not closed, and no order can close it. Outside the door, a family waits with its bags. Do not make them carry the sentence.
