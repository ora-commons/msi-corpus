Jensen Huang admitted violating Nvidia’s rulebook; Nvidia’s ten-member board restored him anyway, without answering the sexual-misconduct allegations that drove him out. He had acknowledged breaching the company code, which warns against personal relationships that create conflicts of interest, after admitting an extramarital affair and amid reports that he had a second relationship with a subordinate. The board voted unanimously Monday after a pressure campaign from Huang’s tech allies and President Donald Trump, who said he [persuaded the board himself](/articles/2026-11-23-nvidia-reinstates-jensen-huang-after-trump-says-he-persuaded-the-board/). Its explanation was assurances from the U.S. government that Huang was not a national-security concern. Not one word about the conduct at issue. Governance experts [questioned the precedent](/articles/2026-11-24-nvidia-reinstates-jensen-huang-as-governance-experts-question-the-precedent/).

The president defended the restoration on CNBC in three words: “Sex sells, fellas.” He followed it with a doctored image of Huang raising a red bra in triumph, posed on the model of Trump’s own photograph after the 2024 assassination attempt in Butler, Pennsylvania. Trump, who has a history of marital infidelity and has faced sexual-assault allegations he says are false, predicted the scandal would help Huang’s reputation. The market delivered its own verdict: Nvidia’s shares rocketed 31.5 percent upon his return. They have fallen since. The precedent has not.

One day after returning, Huang apologized to employees in a livestreamed meeting reviewed by The New York Times, said he was in daily therapy, admitted he had sometimes publicly berated employees and threatened violence against them, and promised that human resources would collect feedback about his conduct. He said his return did not mean other employees could violate the code. Then the sentence: “Sometimes, it’s appropriate to forgive” and “sometimes it’s not.” The forgiveness went to the one man whose absence was showing up in the share price. The code remained for everyone else. That is not mercy. That is pricing.

Just over a year ago, the chief executive of an Ohio tech company was forced to resign after video caught him embracing an employee at a Coldplay concert. The chief executive of McDonald’s was among several pushed out over a consensual relationship at the height of #MeToo. That was the old sentence. Randall Peterson, a professor of organizational behavior at the London Business School, explained that leaders who become involved with subordinates were then in “serious trouble because you cannot rule out coercion. You just cannot.” Now, Peterson said, “you are at risk, as in the days before #MeToo.” The code held exactly as long as nobody irreplaceable tested it.

This is the class: the celebrity chief executive whose value to the company becomes its political armor. Once indispensability is tied to the stock price, the board has a financial interest in the return, Huang’s allies need their leader, and the president has an interest in the rescue. Each incentive points away from testing the allegation. Before the vote came a petition signed by thousands of current and former employees, allies arguing that Huang’s vision, connections, and deal-making were indispensable, and fears that Nvidia’s collapsing stock would pop the AI bubble. The workers got the code and the promise of feedback. That is not governance. That is permission with a stock ticker.

Ann Lipton, a University of Colorado Boulder law professor who specializes in corporate and securities law, said the saga had made very clear that Huang was “untouchable.” Cheyenne Hunt, the attorney who founded Reckoning Action after helping bring the sexual-assault allegations against powerful men into the light, said the message is that there is a class of men who see “violence against women, coercion of women as a currency of power.” It is “open season on their direct reports,” she said. Mark Zuckerberg told corporate culture it needed more “masculine energy” last year. The board has now delivered. For that service, the board minted him a title: the indispensable man. It fits. The code binds the people whose exit costs nothing. It bends for the one whose exit moves the chart.

Women make up just 21 percent of Nvidia’s global workforce and an even smaller share of its managers and leaders, according to the company’s latest sustainability report. Its code tells employees they “must report” suspected violations and promises protection against retaliation. The direct reports whose exit moves nothing get a complaint system. Huang’s own conduct will be handled by the human-resources department that works for him. Complaint forms travel upward, as complaint forms do.
