Alabama is trying to kill Jeffrey Lee by treating the Eighth Amendment as a loophole.

The governing right is not Alabama’s execution schedule. It is the Eighth Amendment, incorporated against the states through the Fourteenth Amendment. The Amendment forbids “cruel and unusual punishments.” Its original public meaning rejected punishments that were barbarous, degrading, or needlessly painful. Modern doctrine preserves that prohibition through a historical judgment about accepted punishment and a practical inquiry into whether the chosen method creates a substantial risk of severe pain when a feasible alternative exists. *Baze v. Rees*, 553 U.S. 35; *Glossip v. Gross*, 576 U.S. 863. The State must meet that constitutional yardstick for the method it actually uses.

The strongest case for Alabama is narrower than the State’s conduct suggests. The Constitution does not give a condemned prisoner an unlimited right to select the method of execution. The courts may reject a proposed method when the State demonstrates that its alternative is lawful, available, and not cruel. A federal injunction against one execution protocol does not automatically prohibit every other protocol. Judge Emily Marks therefore ruled that the injunction reached Alabama’s nitrogen method, not lethal injection. That is a recognizable procedural position.

It is not a constitutional finding that lethal injection is safe. It is only a ruling about the scope of an injunction.

The record described here supplies the missing audit. Alabama tried to use nitrogen gas after [the State’s nitrogen plan drew federal-court scrutiny](/articles/2026-06-09-federal-appeals-court-raises-concerns-over-alabama-nitrogen-gas-execution-method/). Two lower courts rejected the method, and the Supreme Court disallowed its use for Lee. The American Thoracic Society filed a brief stating that “nitrogen hypoxia executions cause intense, inhumane suffering.” Witnesses to Alabama’s prior nitrogen executions described inmates writhing and gasping for air longer than the State advertised. That evidence establishes a serious constitutional dispute about nitrogen. It does not establish that lethal injection is constitutional by subtraction.

Lee’s lawyers say he is not seeking to escape the sentence. They say he wants the method he chose carried out in a way that does not violate the constitutional ban on cruel and unusual punishment. He has also requested a firing squad, which Alabama does not permit. The State may reject those requests under lawful rules. It may not convert Lee’s method preference into a waiver of the Eighth Amendment. No prisoner’s consent can make a cruel punishment constitutional.

Alabama initially agreed not to use lethal injection, then revived that method after losing twice over nitrogen. The State’s lawyers call the switch a lawful fallback. That description identifies the procedural posture but avoids the substantive question: what evidence shows that the fallback method satisfies the Eighth Amendment? The source record gives no answer. It gives a new execution date set by Gov. Kay Ivey and a method the State once agreed to abandon. That is not a completed constitutional analysis. It is an attempt to reach an irreversible result before the method itself receives the scrutiny the Constitution requires.

The jury voted 7 to 5 to recommend life without parole for Lee after his conviction for murdering Jimmy Ellis and Elaine Thompson during a 1998 pawnshop robbery. The trial judge imposed death anyway. Alabama ended judicial override in 2017, but that reform did not reopen Lee’s sentence. The jury’s recommendation does not erase the murders or the sentence that followed. It does show why every layer of state power must be held to its own legal limit when the punishment cannot be undone.

The State may carry out a death sentence only through a method that survives the Eighth Amendment. Alabama has shown that nitrogen gas is contested as cruel and unusual. It has not shown that lethal injection becomes constitutional merely because a federal injunction did not reach it. The State is not entitled to outrun the Amendment by changing methods. It must satisfy it before killing Jeffrey Lee.
