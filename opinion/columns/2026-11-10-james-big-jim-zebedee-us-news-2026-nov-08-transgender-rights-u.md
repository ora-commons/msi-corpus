Trump is erasing transgender people from federal recognition, health care, and public life.

The damage lands at work, not on a campaign page. A service member can need gender-affirming care while federal policy narrows identity documents and medical coverage. A patient can arrive at a hospital and find the clinic paused. A school employee can be required to enforce a rule saying which child may play. At home, a family can be left to find care while the rules change underneath them.

On January 20, 2025, Trump signed the executive order “Defending Women from Gender Ideology Extremism and Restoring Biological Truth to the Federal Government.” It halted federal data collection on transgender, nonbinary, and intersex people, directed the termination of grants to projects acknowledging their existence, and redefined sex for the federal government as genetic makeup at the time of conception. It also ordered the State Department to stop issuing passports with an X marker or a sex marker different from the one recorded at birth.

These are not semantic adjustments. They are instructions for removing people from federal sight and from the documents proving who they are. At the State Department, that means a passport application can no longer carry the marker the holder has legally used or the marker matching the holder’s legal identity. For a service member, it means the federal government can demand service in uniform while narrowing the documents that identify that service member and the medical care available to them.

That reaches the armed forces. Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth has made an anti-trans position a priority in the Pentagon. The military’s policy history is longer than the second Trump administration. Obama lifted the ban on transgender service members, Trump’s first administration rescinded the protections, and Biden restored them. The second administration changed the speed, reaching defense rules, health funding, identity documents, data collection, education, sports, and litigation at once.

For a transgender service member, this is persecution with a uniform attached. A uniform does not cure a policy that turns identity documents and medical care into instruments of political retaliation. The Pentagon exists to defend the country, not to enlist the Defense Department in a domestic campaign of erasure. Its duty is plain at the shop-floor level: identify the people it sends into harm, provide lawful medical care, and keep a service member from being erased by the bureaucracy that is supposed to protect them.

Andrew Bacevich, in *The New American Militarism*, warns of a republic in which the professional military becomes an instrument of domestic political ambition and of what that does to civilian control. That warning lands here because the service member, not a foreign enemy, is being made to carry the domestic campaign. The service member’s obligation runs to the constitutional republic and the people in the next rank, not to a president’s campaign. A uniform is not a campaign banner.

The purpose is not only to prohibit particular conduct. It is to make prohibition itself politically safe and to build a political machine.

The direct beneficiaries are candidates who need a vote-ready issue, campaign strategists who need a method that works, donors who want their money tied to visible victories, and officeholders who can demand political loyalty. Hospital and school administrators get a short-term shield from federal funding risk. The people who carry the result are clinic workers, teachers, coaches, patients, families, and service members.

It is good machinery: a donor’s check, a strategist’s memo, a bill, an agency rule, a hospital budget line, a school policy. The political interest travels through paper, then lands on a work shift, an appointment book, and a service member’s medical file.

The method has a history. In 2016, when North Carolina wrote its “bathroom bill,” HB2, into law, Attorney General Loretta Lynch announced a federal lawsuit. Within a year, almost 200 corporate leaders had asked for repeal, the NCAA had pulled seven tournament games out of the state, and the law was gone. Shelby Chestnut, then executive director of the Transgender Law Center, called the repeal the first phase of the next round of legal protection. The law was wrong, the legal challenge was formidable, and the federal government chose to stand with the people it meant to protect.

Barbara Tuchman’s test in *The March of Folly* asks whether policy served the actor’s declared interest, whether an alternative was available, whether a group rather than one leader chose it, and whether contrary evidence ended the course. HB2 answers every question. Kansas and Idaho later passed bathroom restrictions of their own. In Idaho, using the wrong bathroom became a misdemeanor for a first offense, punishable by up to one year in prison, and a felony for a second, punishable by up to five years. The course did not end when the evidence arrived. It learned.

After the 2022 midterms, the American Principles Project ran a study of what had worked at the ballot box and issued campaign guidance. Groups such as the Alliance Defending Freedom funded and organized around the method. The strategy has a sequence: target selection, test cases, organizational funding, ballot-copying. By 2024, 26 states had enacted bans on transgender students participating in school sports, and 27 had restricted gender-affirming care for minors.

The method has now reached research, prisons, and medicine. Within a month of the first-day executive order, separate directives cut federal funding from institutions providing gender-affirming care to minors and directed Medicare and Medicaid not to cover that care. The Justice Department [funded research into gender dysphoria and violent crime](/articles/2026-11-03-justice-department-funds-research-on-violence-and-gender-dysphoria/). A proposed federal prison policy [would bar transition-related treatment](/articles/2026-10-21/federal-prison-policy-would-bar-transition-related-treatment/). The Department of Education [finalized the repeal of the Title IX protections for LGBTQ students](/articles/2026-09-28-education-department-finalizes-repeal-of-title-ix-lgbtq-protections/).

More than 40 hospitals have closed or paused gender clinics for minors, including in states that did not approve such bans, often because administrators saw the funding risk and moved before the courts finished. At the clinic, the appointment list loses a service. At the hospital, the funding letter supplies the reason. At the school, an employee applies an eligibility rule to a child. At home, the family begins the search for care again.

That does not pencil out as public safety. It pencils out as political power built on other people’s erasure.

In *Little v. Hecox*, the Supreme Court upheld Tennessee’s sports restriction on the merits under intermediate scrutiny, the less demanding constitutional test it applies to sex classifications rather than the strict scrutiny it applies to race. In the companion *United States v. Skrmetti* case, it did not approve the medical-care ban on the merits; it reversed the lower-court judgment and remanded the case for proceedings under the correct standard of review. Those dispositions did not end the campaign. They made it easier for hospitals, schools, athletic organizations, and federal agencies to treat the restrictions as operational while litigation continued. The government need not win every case to make institutions behave that way. The threat is enough.

The campaign has also changed the political ground. Pew Research found that support for laws and policies protecting transgender people from discrimination fell eight points from 2022 to 2025. The operatives watched the reaction, identified the issue as useful, and helped turn a set of state restrictions into a national campaign.

In 2026, ballot bans passed in Washington, Colorado, Arizona, and Nebraska. Washington’s measure requires medical verification of sex for every girl athlete in the state. Not every state has moved in lockstep, and not every official has taken the same position. This is not the country divided between two equal positions. It is the campaign arriving with enough force to reach places once thought secure. California Governor Gavin Newsom, Iowa senator-elect Josh Turek, South Carolina senator-elect Annie Andrews, and Texas senator-elect James Talarico have each said they are against allowing transgender children to play in girls’ sports, while Senator-elect Abdul El-Sayed in Michigan and Senator-elect Troy Jackson in Maine maintained support for transgender rights.

Bait does not need to win a majority. It needs frightened candidates, compliant institutions, and enough voters who have been made to regard persecution as routine.

That is where Christians must be especially exact. Some evangelical political leaders sincerely believe that sex is binary and immutable. Public argument is one thing. Government coercion is another. A sincerely held conviction does not become a warrant for closing clinics, narrowing identity documents, and making one class of citizens easier to remove. A faith that calls every neighbor an image of God cannot be conscripted into the erasure of that neighbor.

I have no quarrel with a sincere Christian conviction that sex is binary and immutable. Conviction is not proof, and belief does not become public policy merely by becoming common. The trouble begins when government power is built to make one theology the only legal description of a person. The Bible I know stands on the side of the powerless, not with the power that wants them rendered politically convenient.

Congress should enact anti-discrimination protections that apply regardless of what an executive order says about sex, and agencies should enforce Title VII in employment, Title IX in federally funded education, and Section 1557 in federally funded health care, according to the statutes’ text and the Constitution. The State Department should restore X markers and allow a passport sex marker consistent with the holder’s legal identity; the Defense Department should protect service members’ access to medically necessary care; medical decisions should remain with patients and licensed clinicians; and the government may set service and medical standards only through evidence, law, and the powers the Constitution assigns to it. Courts must decide equal protection and statutory rights on the law, not on which people an executive order prefers not to count, because the government cannot make recognition itself a partisan punishment.

Trump has put transgender troops on the block. His paperwork erases them from data, grants, and documents. It cannot erase the people.
