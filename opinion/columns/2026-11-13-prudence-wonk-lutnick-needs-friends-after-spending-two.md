Howard Lutnick turned export licensing into a tribute system.

The receipts arrived within twelve hours of each other. Jensen Huang was ousted as Nvidia's CEO, and less than twelve hours later the top Democrats on two congressional panels sent the Commerce Secretary a letter opening an investigation into the administration's export plans as they relate to Nvidia — a multibillion-dollar stretch of national security and trade policy and, on the investigators' account, an opening for wrongdoing and corruption. The Wall Street Journal's morning politics brief identified the letter's subject by his money rather than his office: an investigation into "Trump's top fundraiser." Both descriptions are accurate. They describe the same desk.

Here is the number the letter is chasing: fifteen percent. On a conventional basis, an export license is adjudicated, not sold — the fee is fixed, not floated. The Bureau of Industry and Security — the Commerce office that decides which American technology may leave the country — reviews license applications one by one under the Export Administration Regulations, the rulebook for that traffic, against the item, the end user, and the end use. The lawful instrument for that review is a processing fee. A percentage of the licensee's revenue is not a fee, and the announcement that created the fifteen percent cited no statute authorizing the department to take one. In August 2025 the administration announced, and Nvidia confirmed, the arrangement that did exactly that: licenses to resume China sales of the H20 — the China-legal processor Nvidia built to fit under the 2023 controls — in exchange for fifteen percent of the resulting revenue, paid to the federal government. A regulator prices rules. A tribute system prices access. The letter is chasing the price. The investigation is chasing the desk that set it.

The pricing posture does not live in the Bureau's review cubicles. It lives in the Secretary's office, and the Secretary is the man who raises the president's money. Anyone who has read an export-license application knows the form asks about the item, the end user, and the end use. It does not ask what the applicant is worth to the official holding the pen. Mr. Lutnick's colleagues have registered the discomfort on the record: he and Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent have tangled publicly, and his brash bravado has unsettled a number of other top officials. Money beside the licensing pen is the oldest corruption in the regulatory repertoire, and it recurs across both parties' decades. Structural corruption of this kind does not require a bribe. It requires only that the pen and the ledger share a desk. The appointment placed them on the same one.

The sharpest question now on the record is a date. Lawmakers want to know when Mr. Lutnick learned of the contact between Mr. Huang and a suspected Chinese asset, [a question put to him earlier this week](/articles/2026-11-13-lawmakers-ask-when-lutnick-learned-of-huang-s-contact-with-suspected-chinese-ass/), because the answer establishes whether the department setting the terms of the China licenses knew, at the time it set them, that the chief executive of the licensed company had been in contact with a suspected Chinese asset. The timing is not decoration. Terms set before the knowledge and terms set after it are different documents.

The White House has answered the question it cannot: advisers have spent the week telling colleagues the Nvidia fallout belongs to Mr. Lutnick, [the blame assignment landing on the record yesterday](/articles/2026-11-12-white-house-advisers-blame-commerce-secretary-lutnick-for-nvidia-fallout/). Whether he keeps the office is a political question. Whether the office was converted into a collection agency is a documentary one, and the letter proceeds on the second question no matter how the first resolves.

Nothing in the mechanism is new, and nothing in it belongs to one party. Export licensing runs through the Commerce Department under an authority that predates this administration. The last public figure to answer the corruption question honestly ran a savings-and-loan. Charles Keating, asked whether his political contributions had bought influence: "I want to say in the most forceful way I can, I hope so." The senators who pressed his regulators on his behalf were four Democrats and a Republican. The fifteen percent is this administration's design, and the design is what the letter investigates.

The scoring apparatus I served for thirty-five years never carried this category, and that is its own finding. The government publishes tax-expenditure tables every year — about 165 provisions, roughly $1.8 trillion — because a tax expenditure is a spending program run through the tax code: revenue forgone through a special exclusion, exemption, deduction, credit, preferential rate, or deferral. No table in the federal system prices the value of an export license. The most valuable regulatory privileges Commerce issues appear in no budget projection, no outlay table, no distributional appendix, so no score ever told Congress what a license is worth, or what it costs when the official who prices it is the official who raises the president's money. The blind spot is the camouflage. The distributional tables I built counted tax expenditures line by line and never counted a license. The remedy follows the finding and does not wait for the investigation: strike the revenue conditionality outright, because a national-security control that generates income for its issuer has stopped being a control; put the licenses back on case-by-case adjudication with every condition written, every fee fixed in regulation, and every delegation disclosed; and separate the desks in writing before the next application arrives — an official raises a president's money or prices the nation's export privileges, not both.

The letter now sits on the same desk as the licensing pen and the fundraising ledger. The file has one name on it. Mr. Keating's answer still stands as the most honest reply anyone has given to the question the letter asks. The fifteen percent, the resumption, the dates — all of it is already in the record; the investigation need only read it. The license is the license.
