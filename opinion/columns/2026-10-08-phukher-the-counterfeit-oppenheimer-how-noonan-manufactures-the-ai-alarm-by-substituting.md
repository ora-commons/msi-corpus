> Some compare the development of AI to the development of the first atomic bomb by the Manhattan Project. But the single most crucial fact of both undertakings makes them polar opposites. The U.S. government initiated and oversaw the entire enterprise that unleashed the atom and made the bomb. After its use, the federal government controlled nuclear technology in the U.S. and attempted to stop or slow its nonpeaceful use in the world. The government understood the exact nature of the science and technology in which it was involved. Not so in the development of equally world-changing AI. The quest for artificial intelligence was privately initiated, funded, carried out. There was no oversight from a government representing the people and responsible for their physical safety. No high levels of the government have demonstrated full or timely comprehension of what the scientists, engineers and machines are up to even now in the frontier labs.
>
> *— Noonan, paragraphs 1–3*

**The counterfeit analogy.** The analogical move that anchors the entire column: the Manhattan Project as a model of successful state oversight. The frame relabels the actual record on both ends. On the nuclear end, the column's version of the record is rhetorically tidy and historically thin. The U.S. government initiated the project, used the bomb on two Japanese cities in August 1945 — on the order of 200,000 people killed by year's end, with later deaths from radiation bringing the total higher — and lost control of the technology within four years: the Soviet bomb in 1949, the British in 1952, the U.S. hydrogen bomb by 1952, fifty years of Cold War, and a perpetual problem of proliferation to states and then to non-state actors. The line about the government "understanding the exact nature of the science" is tidy. The actual record in dual-use technology governance is the Atomic Energy Act of 1946, the export-control regimes that lasted roughly a generation, the test-ban treaties of the 1960s, and containment under continuous failure pressure, not successful control.

On the AI end, the column's version of the record is contradicted by the documented record two clicks from the page's readers. Biden's October 2023 executive order on AI — EO 14110, "Safe, Secure, and Trustworthy Development and Use of Artificial Intelligence," signed October 30, 2023 — required frontier-model developers to share safety-test results with the federal government, established the AI Safety Institute at NIST, and directed agencies to study labor-market effects. The EU AI Act, in force since August 1, 2024, with phased implementation running through 2027. The Blumenthal-Hawley bipartisan Senate framework of September 2023, the proposed federal AI agency legislation, the frontier-model evaluations the AI Safety Institute had been conducting. None of it appears in the column. The column arrives at "the federal government should oversee AI" by routing through a 1945–47 nuclear-governance model that doesn't transfer to a multi-firm, multi-jurisdiction frontier-engineering ecology, while suppressing the documented AI-governance record the reader can verify in a single search.

The analogy is being deployed to license a particular AI policy conclusion. > Those opposed to federal regulation of AI point out that early on, Manhattan Project scientists worried that the first atomic test might ignite the earth's atmosphere. It didn't. **The strawman and hasty generalization in concert.** The column's load-bearing empirical claim is asserted without a citation: "not some, not a majority, but all of AI's inventors and leaders admit it may bring about physical disaster." The intensifier stack — *not some, not a majority, but all* — converts what is in fact a documented and heterogeneous research community into a single rhetorical unit. The AI Impacts 2023 survey of machine-learning researchers — the column is gesturing at this work and others like it — found meaningful minorities expressing concern about catastrophic outcomes, with the survey's own qualifications about self-selection, framing, and the surveyed population sitting in the methodology footnotes the column doesn't reach for. The column lifts the alarm version, drops the qualifications, treats the lift as the categorical fact, and uses the categorical fact to authorize the analogy's prescriptive payload.

The actual public record is messier than the intensifier stack allows. Altman has publicly said AI could cause catastrophic harm and has also said "we'll all get better stuff" — both quotations appear in Noonan's own text. Yann LeCun has publicly criticized the doomer framing as a distraction from real AI risks. Andrew Ng has publicly questioned the extinction framing and argued it has distorted AI research toward a hypothetical far-future threat and away from present-day concrete harms like bias, labor displacement, and misapplication. Many working researchers in the field reject the disaster framing. The "all" is rhetoric, not record.

This is the broadcast-news-segment construction at the column-form. The pattern is consistent: find a real survey; restate the most alarming version as a categorical unanimity; deploy the restatement to authorize the next paragraph's policy recommendation; never reference the survey's qualifications unless they support the column's thesis. The intensifier stack is the operator, not the finding.

The Oppenheimer/Frankenstein binary that follows in the column — the two literary images, not policy positions — collapses a real disagreement into two archetypes and assigns the dramatic one to the target. This is strawman in the catalog sense: the column engages the maximalist version of the position it opposes rather than the actual range of positions the developers and their critics occupy.
