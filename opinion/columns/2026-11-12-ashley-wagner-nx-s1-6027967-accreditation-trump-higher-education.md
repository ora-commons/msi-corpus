Trump is stripping student protection to police college speech.

He said so himself, in a 2023 campaign video: "Our secret weapon will be the college accreditation system." In the same video he promised to "fire the radical-left accreditors that have allowed our colleges to become dominated by Marxists, maniacs and lunatics." His Education Department finalized the rules that deliver the weapon at the end of October, and the news arrived wearing one of the dullest mechanisms in the federal government — which is precisely why almost nobody flinched.

Accreditation is the dullest word in higher education, and it is the switch that turns on the money. The federal government does not write checks to whoever asks; it routes grants and loans through colleges a third-party accreditor has vouched for — a pipeline of roughly $1.6 trillion owed by more than 40 million borrowers. Colleges pay dues to private accreditors who check the curriculum, the faculty, the finances, whether the place is real. Congress built the independence into the design on purpose: Antoinette Flores at the Council for Higher Education Accreditation says lawmakers "wanted agencies to set standards independent of the federal government." A gatekeeper independent of the man who holds the keys to the treasury is not bureaucratic decoration. It is the entire point.

Two things happen in this rule, and only one of them is about quality.

First, the political test. The department now requires accreditors to evaluate school policies on "intellectual diversity," "ensuring that students are exposed to a range of viewpoints." Its own statement to NPR says the regulations do not "give the Department any authority to police speech." The rule requires the viewpoint audit anyway — performed by accreditors the department oversees, with a school's federal aid as the forfeit. Bob Shireman, an accreditation expert and former top official in the Obama Education Department, states it exactly: the rule gives the department "the authority to meddle in the internal affairs of colleges and universities in ways that they never have been able to in the past," opening "an avenue for the federal government to go ... through accreditors to colleges, and tell them what balance of points of view the federal government thinks they should have." The department answered through Ellen Keast, communications director for the under secretary: the regulations "do not mandate any specific policies," and they give the Department no "authority to police speech." Read that beside the campaign video, where the same man called the accreditation system his secret weapon. The stated purpose is on tape.

Beth Akers, an economist at the American Enterprise Institute who supports most of this overhaul, balks at exactly that piece — it puts the administration in "a seat of judgment over the institutions, over the accreditors, about whether or not they are appropriately teaching ideology," and "that is something I'm uncomfortable with." When the market-friendly economist at AEI is the one who is uncomfortable, the discomfort is data.

Second, the guardrail goes to bid. Under the new rules, schools can shop for an accreditor and switch mid-stream, and it gets easier for states and colleges to start new ones — the department's own words are that "the rule promotes greater competition and innovation." Competition sounds great until you ask who the customer is. An accreditor that starts finding problems loses the school to a competitor; Shireman calls it "whack-a-mole." His fire-extinguisher line deserves framing: "One person's red tape is another person's consumer protection: Checking to see if the fire extinguishers have expired is red tape." A college that picks its own inspector, and can fire him for finding something, is not being inspected. It is being flattered. An inspector who must win the building's business will find the building very agreeable. That is not oversight. That is courtship.

Here is what a loose gate costs when it fails. The last time an accreditor looked the other way, the schools were Corinthian Colleges and ITT Tech, certified by the same accreditor, ACICS. Corinthian collapsed in 2015; the government eventually discharged $5.8 billion of its students' loans. ITT went down in 2016, stranding roughly 35,000 students mid-degree; another $3.8 billion erased. That is $9.6 billion in federal borrowing — borrowed against the futures of the families who attended — spent on credentials an accreditor was paid to check, and the accreditor itself was revoked, restored, and revoked again. When the next Corinthian folds, the bill does not go to the accreditor or the college. It goes to the students, whose loans do not vanish, and to the taxpayers who eat the discharge.

Jonathan Kozol wrote of public schools that "the state, by requiring attendance but refusing to require equity, effectively requires inequality." The higher-ed version is arriving on schedule: aid requires accreditation, and the definition of accreditation is now negotiated school by school, with the students holding the difference.

Then the family math. My father spent thirty-eight years at the Postal Service and retired in 2019 from a federal government that has since cut the Education Department's workforce by roughly half. The department was created in 1979 with fourteen Republican co-sponsors in the Senate; my parents' country built it and wrote the accreditor independence into law. The Pell Grant covered about 80 percent of the cost of attending a four-year public university in my parents' generation; Sara Goldrick-Rab's accounting puts it at 25 to 30 percent now. I got through that paperwork because my parents could read it. A first-generation borrower does it alone, and the people deciding what "accredited" means will never meet her. Pew has been measuring the feeling since before this cohort could vote: about four in ten millennials and Gen Z adults say it is harder to feel financially secure than their parents were at the same age, and the line keeps going the same direction.

My college-educated parents could read the financial-aid fine print themselves, and David's grandmother's estate is why we hold a rowhouse instead of a lease — head starts this argument runs on. For a family without them, the accreditor was the fine print: one institutional somebody reading the contract before they signed the next thirty years of their income away.

My kids are one and four, and Eva fills out her first federal aid form in about a dozen years, when this rule will already be a decade old — and because it moved through ordinary rulemaking, it will be hard to undo. Tonight at eleven I will open the spreadsheet, because that is what I do, and run the columns again: the mortgage at 7 percent, daycare at $2,400, and a row for the year Eva starts college that has no number in it yet. I can leave it blank. What I will not do is pretend the number that eventually fills it will be set by a classroom, or by her, or by the math. It will be set by whoever holds the gate.

One piece of this rule is genuinely good and had support on both sides of the aisle: accreditors will have to weigh outcomes — what graduates earn, licensure pass rates, graduation, retention. A college should have to show what the degree buys. But if outcomes were the weapon, that provision alone would have been the weapon. The "secret weapon" language, the ideological-diversity mandate and the easy-switch provisions are the cargo; the rest is packaging. If competition genuinely raises quality, the people who wrote this rule can show me the gate that got stronger. What the record documents so far is a pile of schools that got out from under one — and a White House holding a lever over what every campus in the country is permitted to teach. Annie Lowrey's argument applies without adjustment: safety-net failure is not accident but deliberate design, the product of decisions by named actors at named moments rather than the working-out of natural economic forces.

We have already watched this operation run on the information supply, where [chatbots began citing political ads dressed up as local news](/articles/2026-10-30-chatgpt-cites-political-ad-sites-disguised-as-local-news/); accreditation is the same move one layer up, aimed at the machinery that validates the degree. Meanwhile the protective instinct is alive wherever children are the ones inside the risk: California has [banned addictive social-media features for kids under 16](/articles/2026-09-10-newsom-signs-law-banning-addictive-social-media-features-for-users-under-16/) and New York has [restricted chatbot conduct with children under 18](/articles/2026-10-28-new-york-restricts-chatbot-conduct-with-children-under-18/). Statehouses will still put a guardrail where a child stands. The gate on $1.6 trillion is being loosened in the same season.

What protection actually looks like is unglamorous and already exists in pieces: standards published in public and enforced whether or not the school likes them, weighted toward outcomes a student can feel — graduation, licensure pass rates, what graduates earn — and a Pell grant that still covers most of the bill. Diane Ravitch has the line for this moment: "I will not allow the term reform to be hijacked by those who have a hidden agenda."

The list I grew up with at St. Stanislaus — feed the hungry, visit the sick, shelter the homeless — was never put out to bid. It was a list of jobs, not a market, and nobody applied for the work. Some things are duties, and the moment you make them bids, the duties go to whoever needs the work least.

Swift gave the title to what this country hands a kid at eighteen and again at thirty: you're on your own, kid. Picture a fire inspector filling out an application, describing how thoroughly he would check the extinguishers, hoping the building picks him. That is the accreditation system they just built — and the students are the ones standing in the smoke.
