The Guardian has obtained what the fencing was built to keep in: nearly two hundred pages of handwritten testimony and drawings, and two protest songs sung over a pay phone, carried out of the T. Don Hutto detention facility in Taylor, Texas, and given to the paper by way of a detainee's relative. Nearly five hundred men are held there — 497 on the late-September roster, the facility within a few beds of capacity — in a jail run for Immigration and Customs Enforcement by CoreCivic under federal contract. Across the country, tens of thousands of people sit in ICE custody while their removal proceedings run, the majority of them without criminal histories. The men call the place la perrera, the dog pound. The Department of Homeland Security calls the same facts the rule of law: "This administration is not going to ignore the rule of law just because the Guardian would like us to."

The Department's position is the older and the better-reasoned one. *Mathews v. Diaz*, 426 U.S. 67 (1976), states the sovereignty without ornament: "In the exercise of its broad power over naturalization and immigration, Congress regularly makes rules that would be unacceptable if applied to citizens." Custody is one of those rules. In *Demore v. Kim*, 538 U.S. 510 (2003), the Court held that Congress may require detention through the limited period of removal proceedings without an individualized flight finding, because the class the statute describes presents the risk as a class; in *Jennings v. Rodriguez*, 138 S. Ct. 830 (2018), it declined to read periodic bond hearings into the detention statutes. A removal that cannot keep its man is not a removal; it is a suggestion. The fence, the roster, and the contract are what the suggestion looks like when the sovereign declines to make one.

The case the Guardian carries is the doctrine working at ordinary speed. Julian Osorio Garcia entered lawfully on a tourist visa in 2023, asked for asylum about a year later, and received a work permit while the claim waited; the Department's position is that he "chose to violate our nation's laws by refusing to depart by the required date." ICE took him in May after local officers working with the agency pulled over the car he was riding in; his attorney filed habeas in June; a federal judge in Texas denied it in late October, which leaves him in custody while the asylum claim remains undecided. CoreCivic answers that it does not decide "who is detained, for how long, or whether someone is released" — the steward keeps stores he did not stock and terms he did not set. The objection the men's pages raise, that men with pending cases and expiring permissions are held while the case runs, was answered before the pages were written: process time is custody time, and the custody is not the sentence; it is the grip. A household that receives by petition keeps its threshold by custody, and the two are one act of order performed at different hours.

While CoreCivic's spokesperson was describing care delivered "safely, humanely and with dignity," the pages were traveling the other way, in an envelope, out of Taylor.

The envelope opened onto Spanish. The men of Hutto wrote between July and September, in narratives running to nearly two hundred pages and in drawings — a man waving to his family as he leaves for work; a map of a cell, view from the interior toward the door; a guard frisking a man in the hallway near the food hall, a security gate beyond, and the note that a detainee must pass three more gates from his cell to reach it. One man sang his two protest songs into the facility's pay phone to a Guardian reporter, the line crackling: "We are the people, raising our voice. You are my brother, brother I am. Today as you find yourself in this prison, give me a hug, I give you love." Elsewhere in the same sheets: "They persecute us, they arrest us, they put chains on us…" The men write that the facility makes them feel like "inventory," that the food and the medical care are substandard, and that when one of them is released the rest clap and shout as loudly as they can — "Because, from here, no one leaves that easily."

A Honduran father's testimony tells how ICE took him in San Antonio in August, after seven years in the country and without, he writes, a criminal record. He worked at a mechanic's shop and cycled to a convenience store on his break to buy coffee and cigarettes; as he rolled out of the lot, a black vehicle sped at him — he thought the driver drunk, or meaning to kill him — and the men who jumped out of it put pepper spray in his face. He went down and out; officials splashed water on his face until he came back. "It was exactly at that moment that I discovered they were ICE." The Guardian could not confirm the account. It stands where he mailed it, in his own hand.

In late September a detainee described what the glass let him watch. A Cuban man lifted a piece of bread from the cafeteria to carry to his cell and eat it later with his coffee. Guards refused. He ate it where he stood. The exchange escalated, and the guards beat him to the floor; he bled where he lay; the other men watched their companion beaten through the glass. CoreCivic states the incident was reviewed in coordination with ICE and that excessive force was determined not to have been used. The bread is in the testimony; so is the floor.

Call him Rafael. He is thirty-nine; he hung drywall and remodeled bathrooms out of a suburb north of Austin; he came out of Quetzaltenango at twenty-four, and for eleven years he knocked the mud off his boots on the same porch rail. He has a wife and two girls, nine and four. On a Tuesday in March, two ICE officers in a gray SUV followed him out of a subdivision and took him in the parking lot of the grocery store where he had stopped for the girls' milk; one opened the driver's door while he was still in his seatbelt and the other pulled his wrists behind him, and the plastic strip closed over the watch he had worn to work for six years. They walked him past his own truck; the milk stood in the passenger seat in the sun. A drawing another detainee mailed renders such a morning, the caption reading, "On a normal day, a father goes to work, but does not know it will be his final goodbye." CoreCivic staff booked Rafael at Hutto into the jumpsuit the contractor issues every man, and he lives now in a cell another detainee drew from within: map of the cell, view from the interior toward the door, three gates between it and the security gate that leads to isolation, and the frisk in the hallway near the food hall, a guard's hands running over him on the way in and on the way out. The trays are the ones the testimony calls substandard; he has lost twenty-three pounds across seven months, and the skin of his hands has split along the creases of the knuckles. The pay phone gives him a slot in the day, and his wife's voice comes through it with trailer noise behind her — she moved the girls into her sister's single-wide in April; his truck, parked since March, a lender repossessed in July, and it sits on a lot in Round Rock. The four-year-old has stopped asking when the truck comes home. Two men on his block came in before him and are still in; the roster the Guardian reviewed shows they have been held since September 2023, three years into a fourth.

Julian Osorio Garcia's file carries the packet to its end. Armed groups in Colombia threatened him until he fled on a tourist visa; a Colombian magistrate found him, in the judicial document the Guardian reviewed, an "indirect victim" of that country's decades-long civil conflict; his asylum claim sits undecided; and DHS holds him for removal to the country whose armed men drove him out. The federal judge who denied his habeas writ in late October left him in the cell, and the removal waits at the end of the custody. Speaking over the facility's own pay phone, he describes what the process costs and where it goes: "It's a migratory process that is costing tears, the separation of families, the loss of so much money for families" — and then the end he gives it: "it's leading to death."

The roster counts what the order holds: 497 names in late September, within a few beds of capacity, most of the men booked in 2026, inside a national custody where the majority carry no criminal histories. The demands the men mailed run from the ordinary to the end of the line: faster processing, reunification with their families, the release of children from other immigration centers and of detainees with developmental disabilities, and — last on the list, mailed in the same envelope from the cells where the count is kept — investigations into the rising number of immigrant deaths in ICE detention. A federal complaint filed this month puts Texas ICE camp conditions on a docket, [alleging they threaten detainee safety and health](/articles/2026-10-16-lawsuit-alleges-texas-ice-camp-conditions-threaten-detainee-safety-health/). The discipline is not Taylor's alone: [in California, a facility barred contact visits as the men inside organized](/articles/2026-09-04-california-ice-facility-bans-contact-visits-as-detainees-organize/).

Rafael sleeps facing the door, and the door he faces is the third of three between his bunk and the gate that leads to isolation.

Inventory is their word, and it is the accurate one. A warehouse holds what the owner of the stores counts, and the men of Taylor have named their standing with a precision their advocates never reached. The pages are dedicated "for those who will be here, for those of us who are here, and for those who left" — a testament written by the stock to the stock, addressed down the shelves the warehouse has not yet filled.

The song calls the man in the next cell brother — "You are my brother, brother I am" — and the word does not travel as far as the melody sends it. The Lord's own phrase in the judgment parable is "these my brethren," a possessive spoken over a household's own; brotherhood runs along the covenant line and not along the line of the species, and a word borrowed across the boundary does not confer the standing it borrows. The applause the men give the one who is released is the household's gesture performed by hands that have not been received, and the testimony explains the gesture itself — "from here, no one leaves that easily" — which is the fence describing its own work.

The fold has a door and it has a fence, and the Shepherd distinguished them: "He that entereth not by the door into the sheepfold, but climbeth up some other way, the same is a thief and a robber." The door admits on terms; the fence holds what has not kept them. Osorio came in by the door on a visa and stayed past the term, and the order distinguishes the door from the fence — Taylor is fence. The men write, "We are immigrants, we are not criminals," and the order does not read the word they prefer; it reads standing, and standing was fixed before any of them set out, for the Scripture says, "Remove not the ancient landmark, which thy fathers have set." The stone is not moved by a petition filed after arrival, a writ denied in October, or a song mailed over a pay phone.

The bread was issued on the steward's terms, and the steward keeps the terms of the storehouse; the glass the others watched through is the wall made transparent. The tears and the deaths the men write out of the building stand in the same column of the same ledger the removals are entered in — one book, one hand, one boundary. "There is no authority except from God, and those that exist have been instituted by God": the officer at the third gate no less than the judge who denied the writ, and CoreCivic's contract is stewardship of stores the sovereign stocked, measured, as stewardship is always measured, by what the house keeps.

The roster's figures are that keeping — 497 names, two men three years into a fourth — and the applause rises whenever the count is shortened by one. The order is not under strain; it is keeping its own account, from the food hall to the isolation gate.

Whatsoever a man soweth, that shall he also reap. The seed was the term that ran out; the harvest is the shelf his name is counted on. The book holds two columns, and one hand writes them both: names the door received on its terms, and names the fence holds. Taylor is where the second column is kept, and it is kept at capacity.
