Brazil's Senate voted 75 to 1 on Wednesday to cut the legal workweek from 44 hours to 40 and abolish the six-day schedule that keeps sixteen million people on a single rest day. One senator said no: Hamilton Mourão, Jair Bolsonaro's former vice president. Everyone else voted yes — including Flávio Bolsonaro, who is about to face Lula in the October 25 runoff, and who had spent months calling this exact proposal a recipe for "mass unemployment." It was a policy win, a political convenience for the incumbent, and, more interestingly than either, a working model of how you actually shorten a workweek in a country where the employers' associations have been predicting ruin since the ruin never showed up.

Let me concede the obvious first, because it's real. Lula timed this. He championed the amendment to cut into Flávio's first-round lead; his support has bled in exactly the retail, restaurant, and hotel sectors this covers; and [the runoff math is genuinely tight after the Bolsonaro family's first-round sweep](/articles/2026-10-04-fl-vio-bolsonaro-leads-lula-in-brazil-first-round-runoff-set-for-oct-25/). Nothing takes effect before October 25 — six days become illegal sixty days after promulgation, then the week drops to 42 and finally to 40 fourteen months later. The votes get counted before the first worker feels it. All of that is true, and none of it is the argument.

The argument is against the case that killed hours legislation for a century: that if you make the week shorter, jobs vanish. This is one of the most thoroughly tested predictions in the history of economics, and it has lost every single time. When Henry Ford moved his plants to a five-day, forty-hour week in 1926 — over the objections of manufacturers who swore it would break them — he did it because the plants ran better, and the rest of American industry followed within a decade. When the 1938 Fair Labor Standards Act made forty hours the legal ceiling, the ruin chorus sang on cue, and the following decade produced the largest expansion of working-class income in the country's history. France legislated thirty-five hours in 1998 and 2000; the French economy did not shed a third of its workforce. Iceland ran large-scale shorten-the-week trials in Reykjavík from 2015 to 2019, covering thousands of public-sector workers, and found that productivity held or improved while the workers came out ahead. Britain ran sixty-one company trials across 2022 and 2023; ninety-two percent of them kept the shorter week afterward. Spain's government put up public money in 2023 for firms willing to test three-day weekends. None of these are socialist thought experiments. They are post-reform employment records, and every one of them contradicts the prediction that made them controversial.

Here's the mechanism the "mass unemployment" chorus leaves out, and it's the only part of this that matters: hours are a lever you pull *before* you fire somebody. Germany figured this out and named it — Kurzarbeit, short-time work, where firms cut hours across the payroll instead of laying off a slice of it. Through 2008 and 2009, and again through the pandemic, German unemployment didn't do what unemployment does in a downturn, because the shock was absorbed in hours first. A week of forty hours instead of forty-four, at the same monthly pay, is that same adjustment written into law: the work gets shared, and the headcount stays. The people who keep insisting this arithmetic doesn't close are the same people who have been insisting it since the eight-hour day, and the eight-hour day is on the calendar.

What the six-day schedule actually costs is on the record too, and it isn't measured in GDP. Marilane Oliveira Teixeira, a researcher at the University of Campinas who has spent months studying the 6x1 week, documented what one rest day does to a body: illness, workplace accidents, exhaustion — "especially for women, who still carry the burden of unpaid domestic work." A saleswoman in Salvador, Lilian Cristo dos Santos, put it plainer: no time for the doctor, no time to study, and the one free day consumed by cleaning and family. That's the ledger the employers' associations never print. A workforce that can't schedule a medical appointment is not a productive asset being protected from ruin. It's a cost being pushed onto the workers, their families, and the health system that picks up the pieces — and the four-day rest is simply the bill arriving.

So when Flávio Bolsonaro told business leaders, per Folha, that he'd review the amendment if elected, and then told reporters he "supports something even better" — a proposal to pay by hours actually worked — you can hear the carveout click shut. Same week. Less money. A forty-hour week that arrives with the monthly pay quietly reattached to the clock is not a better reform; it's the reform minus the raise. And as for the lone dissenter: somebody has to be the last person in the room betting against a 70-percent supermajority, and Mourão drew the short straw. He was the only senator in Brazil willing to be wrong on the record, which is a job someone always has to take.

Now the part that matters after the confetti: what this reform *is*, structurally, and why it's worth copying. It is a statutory floor. Not a perk negotiated shop by shop, not a benefit an employer can dangle and withdraw, not an option that exists in the industries with the best margins — a floor in the constitution, under 30 million people, that no firm may dig beneath. That design choice is the entire difference between working time as a *right* and working time as a *productivity variable*. When hours are a variable, the market "solves" overwork by giving the people with the fewest options the most of it: retail, restaurants, hotels, the same low-wage sectors everywhere. When hours are a floor, the floor applies to the corner bookstore and the telecom alike, and no firm gains a competitive edge by making its workers sicker than the next firm's. That's not anti-market. It's the rules of the game being set before the game starts, so the competition runs on who sells the best thing, not on who can extract the most hours.

And the coalition that passed it is the second model. The 70-percent support crossed class and crossed party: a majority of Flávio Bolsonaro's own voters wanted it, which is why he folded in a day. That's the durable kind of redistribution politics, and it's durable for a boring structural reason — universal programs are the ones people defend. A middle-class shop assistant with her Sundays back and a lower-middle-class one with hers back are receiving the same thing from the same law, and neither will quietly let it be repealed, because there's no seam to peel it at. It's the same design logic behind the public library and Social Security: everybody in, everybody covered, everybody a stakeholder in keeping it. The means-tested program becomes a program for the poor. The constitutional floor becomes a fact of life.

Which leaves the honest comparison: America hasn't moved its own statutory floor since 1938. Forty hours has been the legal maximum for nearly ninety years — through a transformation of retail, logistics, and service work that has no precedent — and the number hasn't budged, because here hours are treated as a bargaining item rather than a floor, and you can see the result by looking at the sector this amendment covers. The Brazilian reform is a portable template precisely because it doesn't require the machinery we lack — no sectoral bargaining, no century of union density. It requires a legislature willing to write a floor and defend it, which is the one thing a legislature is for. Brazil's wasn't willing until a pharmacy assistant with a hashtag and a 70-percent coalition made it unavoidable. That's the sequence worth remembering: build the cross-class majority first, write the floor second, and let the employers' associations make their ruin forecast one more time. They'll get it wrong again, and by the time the second rest day is on the calendar, nobody will remember what the argument was.
