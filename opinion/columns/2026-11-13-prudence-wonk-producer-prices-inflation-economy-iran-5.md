Corporations chose these prices; the Fed is billing families for someone else's war. Here are the numbers. The Labor Department's producer price index — prices businesses charge one another before anything reaches a shelf — rose 0.3 percent from September to October and 6.5 percent from a year earlier, the largest annual increase since November 2022. A year before, wholesale inflation ran 3.1 percent.

The monthly increase cooled from 1.4 percent in September to 0.3 percent, largely because energy prices levelled off. Strip food and energy out and core wholesale prices are still 5 percent above a year earlier, unchanged from September. Anyone who has read a producer price index release knows what the core line is for. It is the part of the index a fuel shock cannot explain.

The fuel story has a test in the same table. Wholesale diesel fell 7.9 percent from September to October. Truck freight prices rose 3.9 percent in that month, and food prices rose 1.3 percent; food, like most goods, is mostly shipped by truck. The report attributes the freight increase to more expensive diesel since the Iran war working its way through the system. It also records the fuel bill falling 7.9 percent in the month the freight price went up. Both lines are printed in the same October table. A war's diesel bill does not disappear when spot fuel eases; it arrives downstream, in the freight line and then the grocery line, on a lag the household pays and did not set.

The second engine is demand, not fuel. Electronic component prices rose 4.2 percent in the month and industrial chemical prices 3.1 percent — the demand signature of the AI buildout, a construction program bidding for components and feedstocks everyone else also buys, the same broadening already visible [when computer prices began lifting the index in October](/articles/2026-10-14-inflation-broadens-beyond-energy-as-ai-buildout-lifts-computer-prices/). Data-center buyers bid up inputs the rest of the economy shares, and the price-setters pass the bill through. The buildout gets the components and the chemicals; the rest of the economy gets the higher index, and some of that index feeds the Fed's preferred gauge. Two engines. One was chosen in a war. One was chosen in capital-budget meetings. Neither was chosen at a kitchen table.

The response is on the record. Tuesday's [consumer price index report showed October prices rising four-tenths of a percent](/articles/2026-11-10-u-s-consumer-prices-rise-four-tenths-of-a-percent-in-october/), and many Federal Reserve officials said in response that they will almost certainly need to raise their key short-term rate further, perhaps more than once — the posture carried in [officials weighing faster hikes as inflation broadens](/articles/2026-11-11-federal-reserve-officials-weigh-faster-hikes-as-inflation-broadens/). That body is the Federal Open Market Committee — the committee that sets the short-term interest rate — and its instrument, the federal funds rate, is the overnight rate at which banks lend to one another. It anchors what a household pays on a mortgage, an auto loan, a credit-card balance. When the committee lifts it, those costs rise with it. The instrument aims at demand. Its invoice is filed under household credit. The price increase is decided where prices are set; the bill for it arrives as interest.

The Federal Reserve's yardstick is 2 percent, and Friday's release leaves it well out of reach. The committee measures that target against the personal consumption expenditures index — what households actually spend — and against its core version, which strips out food and energy. That core gauge stood at 3.1 percent over the year as of November 13, 2026. The consumer price index is not the yardstick.

The number itself is a convention. Paul Volcker, who knew what disinflation costs, spent his last years warning against the false precision of numerical targets. He defined price stability as "the state in which expected changes in the general price level do not effectively alter business or household decisions," and nobody has demonstrated that the state arrives at 2.0 rather than 2.8. The caution is in his memoir, *Keeping at It*.

Economists at Morgan Stanley estimate that Friday's producer prices put the core gauge at 0.3 percent for October and 3.2 percent over the year, up from 3.1 percent. The gauge is moving away from the target it is supposed to reach, and the instrument for closing the distance is the household's borrowing rate. The tool that reaches the person who set the price is competition and enforcement policy aimed at the price-setters. The tool the Fed has reaches the borrower. One addresses where the number came from; the other only decides who pays it.

The rate is a broad instrument and the causes are narrow. No overnight-rate move reverses a war's fuel cost. The freight and food lines are that cost arriving on schedule, and the committee cannot set a rate that makes diesel cheaper. The buildout's demand is concentrated — the largest balance sheets in the market, competing for the same components and chemicals — and the funds rate reaches it only by making credit more expensive everywhere and waiting to see what gives. The release already names who collects the increase: carriers, food sellers, the suppliers of components and chemicals. What the adjustment prices is on the record as well — mortgage, auto, and credit-card credit, the borrowing that cannot pass its costs through. I spent years building distributional tables, and their honest limitation was always the same: they showed who pays. They never showed who chose.

The language attending each increase in this cycle has been standardized: necessary medicine, painful but responsible, shared sacrifice. Name the operation underneath. The war was a choice. The buildout was a choice. The adjustment — the mortgage rate, the freight surcharge, the groceries financed on a card — is routed to households the way freight is routed: to a single consignee. Nothing about it is shared. "Shared sacrifice" is the label on a routing decision. Symmetry requires the same sentence for the other ledger: spending deficit-financed for causes chosen elsewhere — wars, bailouts, the rest — files its incidence to the same households, and the fiscal-responsibility chorus that greets it is the same routing under a different name. The instrument's invoice does not check the sponsor.

The committee has run this experiment before, and its own paper trail documents the results: rapid escalation against an inflation that began as supply, an early reverse, governors dissenting from their own committee's decisions. Then, on August 22, 2025, the committee rewrote its own Statement on Longer-Run Goals and Monetary Policy Strategy, retiring the 2020 framework that Ben Bernanke, a former chairman of the institution, had endorsed in print. The record shows the framework is a convention, chosen and revised by choice. It is not weather.

The honest alternative is not standing down. Inflation is the binding constraint, and a committee that looked away would fail the same test from the other direction. The affirmative work is matching the account to the invoice: state in the minutes which portion of this inflation the funds rate can reach and which it cannot, publish the incidence of the chosen path the way the Labor Department publishes prices, and put the war's checkout cost in the budget tables where a war's cost belongs.

October's table reads plainly: diesel fell 7.9 percent in the month truck freight rose 3.9 percent. Corporations set those numbers. A family is charged twice — once at the checkout, once on the loan statement.

The October release carries the war in its freight line and the buildout in its component line. The minutes carry the votes. The household carries the rest. There is no other way to read the record. The bill is the bill. The address on it is a choice.
