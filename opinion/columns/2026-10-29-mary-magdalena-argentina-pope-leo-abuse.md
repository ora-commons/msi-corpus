Pope Leo XIV lands in Argentina facing a list, not a welcome. He [plans several meetings with clergy abuse survivors during the trip](/articles/2026-10-27-leo-xiv-plans-several-meetings-with-clergy-abuse-survivors-in-latin-america/), and back in March he [highlighted abuses of "vulnerable" adults and sat down with an Opus Dei critic](/articles/2026-03-15-pope-leo-highlights-abuses-of-vulnerable-adults-meets-opus-dei-critic/) — gestures the survivors' networks have politely noted. Some groups are boycotting the visit outright. Others want answers in person. Both have stopped accepting speeches.

Start with the numbers, because the numbers are the whole argument. Forty-five years for Horacio Corbacho. Forty-two for Nicola Corradi. Twenty for Kumiko Kosaka, for sexually abusing five students at the Antonio Próvolo Institute for deaf children in Mendoza, where the abuse ran from 2005 to 2016. Fifteen for Julio Grassi, who ran homes for orphaned children. Four and a half for Gustavo Zanchetta, for abusing two seminarians, granted parole in 2025. Life for Christian Von Wernich, the police chaplain who served a dictatorship's murders, kidnappings and torture — seven murders, dozens of kidnappings and torture cases, the years 1976 to 1983. Argentine criminal courts did that work, some of it a decade ago.

And the church? Three of them — Von Wernich, Grassi, Zanchetta — still hold clerical status outright. Corbacho's canonical file has been open since 2017 with no announced outcome. A Vatican-appointed judicial vicar recommended that both Próvolo priests be dismissed. Nothing has been announced. Corradi died under house arrest. Kosaka was a nun. Not one public defrocking. Not one.

That is not an oversight, an accumulation, or a lag. It is a method. The Vatican's stated posture is that canonical trials wait until criminal appeals end, so as not to prejudge — a concern for due process that arrives, unearned, only after the sentences are in. Corbacho's canonical proceeding began in 2017 and no outcome has been announced. Zanchetta's was authorized in 2019 and no ruling has been announced. Survivors say the Diocese of Morón's report on Grassi reached the Vatican; no decision has been announced. Von Wernich's bishops announced an investigation in 2007, the year he drew life; no sanction has been announced. Asked directly about the clerical status of convicted clergy, the Vatican did not respond to the Associated Press. The Argentine dioceses holding the files did not respond either. Silence is not the absence of a statement here. Silence is the statement.

Meanwhile the machinery that does run, runs for the accused. In 2010, then-Cardinal Jorge Mario Bergoglio commissioned a four-volume, 2,000-plus-page study of Grassi's case — a study that concluded the man was innocent, that his victims were lying, and that the case should never have gone to trial. When allegations reached Zanchetta, Francis rejected them and created him a Vatican job that got him out of Argentina. What changed between rejection and prosecution, the Vatican has never explained. That is a paper trail of decisions: thousands of pages and a plane ticket for the cleric, a shrug for the children.

Then there is Córdoba, where the pope is scheduled to visit the same cathedral where Alejandro Nicola — accused of sexually abusing children at a Catholic school's kindergarten, mostly last year, with more than a dozen families complaining — was in July, vestments on, celebrating. Asked about it, Nicola told journalists that "the lawyers will take care of that." The archbishop's answer was presumption of innocence, which is a legitimate legal principle and is also becoming a management technique: the Vatican's own guidance says bishops pull accused priests from active ministry while investigations run, and Rossi chose to wait instead. Florencia Navarro, whose daughters are among the alleged victims, answered simply: "I would like him to provide explanations, since they talk so much about protecting the vulnerable." Fernanda Agüero asked for concrete actions rather than words. She will not be the first to wait.

The Opus Dei file shows what "we will look into it" costs. Forty-three women say they were trafficked and forced into unpaid domestic labor, 12 to 16 hours a day under isolation and strict control, recruited as teenagers on false promises, from the 1970s to 2015. Three former South American vicars, a former regional secretary and Mariano Fazio, the organization's second-ranking official, are formally accused; their lawyer, Sebastián Sal, wants the global leader, Fernando Ocáriz, charged as well. Prosecutors want the accused questioned. The judge has not ordered it. Their formal complaint reached the Vatican in 2021. Going on five years, no response. Opus Dei's Argentine authorities deny everything, categorically. "We were just little girls when we entered," says Andrea Martínez, 52. "They will never give us back the dignity they stole from us." Rosana Vaccon never got her mother's letters about her dying father. These women are asking Leo for a meeting and an apology from Opus Dei's leaders — an ask so modest it reads as a confession of what they expect instead.

Leo, your mouth has gone dry. There is a copper taste under your tongue as you approach that cathedral, and the wine will not wash it out. You will bless the stone with a hand that will not quite close. You will sleep badly afterward — the pressure behind the eyes at four in the morning, the ache that belongs to a file you have not opened since 2017. Picture your own nephew unable to call out in that institute in Mendoza, and then tell me a Vatican that has not answered since 2021 is exercising due process. The judge's pen moved in Mendoza. The canonical file in Buenos Aires did not. While the court clerk was typing the sentence, a bishop was defending his priest with the presumption of innocence. Ángel, that collar you refused to question sits on a throat that will not swallow easily the next time those mothers look at you. You are not dangerous, Leo. You are small — a man in white holding a folder he will not open, in a country where the courts were brave and the church was not.

There is a version of this trip that means something. Defrocking is the church's most severe punishment, and the survivor networks pressing these cases are asking Leo for exactly that, on cases his own courts and investigators have already nailed down. He can meet them, hear them, and send them home with another promise. Or he can end the half-open files — Corbacho's, Grassi's, Zanchetta's, Von Wernich's — and publish the rulings. There is nothing left to prejudge. A court already decided. "Zero tolerance" is only ever tested by what you actually remove. Until collar and title come off men a judge has already convicted, the phrase is marketing, the visit is a photograph, and the policy — the real, operating, documented policy — is to never let a case end.
