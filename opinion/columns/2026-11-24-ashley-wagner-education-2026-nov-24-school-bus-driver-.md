School districts and bus contractors exploit school bus drivers to keep transit costs low.

America’s schools are cheating the workers who carry twenty-six million children a day, and the wage schedule says how. The Economic Policy Institute’s 2026 analysis found that the school-bus driver count fell 5 percent in one year—a loss of nearly 10,200 workers—and 15 percent, or almost 34,000, since 2019. It was the largest single-year decline since 2022 for the country’s largest mass-transit program.

Run the actual ledger.

Dawn Freedenburg has driven for sixteen years in Chicago. Her district guarantees five hours a day across 174 school days—870 paid hours a year—while her work can begin at 5:45 in the morning and stretch until 5 p.m. New drivers need a commercial license, extensive training, and background checks; the process can require weeks of unpaid work. Most drivers do not average 40 paid hours during the school year, and they receive no pay during school breaks. Few contractors or districts provide health insurance or other benefits.

Five hours for 174 days is 870 hours. At the 2026 median hourly wage of $22.49, that is $19,566 in gross pay before taxes, benefits, or the field trips that may occasionally stretch a route. The guaranteed part of this job does not cover the cheapest apartment in the part of Connecticut where Peter Sandler drives.

Sandler retired in 2016 and returned behind the wheel in 2020. He says adding summer work and charter runs makes it difficult to get past $40,000 annually. Even treating that $40,000 as gross, it is $3,333 a month. At $1,600 to $1,700 for the cheapest one-bedroom apartment in the area, that leaves about $1,633 to $1,733 before tax. In better areas, where rent exceeds $2,000, it leaves less than $1,333. Food, health insurance, training, and the unpaid months between school years disappear from that calculation because they are assumed to be free.

The median for every other bus driver was $23.89 in 2026. School-bus drivers make up 39.4 percent of the bus-driving workforce, and theirs is the segment whose inflation-adjusted pay fell—by 0.7 percent from September 2025 to September 2026—while the rest of the field held steady. The job with the children in it is the job being repriced downward.

Those facts are true. They do not explain why real wages are falling while the responsibilities are not. This is not a labor shortage. It is a compensation shortage converted into a staffing shortage.

Anne Helen Petersen’s structural correction to burnout in *Can’t Even* matters here: people do not stop caring about children because they have become less generous or less capable of hard work. Driver attrition is evidence that the job has been designed to require workers to finance the shortage themselves. No productivity app can pay for a commercial license or July.

Districts are under real pressure. Higher diesel costs amid the war with Iran and Russia’s war on Ukraine added an estimated $1.1 billion to school-district expenses from November 2025 through October 2026 compared with the previous year. A budget shortfall does not make unpaid labor fair. It forces a choice: retain trained workers at a wage they can live on, or preserve the budget by making attrition perform labor for free. Districts and contractors chose the cheaper labor.

A 2025 AP-NORC Center survey found that 80 percent of school districts had been affected by driver shortages, with 73 percent identifying budget shortfalls as a cause. The response has been route cuts and consolidation. Sandler’s former contractor packed buses to capacity, making the driver’s work harder and leaving more children to manage. Drivers are often caught between administrators and parents, with student behavior worsening since the Covid-19 pandemic.

Follow the cost down, because it does not disappear; it moves.

It moves into fuller buses, longer routes, lost preparation time, and the unpaid hours between the guaranteed shift and the school bell. It moves into a parent’s morning when the bus route is rewritten. A parent clocking into a hospital shift does not get the same flexibility as someone whose desk job can absorb a late start. This is the same retrenchment now producing [school closures at their highest count since the pandemic](/articles/2026-11-09-u-s-school-closures-reach-highest-count-since-the-pandemic/).

At my table, the shape of it is familiar. Our two incomes produce $8,800 a month after taxes. Childcare is $2,400, consuming 27 percent before housing. We have a 7 percent mortgage made possible in part by David’s grandmother’s estate, two cars, and a desk job that forgives a late start. We can absorb a missed bus. Our margin for an extra school run is zero.

The privilege matters because it lets us mistake a household adaptation for a functioning system. Our fixed bill stays visible; the driver’s missing year disappears from the transportation budget. This belongs in the same ledger as [public school budgets tightening as enrollment falls and teacher counts rise](/articles/2026-11-18-public-school-budgets-tighten-as-enrollment-falls-and-teacher-counts-rise/): fewer resources reaching the classroom, then fewer drivers moving children to it.

But the shortfall has authors further up.

Underneath the fuel bill sits a fifty-year federal default. In 1975, Congress authorized the federal government to cover 40 percent of the annual cost of educating children with disabilities. The federal share has never reached 40 percent. In recent fiscal years it has remained near 13 percent, leaving an annual shortfall of roughly $24 billion—more than the entire Title I appropriation. The difference comes out of every other local school-budget line, and transportation is the flexible one.

The two oldest Title I funding streams, the Basic and Concentration Grant formulas, have remained frozen at fiscal-year 2001 levels since No Child Left Behind. The base has never moved; incremental money has flowed through the newer Targeted and EFIG formulas. In Pennsylvania, a court found the state school-funding system unconstitutional in February 2023. The IDEA statute was signed by Ford, and no president since has supplied the promised share. In March 2025, the Department of Education cut nearly half its workforce while an executive order directed the department’s closure. The money question was answered upstream, before any driver clocked in.

The bus is where the country’s care policy becomes a wage-and-time transfer. Read as a diagnosis of American infrastructure rather than a sentimental description of one song, the title of Taylor Swift’s “You’re On Your Own, Kid” becomes the operating doctrine of care: make the household absorb what the public system refuses to fund. The child is a public responsibility; the driver becomes the residual funder. When the route is cut, the safety net is lateral—the mothers in the group text dividing the driving between them. We manage because we have two cars and a forgiving desk. A parent leaving for a hospital shift does not.

My Lansdale parish formation understood the difference between a shared obligation and a motivational speech. The corporal works of mercy named concrete burdens communities had to shoulder: feeding, clothing, sheltering, visiting. They were not personal failures that required better discipline. The contemporary bus budget turns a public obligation into a worker’s sacrifice and then calls the sacrifice service.

Leo XIII stated the principle in 1891: “Wages ought not to be insufficient to support a frugal and well-behaved wage-earner.” A paycheck that cannot cover the cheapest apartment in the driver’s own region fails a test the Church stated 135 years ago.

The experiment has already been run, and the result is in.

Antonia Namnath drives for Oregon’s Beaverton school district. Starting wages run from $26 to $33 an hour, with annual increases through the Oregon School Employees Association contract, Chapter 48. She receives health, vision, and dental benefits. By her account, the driver shortage is not an issue there.

One district is not a national counterfactual. It is a comparator, and it indicts the claim that the shortage is weather. Same country, same commercial license, same children; different contract. Where the agreement exists, the shortage does not. That is what “hard to hire” actually means: the wage is below the work.

Melissa Fay France, who drives outside Kalamazoo, has no medical insurance. Low pay and inadequate benefits make leaving attractive. She knows what the job requires—the safe transport of children, the management of their behavior, the maintenance of her license—and she puts the failure where it belongs: the schools are not living up to their end.

What paying them looks like is not complicated. Treat the commercial license and training weeks as paid onboarding. Guarantee the hours or pay an annual professional wage instead of an hourly scrap sized to avoid benefits. Fund health coverage, paid leave, collective bargaining, and enough drivers to keep children off overcrowded buses. Fund IDEA at the promised 40 percent; the IDEA Full Funding Act, reintroduced in 2025 with more than thirty Senate and sixty House cosponsors, has not advanced.

The model is not theoretical. Beaverton is the receipt. Public systems should pay for training, paid hours, benefits, and bargaining, then hire enough drivers to keep children safe. The driver gets another shift without a paycheck. The budget calls that efficiency. That is what exploitation looks like when the public calls it transportation.

When Eva’s year comes, I will stand on a Fishtown corner with her backpack on my shoulder. Either the bus comes or it does not, and the difference between those two mornings will have been decided in a budget meeting by people who never had to wait at the curb. Pay the drivers. The route exists for the children. The check clears, or the children wait.
