Donald Trump abandons Israel and taxes rural families through war.

At my one-man shop in Adams County, I do not buy Brent crude. I buy gasoline for my 2014 Chevy Silverado and diesel for the shop tractor, and Brent is one price in the chain that ends at my pump. A war shock does not stop at the Gulf. It moves through fuel dealers and freight, then into seed, feed, hardware, and the cost of rebuilding a Kohler engine. A three-percent move in Brent is not an abstraction. It is diesel before I drive to the co-op for parts. It is gasoline for the Honda EU2200i when the power goes out. It is propane for the house and electricity for the freezer.

When diesel rises, I can defer a part order, raise a repair bill, or drive one fewer truck. None of those is a policy. All of them are a tax. One afternoon on the market does not rewrite a monthly bill. It still sets the direction under every bill.

On Fox and Friends on Tuesday, Trump said he had made a deal with Benjamin Netanyahu: the United States would stop paying Israel $4 billion a year in military funding by the end of 2026, though Washington would help if Israel “needs it.” *The Guardian* reported that Trump and Netanyahu had made the deal months earlier and that Israel no longer needed the money. The US-Israeli war against Iran began on February 28.

A promise of help after the money ends is not an annual military guarantee. The guarantee ends while the shooting continues. Trump’s shifting war policy holds rural fuel budgets hostage, and the market treated each of his sentences as an oil input.

Before the war began, Brent traded around $72. It has not been back there since. On Monday, Trump reportedly said he was willing to give diplomacy more time, and Brent fell 11% to $103.28. The US reportedly launched limited strikes on Iran’s Qeshm Island late Monday. Early Tuesday, Brent traded 1.7% higher at $106.55. By the morning print, it had climbed $2.38 a barrel to $107.15, a 2.2% gain.

Then Trump announced the military-aid cutoff, and Brent reversed, sliding as much as 3%. By the final reading, it stood at $104.08, down 0.6%. At $104.08, Brent was roughly 45% higher than its pre-war level of around $72. British front-month gas dropped 6.1%. The European benchmark fell 6.8% to €88.41 per megawatt hour.

A market can move on a supply report. It can move on an attack on an oil terminal or a shipping lane. This market was moving on presidential sentences.

This was not the first time oil took Trump’s timing as a signal. On October 9, Brent and WTI fell after [Trump said the United States was not planning Iran strikes before the midterms](/articles/2026-10-09-brent-wti-fall-as-trump-says-u-s-is-not-planning-iran-strikes-before-midterms/). On October 19, Brent fell another 1.8% after [Trump waived Russia sanctions](/articles/2026-10-19-brent-falls-1-8-as-trump-waives-russia-sanctions/). Sanctions waivers, election deadlines, peace-talk signals, aid cutoffs. The market prices the sentence before it prices the barrel.

Taken together, these are not three unrelated market notes. They show the operating rule. The oil market does not need a doctrine. It needs Trump’s next sentence. It prices the orders, postponements, threats, and reversals he has made. One man’s position becomes a global risk premium, and the risk premium lands on every diesel invoice, power bill, and farm loan made on the strength of it.

Trump has turned an ally and a military-aid package into another oil-market instrument. The market called it for him. A statement that Israel no longer needs $4 billion lowers crude. A strike on Qeshm raises crude. A sentence about diplomacy lowers it again. Foreign policy has become a set of dials connected to the pump.

That is what it means to have energy security outsourced to one man’s judgment. Oil’s old geography still governs the new one: production and sea lanes. The map in 2026 runs through the Strait of Hormuz. But the more immediate map runs from Qeshm to Brent to a diesel invoice on County Z.

The announcement lowered the benchmark. That does not rescue the policy. It proved the point more cleanly.

Meanwhile, the world’s biggest oil companies were on track to collect about $100 billion in combined profits during July, August, and September. Aramco said its profits rose by more than a third in the latest quarter despite attacks on its facilities. The war had reduced its output, but higher oil prices offset the loss. The arithmetic is ugly. A business can lose production in a war and still gain revenue because the war raises the price. The oil companies did not have to win a single fight. They could profit from the uncertainty surrounding the fights.

The rest of us do not receive a dividend check for that arrangement. A farmer pays more for diesel. A cooperative pays more when it buys electricity or moves grain. A small shop pays more when every delivery crosses the country in a truck burning fuel somebody else set the price for. If we sell a barrel or an acre, we may get a better price for the trouble. In Adams County, the ordinary year leaves us with the bill.

Australia’s central bank showed the next step. The Reserve Bank of Australia raised its cash rate by 25 basis points to 4.85%, its fifth increase this year and the highest rate in 18 years. Ipek Ozkardeskaya, a senior analyst at Swissquote, told the live blog that higher energy prices strengthened the case for tighter monetary policy from the Fed and other major central banks.

The RBA forecast unemployment would rise above 5% in the next couple of years. It forecast headline inflation at 4.5% in the fourth quarter before easing to the target range of 2% to 3% in the second half of next year. A surprisingly narrow 6–3 vote indicated policymakers may have been approaching the end of their rate hikes.

An energy shock had become an inflation shock. Inflation then became a borrowing-price shock. The distance between Hormuz and a one-man shop is shorter than a spreadsheet. A higher rate reaches the business through used-equipment loans, working-capital lines, and the price of financing a pickup that has been repaired too many times. It does not arrive as a Treasury notice. It arrives as a shop that cannot buy the new compressor and a farm that cannot add the freezer.

Steel, cement, nitrogen fertilizer, and plastics run on fossil energy. That is why this is not merely a barrel-market curiosity. A dairy barn runs on the whole chain. Electricity refrigerates the milk. Natural gas helps make the nitrogen in the feed. Diesel moves the feed, fertilizer, and fuel. The war does not arrive at the farm as a geopolitical opinion. It arrives as three bills from three directions.

And the Binance heist is not a sidebar; it is the same pattern arriving as software. Binance said hackers used a bogus job interview to compromise a computer and an internal artificial-intelligence tool, gain control of the company’s wallets, and steal $6.3 billion from customer accounts—in what could be the largest-ever financial heist.

The path began in June, when a wallet-team employee downloaded a tampered version of a coding tool from an advertisement. In September, a second software engineer downloaded a malicious code repository after interviewing for what he thought was a legitimate job, including a virtual meeting with the CEO of a fictitious company. The FBI blamed a North Korean hacking group, a claim North Korea rejected. Binance said hourly deposits had exceeded withdrawals, suggesting flows were stabilizing, and it would repay all customers who lost funds.

At a one-person shop, the software tool downloaded to do the job and the interview used to get it are links in the same chain of concentrated power. The operator does not choose the code repository, the model, the payment rail, or the terms on which the system compensates the loss. The code repository did not stop at the exchange. The pressure to absorb risk runs the other way, down to the smallest operator.

Wendell Berry’s *The Unsettling of America* offers the relevant warning: a system can solve one thing at a time while quietly wrecking the pattern around it. Foreign policy is tuned for the next exchange. Markets react. Profits gather at the top of the oil chain. Uncertainty and higher prices descend on households and small businesses. Every participant can point to narrow success: a lower gas price for one afternoon, an aid deal on television, a resilient oil company, a central bank claiming control of inflation. Nobody owns the whole pattern. The county does.

The worker-self-exploitation contradiction named in *We Too*, Chapter 16, is plain here. The one-person shop is told that self-reliance is the answer to an economy that cannot carry ordinary operators. So I absorb the customer delay, the parts bill, the freight charge, and the fuel bill because there is no institution standing between my bench and the next shock. Independence is useful when you own the machine and the risk is yours. It is a fraud when the operator owns the machinery of production but somebody else controls the price of its fuel.

A serious rural energy policy would put a floor under farm and home budgets when a war shock arrives, using targeted relief rather than a blanket subsidy. It would put more generation and grid ownership in member-owned cooperatives and public utilities, move heat, farm work, and rural transport onto electricity, and use storage, efficiency, public purchasing, and emergency capacity so a fuel-price spike does not become household debt or lost farm income. None of that requires pretending the next presidential sentence is wisdom. It requires building systems that do not leave a rural family at the mercy of one.

An economy that counts money while pretending land and people are free eventually has to count them. That is what the oil market did this week. It counted the risk. Wisconsin received the invoice.

The fuel receipt from the co-op is still clipped above my bench. It carries Hormuz, the aid deal, the rate hike, Aramco’s profit, and the price my children will meet every time they drive.

My children will inherit this county one way or another. I want it to have power that stays on, shops that can borrow at sane rates, and families that can heat a house without bargaining against a war market. That is the country we made: the people who set the risk can trade it, while the county keeps the receipt. We can make it differently by putting the shock where the power is before it lands where the income is. The pump handle is in the same place every morning. It does not know whose foreign policy moved the barrel. It only asks what the barrel costs. A president can change his sentence. A county should not have to live with the whole sentence.
