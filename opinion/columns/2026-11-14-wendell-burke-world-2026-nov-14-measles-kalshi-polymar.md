You can put money down today on whether American children will catch the measles this year, and the movement that spent a generation promising it would defend the family has not said a word about it — while the exchanges that carry the wager sell the whole thing to you as transparency. Melody Schreiber reports in [The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/nov/14/measles-kalshi-polymarket-betting) that Kalshi opened its health markets over the summer — [a plan this publication tracked in August](/articles/2026-08-04-kalshi-plans-to-let-users-bet-on-fda-decisions-clinical-trial-results/) — with wagers on when the FDA will approve a skin-cancer vaccine, whether the CDC will change its vaccine recommendations, how many measles cases the country will see in 2026, whether the United States will lose its measles-elimination status this year, and how drug trials and approvals will resolve. The book has since grown to include the human beings who decide those things: contracts on when officials will leave the health agencies, and on whether states end their vaccine mandates, with no apparent law against the advocate who bets on the mandate's end and then writes the law that brings it about. Polymarket has followed. Beside a Brooklyn elementary school, on a construction fence, someone has pasted a sign that says VACCINES in blue stars and stripes; beneath it, in smaller letters, sits the question the sign was really carrying, with the logo of a prediction market beside it: Will any state end vaccine markets? The fever chart was one of the last commons left in American life. Strangers now hold positions against it.

Give the defense its strongest form, because it deserves it. Knowledge is dispersed — I believed that on the trading floor and I believe it now; no ministry knows what a thousand farmers know about a crop. Kalshi's spokesperson, Dani Lever, gives the honest version: "the people who best know whether a trial will succeed are scattered, and their insight rarely reaches the public," and public markets, she says, avoid those barriers. Let them, then. And Matthew Eisenberg at Johns Hopkins concedes the limit of the thing: these health markets are small, prices that thin can be moved by a single trader, and a few-hundred-dollar contract on the childhood vaccine schedule "is not a forecast of anything" and should not be read as public opinion. Granted on both counts. A price does aggregate what no committee can.

It also changes what the knowledge is for. A futures market has one load-bearing property, and it is not the mathematics: the thing being priced cannot be touched from the desk. I used to trade paper claims on corn that was still in the ground, grown by people exactly like the neighbors I had left behind, and the machine worked because no man in that building could make it rain on Illinois or keep the rain off — the crop's indifference to our book was the entire integrity of the instrument. These contracts stand that property on its head. They pay on outcomes with hands on them: a reviewer's draft recommendation, an investigator's endpoint, a staffer's bill. Eisenberg is precise about the mechanism — the risk "isn't necessarily that a reviewer takes a bribe — it's that the market now rewards leaks." In Chicago we had a name for trading that moved what it traded on: manipulation. The exchanges policed it, because a price its own traders can move is not a price at all; it is a payout schedule. And when the knowledge concerns your own child, a price gathers nothing; it purchases silence, or it purchases behavior — trial participants changing what they do, drug companies moving when the trial runs. What is being sold here is not information. It is a position in somebody else's sick child.

Lia Nower at Rutgers is blunt about what the thing actually is: functionally identical to regulated gambling, run through a loophole that calls it a commodity so it can sit under federal futures law with the Commodity Futures Trading Commission for a watchdog, outside every state system written to limit this. "Gambling right now is like the wild, wild west." Several states have sued, alleging the platforms function as gambling sites, and [the states have been pushing to shut the door](/articles/2026-09-08-states-push-to-ban-prediction-markets-as-election-betting-expands/) since the election-betting fights. The federal government has no gambling regulator to speak of and no research center at the National Institutes of Health for this, though it keeps them for alcohol and drugs. "It's just unaddressed at the federal level."

The damage is not hypothetical. Nower's new national study counts more than half of young people between thirteen and twenty-seven having gambled in the past year; among the young gamblers, 39.5 percent showed some degree of gambling-related risk and 10.3 percent sat at high risk. Eisenberg's research on legal sports betting found measurable increases in depression and anxiety treatment among young men in the states that opened it. His own summary is the plainest sentence in the reporting: these are "gambling products that have exited the only regulatory system designed to limit gambling harm" — deposit limits, self-exclusion, advertising rules, treatment paid for out of the betting take. Very little of the revenue reaches the states absorbing the damage. The exchange keeps the take and the town absorbs the wreckage. The cost did not disappear. It moved, which is the oldest move there is. Aim the anger at the book that pays no taxes to the county whose boy is up at two in the morning with the phone — not at the boy. He is the gulled, and the gulled get grief, not contempt.

I do not want a new federal bureau to be the guardian of my neighbors' health either, and not because these platforms deserve to be left in peace. Concentrating that guardianship in Washington is the same mistake in a different coat — it buys the cure by handing the common thing to the most distant hands in the country. But that refusal cannot become an alibi. The failure is not chiefly that Washington regulates too little; it is that we have arrived at a place where a child's health is the kind of thing that can be owned at all.

Conserve what, exactly? That is the question this industry puts to anyone still using the word. The elimination status they are taking positions against was not issued by an agency; it was earned across generations, shot by shot, in church basements and school gyms and county clinics, by nurses and family doctors and parents who never once thought of it as an asset. Edmund Burke called society a partnership between those who are living, those who are dead, and those who are to be born. A contract that pays if the fortress falls is not an instrument of that partnership; it is a short against the inheritance. When the school nurse and the parish phone tree — the small bodies that actually do the work — are abstracted into a number strangers can hold, the old principle still applies: Pius XI called it a grave evil to assign to a higher body what a lesser one can do. This was never a debate about the vaccine schedule, and I will not let it become one. Some things are not priced and then defended. They are held in common or they are lost. The earth was given for all; a county's fever chart is not an asset class.

Honesty requires the harder sentence, and it lands on my own side. For forty years both parties sold gambling as economic development — lotteries for the schools, casinos for the river towns — and a movement that talks of virtue blessed the vice whenever the cut went to the treasury. This was never a debate about the vaccine schedule. The arbitrage these platforms ran did not appear out of nowhere. It walked through a door we spent decades holding open.

What stands against a casino on every disease is old, local, and slow. The mutual: neighbors pooling a risk they can see, the way Philadelphia tradesmen insured one another's houses in 1752. The lodge-hall raffle with a fixed pot that rebuilt the parish roof and ended when the last ticket was sold — even our vices used to have a purpose and a place and a closing bell. Adams County Memorial Hospital went up in 1959 on land George and Mary Polivka gave the county, paid for substantially out of the proceeds of the Friendship centennial two years earlier — a town that decided its sick were not a market. The Adams-Columbia Electric Cooperative, headquartered here in Friendship, has carried some thirty-one thousand member-owners across twelve counties on one member, one vote, with no absentee anywhere holding a claim on it. That is the distributed answer: it centralizes nothing, and it puts the decision in the hands of the people who live with the consequence. It is harder to build now than it was then — we are the county with the lowest labor-force participation in the state, 48.6 percent, a quarter of the city of Adams below the poverty line, and nearly half the houses standing empty most of the year for people who will never vote here — and it is difficult to start institutions when the people who would staff them are working the resort. We started the hospital and the co-op anyway, once, out of a centennial and a membership roll, with the frame parish the county was built around holding the rest of it together. When measles reaches a school in a county like this, there is no contract to hedge it. There is the nurse with the little cards, the phone tree out of St. Leo's, the memory of the last country doctor making his rounds over the sand roads by buggy, some twenty-five hundred babies delivered into a county of nine thousand souls. None of it scales. None of it trends. It pays no dividend, and it must be staffed, called, and kept — which is to say it is the harder thing, and the harder thing is the thing. The familiar to the unknown, the actual to the possible. That is not nostalgia. It is the only direction the answer can go.

Which returns us to Brooklyn. Within a week, the Polymarket sign went the way of all flesh — the bottom, with the logo and the text inviting the wager, torn clean away. Now the sign simply reads: Vaccines. A stranger, with no committee and no campaign, took the bet out of the sentence and left the common good standing in the weather. That is the whole of what I would build, in one gesture, and it does not scale either. Keep the parish list current. Fund the nurse. Teach the boy the odds before the app does. The dead built the fortress; the living keep it; and no book, in Chicago or anywhere else, gets a position on it.
