Two trillion dollars of paper grief fell over Wall Street this week, and not one drop of it was for the water. On Thursday the market put a price on American self-government: two days after Democrats won the midterms overwhelmingly, the tech-heavy Nasdaq dropped more than five percent and more than two trillion dollars in market value evaporated — its worst day since the tariff shock of April last year. That is the plain meaning of what NPR's Rafael Nam reported in ["AI shares sink after blue wave"](https://www.npr.org/2026/11/05/nx-s1-6030828/ai-shares-sink-after-blue-wave): investors fear that the incoming Congress — and statehouses across the country — will crack down on the AI data centers that became a grievance in this election. Markets had rallied the day before, relieved that the elections would not be contested; the hangover came when anyone counted what the winners had promised to do. Nam reports it fairly, and the fear is not irrational. It is an admission.

Grant the investors their strongest point first, because the point is real. The data centers are load-bearing: billions have plowed into Meta and Alphabet and their peers, the models cannot be trained without the buildings, and the machines do real work. Roughly half the S&P 500 is now AI-related, which means this trade is not some Wall Street casino; it is the plumber's 401(k) and the schoolteacher's pension, held by "people like you or me," as Nam put it — and if the buildout truly stalls, the losses will not stay in Manhattan. They will land here, in Adams County, on people I see across the co-op counter every week. David Busch, chief investment officer of Trajan Wealth, gave the fear its honest form: "far more regulations coming to AI technology," and "a pushback on the build-out of data centers across the country." Nam himself allowed that if the pushback lands, investors "may not see the returns they were hoping to get" — while noting that cracking down on the data centers may be a winning issue for Democrats, even if he could not say whether it "would be such a good thing for our own stock portfolios." That is an honest accounting. Grant every word of it. And I will say what few will: the voters' anger is not a plan. A Congress that stops the machine without building anything leaves the towns with neither the water nor the work. My county knows what it is to lose the thing that produced without anything arriving to replace it — the mill at Wisconsin Rapids idled in the summer of 2020 and took roughly a thousand paychecks out of the region, and nobody ever mailed us the replacement.

But look carefully at what happened Thursday, because the lesson is in the mechanism. The voters instructed their representatives. The market answered by repricing the country. That is not information moving; that is a veto, delivered without a vote. And the veto needs no one to pull its trigger: the sell-off makes capital dearer, and capital that costs more walks, and the threat enforces itself. Nothing in the week's news said the water had gotten scarcer or the grid weaker; what fell was a claim. Two trillion dollars is not a number anyone can hold — it is the market's estimate of future profit, repriced because the people who drink the water might be allowed a vote about it. A price is a verdict. The verdict handed down Thursday is that the country belongs to whoever owns the machine, and that the people who own the watershed are a risk to it.

I know how the threat is built. I spent the years before I came home trading agricultural futures in Chicago — paper claims on corn and beans grown by men I grew up beside — and I watched this trick performed on smaller stages all those years: entangle your risk with everyone else's retirement until nobody dares pull the lever. Half the index. Pensions and 401(k)s with it. The threat is never spoken aloud; it is priced. And when half the index is one trade, every retirement in the country is stitched to it, and the stitch is presented as a fact of nature. The man who works the co-op floor is instructed that his own future depends on the survival of the machine drinking his county's water — that he must pray for a thing he never chose. Belloc had a name for an arrangement in which the propertyless trade their independence for a security someone else controls: the servile state. He imagined the wage. He would have recognized the pension.

Now name what Thursday actually dissolved, because the words for it are old ones. A data center is a factory with no town. A paper mill at least made paper with the town's hands, and the rail yard in Adams once fed union wages into half the town's kitchens; this machine is the opposite of both. It is a building with almost no one in it, owned by no one from here — drinking the aquifer, loading the substation, drawing the rain and the current of a place it will never belong to, paying the tax agreement its lawyers negotiated, answering to owners who will never stand in the county where the water was. It is absentee ownership distilled, the function severed from the place so completely that the place is only the plumbing: it rents the water and the electrons and mails the margin elsewhere. Abstraction does not stop at the county line — I traded futures in corn I never grew, and I know exactly how little the men in that building thought about the men in the field. At least the corn fed somebody. This paper drinks the rain.

That is property without stewardship, and the tradition I keep has names for it. Burke called the commonwealth a partnership between those who are living, those who are dead, and those who are to be born; the water under a county's sand is part of that partnership's trust, and no one elected an index to spend it.

Notice, too, what the voters actually asked for. They watched the data centers consume electricity and water by the county-full, they feared the machines would take the work, and they elected people who promised the places would have some say — in Congress, and in Texas, and wherever the aquifers run. That is not confiscation. That is the oldest question conservatism ever asked: who decides? The old Catholic word for it is subsidiarity — decisions belong at the lowest level that can carry them — and Pius XI called it "a grave evil" to assign to a higher association what the lesser ones can do for themselves. The evil does not become a virtue when the higher association is a fund. The water under this plain was not created to be a line item. By that measure the siting of a server farm belongs with the people whose water it will drink — not on a balance sheet in Menlo Park, not in a spreadsheet on Capitol Hill. The states have already been acting where Washington would not: [New York has restricted how chatbots may conduct themselves with children](/articles/2026-10-28-new-york-restricts-chatbot-conduct-with-children-under-18/), and [California banned the addictive feed for users under sixteen](/articles/2026-09-10-newsom-signs-law-banning-addictive-social-media-features-for-users-under-16/). The principle arriving by the side door is one the encyclicals carried through the front for a century: the decision belongs at the level that bears it. And so what the investors repriced on Thursday was not a possibility. It was a memory.

My county knows this arc by heart. The railroad built Adams in 1911 and pulled the work out one diesel at a time until the last regularly scheduled passenger train ran in 1963; the paper mills along the river carried the region's paychecks until their century was done; the family dairy farms consolidated into a handful of great irrigated potato operations growing for the snack-food giants; the little frame parish founded in 1884 was consolidated when the families thinned. And the consolation, when the work was gone, was to become scenery — a golf resort planted on the old sand barrens as deliberate economic rescue, lake subdivisions rising on Castle Rock, nearly half the county's houses standing empty because they belong to somebody's summer, a labor-force participation rate now the lowest of any county in Wisconsin. The next rescue, we are told, is the data center: other people's calculation, again. Wisconsin has already met the new tenant: Microsoft building where Foxconn walked away, out in Mount Pleasant; Meta's campus at DeForest. When the offer crosses into the sand counties, as it will — we are cheap land with water under it and power corridors through it — Thursday's message will arrive with the surveyors: your water is spoken for; your consent is a permitting detail; interfere and the index will bill you. It is the oldest pitch there is, the familiar traded away for the possible.

For two generations, the movement that governed in the market's name taught us that the market was the vote — the consumer's dollar, the shareholder's ballot, the only poll that counted because it cleared in cash. This week it counted once more, and came back five percent down and two trillion gone. Ask the salesmen who spent forty years promising that the market and the town could live as neighbors: conserve what, exactly? The promise has been repriced.

A word now to the incoming majority, with more respect than it is used to receiving from a man like me: stopping is not stewardship. If the answer to Thursday is a federal preemption, a national compute authority, a permitting regime written in Washington, then the cure is the disease in a kinder coat, and the same rope binds the winners — power concentrated to do good will concentrate to do whatever it likes once the good is forgotten. Nor should anyone expect Washington to leave the question alone: the likelier fight is preemption itself — a Congress or a federal agency striking down the state and local rules, New York's and California's included, so the market never has to meet a vote at all. Those side-door gains are precisely what has to be defended. And a harder word for the voters who cheered on Tuesday: they did not only vote against the data center. They want the electricity cheaper and the hospital kept open and the kids able to come home. You cannot win a county by refusing its only offered future unless you bring it a real one. Anger wins an election. It is not an economy.

What building looks like sits two miles up the road from my desk. George Polivka electrified Friendship and Adams in 1914 with a dam he built himself on the Little Roche-A-Cri, and when neither the market nor the government would carry the wire out to the farms, Congress answered in 1936 with the Rural Electrification Act — low-cost loans routed not to corporations but to member-owned cooperatives — and the co-ops carried it: the wires, the votes, and the bills in member hands, from roughly one American farm in ten lit to nearly every farm within a generation. The proof that it lives here still is the Adams-Columbia Electric Cooperative, headquartered in Friendship, more than 31,000 member-owners across a dozen counties, the largest rural electric cooperative in Wisconsin by its own count, governed by a board its members elect — one member, one vote, its books open to anyone who owns a share of it, which is to say anyone it serves. It is the standing proof that a place can own the decision about its own infrastructure.

Nobody ever made the same arrangement for the water itself. The villages own their wells, the rural water districts pipe their lines, but no body of the people who drink the aquifer owns the decision about it. So let the data centers come to terms with owners who can say no — water governed by the town like the commons it is, land leased on the town's terms with votes attached, the way the farmers once pooled under Capper-Volstead in 1922 to face the elevators and the packers who set their prices. Whether the aquifer powers somebody else's machine should be decided the way the co-op decides about a substation: in a meeting room, on a recorded vote, by the people who drink the water, with the price laid out in public. Not by a hyperscaler's site-selection team, and not by a commission in Washington managing the buildout from above — that would be the same disease in a different coat, and I have no interest in trading one absentee owner for another.

And let no one pretend the co-op is a fortress. A data center arrives with a load that could transform the balance sheet, the board will be tempted, and the members themselves may vote for the money — the buyout check sits on the table at the next zoning meeting, and the members' children are in Madison and Chicago. A cooperative takes a generation, and patience is a crop this county learned to raise in sand. Democracy guarantees no outcome. It guarantees only this: that the people who bear the cost are in the room where the cost is weighed. Someone will say a county that refuses only sends the surveyors over the line into the next one; so be it — a membership veto prices the water wherever the surveyors go next, because the next board faces members with votes, not taxpayers with rate orders. The record is plain about what happens when the market will not serve a place: the members did.

The two trillion will mostly come back; markets are very good at mending paper. The rain over the sand plain does not mend, and it has never once been traded on an exchange. I have sat through co-op annual meetings that ran long, argued hard, and decided little, and I would sit through a hundred more rather than watch a market settle the question in an afternoon because the people who drink the water asked it. The earth was given for all. The town keeps an older answer than the index — cheaper than an index and harder to repossess: the vote that comes with membership, and a trust that holds the water for the living and the dead and the unborn together, and does not put it up for bid. Leave the town its life.
