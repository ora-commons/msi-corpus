Steinbeck wrote that the Salinas Valley would one day be "a valley of great richness" where "happy people will live here, thousands and thousands." The Brookings Institution has a newer prophecy: Salinas is the least affordable city in the United States for the middle class, and nearly 60 percent of that middle class cannot afford basic necessities. That is not a dream deferred. It is a promise broken — and anyone who tells you the people moving in prove the promise is working is describing a housing crisis that has simply finished moving in.

Let me be honest about the baseline, because the baseline is the whole argument. The middle class here is the middle 60 percent of earners, households running roughly from $38,000 to $193,000. Of those households, almost six in ten cannot cover necessities. That's double the national average. Three-quarters of the Latino middle class — 40 percent of all middle-class households in the valley — are stretched thin. Those numbers are not a footnote. They are the state of the valley, and they are as serious as they sound.

Andre Perry, the Brookings senior fellow, put the soft version nicely: people come to Salinas "as a reprieve from San Francisco and some of these high-priced cities." A reprieve is where the pressure lets up. If leaving the Bay Area dumps you in the least affordable city in America, you have not found relief. You have found the leading edge of the same fire. Housing crises do not stop at county lines; they migrate. From San Francisco to the East Bay, from the East Bay to the Central Valley, from every coastal metro to every town with a hospital and a decent school. Salinas is what the migration looks like when it lands: the same rents, transferred onto a farm town's wages. The people moving in are not proof the dream is working. They are proof the dream is being relocated.

The Steinbeck scholar Gavin Jones calls the valley both "the promised land" and "a fallen paradise." The literary line is elegant. The economic one is simpler: the promised land has been priced, and the price list was not written for the people already standing on it.

Consider Gaye Freedman, 75, who earned a doctorate at USC and lives with her husband in a $1,850 one-bedroom that only became rent-stabilized in 2024. After rent and Medicare, there isn't much to spare, so she cooks stews and spaghetti that last several days. That is not a successful case study. That is a smart, educated woman propping up an unaffordable structure with her own body and her grandmother's recipes. Consider Salvador Herrera, a former construction worker injured on the job, raising two children on disability, some weeks left with $50 for groceries after rent. He stays because he was born there and his children are there. Do not read that as pluck. Read it as the price of exit. For a disabled father, leaving isn't a choice; it's a cliff. People absorb the failure because the alternative is worse, and then we call them resilient so we don't have to call the structure broken.

Mayor Dennis Donohue has a joke: his town exports "vegetables and too many of our kids." It's a good line. It's also a confession. Amy Salmina, whose family has been in Salinas for four generations, is planning for her firefighter son to leave unless he marries into a second income. That is not the dream crowded back in. That is the town exporting its own children. The K-shaped economy in Salinas isn't a fork; it's a chute. One prong feeds the owners and the investors. The other holds the residents still while the rent eats.

Rent stabilization is a tourniquet, not a hospital. The November referendum is worth winning — a cap on annual increases is the least the city can do, and the council member whose district is 70 percent renters was right to vote for it — but a cap doesn't build a single unit. The 11,500 units the city approved in 2008 were killed by the Great Recession, then by Covid, then by mortgage rates above 7 percent. What survived all of that? Not a building owned by anyone who lives there. That's where the build begins.

If Salinas wants the valley to be rich with happy people, it should take the land out of the speculation market. A public/community land trust with binding affordability covenants can do that: the trust owns the ground, the family owns the building, and a covenant locks the resale price to a formula so the unit can't be flipped into an asset class. Burlington has run a version since 1984; the Bay Area is running several now. It isn't a slogan, it's a deed. Pair it with a municipal acquisition fund authorized to buy the 2008 pipeline and the stock that's been rolled up since, and fund it the honest way: a transfer tax on the farmland flips that keep converting the salad bowl into subdivided lots, a land-value tax on the appreciation the city's own investments create, and a state housing bond paid back by the savings in shelters, evictions, and emergency rooms. That is not an Airbnb press release. Public relations doesn't pay mortgages. A public balance sheet does.

And because this is Salinas, the dream has to run through the fields. The people who harvest more than half the nation's lettuce and nearly a third of its strawberries deserve the same instrument every other worker in this town needs: the power to bargain with a whole industry behind them. Sectoral bargaining for agricultural labor — through state wage boards and the co-ops and unions already in the valley — would do that. It's harder here, because agricultural workers were carved out of federal labor law generations ago, so the work has to happen at the state level. Harder here is not impossible here. It's just the fight.

The mayor says that if any city should know how to tell a story, it should be this one, and he's confident the book will have a happy ending. Fine. A happy ending is not a sentence. It's a deed held in common, a wage that covers a rent-stabilized one-bedroom, and a land trust that's still standing when the next boom comes. Build that, and the valley can have its thousands and thousands. Keep betting on newcomers and press releases, and Salinas will go on being what it is today: a brave town doing heroic work to hold up a structure that is failing it.
