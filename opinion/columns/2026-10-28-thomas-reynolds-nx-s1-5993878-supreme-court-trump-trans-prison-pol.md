The Trump administration brought fear instead of evidence to erase transgender prisoners’ medically necessary care.

More than 2,000 people in Bureau of Prisons custody identify as transgender, according to Li Nowlin-Sohl, a senior staff attorney with the American Civil Liberties Union involved in the case. In *Kingdom v. Trump*, 1,000 inmates have been diagnosed with gender dysphoria. About 800 were prescribed hormone therapy, and many also received accommodations for social transition. They are federal prisoners, not hypothetical beneficiaries of a policy.

The Trump administration can issue an executive order declaring that America has two sexes. It can order the Bureau of Prisons to stop providing hormone therapy and other transition-related care. What it cannot do, at least for now, is make the Supreme Court rubber-stamp the switch.

On Wednesday, the Supreme Court denied the administration’s emergency request to pause a lower-court injunction blocking the Bureau of Prisons policy. The existing care regime remains in force while the litigation proceeds. Technically, the Court did not issue a final ruling on the merits. It denied a stay. The unsigned order in *Trump v. Kingdom*, No. 26A416 (U.S. Oct. 21, 2026), states: “The application for stay presented to The Chief Justice and by him referred to the Court is denied.” The order gave no explanation, although Justices Clarence Thomas and Samuel Alito said they would have granted it. The majority did not announce a sweeping constitutional right. It simply refused to rush the administration’s policy into effect.

The ruling was narrow. That does not make it weak. It means the administration must continue litigating the policy through the courts; it cannot implement it now.

The administration’s case was not medicine. It was prediction. Its [application for stay in *Trump v. Kingdom*, No. 26A416](https://www.supremecourt.gov/DocketPDF/26/26A416/425762/20260928095458720_Kingdom_Application_FINAL.pdf) (U.S. Sept. 28, 2026) argued that recipients of specialized care could become “targets for attacks,” and that providing treatment would “breed resentment” among other inmates. Security and order matter inside a prison. A government may lawfully consider both. But concerns do not become evidence because the government controls the cells, the guards, the medical visits and the medication.

The administration brought fear where it needed proof. The ACLU told the Court that had Bureau doctors found the treatment unhelpful or harmful to patients, or had the agency found that it compromised security, the government could have said so in the administrative record. It did not. That sentence appears in the organization’s [opposition to the application for stay, No. 26A416](https://www.supremecourt.gov/DocketPDF/26/26A416/428868/20261008145929279_2026.10.08%20Respondents%20Opposition%20to%20Stay%20Application%20-%20Trump%20v.%20Kingdom%2026A416.pdf) (U.S. Oct. 8, 2026).

The ACLU’s opposition described the omission as “glaring” and the government’s arguments as “contrived.” In its filing, the organization noted that the administrative record contained no indication that Bureau doctors had found the treatment harmful or that the agency had found that it compromised security. The order was unexplained. The administration’s case was not. It had more than three decades of experience with the policy it wanted to end, and instead of a record showing harm, the stay application offered a theory of targets and resentment and called that a record.

The medical evidence runs the other way. The [American Medical Association](https://www.ama-assn.org/press-center/ama-press-releases/ama-reinforces-opposition-restrictions-transgender-medical-care) describes access to gender-affirming care as medically necessary for treating gender dysphoria. The [World Professional Association for Transgender Health](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/pdf/10.1080/26895269.2022.2100644) identifies hormone therapy and gender-affirming surgery among possible interventions. Without treatment, individuals can experience severe anxiety and depression, and some turn to self-harm or suicide. The labels are rhetoric. The medicine is evidence.

The Bureau of Prisons has treated people diagnosed with gender dysphoria since at least 1992, according to the ACLU. Then the administration erased the population it wanted to govern. After Trump took office in 2025, the Bureau stopped [publishing transgender-inmate data](https://www.bop.gov/about/statistics/statistics_inmate_gender.jsp). It now records incarcerated people only as male or female. An administration that erases the count of the people affected by its policy cannot expect courts to treat its assertions about them as settled science.

The government’s own stay application put the danger in its hands. It could reduce the risk through its own policies. Instead, it asked the judiciary to let the policy take effect while warning the Court that the care it wanted to end could leave prisoners exposed as targets. That is not neutral risk analysis. It is the government warning the Court about danger it has the power to manage.

The word “risk” has become political currency—[Common Sense Media recently found ChatGPT teen safeguards an “unacceptable risk”](https://msi.news/articles/2026-10-07-common-sense-media-finds-chatgpt-teen-safeguards-an-unacceptable-risk/)—and in this case it became the administration’s substitute for evidence. The filing joined a familiar “risk first, remedy second” reflex. Here, however, the government had to answer a record. The medical consensus supports the care. The administrative record described by the ACLU contains no indication that Bureau doctors found the treatment harmful or that the agency found that it compromised security.

The prisoners’ class action contends that the policy violates the Eighth Amendment’s ban on cruel and unusual punishment. U.S. District Judge Royce Lamberth barred the Bureau of Prisons from implementing it this summer. The U.S. Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit declined to pause his order, and Solicitor General D. John Sauer asked the Supreme Court to intervene.

The Court’s wider record makes the result stranger. In 2025, it upheld state laws making it illegal for doctors to provide gender-affirming care to minors. In June 2026, it ruled that states can ban transgender girls from participating in sports at publicly funded schools. Yet this week it refused to let the federal government terminate established care for transgender prisoners.

Whatever the doctrinal distinctions, the pattern is ugly. The administration can pursue its anti-trans project through elected government when the target is school athletics, medical treatment for children or recognition outside prison. When the consequences land inside a cell and prisoners have little power to resist, the Court drew a line. On this record, the administration’s preferred view of sex did not command a majority of the Court.

Other transgender-rights cases are waiting at the Court, including disputes involving a transgender girl in high school wrestling, religious preschool funding and parental consent for gender-affirming care. But the prisoners inside the federal system cannot wait for every case to be briefed.

For now, the injunction holds. The anti-trans prison policy did not become law. The administration’s emergency request failed, and the care continues. It had to win permission before it could pull care. It brought fear and resentment. It did not bring a medical record.

Wednesday’s order was unsigned and shallow. It will not guide much future doctrine. For hundreds of prisoners, however, it was concrete: their established treatment did not disappear because a president wanted it gone.

The Court did not end the litigation. It stopped the administration from acting as though the litigation had already been won. In a system built on the premise that the government may confine people but may not casually withdraw their health care while doing so, that distinction is the whole point.

The administration brought fear and resentment. It did not bring a medical record. This time, it had none.
