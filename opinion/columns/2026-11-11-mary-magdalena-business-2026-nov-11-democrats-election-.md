Last week's elections swept in a Congress that campaigned on [an affordability agenda](/articles/2026-11-09-democrats-prepare-affordability-agenda-after-the-midterm-win/): Medicaid and food stamps restored, the Affordable Care Act subsidies brought back from expiration, Medicare for All endorsed from the stump. The promises are already being priced against the sentence now running through the coverage: there is no money.

The promises arrive into arithmetic that does not bend. The federal deficit is running at about 6.2 percent of the economy. The debt stands at $40tn, more than 120 percent of gross domestic product. Paying interest on it costs roughly $1tn a year, more than the entire defense budget. October alone, the first month of the fiscal year, ran a $409bn deficit. The Congressional Budget Office projects the debt and its interest outgrowing the economy for decades.

The Center on Budget and Policy Priorities prices the repair. Restoring the insurance subsidies costs $460bn across the 2030s. Undoing the cuts to Medicaid and the food-stamp program costs $2.2tn — the cuts Donald Trump made to partly fund the tax cuts inside the One Big Beautiful Bill Act. "Reversing the harms is going to be very expensive," said the center's senior director for federal budget policy, Brendan Duke. The Social Security trust fund runs dry in under six years, while the new senators sit in their first term, at which point the law itself cuts every check by 22 percent. Automatically. No vote, no signature.

The Committee for a Responsible Federal Budget puts the law's ten-year addition to the debt at between $4.1tn and $5.5tn, depending on whether its temporary tax cuts are made permanent by a future Congress. Bruce Bartlett, who advised Ronald Reagan on the budget, told the Guardian the design is older than this bill. Cut the taxes, strangle the budget, tie the hands of whoever governs next. The debt, he said, will "make sure the next Democratic president is a complete and total failure." The record on raising the money back is documented. Bill Clinton's 1993 increases, worth $3.3tn across a decade in today's money, produced the first surplus since the 1960s — George W. Bush spent it on a tax cut. The Affordable Care Act raised the equivalent of $1.1tn, and Barack Obama made parts of the earlier cuts permanent. Joe Biden's Build Back Better passed the House at $2.3tn and left the Senate at roughly $700bn in the Inflation Reduction Act. The last time a government with any Republican in power delivered a tax increase was the early 1990s, under George H.W. Bush, and he lost his office for it. Total US tax revenue runs below 26 percent of GDP — nine points under Britain, nine and a half under Canada, twelve and a half under Germany, sixteen under Sweden. Several OBBBA tax cuts expire within two years: the tip and overtime exemptions, the new seniors' deduction, the enlarged deduction for state and local taxes. Extending them costs about $1.2tn, and the Center on Budget and Policy Priorities finds they give the bottom rungs of the income distribution next to nothing. This is the net being argued over — the one that lets the United States run the deepest poverty in the affluent world, by the OECD's own tallies.
