MEXICO CITY — President Claudia Sheinbaum did what two years of "cooperation" with the Trump administration had made unthinkable: she said no. Not a public no, not a rally no — a closed-door no, delivered at dawn inside the National Palace to the very security chiefs who have been her most loyal operators.

The pretext was a U.S. Treasury sanctions package targeting 21 individuals for alleged Sinaloa cartel ties. Among the named: Carlos Torres, the ex-husband of Baja California Governor Marina del Pilar Ávila. The Treasury's accusation — that Torres used his political influence to "facilitate cartel activities" and "protect the Sinaloa Cartel" in Baja California, with his brother Luis allegedly collecting monthly bribes — is the kind of charge that, in any sober bilateral relationship, would be routed through an extradition request, evidence-sharing, and mutual legal assistance. Instead, Washington opted for the extraterritorial shortcut: blacklist the names, freeze the dollars, dare Mexico to comply.

Sheinbaum's security and intelligence team, led by her longtime confidant Security Minister Omar García Harfuch, had already drawn up the corresponding domestic account-freeze orders. They assumed Washington had the goods. Sheinbaum read them the riot act. Going along on Washington's say-so, without independent Mexican verification, she told them, was exactly the kind of "interventionism" and "violation of territorial integrity" she has been warning about from the Zócalo. The U.S. measures, she said, were an effort to destabilize her government.

She ultimately consented. Refusing would have been a much bigger fight than her administration is ready for, given how thoroughly the bilateral relationship has been wired through Treasury, DEA, and the [recent Los Mayos sanctions package](/articles/2026-09-30-treasury-targets-los-mayos-faction-with-sanctions-on-46-individuals-entities/) that touched 46 individuals and entities. But the consent was extracted, not given. The Mexican Financial Intelligence Unit's public language was carefully scrubbed: it blocked the accounts citing "risky financial transactions and irregularities reported by financial institutions," with no reference to organized-crime allegations. That phrasing is the diplomatic equivalent of "we're doing this because you made us, not because we agree with you."

Read the episode next to the rest of the Sheinbaum record and a coherent posture snaps into view. She [forced Rubén Rocha to resign](/articles/2026-08-28-sheinbaum-forces-out-sinaloa-governor-rocha-after-u-s-indictment/) as Sinaloa governor after his U.S. indictment, then refused Washington's extradition request — drawing her red line in public: "First they come for some, then for others, until offices of the Department of Justice decide elections in Mexico." The Trump administration has revoked the U.S. visa of a son of her predecessor and mentor, Andrés Manuel López Obrador. Washington has sanctioned three Mexican financial institutions over money-laundering allegations that Mexican authorities say they could not substantiate; her administration had to take over those firms to stop a deposit run.

That is the "close cooperation" the commentariat keeps praising. It is cooperation under duress, structured to maximize U.S. leverage and minimize Mexican agency. The Ávila case makes the structure visible. The State Department revoked her visa in May 2025 — a sitting governor of a Mexican border state — without disclosing the reason. Alberto Capella, a former security chief of Tijuana, called it "a massive slap in the face, primarily because the U.S. is your main partner in every respect." That phrasing is the giveaway: the U.S. is the main partner, and that asymmetry is the whole point of the sanctions architecture. Ávila filed for divorce from Torres after the visa action. She was not on the Treasury list. The visa revocation did not need evidence; it did not need due process; it operated as pre-punishment.

The Treasury's own language on Torres — "uses his political influence to facilitate cartel activities and protect the Sinaloa Cartel from government and law-enforcement intervention in Baja California" — is the kind of allegation that, if the target were an American politician, would generate years of courtroom combat. Against a Mexican official, it generates a Treasury press release and an extraterritorial asset freeze. The asymmetry is the policy.

Sheinbaum's late-month Zócalo rally — "We Mexicans don't want interference, interventionism, or violations of our territorial integrity," delivered to tens of thousands — is not the outburst of a leader "playing nice" who suddenly got "fed up." It is the steady-state rhetoric of a government that has learned, sanction by sanction and visa revocation by visa revocation, that the U.S. counter-narcotics relationship is not a partnership. It is a portfolio of unilateral instruments aimed at Mexican sovereignty, dressed up as law enforcement.

The frozen accounts will be frozen. The Treasury press releases will keep coming. The question is whether Mexico's "close cooperation" finally breaks open into something more honest — a public ledger of what Washington is actually demanding, what Mexico is actually giving up, and which side of the bilateral ledger each side believes it sits on. Sheinbaum's dawn tongue-lashing of García Harfuch is the closest her administration has come to admitting that the cooperation is not, and has never been, between equals.
