Trump is liquidating federal capacity and treating federal workers as disposable.

Mark Warner won a fourth term in Virginia, and the condition was plain. Warner held his seat while the administration's cuts were landing on Virginia's federal workers. The [Associated Press called the race](https://apnews.com/article/virginia-senate-07cd2d398f0086aee5e017a5db92eb2c) at 7 p.m. Eastern on Tuesday. Warner defeated Republican challenger Bert Mizusawa, and he returns to the chamber as vice chairman of the Senate Intelligence Committee.

Virginia has some of the highest numbers of federal workers in the country, and those workers bore the brunt of President Donald Trump's early drive to slash the government workforce in his second term. The Senate result came amid a broader Virginia campaign in which [Republicans defended two House seats as Democrats pressed a midterm challenge](/articles/2026-10-30-virginia-republicans-defend-two-house-seats-as-democrats-press-midterm-challenge/), and [Virginia Beach's House rematch has already been treated as a bellwether for House control](/articles/2026-08-24-virginia-beach-rematch-emerges-as-bellwether-for-house-control/). Whatever the House returns say about the chamber, Virginia's Senate vote gave voters a concrete measure of the direction of Washington: preserve the people who run the government, or treat them as disposable when the political mood changes.

Mizusawa is a retired Army major general and an Afghanistan war veteran. His service was real, and it deserves respect. It should not be reduced to a campaign accessory. But a record of service is evidence of service, not a substitute for a strategy. Virginia did not hand him the office, and the résumé is not a governing plan.

John Fogerty's "Fortunate Son" was never decoration. It named the distance between the privileged sons who escaped the draft and the sons who were sent to fight in their place, and it named the arithmetic of who pays and who walks away. Mizusawa's uniform deserves respect. It does not make civilian public service disposable.

Clausewitz puts war inside politics, not outside it. Judgment, information, and execution therefore belong inside the political calculation, because government cannot sit backstage once the shooting starts. A federal workforce is part of the means. The state that treats civilian experts, intelligence analysts, acquisition specialists, and military planners as interchangeable office chairs has not removed excess. It has removed memory.

Every institution has its own friction. Experienced workers know which duties cannot be rushed, which processes duplicate for a reason, and where fewer hands mean less capacity rather than greater efficiency. The man on the organization chart is not the work. The work is what the organization must still perform after the chart changes.

The military lesson is plain. A unit does not run on its command chart. It runs on the people who make the chart work. I learned that in an Abrams crew in Iraq, from where I sat in the gunner's seat. The chart tells you who is where. It does not tell you what the crew can do.

That is capacity arithmetic. If the work still has to be done, fewer hands do not make it disappear. They make it harder to finish. Trump's drive to slash the workforce removes capacity wherever those cuts land and leaves the duties on paper. A voter may call that restraint. The worker carrying the remaining workload calls it what it is.

Trimming a roster is not the same as trimming waste. A spreadsheet can count chairs. It cannot preserve what a failed procurement sounded like, the warning buried in an old assessment, or the judgment of the specialist who understood how both pieces fit. When those people are removed, the plan meets less knowledge. It meets more meetings, more reporting layers, and more people forced to reconstruct what used to be written down. The cost is easy to hide because those who lost their jobs are no longer in the room when the next decision is made.

Efficiency is what politicians call a cut when they cannot or will not name the capability they have liquidated. That does not pencil out as sound government, and it does not pencil out for the men and women in uniform. It is a strategic decision made by officials who can transfer the immediate price to federal workers while the national consequences arrive later on someone else's desk.

There is a moral judgment here, and it is not partisan ornament. A republic that treats the people who carry its public duties as disposable has misunderstood stewardship. Stewardship is not a soft decoration. It is the doctrine that public authority exists to serve the common good, including the workers who make the machinery possible.

Congress should use its Article I power of the purse, its appropriations authority, and its committee oversight authority to require an agency-by-agency accounting of every workforce reduction, proposed or already made. Each agency should be made to explain which legal duty disappears, who absorbs the work, whether the remaining staff can perform it lawfully, and what capability each cut erased. Congress must protect the civilian and military knowledge the country needs to plan, buy, and conduct its defense, and it must stop treating federal employees as a partisan savings account. Civil servants should remain accountable for their performance and for the use of public money. They should not be made vulnerable to partisan punishment for doing their jobs. Reform that cannot explain what practical power the government has lost is not reform. It is disarmament by ledger.

Warner will return to the Senate, where he can question what the administration's cuts leave the country less able to see and meet. Trump cut the federal workforce. Warner kept Virginia's Senate seat. The workers bore the bill. The next question is whether Congress will keep the government itself.
