Donald Trump Is Sending Armed Agents to Hunt Families Honoring Their Dead. The record sits in Henderson, Kentucky — twenty-seven thousand people on the Ohio River, maybe one in twenty of them Latino, a square built for a feast. Last year the town's Día de los Muertos did not happen. Not for lack of love. For fear: the Mexican families who sold the aguas frescas and the sweet bread, and built the altars, told the organizers plainly that they were afraid — afraid that agents of Immigration and Customs Enforcement would use the crowd to find the undocumented among them while they honored their grandparents. The mariachi stayed home. The food stalls stayed home. The plaza, made for welcome, held no one. A festival of the dead was called off in a free American town because the living could not guarantee one another's safety while they mourned.

You should know what was lost. Día de los Muertos is older than every government now policing it — thousands of years older than the border — and the belief beneath it is plain: death is not a wall but a door in a larger cycle, and on these days the souls of the departed come back, and the ofrenda — the altar, the marigolds, the water, the bread, the photograph — lights the way home. In Henderson one family built an altar for a grandfather who played the violin and died in Mexico in 2023. They laid his violin on the flowers. I was raised on this feast in El Paso. I have carried marigolds to a graveyard before sunrise. I know that bread by its smell. That is the hospitality this campaign has made dangerous.

Look at the map the fear has drawn. In Worcester, Massachusetts, last year's observance went ahead only in a private place — kept off social media, a room no stranger could walk into, parking arranged close to the door so no one had a long walk to a car. Sit with that detail. A community planned the distance from its cars to its door the way people plan it in a war. In Long Beach the festival is back; Council Member Mary Zendejas, who asked to cancel it last year, wanted her city to have it again — she is the receipt, last year's fear and this year's courage in one office. In San Antonio, ICE has signed $51 million in leases and is readying a detention center that can take in 1,500 people a day, and Planet K, which threw the city's Day of the Dead party for twenty years, canceled it: "We refuse to bring people together under ICE terrorism." Asbury Park canceled. Fresno canceled. Arrests and detentions rose again in June and July, by the count of the Deportation Data Project. And in July, agents took eight people in Henderson — members of Julia's church, and one of her relatives, "the one that brought in the money," she said.

Understand the mechanism. The machine that made them afraid has not grown gentler. It has grown quieter. The agents do not have to come to the plaza. The fear does that work for free: cancel the party, meet in private, park by the door, tell no one. The fear does the work at the festivals; the arrests do the work everywhere else. Adam Goodman, who wrote the history of this machine, counts its three mechanisms: formal removal, so-called voluntary departure, and self-deportation — and the third is the cheapest deportation there is. No agent has to leave the office. The plaza empties on its own. The organizers who canceled were protecting their people, and they were right. The ones who gather this year are defying the fear, and they are right too. What is wrong is not the choices of the frightened. What is wrong is the fear itself, and the government that deploys it.

In late September, [three men died within twenty-four hours during ICE operations in three states](/articles/2026-09-29-three-men-died-within-24-hours-during-ice-operations-in-three-states/). What kind of government needs its people afraid to mourn in public? I will say the plain word the soft language is hiding. A policy that makes families hide their grief is a cruel policy, and the tradition I was raised in has a stronger word for it: evil.

The Torah commands the love of the stranger more than thirty times — more often, by the tradition's own count, than any other commandment — and every repetition gives the same reason: you know the heart of the stranger, for you were strangers in the land of Egypt. Día de los Muertos is that commandment kept at a graveside: set a place, keep a light, welcome the one who arrives. Memory is the mechanism of mercy. A people that remembers its own hunger feeds the hungry; a family that remembers its dead sets a place for them. And a state that wants a people to be expel-able must first break that memory — move the mourning indoors, unannounced, off the record, until the dead have nowhere public to return to. The parking lot in Worcester is that operation, drawn to scale. This administration has taken a country built by strangers and taught it to be afraid of its own dead.

The administration claims the Christian scriptures, so let the Christian scriptures stand beside the conduct, in the same paragraph where they belong. "I was a stranger and you welcomed me," the Lord says in Matthew. "I was a stranger and you did not welcome me," the same Lord says, and names where that lands. The bishops of the United States and Mexico wrote together that the migrant bears "the mysterious presence of the crucified and risen Lord," and that when he came to his own, his own people did not accept him. The conduct now is an apparatus for making sure no one can welcome him in daylight: some forty-five billion dollars appropriated for detention beds, an expansion aimed at one hundred thousand beds, a festival priced in fear here, a facility taking fifteen hundred people a day there. We have inherited the Constitution and the Sermon on the Mount, and we have not yet figured out how to hold both. This administration has decided the question by choosing the wrong one.

I know the empty desk. I grew up in El Paso when classmates vanished in pre-dawn raids and the teacher called attendance as if the empty desks were ordinary. I was the child at the next desk, and I let it go on. I wore this country's uniform in Iraq, and I will not stand outside that accounting and point. The fear in Kentucky is not new to me. Its reach is what is new. The machine that emptied those desks was not new then and is not new now. It is a century old. It has removed roughly eight million people by formal order and driven out tens of millions more by fear and by paperwork that calls a choice between starvation and a bus "voluntary" — a small lie that authorizes a large one. Both parties built it and both parties have run it, and the same naming would fall on any hand that holds the handle. A hand holds it now, and the hand has pointed it at the squares where families welcome their dead. This is the deportation machine turned against the dead.

There is another way, and my church wrote it down. The bishops of the United States and Mexico taught in 2003 that people have a right to find opportunity in their homeland, a right to migrate when they cannot, that nations may control their borders — the Church has never asked for open borders — that those who flee deserve protection, and that the human dignity of the undocumented is not in question while they are here. John XXIII said it plainer still: "the fact that he is a citizen of a particular State does not deprive him of membership in the human family." An orderly border needs working courts and working papers. The immigration courts today sit inside the Justice Department, their judges serving at the attorney general's pleasure, and the average wait for an asylum hearing has stretched past four years. A family asked to wait four years in the shadows while agents patrol its festivals is not being given due process. It is being given arithmetic. And a border like that does not need armed agents in mourning crowds. The terror is not a tool the work requires. The terror is a choice.

The towns found their courage again this year, and the people came back. In Henderson, the families answered the organizers' question at the grocery stores and restaurants with a single word: yes. Julia — nineteen, born a citizen of the United States, studying political science; Julia is the name the Guardian gives her, to protect her — put the reason in one sentence: "I trust in the leadership that we have in our community, and if they believe it's safe to have it, then I trust in that." There is the mechanism laid bare: the fear was never of the plaza; it was of the agents — and where a community had leaders it could trust to say it was safe, the courage came back even while the machine kept running. Her mother has her residency now; her father is still working on his. Last year, with the feast called off, her family built the grandfather's ofrenda anyway — the altar, the flowers, his violin laid on them. This Saturday they will be at the plaza selling aguas frescas and horchata and pan de muerto, keeping the appointment their family has kept with its dead. "I love seeing the same customers come back," she said, "but with more people that they love."

In Worcester, two thousand people filled a public park on the twenty-fourth of October — mariachi, art, and a theme the organizers set for the year: *amor como resiliencia*, love as resilience. "It's not easy to exist and to still bring joy in dark times," said Raquel Castro, who helped build the celebration. She is right. It is not easy. That is exactly why it has to happen in public, where the dead can find it. That is not a festival. That is resistance in its oldest form — a table set in the dark.

The country has noticed. The children of the emptied desks are voters now, and this coming week [new citizens and immigrant-family voters are casting their ballots as a family duty](/articles/2026-10-30-new-citizens-and-immigrant-family-voters-cast-midterm-ballots-as-family-duty/) — the same families the machine hunts, answering for it at the polls because it is their family. [Immigration enforcement is reshaping the midterm races as disapproval grows](/articles/2026-10-12-immigration-enforcement-reshapes-midterm-races-as-voter-disapproval-grows/), because voters can see the empty plazas too.

In San Salvador, in the cathedral, on the twenty-third of March, 1980 — the day before they killed him — Archbishop Óscar Romero said to the soldiers of his own country: les suplico, les ruego, les ordeno — I implore you, I beg you, I order you, in the name of God: cese la represión. Stop the repression. No soldier, he preached, is obliged to obey an order contrary to the law of God; an immoral law no one must obey. I hold that door open now to the agent who reads this before his shift — in Henderson, in San Antonio, wherever the lists are written. The order that sends you to stand watch over a people mourning its grandfathers is an order against the law of God, and you are not obliged to carry it out. No order obliges you to hunt a family at its grief. You can ask for another post. You can put the list down. You can lay the number down. You can stop. You can come back. No one is beyond return — not even you; that is the whole of what I believe — but the return begins with the refusal, and the refusal begins today. The door was never locked from our side.

On Saturday in Henderson there will be pan de muerto and horchata, and a violin laid on marigolds for a grandfather, in a town that canceled the feast last year and found its courage again this year. The old belief is that the dead come home this week, looking for the ones who loved them. The dead have kept their part of the covenant. The question the country is answering, plaza by plaza, is whether the living will be allowed to keep theirs. Leave the light on. Set the table.
