AIPAC’s allies are weaponizing Jewish safety to discipline Michigan’s Jewish voters.

That is the charge the record supports. It is narrower than saying AIPAC caused the Temple Israel attack, and stronger than calling this merely a “split” in Michigan’s Jewish community.

On March 12, Ayman Ghazali drove a truck into Temple Israel, causing a fire and injuring a security guard. The Guardian reports that the FBI investigation found Ghazali had been inspired by Hezbollah. The attack was antisemitic violence. It was an assault on Jewish life and on a synagogue that should never have happened.

Nothing Abdul El-Sayed said excuses it. Nothing about Israel’s conduct excuses it. Jewish voters do not have to choose between condemning an attack on a synagogue and opposing American military policy. That is the first fact the campaign against El-Sayed keeps trying to erase.

El-Sayed said, after the attack, that “hurt people hurt people.” He apologized for the timing. He later said the attack was not the moment for context: “it wasn’t the time for context.” That apology matters. It acknowledged a real failure of judgment without surrendering his position that Israelis and Palestinians both deserve rights, safety, dignity, and self-determination.

The campaign against him did not stop with that apology. It turned the apology into evidence that he was unfit to represent Jewish voters.

That is not a neutral judgment. It is an organized political act.

The relevant rule is democratic and plain: no lobby, party, campaign, or public official gets to define the political conscience of an entire religious or ethnic community. Jewish voters are citizens, not a bloc whose loyalty can be audited by petition.

The record does not establish that AIPAC or any allied campaign violated a specific federal election law. The source material does not include FEC filings, lobbying registrations, or primary campaign-finance records sufficient to make that claim. We should not invent a statutory breach because a political operation is objectionable. But the public conduct described in the reporting is enough to identify a political breach: an effort to treat disagreement over Israel policy as evidence of betrayal by Jewish voters themselves.

The exhibits are clear.

AIPAC supported Haley Stevens in the Democratic primary and spent tens of millions of dollars trying to stop El-Sayed. El-Sayed won. The preferred candidate lost. The response was not to accept the result or debate El-Sayed’s platform on its merits. The response was to redirect money and influence toward defeating him in the general election.

El-Sayed’s stated position is not hidden. He supports ending U.S. offensive and defensive military aid. He says Israelis and Palestinians deserve “equal rights to peace, dignity, and self-determination,” through whatever political arrangement the two peoples choose. That is a contested position within the Democratic Party. It is not, by itself, proof of antisemitism.

The distinction is essential. Criticism of the Israeli government, opposition to military aid, and support for Palestinian rights are political positions. Antisemitism is hostility toward Jewish people as Jews. A public campaign that collapses those categories does not protect Jewish voters. It makes Jewish safety serve as a weapon against political dissent.

Then came the Nonnegotiable Michigan petition. It demands that candidates affirm “Jewish sovereignty in Israel.”

That phrase may carry different meanings for different Jewish voters. But in this campaign, its practical function is clear: candidates are being asked to affirm a specific ideological proposition or risk being marked as unsafe for Jewish voters. The petition therefore operates as a loyalty test, not as a neutral measure of opposition to antisemitism.

A loyalty test injures Jewish voters who reject that proposition while remaining committed to Jewish safety. It tells them that their Jewish identity does not qualify them to speak about their own political priorities unless they first satisfy an outside political standard. It converts pluralism into suspicion.

The injury is not abstract. Michigan has roughly 130,000 Jewish residents. A 2021 study found that 61 percent of Jewish adults in Michigan identified with or leaned Democratic, compared with 31 percent who identified with or leaned Republican. El-Sayed won the Democratic primary by 15,000 votes. He lost Oakland County, including Orthodox communities in Oak Park, by nearly 20,000 votes. Those results show disagreement among Jewish voters. They do not show that Jewish voters issued a unanimous verdict against him.

The Guardian’s reporting supplies the counter-record.

Jewish Voice for Peace endorsed El-Sayed in June, the first such endorsement in the organization’s history. More than 1,000 Jewish Michiganders signed an open letter backing him through Michigan Jews for Democracy. Jess Newman, a Ferndale school-board member who grew up attending services at Temple Israel, volunteers for the campaign. Jackson Koeppel, of the Detroit-based Jews for Abdul group, described members of his T’chiyah congregation who voted for Stevens in the primary and later attended El-Sayed organizing meetings.

These are not decorative testimonials. They are evidence that the category “Michigan’s Jewish community” cannot honestly be used as a synonym for the campaign’s preferred position. The community contains disagreement, organized dissent, religious commitment, political memory, and different judgments about what Jewish safety requires.

Rabbi Josh Whinston’s position is another exhibit. Whinston joined the Nonnegotiable petition and criticized El-Sayed’s use of the word “bloodthirsty,” a word with a long and ugly history. That criticism deserves to be heard. Words can wound, and public candidates must answer for careless language.

But Whinston also called El-Sayed “an inspiring leader,” said he had “the potential to do amazing things in our country,” described him as moving “in the right direction,” and said his “door is not closed” to voting for him.

That testimony defeats the campaign’s preferred binary. A Jewish religious leader can condemn El-Sayed’s language, remain concerned about his policy, recognize his leadership, and keep open the possibility of voting for him. That is not confusion. It is political judgment.

The campaign’s strongest allied voices have chosen a different method.

Vice President JD Vance traveled to Sterling Heights on August 31, met with a rabbi and a Temple Israel security guard, and called El-Sayed “very, very evil.” Republican donors and right-wing media then urged Jewish voters to support Mike Rogers. The charge presented to voters was not that El-Sayed had rejected democracy. It was that he had refused to accept the limits imposed by a particular pro-Israel political network.

That is an important distinction. “Very, very evil” is not an argument about policy. It is a status judgment. It asks voters to treat a candidate as morally disqualified before they examine his position on healthcare, wages, military aid, or the cost of living. It turns a disagreement within Jewish and Democratic politics into a test of moral belonging.

Mike Rogers is entitled to seek Jewish votes. AIPAC is entitled to advocate for candidates and policy. Jewish voters are entitled to support them. The problem is not advocacy. The problem is the attempt to present one faction’s political program as the boundary of Jewish safety, then place every Jewish voter outside that boundary under suspicion.

The same standard must apply to El-Sayed. His language after the Temple Israel attack was careless. His apology did not erase that carelessness. Supporters should not pretend otherwise. Nor should opponents use one badly timed remark to erase his actual stated position: equal rights, safety, dignity, and self-determination for Israelis and Palestinians.

That is the symmetric test. If a candidate uses language that alarms Jewish voters, examine the language. If a campaign turns that alarm into a demand for ideological submission, examine the campaign. Do not excuse either side because the political consequences are convenient.

I learned, in the Evangelical world, how quickly a community’s name can be claimed by the people with the loudest platform and the largest budget. A denominational leader speaks, a donor network repeats him, a petition circulates, and soon the institution’s position is presented as the conscience of every believer. The people who disagree are not answered. They are classified.

That is what is happening here. Jewish voters who support El-Sayed are not proof that antisemitism has captured the community. Jewish voters who oppose him are not proof that AIPAC owns the community. The community is plural, and plural communities do not owe a single political organization the right to speak in their name.

The Guardian’s headline says El-Sayed’s candidacy has split Michigan’s Jewish community. The evidence shows something more precise: a public campaign is trying to make one policy dispute stand for the whole community, and Jewish voters are resisting that substitution.

The injury falls most heavily on Jewish voters who support Palestinian rights, oppose U.S. military aid, or simply refuse to make “Jewish sovereignty in Israel” a condition of political belonging. They are being asked to prove that their Jewish safety is genuine by adopting someone else’s platform. That is not protection. It is political gatekeeping.

Michigan’s Jewish voters should reject the gatekeeping without minimizing the danger of antisemitism. They should condemn the Temple Israel attack without surrendering their judgment about Israel and Palestine. They should hear criticism of El-Sayed’s words without allowing those words to become a permanent license for outside actors to define Jewish political identity.

No candidate is entitled to Jewish votes. No lobby is entitled to Jewish obedience. No political faction is entitled to convert Jewish safety into a loyalty oath.

The record does not show a community speaking with one voice. It shows Jewish Michiganders arguing publicly about safety, justice, Israel, Palestine, and the responsibilities of American power. That argument is not evidence of communal collapse. It is evidence that the voters still possess political authority—and that several well-funded actors are trying to take it from them.
