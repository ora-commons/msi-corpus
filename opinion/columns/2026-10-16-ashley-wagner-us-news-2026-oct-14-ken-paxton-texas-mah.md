Ken Paxton is hunting Texas pediatricians to buy Kennedy's favor. Ben is one year old and the MMR is the last item on his next well-child visit. That item — a shot, a sticker, a kid who cries for thirty seconds — is what the attorney general of the second-largest state in the union has opened a file on. I am booking that appointment from Philadelphia, where nobody is investigating my pediatrician, and that positional fact belongs in the frame before anything else: I am not a Texas mother. Everything I know about what her state is doing to her doctors, I know because the doctors agreed to say it on the record.

The instrument is the consumer protection division. Its letters demand every "reward, financial benefit, bonus, discount, return, rebate or other consideration" a Texas practice has ever received from a vaccine manufacturer, along with records on "potential misleading or deceptive acts regarding incentives associated with the administration of childhood vaccines." At least one Texas pediatrician has received them and has spent thousands in legal fees answering. The conduct under investigation is the vaccination of children. The president endorsed Paxton's Senate run in May, and Paxton's announcement invoked Trump and Kennedy by name.

Take Paxton's own word, because the word is his: bribing. The theory is that manufacturers clear hundreds per dose while the doctor at the desk collects six, and that the six is the crime. Then run the arithmetic he declines to run. Katherine Wells, who directs Lubbock Public Health, is reimbursed about six dollars to administer a shot and about thirteen when the state supplies the dose free — and neither figure covers a nurse's time to talk to a parent, review a shot record, draw up the vaccine, give the injection, and answer questions. She loses money on every dose and gives them anyway, because it is the right thing. Marjan Linnell, incoming president of the Texas Pediatric Society, keeps the practice-level version of the same ledger: trained staff, cold-chain freezers that can never lose power, and the out-of-pocket replacement of an entire freezer's stock when a five-day regional freeze took the bridge to her clinic. In some cases, she says, the practice barely breaks even. For me the number that matters is not the clinic's margin. It is the line on my side of the ledger. Ben's shot costs a copay, or nothing. The alternative to that copay is a fever, a rash, a missed shift, and a bill for the workup. I will spend more on parking and on the afternoon off than the state will spend on the dose.

Only one person in this story clears real money on vaccines, and he is the man who now writes the schedule. Robert F. Kennedy Jr. has made at least $2.5 million in referrals for vaccine-injury litigation to the law firm Wisner Baum, and held a ten percent contingency stake in a Gardasil suit that reportedly settled for about $50 million in June. Senator Elizabeth Warren asked him about those arrangements at his confirmation hearing. He said he would stop. Then he filed an amended ethics disclosure saying the proceeds would go to one of his sons. Leo XIII wrote in 1891 that it is "condemned by all laws, human and divine" to "gather one's profit out of the need of another" — a sentence Paxton's theory would have to read backward to fit. Six dollars is not a profit gathered out of anyone's need. The referral fees are.

Paxton opened a second inquiry in August, this one into the American Academy of Pediatrics, on the theory that the AAP recommends vaccines for money. He was pointing at the suit the AAP filed this year to stop the sweeping changes to the childhood immunization schedule. The same day Paxton announced the pediatrician investigation, Children's Health Defense — the anti-vaccine organization Kennedy used to lead — sued the AAP for racketeering. Its activists have also reported the doctors treating measles in Lubbock to the Texas medical board. So the movement's demand for transparency is not transparency about books. It is a complaint form addressed to the physician.

Here is where it lands on a family. Texas kindergarten MMR coverage has fallen to 93.3 percent, below the level that keeps measles from moving through a classroom, and west Texas has already counted more than 150 confirmed cases and at least one death. A new state law makes school exemptions easier to claim, so the coverage number is going down while the cases go up. Annie Lowrey's argument — that American deprivation is a choice rather than a scarcity problem — needs no translation here: measles is not returning because the vaccine stopped working. It is returning because the state decided it should. When a case walks into a classroom, the exclusion rule does the rest. A child who cannot show immunity stays out for twenty-one days, the length of measles' incubation window, and so does an exposed classmate. Twenty-one days is three weeks of shifts for a parent paid by the hour with no leave, since the United States is the only advanced economy with no guaranteed paid time off. Three weeks of tuition the daycare will not refund. Three weeks of a second child's logistics. Three weeks of wages that do not come back. None of that is the arithmetic Paxton is running. It is the arithmetic a household runs when the six-dollar shot is the thing the state has decided to prosecute.

Linnell's warning is the one I keep returning to. Texas already has a shortage of primary care, and she puts the subpoena power in the exam room as one more reason not to go into it. Her worry is the small practices — offices where the owner is also the payroll — where a defense bill can decide whether the doors open in the morning. For a family that is not an abstraction about supply. It is a longer wait list, a thinner network, a longer drive, and, if the legal bill ever reaches the parent at all, a copay that goes up. I have changed pediatricians once, for insurance reasons. The cost of it was not money. It was two afternoons, a new portal password, and a shot record I had to reconstruct by hand.

Paul Offit, the vaccine scientist at Children's Hospital of Philadelphia, reads the politics without decoration. A February survey found pediatricians and the AAP ranked as more trustworthy than the federal health agencies now arrayed against them. Paxton is running this, Offit believes, because he is desperate and may lose to Talarico, and this is a stupid, desperate thing to do. Beloved is the word the medical establishment keeps reaching for and it is the wrong word. Love is not the claim. Six dollars is. Trust of that height is the one asset a public-health system cannot manufacture on demand. It can only spend it.

The answer to all of this is not complicated and it does not involve a subpoena: pay the dose at what the dose costs, pay the nurse for the time the nurse spends, fund the twenty-one days of exclusion so a parent who loses them does not lose the wages, and keep the state's investigative power out of the exam room. In the vocabulary I was raised in, vaccinating a child is visit-the-sick. It is on the list. It was always on the list.

Ben's appointment is on a Tuesday afternoon and I will take the time off. The doctor will give him the shot and the doctor will be reimbursed six dollars, by a practice that paid more than that to keep a freezer from losing power and more than that to answer a letter from an attorney general's office. In Texas that transaction has a file open on it. In Philadelphia it has a checklist and a sticker.

Swift titled a song after the arrangement — "You're On Your Own, Kid" — and the title is a fair description of what American care infrastructure offers a parent. Six dollars is what the state pays to keep a child from catching a disease that used to fill hospital wards. Six dollars is what Ken Paxton has decided to call a bribe.
