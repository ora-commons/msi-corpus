Brazil's markets crashed because the money in them had bet on the other guy and lost — not because they rendered a judgment on President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva's governing. The Wall Street Journal's editorial board, in [Markets Don't Vote for Lula in Brazil](https://www.wsj.com/opinion/brazil-election-lula-da-silva-flavio-bolsonaro-markets-c8446089?mod=rss_opinion), reads Monday's 14% plunge, the falling real, and the canceled bond auctions as "a decisive early verdict" — markets "enforcing institutional rules" against Lula. Its core argument: a pre-election lead by Senator Flávio Bolsonaro, the post-vote rout, and a two-hour ballot extension all show that Lula's comeback was bought with government spending and propped up by a Supreme Court stacked with his allies.

Start with what didn't change. On Friday, Brazil was heading for a deficit close to 10% of GDP. Public debt stood above 82%. The Selic rate was 13.75%. The Supreme Court's composition was the same. The balance sheet was the same on Friday and Monday. What changed over the weekend was which name won. You can't price in a known risk and then attribute the price move to that known risk. The move is, by definition, about the part you didn't know: who would occupy the palace.

Here's what actually happened. Flávio Bolsonaro led the first round, so money priced in a win for the right. He lost the second round by 3.6 points — 51.8% to 48.2%. Everyone holding Brazilian assets on the assumption that the other fellow would win raced to sell at once. That is why markets fall 14% in a day. Not because analysts re-checked the fundamentals over a weekend and found a new problem. Because a crowd is running for the same door at the same time. A 14% crash on a name is not an economic assessment; it is a political bet going bad. When Britons voted for Brexit, the pound fell 10% overnight — nobody called that a judgment on the European Union's institutional rules. It was repricing uncertainty. The size of this drop measures the size of the market's mistake, not the size of the threat.

The board has this part right: the Workers' Party has held the presidency for nearly 18 of the last 24 years, and 14 countries have overtaken Brazil in per capita GDP in that span. That's real, and it's damning. But it undercuts the article's own thesis. If the market were rendering a considered judgment on PT-era governance, it would have priced that in over two decades of PT governance. A market that waited until the morning after the fourth PT victory to react to PT's record isn't a market weighing PT's record at all. It's a market covering a bet that went bad.

And the board hands away its own case in its own headline: markets don't vote. Then it turns Monday's panic into a decisive verdict on an election. You cannot have it both ways. Markets don't vote; they gamble, and when the gamble loses the result is a selloff. The only real vote in this story was cast on Sunday.

The two-hour extension is treated as a smoking gun. Justice Cristiano Zanin, Lula's former lawyer, extended voting at the campaign's request, and the electoral tribunal went along — an institution acting, not a lone ally freelancing. That warrants scrutiny. Elections need clean margins, and constitutional questions belong in courts and audits. But Lula won by 3.6 points, comfortably outside any plausible margin that two extra hours of voting manufactured, and the board lets the episode "cast doubt on the legitimacy of Lula's victory" before handing the final word to the market. A selloff is not a constitution. Money leaving the country is not jurisprudence.

As for markets "enforcing institutional rules" — markets don't do that. They price returns. Brazilian equities are cheap relative to earnings after Monday's drop, dividend yields on the major banks are high, and a 13.75% policy rate is exactly the yield that draws foreign capital back when everyone else is selling. Much of what moves these markets is borrowed cheaply abroad, changed into reais, and parked in Brazil to collect that interest. That money has one loyalty: the yield. When the wrong candidate wins it doesn't wait to study the policies — it sells the reais and leaves the same day. Blaming the voters for that dependence is blaming the wrong person. The idea that a one-day drawdown is discipline rather than noise mistakes a margin call for a moral lesson.

Then consider what the voters just did. They lived through four years of Lula — the sputtering growth, the inflation, the rising prices — and they chose the same man again. The alternative was the son of former President Jair Bolsonaro, whose supporters attacked the capital rather than accept his defeat. Faced with that choice, the majority picked the known problem. The board's explanation for that majority is a "gusher of spending." The simpler explanation is that voters saw both options and picked one. One of those explanations respects Brazilians. The other exists to justify overriding them.

So what should Brazil build instead? Not the board's implicit prescription of austerity as punishment for the electorate's choice. Build an economy that can survive an election it did not like: investment that stays for years rather than money that flees overnight, and a deficit plan stated as arithmetic, defended by every government — which in practice means simplifying the state-level ICMS and the federal levies stacking on top of it, the tangle economists across the political spectrum have been trying to reform for a decade; tying infrastructure concessions to long-haul freight corridors where Brazil's productivity gap is widest; and legislating a spending cap that binds in real terms rather than nominal ones, so inflation targeting can run without fiscal drag. None of that requires markets to play government. It requires government to do government's job.

Keep the two jobs separate. Voters choose the government. Markets price the assets. Brazil's real problem is not that the wrong man won. It is that its economy depends on money that stays only when the market's favorite wins.
