ICE unlawfully reimprisons immigrants released by federal judges.

The pattern is not 20 unrelated mistakes. It is the method of a habeas regime that turns release into an interval between arrests. The order ends one detention. The next arrest starts another.

A habeas petition—Latin for “you shall have a body”—asks a federal court whether the government may hold a person in custody while the underlying immigration case proceeds. The government’s strongest argument is narrow, and an immigration lawyer would recognize it: habeas relief resolves the petition before the court and does not confer permanent immunity against a materially different arrest supported by changed facts or law. A lawyer can argue that obeying one order does not bind ICE forever and that each new arrest creates a new custody decision. ICE may initiate a new arrest on a distinct legal basis. The government may litigate new grounds. That is not defiance.

That defense works only when ICE identifies the new ground and submits it for prompt judicial review before removal. Changed facts can support a new arrest. The same detention under a new label does not. Absent that sequence, the “new ground” defense is a second bite at the same detention.

In Minnesota, seven immigrants were re-detained before they even exited the Whipple federal building detention facility on the outskirts of Minneapolis, as [MPR first reported](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2026/10/27/minnesota-immigration-rearrest-case-judge-threatens-ice-with-contempt-sanctions). “I don’t think being taken out of a building and directly into the arms of four or more officers complies with the court’s order,” said Daniel Suitor, an attorney to two of the men. A release order that lasts only to the doors of the Whipple federal building is not a release order. It is a scheduling notice for the next arrest.

Akram Mahmoud Omar was 77 years old, Palestinian, and had just survived a heart attack while detained in “Camp 57,” the ICE detention facility inside Louisiana State Penitentiary in Angola. Then ICE re-arrested him within 10 days of his release on habeas, as [The Intercept first reported](https://theintercept.com/2026/06/10/ice-deport-elderly-palestinian-immigrant/). He returned to detention after suffering a heart attack there.

In October, the [Tenth Circuit denied the government’s attempt to dismiss a habeas petition](https://www.ca10.uscourts.gov/sites/ca10/files/opinions/010111530610.pdf) on the ground that it could re-detain the petitioner. The court had found a “demonstrated pattern of re-detaining” noncitizens who had already received habeas relief. That was a finding about administration, not an accident of one file.

The record then becomes uglier. “We’ve seen a very, very sharp increase in people who are released on habeas petitions, only to be very rapidly re-detained, often in violation of the federal court’s order,” said Bridget Pranzatelli, an attorney with the National Immigration Project.

The lower courts have answered the recapture pattern with enforcement. As [The Banner reported](https://www.thebanner.com/community/local-news/ice-unlawful-arrest-court-contempt-XL43TXX2PFD4NI6GVO3OYPJQYE/), a Louisiana federal judge held a senior federal immigration official in contempt of court after ICE re-arrested Ibrahim Mohammed three times and violated orders requiring his release. Judges in Pennsylvania and Minnesota also have held federal immigration authorities in contempt or threatened to do so. A contempt ruling is a judicial finding that the government defied the court. The message is not ambiguous: a release must remain binding unless the government establishes a different lawful basis for a new disposition. The enforcement gap appears when the next arrest and removal can begin before the courts resolve that basis.

The scale explains why the obstruction matters. [The Guardian has identified at least 20 cases nationwide](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2026/nov/02/immigrants-released-after-court-petitions-ice-arrest) involving people detained after winning habeas relief during the last year, according to court records, attorneys, and media reports.

A [ProPublica tracker](https://projects.propublica.org/habeas-tracker/) records more than 91,000 habeas petitions challenging detentions. Filings since January 25 exceed the combined total from the past three presidential administrations, including Trump’s first term, while [arrests and detentions climb](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/ng-interactive/2025/aug/29/trump-immigration-ice-cbp-data). Detentions have accelerated as the [DHS workforce has more than doubled](/articles/2026-09-28-ice-detentions-accelerate-under-soft-feet-as-dhs-workforce-more-than-doubles/). Courts in Louisiana and Texas are taking months to adjudicate thousands of these cases, according to an analysis by Bolts and the Lens and reporting from the Texas Tribune. The docket designed to test detention has become a queue for testing it after detention has become the default.

Attorneys believe some re-detentions are tied to the administration’s push to [deport people to third countries](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2026/sep/21/what-is-trump-third-country-deportation-policy)—countries other than the person’s country of citizenship. The mechanism is the law. The administration is increasingly sending immigrants who cannot be removed to their home countries, either because the country will not accept them or they hold protections from being deported there, to another country entirely, where they hold no citizenship and often no ties.

“They just can’t be removed to the countries that are listed on the ‘withholding of removal’ order, but they can be removed to third countries,” said Nora Ahmed, director of the Louisiana chapter of the American Civil Liberties Union. “And that has always been the law. It’s just that it wasn’t practiced.” More than 25,000 noncitizens have been removed from the United States to countries that are not their own, despite potentially facing torture or persecution there; human-rights experts say the practice violates the Convention Against Torture.

“It’s really tethered to the explosion of third-country agreements with primarily African nations in the past four months,” said Susanna Booth, a senior attorney at the Amica Center for Immigrant Rights. “Say you win [your petition], but then [the government] is like ‘Well, that’s fine because then we can just send them to Liberia,’ it just kind of feels like you’re playing Whac-a-Mole with every country in the world.”

The connection to removal is not present in every case. Attorneys say other immigrants are re-detained with no apparent removal plan and simply held. That makes the practice more serious, not less. ICE does not need to complete a deportation to keep the detention machinery moving. It needs only the next arrest. The new arrest becomes detention without a completed removal.

The regime is not an occasional overreach around habeas. It is a sequence in which the executive takes custody first and litigates the right to keep it later. The paperwork is part of the machinery. Immigrants have [sued ICE alleging that the agency withheld documents after their release](/articles/2026-10-13-immigrants-sue-ice-alleging-agency-withheld-documents-after-release/). A person without the underlying file must begin the next fight without the record needed to test the next seizure. A government record the detainee cannot obtain cannot support meaningful review.

In Louisiana and Texas, where habeas cases already take months, recapture creates a new custody record before the old case has produced a final answer. The fear of re-detention also risks further trauma for clients and families, attorneys say.

In September, an appeals court ruled that the Trump administration had to provide sufficient notice of third-country deportations and allow immigrants to contest their removals. Shortly afterward, the Court’s six-justice majority [granted an emergency stay](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2026/sep/29/trump-immigration-deport-migrants-third-country), allowing the administration to keep deporting immigrants to third countries quickly without issuing an explanation. The Court said it would hear the case on full argument later. The government did not wait.

This is the shadow docket in plain view: the emergency docket where the Court can change rights without the ordinary full-case process of briefs, oral argument, or public explanation. The government’s interest in avoiding delay is real. But the public order did not explain why notice and an opportunity to contest could not be preserved while review proceeded. The procedure preserved the executive’s tempo and left the lower court’s rule without a public merits answer.

The question the record forces is specific: Could rapid third-country removals without individual notice—and the right of the person removed to contest the removal—have survived ordinary briefing and argument? The stay answered no legal question. It preserved the same tempo: the executive moves while the legal question waits. The government did not wait to find out.

The lawful sequence is simple: ICE identifies a new ground, a judge reviews it, and the person remains free until that review occurs. Attorneys are already pressing judges to write that sequence into their orders. “The court will say, for example, ‘The government needs to give us at least three days’ notice if they’re trying to remove this person [again],’” said Tess Hellgren, an attorney with the National Immigration Project. “It might not sound like much, but that’s actually quite a bit in cases,” she said, where the government may be attempting to put someone immediately on a flight out of the United States.

An honest application of habeas would require ICE to identify the specific new legal basis for any re-arrest, give notice, and submit that basis to prompt judicial review before moving the person toward removal. Until the government establishes a different lawful basis, the release order must control the same detention. The government may litigate new grounds, but it may not convert its own custody into the predicate for indefinite detention.

The sequence wins when it is followed. A court has overruled the Biden-era policy barring most people who crossed the United States-Mexico border unlawfully from obtaining asylum, opening eligibility for immigrants who won withholding of removal and now face third-country removal. Joseph Giardina, a Louisiana immigration attorney, had two Egyptian clients protected under withholding of removal who were re-detained weeks after being released on habeas and threatened with deportation—and then granted asylum.

“The Trump administration knows ‘[the Biden-era rule] was the only reason they were not granted asylum,’” Giardina said, “and they are doing their damnedest to deport them as quickly as they possibly can to another country.” The ground was identified, submitted to a judge, and adjudicated, and it produced a status instead of a flight plan.

The Court keeps the removal machinery moving. ICE seizes the released person again. The lower courts arrive with contempt after the next detention has begun. ICE unlawfully reimprisons immigrants released by federal judges. That is the practice, not the aberration: the executive treats a release order as a receipt for the next arrest.
