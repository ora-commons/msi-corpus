Here is what a federal graduate loan bought: better faculty, modern labs, accreditation, and the credentials that employers increasingly demand. It bought the social worker who stays in the job. It bought the nurse who becomes a nurse practitioner. It bought the first-generation student from a machinist's house who becomes the doctor. Congress has now decided that cohort is over.

Call it tuition coercion. In last summer's tax bill, Republicans capped federal borrowing for advanced degrees, and the universities are cutting prices, cutting scholarships, cutting programs — because there is no longer anyone who can pay the full cost of a graduate degree. The price did not fall. The students did.

The American Enterprise Institute keeps a tracker of the damage. Read it as what it is: a demolition schedule. AEI has identified 38 colleges and programs that have cut published prices or announced boosted scholarships and discounts since the cap took effect. Wayland Baptist University is cutting graduate tuition at its flagship campus by more than 35%. The University of Massachusetts-Global is offering a 25% scholarship for its Master of Social Work. The University of California-Irvine said it is cutting tuition for an MBA program "below the federal loan cap." Santa Clara University will give incoming full-time law school students an annual $16,000 scholarship "to offset the impact of recent repeal of the Graduate Plus federal-loan program." Emory University said it will give full-time students pursuing online degrees in public health a $25,000 scholarship "in light of recent changes to graduate student loan programs."

Read those quotations again. They are not celebrating a reform. They are describing a shock.

Before the cap, a graduate student could borrow whatever the degree cost. That was the bargain, and yes, it let universities price to the loans instead of to the family. The cap did not touch the price. It capped borrowing for most master's programs at $20,500 a year and $100,000 cumulatively, and at $50,000 a year and $200,000 cumulatively for professional programs like medicine and law. Medical school still costs what medical school costs. The cap decides who survives the number: the wealthy, the sponsored, and the indebted willing to carry the rest privately. Everyone else gets filtered out at the application portal and never knows which door closed.

The debt figure is what gets waved at you. Between 2000 and 2020, average student debt for master's degree students rose by 50% after adjusting for inflation, and master's degrees often cost six figures. Granted. That is the indictment, not the excuse. A price that tracked the loans is a price the federal government underwrote for a generation and never once regulated. The answer to that is to regulate the price. The answer is not to disqualify the borrower.

Some of these discounts may not owe entirely to the caps, since lower-tier colleges are already fighting for students. But many universities explicitly cited the loan reforms as the impetus. When the schools that live or die on enrollment say out loud that the cap is why they are cutting, that is not a coincidence. That is a cause.

Colleges dangle scholarships instead of cutting sticker prices because a discount lets you lure a student while protecting the number. Watch what that means now: when the discount has to be written into the offer letter to move a single student, the program underneath it is being hollowed for parts. And what the tracker cannot see is worse than what it can — the unpublished cuts, and the courses, clinics, and concentrations being merged into silence. Programs trimmed, merged, quietly shuttered.

This is a loss for graduate education, and the loss has names: the business leaders, the lawyers, the social workers, the public-health professionals these programs trained — and the ones who were going to.

Some are teaching this as a lesson in fiscal discipline, proof that the whole enterprise was propped up by federal borrowing all along. What it exposes is something else entirely: how fast the country will slam shut a door it spent a generation telling working families to walk through. One line in a tax bill gutted an entire sector of higher education. That is not a market correction. That is Congress weaponizing the credit system to bend a profession to its will, and deciding in advance which families are worth the credit.
