Donald Trump is robbing American families with war and tariffs.

At eleven at night, our kitchen table is where the national story gets its decimal points. Our household takes home $8,800 a month. Childcare for Eva and Ben takes $2,400, or 27.3 percent of our take-home pay: $28,800 a year before we pay for anything else. That leaves $6,400 before the 7-percent mortgage, groceries, health care, utilities, transportation, student loans, or savings. That is not surplus; it is the whole pot. The spreadsheet does not need a philosophy to tell us what is happening. It needs an invoice and a paycheck. The invoice is getting worse faster than the paycheck.

We have cushions, and the ledger has to keep them visible. David’s grandmother’s estate helped us buy the Fishtown rowhouse. We are a white, two-income household with health insurance. None of this makes my household a neutral sample. These advantages also make the betrayal harder to explain: David and I earn more in real terms than my parents did combined, and we still cannot afford for two children what they could afford for three.

My parents raised three children in Lansdale. My father’s postal salary carried our household through my elementary years; my mother’s nursing income later helped pay for Catholic high school, and our parents paid off their house by 2007. A generation with more education and more money should not need two professional incomes plus inherited help to buy a version of childhood my parents could buy on my father’s postal salary. The gap is not a gratitude problem. It is distribution, and it is made by policy.

Pamela Druckerman’s *Bringing Up Bébé* is useful here because it treats French child care as policy rather than parenting temperament. Her comparison rests on paid maternity leave, subsidized crèches, free maternal schools from age three, and school meals. The difference is not that the mothers she describes are less exhausted. It is that the state does more of the work. America keeps insisting the private household absorb what France provides in public.

Anne Helen Petersen writes in *Can’t Even* that millennials “fully conceptualized themselves as walking college résumés.” That is how wage stagnation becomes a personal assignment: bad résumé, insufficient branding, insufficient optimization. As Petersen puts it, “Burnout isn’t a personal problem, and it will not be cured by a productivity app or a face mask.” A spreadsheet cannot prove you failed; it proves a human being cannot be reduced to a billable unit and still reproduce a household.

The Labor Department’s numbers show what those bills do in the aggregate: through September, consumer prices were 3.7 percent higher over the year, and through October, average earnings were up 3.1 percent. Scale those rates illustratively against our $8,800 monthly take-home pay and you get roughly $326 of price increases against $273 of earnings growth. A household’s basket will not match the index exactly. The squeeze does not require it to.

And the deeper figure is uglier. The Labor Department reports that the share of economic output going to workers as salary and benefits is the smallest in the data going back 80 years. The economy added an average of 137,000 jobs a month over the past three months while unemployment remained low. An economy can be strong and still make workers poorer in relative terms.

Tom Green’s résumé is the same arithmetic in one person. The 29-year-old Columbus software engineer was laid off seven months ago from an $89,000-a-year bank job, sent 500 résumés, received invitations to only 12 interviews, put his home-buying plans on hold, and pays cash for his medication without health coverage. That is not an application deficit. It is the squeeze arriving outside the résumé.

Now name the accelerants. President Trump’s decision to attack turned the Iran war into an energy shock. Gasoline prices, set largely by global markets, rose and could remain high even if the war ends. His tariffs contributed to rising prices, and the reporting also links price spikes to AI-driven demand. War raises the fuel bill. Tariffs raise the price of goods. The robbery is not an abstraction. Policy choices increase what families must pay while a larger share of growing national income goes to investors rather than employees.

Then there is borrowing. The Federal Reserve is institutionally separate from Trump’s choices, and the point is not to hide behind institutional distance. The Fed raised its benchmark rate by a quarter point in September, pushing borrowing costs higher. On a $300,000 mortgage, 7 percent is roughly $1,996 a month in principal and interest, before taxes and insurance. That locks many would-be buyers out and makes every price increase compound. No conspiracy is required. The same payer absorbs each increase: the household trying to consume, work, and save.

The cheap-labor story asks us to treat the wage floor as the source of the crisis. The household ledger shows the opposite. When wages are held below what families need, the missing money reappears as Medicaid, SNAP, an earned-income tax credit, a county clinic, or unpaid care. The Penn Wharton Budget Model’s “Impact of Inflation by Household Income” analysis found that lower-income working households faced 6.9 percent inflation in 2021, against 6.0 percent for higher-income households; lowest-income earnings rose only one-third as fast as their cost of living. The employer gets the lower wage. The family and the public get the bill.

The election changed the votes. It did not automatically change the balance of power.

The new congressional majority’s [affordability agenda](/articles/2026-11-09-democrats-prepare-affordability-agenda-after-the-midterm-win/) matters. But Democrats have won 54 Senate seats so far, with votes still being tabulated in two other races, and most bills need 60 votes to overcome the filibuster. The president who launched the war and tariffs can veto the repair. The kitchen-table version of 54 Senate seats is still 54 votes, not 60. A spreadsheet cannot repeal a filibuster. Winning the majority is not the same thing as being able to act.

That is not an excuse for passivity. It is the architecture. Governing is not campaigning. Democrats have to use every vote and every state instrument that exists.

At the state level, they won the governor’s seat and both chambers in at least six states, including Michigan, Arizona, and Nevada. New Democratic governors in Ohio, Texas, and Georgia have room to act on housing, wages, taxes, and data-center development. House Democrats have made reversing last year’s Medicaid and Affordable Care Act subsidy cuts a first goal. Raising minimum wages and making it easier for workers to form unions are also under discussion. Some of this is money; all of it is power. A tax credit writes a check. Union law changes who can bargain for the next one.

Sherrod Brown wants to cap utility-rate increases and control prescription-drug prices. Ohio Gov.-elect Amy Acton has promised medical-debt forgiveness and family tax breaks. Michigan Gov.-elect Jocelyn Benson plans a $5,000 caregiver tax credit, expanded medical-debt relief, a prescription-drug affordability board with authority over price increases, and a $60,000 minimum starting salary for teachers. Benson’s caregiver credit equals $416.67 a month spread across twelve months. Make it refundable so families without enough tax liability actually receive it. State-level help can cushion the bill while the national rules that produced the squeeze stay in place.

The Nasdaq plunged Thursday when investors feared that a Democratic-controlled Congress would crush the AI boom. The boom has a constituency. Brown and Acton pledged to curb data-center construction that raises consumers’ electricity bills. Who gets the power bill? Large data-center operators should post and pay their interconnection costs, finance the grid upgrades their demand requires, and answer to public rate hearings. Otherwise households subsidize compute through their utility bills while the benefits of that infrastructure travel elsewhere. A cap that freezes bills without forcing new users to cover their costs is not consumer protection. It is cost-shifting with better branding. That is private investment growth with a public utility bill. The voters who elected these officials did not ask to finance the boom at the meter.

There is real good news in the bipartisan [energy permitting overhaul](/articles/2026-11-05-senate-nears-vote-on-bipartisan-energy-permitting-overhaul/): it can ease prices by adding supply, and it should pass. Supply matters, even if permitting cannot refund what war and tariffs already took; a bill passed tomorrow does not lower today’s gasoline. The affirmative program is not mysterious: restore the Medicaid and ACA subsidy cuts, expand universal health coverage, put paid leave and child-care support into the federal tax-and-spending system, end the Iran war, and repeal Trump’s tariffs. Rebuild bargaining power by raising the wage floor and making union formation easier, require large data-center loads to pay their own marginal grid costs, and pass permitting reform because added supply can ease prices even as war, tariffs, and investment-driven demand remain separate pressures. The point is not to pretend supply alone can repair a distribution rigged against workers.

At St. Stanislaus, my mother’s Altar-Rosary society brought a meal when a neighbor was sick, while the parish’s Saint Vincent de Paul conference arranged rent or utility help when a family fell behind. I do not romanticize it: parish charity could not repair a collapsing factory wage or replace health coverage. It could bridge a crisis that policy had made unnecessarily severe. Today I see the same work in meal trains, gift cards, and group chats. What remains is mutual aid, and mutual aid is not a substitute for public capacity.

On *Midnights*, Taylor Swift’s “You’re On Your Own, Kid” catches the assignment this economy gives parents: absorb the shock alone. The song leaves care between a person, a job, and friends. Friendship can bring dinner, but that turn is not a solution; it shows how far the public safety net has receded until lateral love is all that remains. The title is the national care brief: when prices rise, you are on your own, kid. Friendship is the love that survives; policy is supposed to make the bill smaller.

At eleven at night, the cold tea sits beside the spreadsheet when I close the laptop and move the same numbers between the same two columns. The columns still do not balance. That is not my failure. It is what happens when policy robs a household and calls the empty space character. This is how Washington robs without a receipt: the family absorbs the difference at the kitchen table.
