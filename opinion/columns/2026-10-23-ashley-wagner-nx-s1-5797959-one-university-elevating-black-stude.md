Trump's administration is erasing Black students' futures by making universities surrender race-conscious programs.

At a kitchen table, families balance childcare against take-home pay. Take a couple paying $2,400 a month in childcare against $8,800 in combined monthly take-home pay. That puts care at $28,800 a year, or 27 cents of every post-tax dollar before groceries, heat, or a college fund. These are illustrative numbers, not one household's accounts; the arithmetic shows what families face when care, learning, and opportunity are pushed onto private budgets.

A household may get help from relatives or navigate aid forms with skill; that margin keeps the worst of the arithmetic at bay, but it does not make the arithmetic comfortable. The fight over race-conscious higher education is not remote. It lands in whether a parent can work, whether a student finds a mentor, whether a classroom can explain the people and histories in front of the student.

Rent, utilities, food, and childcare come first. If what remains cannot cover tuition, books, transportation, and the cost of leaving home, a public institution is not affordable merely because its doors are open. That is not a moral argument for shifting the bill onto somebody else. It is the reason to see the transfer when public education is made to look like a private favor.

After the Supreme Court's 2023 affirmative-action decision, the Trump administration launched civil-rights investigations targeting programs that single out a specific race. In the last two years, dozens of colleges and universities have rolled back or eliminated such offerings to avoid accusations that could cost them research funding and bring other sanctions. In September, the Treasury Department proposed stripping private schools and colleges of tax-exempt status if they provide help based on race.

That enforcement machinery turns legal uncertainty into institutional surrender. Whatever legal questions race-conscious programs raise after 2023, those questions do not neutralize the threat. The threat attaches money and sanctions to the presence of targeted support, so administrators can erase a program without waiting for any final legal ruling. The administration's method is coercion: make the threat, then count the withdrawal as voluntary.

The 2023 decision concerned affirmative action in admissions. Teaching an honors college from a Black-studies perspective is a different mechanism from reserving an admission slot by race. The administration's threatened sanctions do not make that distinction disappear; they make institutions erase programs anyway.

Compare [a bill letting merchants pick card networks](/articles/2026-10-09-trump-vance-back-bill-letting-merchants-pick-card-networks/). The bill puts the merchant in charge of the choice; the campus fight puts administrators in charge of the risk. At Sacramento State, that risk lands on Black students.

That is what the administration's politics of choice produces. Choice can conceal concentrated power dressed as discretion. At a university, the federal price of building a program for Black students is legal exposure. Choice becomes an accounting fiction when one actor gets options and everyone else gets exposure.

Who really pays when a university deletes a program? The institution gets some legal risk removed from its books. The student still gets the tuition bill, plus a semester without a course, a mentor, or a room in which to be understood.

When Hayden O'Neill, a University of Michigan senior, saw her scholarship program shut down last year, she said she was sad that her university had complied with federal guidelines and no longer felt as comfortable as she had expected. O'Neill is not a mascot for a policy category. She is a student whose school made a risk calculation and handed her the loss.

The line item that never appears in the federal guidance is a young person's time, identity, and sense of possibility. Those are not soft benefits. They are the conditions that make education mean something rather than merely certify attendance.

The rollback arrives as families are asked to carry more of the cost of public higher education. The gap has to be financed. A family with assets builds a bridge; a family without assets signs a note. Calling the rule race-neutral does not make anybody's balance sheet race-neutral.

This is Heather McGhee's drained-pool problem: a public good hollowed out until private wealth fills the empty space. A university can remain open in name while the common thing it was built to provide becomes harder to afford. A student can carry tuition, books, transportation, and rent without ever feeling that the institution was designed with someone like them in mind.

Read in its class register, Taylor Swift's “champagne problems” names the affluent version of the problem: tuition is adjustable, a tutor is a purchase, and a legacy admission is a preference. The rest of us get the invoice. For a Black student, this administration adds a second bill—the tuition bill and the bill of being told you cannot study your own history.

That is the system Sacramento State has refused to surrender. According to an Oct. 23, 2026 report, its Black Honors College now includes more than 800 students inside a university of more than 30,000. Zyearre Amoah was there in 2023, when, on a campus that size, it could feel as though Black students had to look for one another. He graduated in 2024, just as the university launched the college, and now works there recruiting students.

“Black students on our campus are actually showing their faces,” he said, laughing. “That is really helpful for everyone here. We're finding each other relatively easier.”

Most students in the college identify as Black, African, African-American, or mixed race, though students of other races can apply. President Luke Wood says the college is nondiscriminatory and complies with federal and state laws. Every course is taught from a Black-studies perspective; even a math class includes stories of famous Black mathematicians. Faculty are chosen partly for their track record with Black students, including low withdrawal and failure rates.

Women are now a majority of enrollment, a pattern Dean Boatamo Ati Mosupyoe says is also true on most U.S. campuses. She wants more male role models and mentors both to encourage men to enroll and to support them once they do. A program serious about belonging has to notice who is not in the room.

Wood reports that Black enrollment is up and that more Black students are re-enrolling and graduating. This is not a soft anecdote about campus feeling. It is a curriculum, a staffing rule, a mentoring plan, and a set of outcomes the university says are improving.

Naomi Thrower, a junior studying Deaf Studies and American Sign Language, wanted to attend a historically Black college. Because California has none, Sacramento State's honors college felt like the closest alternative. After being bullied and called “whitewashed,” she wanted classmates who shared both her racial identity and her academic drive.

Thrower runs a natural-hair business on campus. Two friends helped take down her hair: they detangled it, washed it, listened to music, and simply hung out. It became an all-day wash-and-brush-out ritual, something friends had not understood in high school.

The moment looks small. It is not small. It is what belonging looks like when it reaches the body, and what a public university is for beyond credentials: a place where students can become more fully themselves.

The human-capital model treats education as a private ledger of credentials, optimization, resilience, and a personal story about failure whenever the machine jams. Sacramento State's program rejects that accounting. It says a student's racial and cultural history is intellectual material, not a deficit. Black history is not a favor handed to a group; it is knowledge the institution has an obligation to teach.

The program also puts responsibility where it belongs. Recruiting faculty partly for their results with Black students and offering male role models and mentors is not an apology for universities that failed Black students. It is one repair for a failure that universities and federal policies making accountability look expensive cannot push onto families.

Read carefully, Taylor Swift's “The Manuscript” catches the exact hinge: a familiar story has been rewritten, and suddenly you read your own life differently. The public-school promise has long been that common institutions can prepare people for a more complicated life. The federal story is becoming that a university must tear up the rooms where a child can learn the history of people like them because doing so can trigger federal sanctions. The betrayal is not that our generation forgot to value public institutions. It is that policy is teaching us to treat them as traps.

We should not romanticize one system or pretend it is the same as Sacramento State's. The principle survives: if a society claims to support families while withdrawing the public goods families depend on, it has converted solidarity into a bill. A university cannot repair the entire aid system. But it demonstrates what a system needs. Access means more than a seat in the room. The student is not left to purchase the course, the mentor, and the community one private lesson at a time.

A genuinely pro-family, pro-student agenda does not have to choose between universal support and targeted support; it should fund the whole pipeline rather than scold families for failing to buy it alone: stable childcare, adequately funded public schools, affordable public colleges with a real Pell Grant, and universities allowed to teach the histories students need. Congress should restore Pell's purchasing power and make tuition-free public college a baseline, while federal officials protect race-conscious teaching, mentoring, and scholarships from political retaliation and states build the childcare and paid-leave infrastructure that lets a student attend at all. It would not make a family's monthly budget the substitute for a functioning public economy, and it would not call the disappearance of a Black-studies program “efficiency” when the tuition bill still arrives and the opportunity does not.

At Sacramento State, the bell above the library door rings often: an adviser meets a student, someone asks about tuition, a professor gathers notes for class. Inside, students do not have to turn their identities into a defense before they can learn. That is public education at its best—not sameness, but a common room where every person can arrive as more than a category.

Students at the college quote Ubuntu, the South African philosophy of interconnected life, usually translated as “I am because we are.” At Sacramento State, that idea has institutional form: shared intellectual work, faculty chosen with Black students' outcomes in view, mentors, and an office in the library with a bell above the door. The administration's policy answers with an invoice: I am because I pay. That is the betrayal. The bell rings. The door stays open.
