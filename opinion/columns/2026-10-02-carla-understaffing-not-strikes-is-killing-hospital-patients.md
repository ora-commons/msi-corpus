Banning nurses from striking won't save a single patient. It will just give hospital executives a cheaper way to run skeleton crews. Thomas Beck, the former head of labor relations for the nation's largest hospital system, argues in [Hospital Strikes Should Be Illegal](https://www.nationalreview.com/2026/10/hospital-strikes-should-be-illegal/) that the 105 nurse strikes between 2021 and 2025 prove hospital workers should be stripped of their right to walk off the job, the way the Railway Labor Act and the Federal Service Labor-Management Relations Act already strip railroad workers and federal employees. That prescription would outlaw the only leverage hospital workers have to fix the conditions that actually put patients at risk. It protects hospital budgets, not patients. He's wrong. The strikes aren't the disease. They are the symptom of a healthcare system that has decided patient safety is a budget line.

Start with the study Beck will actually cite. The 2010 NBER examination of New York nurse strikes found a 19.4 percent increase in in-hospital mortality during the strikes themselves. Real association; it deserves a real answer, and here it is: the mechanism was never the walkout. It was the fact that the hospital was running so thin that unfamiliar replacement workers crashed an already-fragile system. The most plausible reading of the data is the most boring one — when a hospital drops below its already-thin staffing floor and replaces regular nurses with travelers who don't know the unit, mortality ticks up. The same mechanism operates on the non-strike days, just at a lower amplitude, and the study does not isolate which is which. A well-staffed hospital with proper float pools and handoff protocols absorbs a strike the way a normal business absorbs a snow day. A hospital running one nurse to twelve patients on a good day collapses when that nurse leaves. The study documented the cost of chronic understaffing. Beck read it as a case against strikes.

The mechanism is concrete, and Beck never touches it. A nurse on a med-surg floor is typically assigned five or six patients on a good day. When the census spikes, that number climbs to seven or eight, and the nurse begins to skip the work that doesn't get noticed when it is skipped: the second medication check, the ambulation that prevents a post-op blood clot, the quiet half-hour of monitoring that catches a post-surgical bleed before it becomes fatal. Those are the failures the Joint Commission catalogues in the same paragraph Beck quotes — communication breakdowns, medication errors, delayed treatment, wrong-site surgery. They are not caused by a temporary strike. They are caused by a permanent ratio that no one fixes unless someone with standing forces the issue. That someone is, in practice, the union. Nurses strike rarely and reluctantly, because a strike is the most expensive form of leverage a worker has. They strike when years of internal complaints about unsafe ratios have produced nothing, when the staffing committee is a rubber stamp, and when management's answer to a near-miss is another mandatory in-service. Beck is right that strikes are dangerous during the days they last. He never mentions the months of unsafe staffing that preceded the walkout and that the walkout was, finally, an attempt to end.

Beck leans hard on the cost figures. Kaiser Permanente — a system with annual revenues north of $100 billion — absorbed $1 billion in 2026 strike costs. New York City hospitals took a $100 million hit in their 2025 action. A community hospital in State College, Pennsylvania, lost more than a million dollars a day during its walkout. These numbers are large. They are also small against the system. A billion-dollar hit is roughly one percent of Kaiser's year, painful but absorbable, and a fraction of what chronic understaffing costs in readmissions, never-event malpractice payouts, and contract-labor spend that runs many systems at multiples of the cost of a staffed employee. The strike is a one-time shock. The understaffing is the chronic condition. The chronic condition is what kills.

Beck notes that more than 40 percent of hospital revenue comes from Medicare and Medicaid, so taxpayers eat the strike costs. He doesn't mention that taxpayers also eat the hospital-acquired conditions, the readmissions, and the pressure ulcers that come with thin staffing. A single pressure ulcer costs a hospital tens of thousands of dollars to treat. A central line infection runs five figures. Medicare stopped paying extra for many of those events years ago. The same hospitals complaining about strike costs are writing off millions in unreimbursed complications from understaffing they themselves chose. And the peer-reviewed research on nurse staffing consistently finds that each additional patient added to a nurse's workload measurably increases the risk of in-hospital death. Nobody needs a strike to produce that mortality. It runs on every understaffed shift in the country.

Beck's strongest move is the Railway Labor Act, and it deserves a real answer. A rail strike halts the entire network because there is no substitute for a freight crew on its own track; air-traffic control is a sequential choke point where one walkout closes the national airspace. Hospital care is geographically distributed across roughly six thousand facilities. The op-ed's own data — 105 nurse strikes between 2021 and 2025, touching 212 hospitals, less than four percent of the system — demonstrates the disanalogy. Walkouts are local events, absorbed by neighboring facilities, transfer agreements, and surge capacity hospitals already maintain for flu season and mass-casualty events. A patient in a striking hospital is rerouted, the way patients are rerouted every winter when emergency departments hit two hundred percent of capacity. She is not stranded the way a stranded airline passenger is stranded. A grounded plane doesn't kill the passenger on the ground. A halted train doesn't bleed out in transit. Hospitals run twenty-four hours a day on the bodies of the people inside them.

And there is a second fact about the Railway Labor Act that Beck doesn't mention. It does not just ban strikes. It requires mediation, cooling-off periods, and binding arbitration before a strike can be authorized. Beck wants the prohibition. He doesn't want the arbitration. That tells you which side of the bargaining table he sits on.

Concession, brief, because the Joint Commission point is real. Replacing an experienced neonatal nurse with a traveler who has never seen your NICU is a genuine risk. Communication failures during handoff do kill patients. But stripping nurses of the right to strike does nothing to fix the handoff. It just means nurses can't refuse to walk into a unit that was already unsafe to begin with. The unit doesn't get safer when you take away the one person with standing to say so.

The fix is not to take the lever away. It is to make the lever less necessary and the safety net it provides more reliable. Amend the National Labor Relations Act the way the Railway Labor Act actually works. Put a patient-safety floor in every hospital labor contract: minimum nurse-to-patient ratios, written into law, with fines that bite. Congress should require every hospital that takes Medicare and Medicaid money — which is to say almost all of them, since those programs make up roughly forty percent of hospital revenue — to publish unit-by-unit ratios and to staff to those ratios as a condition of federal participation. Disputes over safe staffing should go to binding interest arbitration before a strike ever has to happen, with patient-care standards set in advance so the arbitrator isn't reinventing the right ratio in the middle of a walkout.

Hospitals that violate their published ratios should face per-shift penalties that exceed the cost of the nurse they did not hire. And hospitals that post record profits while running unsafe staffing levels should be barred from billing Medicare for the complications their own choices created. Give the nurses who show up every day in those understaffed units the same federal protection railroad workers have: a seat at the table that doesn't require them to abandon their patients to claim it.

Beck wants to outlaw the symptom. The disease is a hospital industry that decided a few thousand saved on labor was worth more than the patients in the beds. Treat the disease — the floor, the arbitration, the fines, the seat at the table. Don't criminalize the only symptom that has ever forced a fix. The patients will follow.
