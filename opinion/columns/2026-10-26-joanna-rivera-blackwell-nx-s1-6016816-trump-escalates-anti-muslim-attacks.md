Donald Trump transformed Abdul El-Sayed’s Muslim faith into a disqualifying security test. The metal detector was the tell.

Abdul El-Sayed was born in the United States to Egyptian immigrant parents and raised by his father after his parents divorced. He is a former Detroit health official. If elected to the Senate, he would be the nation’s first Muslim senator. On Sunday in Port Huron, Michigan, Trump reduced those facts to a security scenario staged before a rally crowd.

He said El-Sayed would have to pass through a metal detector before being allowed into the Senate if elected. He called him a “jihadist” and said he was “on the side of the terrorists.” He tied the performance to Sept. 11, comparing El-Sayed’s possible entry to the passengers who fought back aboard United Flight 93.

That was no longer political criticism. It was a security theater with a human being inside it.

I know this grammar because religious belonging has been made to do political work in my own tradition too. The vocabulary changes from one campaign to the next; the moral structure does not. Take a person’s faith, attach an unseen political allegiance, and ask decent voters to mistake fear for discernment.

The method is now unmistakable: make religious identity a qualification for office. Article VI says no religious test can be required as a qualification to federal office. Trump was demanding the reverse. He was not asking voters to compare El-Sayed’s policies with Republican candidate Mike Rogers’s policies, or to weigh which candidate might help determine control of the chamber. He was asking them to decide whether a Muslim could be trusted to sit in the United States Senate.

The metal detector dramatized that question before policy could enter the room.

Republicans spent the final weeks of the campaign trying to link El-Sayed to the Muslim Brotherhood. There is no evidence of any such affiliation, and he has denied ties to extremist groups. His half-sister wrote in 2012, “my family has long been MB,” and described their mother’s involvement with the Muslim Brotherhood in the early 1970s. That is family history, not proof of an affiliation. The United States government has designated some branches of the Muslim Brotherhood as terrorist organizations; it has not turned a relative’s old political history into a terrorism charge.

Trump’s alternative to evidence was 9/11.

Mike Rogers had already repeated the allegation and questioned El-Sayed’s loyalty, asking, “Are you for America, or are you for these terrorist groups overseas?” Rogers said the Muslim Brotherhood had celebrated exactly one victory in a U.S. primary this year: El-Sayed’s. Trump went further, attacking Democratic Rep. Ilhan Omar and warning of a “jihadist Muslim Brotherhood member” headed for the Senate.

Rogers supplied the accusation. Trump supplied the metal detector.

The detector was the stage version of the same argument: You do not need a policy argument if you can make the opposition itself look dangerous.

The location sharpened the insult. Port Huron is a Canadian border city about an hour north of Detroit, near communities where Trump made significant gains among Arab American voters in 2024. He had become the first Republican presidential candidate since 2000 to win Dearborn, home to one of the nation’s largest Arab American populations. During his first term, he imposed a travel ban targeting several predominantly Muslim countries. Now the same man was back near those communities, turning Islam into an accusation and Arab political belonging into a loyalty test.

The geography was the message: a Muslim American could win Trump’s trust only by accepting his right to be suspected.

That contradiction produced a rebuke from inside the coalition Trump built. Bishara Bahbah, founder of Arab Americans for Trump, a group that helped lead Trump’s outreach to Arab American voters in 2024, called the statements unacceptable and “out of line,” said they were “crossing a red line,” and called them racist. The organization has since changed its name to Arab Americans for Peace.

Bahbah was not an outsider lecturing the president about tolerance. He was one of the architects of Trump’s Arab American outreach, telling him that his own coalition had limits. He warned that Arab and Muslim voters, including Christian Arabs, would remember the remarks on Nov. 3: “people are going to remember that on Nov. 3.”

The people Trump had courted for electoral gains were telling him the electoral price of his new language.

The material contrast at Port Huron was equally revealing. Earlier this month, Trump and Vice President JD Vance [backed a bill letting merchants pick card networks](/articles/2026-10-09-trump-vance-back-bill-letting-merchants-pick-card-networks/). At Port Huron, Trump announced no new economic handout for an automobile state and repeated investments domestic automakers were already making. Nicki Minaj appeared and urged voters to support the Republican ticket.

The event generated celebrity, not a new governing idea. Its genuinely new product was fear of El-Sayed.

That is how the campaign works when persuasion on material questions falters. The machinery is familiar: inflation, high fuel prices, an Iran war, a Senate campaign that may determine control of the chamber. When a governing party cannot or will not discuss what it is doing to people’s lives, it can always talk about enemies. Trump’s answer to all of it is another existential threat. This one wears a candidate’s name and faith.

El-Sayed did not answer with another name for the president or try to mimic Trump’s mangling of his own. At a rally in Grand Rapids later Sunday, he told supporters he did not care how Trump pronounced his name. “I will be Senator Muhammad to you, bro,” he said, drawing cheers.

Sen. Bernie Sanders said what the performance concealed. At the Grand Rapids rally, Sanders said Trump and his allies were using Islamophobia because they had “nothing to say” about health care, housing, and income inequality. “All they can do is foment hatred and divisiveness,” he said.

To make El-Sayed’s identity politically radioactive, Trump had to keep naming him. To make ordinary suspicion look like national security, he had to repeat the charge until repetition itself became the message. El-Sayed’s crowd answered with laughter. Trump’s answer was a metal detector.

El-Sayed has criticism of his own. He has appeared with Hasan Piker, who has made incendiary statements about Israel, Jews, and 9/11. After an attack on a synagogue in the Detroit area, El-Sayed said, “hurt people hurt people,” and later apologized, saying the remark was not intended to justify the attack. Republicans will cite those episodes, and they should face scrutiny.

But scrutiny is not the same thing as the metal detector. A candidate can say something ugly and apologize. A candidate can have controversial associates. Neither fact makes his religion a security credential. Trump’s rhetoric demands that Americans skip that distinction and regard a Muslim American as inherently foreign at the moment he seeks office.

The detector matters because it changes the terms of the election. It does not say El-Sayed has a policy disagreement with Trump. It says his presence itself is dangerous. It casts the Senate as a place that must be protected from him and asks Michigan voters to choose not between plans for their lives but between a familiar American and a foreign-looking one.

That is how anti-Muslim rhetoric becomes ordinary electoral architecture: first as theater at a rally, then as a test voters are invited to apply to a human being.

Trump’s core advantage is that fear is cheaper than arithmetic. If fuel prices remain high, blame those threatening the nation. If the war and inflation expose the limits of his record, discover another enemy. If a campaign cannot win on the economy, make opposition patriotism sound like conspiracy.

El-Sayed cannot erase his own controversies. He can make the contrast unmistakable: He is a former Detroit health official born in the United States, a Muslim American seeking a Senate seat to represent his state, not a hijacker headed for the chamber door.

On Nov. 3, Michiganders can reject the metal detector. They can hear what Trump is actually saying beneath the stage props: that a Muslim who wins a Senate seat is a security threat to the country he was born in. Bahbah believes voters will remember that on Election Day. They should.

Michigan does not need permanent emergency to decide this race. It needs only the nerve to look at the threat, name it for what it is, and refuse it. The president was holding the scanner, and asking the country to call that security. We can answer him plainly: A detector cannot measure loyalty. It can only reveal who has been taught to fear Muslims.
