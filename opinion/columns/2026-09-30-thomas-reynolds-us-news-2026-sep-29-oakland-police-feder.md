A federal judge just let Oakland Police police itself after twenty-three years of misconduct.

Twenty-three years of federal oversight of the Oakland Police Department—the longest such arrangement in American history—ended this week when the judge signed off on OPD’s release from court-ordered reform. The ruling does not prove that the department has cleaned up its act. It proves only that the court decided Oakland had been supervised long enough to govern itself.

That trust is unwarranted.

The riders scandal put the department under federal scrutiny in the first place. In the early 2000s, a group of Oakland officers was exposed for planting evidence, falsifying reports, and using excessive force, primarily against young Black men. The victims filed more than 100 civil lawsuits. In 2003, Oakland entered a court-ordered reform program with more than 50 required tasks, including data collection on racial profiling, faster complaint handling, and updated community-policing practices.

By 2012, OPD’s compliance was so unreliable that a federal judge took direct supervision. The monitor stayed because the department had not demonstrated that its own promises could substitute for independent enforcement.

Then came the trafficking case.

An investigation that began in 2015 found that several Oakland officers had sex-trafficked a teenage girl. The city paid her nearly $1 million in 2017. Nearly a dozen officers were fired or suspended. The case did not occur in some remote institutional past. It occurred while OPD was already operating under federal supervision.

The monitor’s work after that case mattered. Someone outside the department had to verify that its internal-accountability machinery actually functioned, rather than merely reporting that it did. Federal oversight supplied the external pressure that kept the case from disappearing into the department’s own machinery.

That machinery now has its independence back.

The Anti Police-Terror Project put the lesson plainly: “Police cannot police themselves. Oakland’s history has already taught us that lesson.” The group is right. The end of federal oversight does not mean the end of police misconduct. It does not erase the history that brought OPD under supervision. It does not mean Oakland should trust OPD to hold itself accountable.

A department that needed a federal monitor to count its stops and track its complaints is now its own monitor.

The city’s reform record was *uneven*. That is the polite word for *it did not take*. The federal court has ruled that Oakland has done its time. Oakland’s Black residents, the victims of the riders, the teenage trafficking survivor, and the families who filed those lawsuits did their time too. Those are not equivalent burdens. The department received twenty-three years of compelled scrutiny. The people harmed by its officers received the injuries, lawsuits, settlements, and years of waiting.

The distinction matters because the court’s decision is not a verdict that OPD has changed. It is a decision that OPD has been supervised long enough. A verdict on whether the department has changed would require evidence that twenty-three years of federal supervision produced durable accountability rather than procedural compliance.

The window was closing before the judge walked through it. In the spring, [Oakland police were already on track to exit federal oversight by September](/articles/2026-05-30-oakland-police-on-track-to-exit-federal-oversight-by-september/). That timeline was treated as a milestone of reform. It was also the timeline OPD spent much of its tenure trying to accelerate. The judge obliged.

What Oakland gets now is not accountability. It gets the appearance of accountability through an Office of Inspector General, a Citizens’ Police Review Board, and a Police Commission. Those bodies can matter. But each depends on institutional cooperation, access, and the ability to obtain records. The federal monitor was the mechanism that could compel a document and enforce a deadline under threat of contempt. The city’s oversight bodies do not possess the same power.

A monitor can leave. A department can announce compliance. A commission can hold a meeting. None of those facts establishes that the underlying power relationship has changed.

The same OPD that needed outside supervision to measure racial profiling is now expected to measure itself. The same department that produced the original riders scandal operated under court order when officers trafficked a teenager. The same internal-affairs bureaucracy, civil-service protections, and union contracts remain part of the system that Oakland is now asking the public to trust.

This is what opportunity looks like when nobody seizes it. For twenty-three years, Oakland had a federally supervised, court-enforced mechanism that could force the police to disclose what they were doing, remove officers who needed to be removed, and compensate people the department had harmed. The mechanism has been returned. The problems it was managing have not.

The next falsified report will not care that the consent decree ended. The next family whose complaint disappears will not care that OPD has met its reform tasks. The next Black resident beaten in custody will not experience the absence of a monitor as institutional progress. And if officers again exploit a teenager, the question will not be whether Oakland has a Police Commission. It will be who, if anyone, can compel the department to tell the truth.

The monitor left. The history did not.
