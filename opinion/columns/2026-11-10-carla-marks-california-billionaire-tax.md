The rejection of California’s billionaire wealth tax was not a verdict on whether wealth can be taxed; it was a verdict on what $333 million can buy. [Associated Press reported](https://apnews.com/article/california-billionaire-tax-4ae8cbdc057b99579818016f02ac9d9e) that voters rejected Proposition 40 on Nov. 3, the first-in-the-nation measure to impose a one-time 5% tax on Californians whose net worth exceeded $1 billion. The “no” side raised roughly ten times as much as the “yes” side. Building a Better California collected more than $333 million, Sergey Brin alone put more than $100 million into a committee working to defeat the measure, and [campaign filings](https://mainstreetindependent.com/articles/2026-11-10-campaign-filings-show-opposition-to-california-proposition-40-neared-425m/) put the broader opposition effort near $425 million. Private fortune, sitting on the scale.

And no, the money did not have to manufacture the opposition. A [PPIC poll](https://mainstreetindependent.com/articles/2026-11-03-ppic-poll-shows-52-of-likely-voters-opposing-proposition-40/) had already found 52% of likely voters opposed. That fact narrows the claim, which is good. Claims should survive narrowing. The money did not need to invent a majority. It only had to make that majority hear one argument at ten times the volume and make the case for the tax look like a reckless experiment before it could produce evidence. Health-care money does not get a lobbyist. Education and food assistance do not get to spend $333 million defending the budget line they were supposed to receive. Public service was made to compete with a private fortune for political voice.

Robert Swonger, an auto-glass technician living near Sacramento, put the honest voter dilemma plainly: “Obviously, I would like billionaires not to have as much money as they do. But I don’t believe taxing them out of existence is going to work.” That is not worship of wealth. It is a question about incentives.

Now, the opposition had a legitimate case to make. Wealth is not a paycheck. It is harder to value. Holdings can be illiquid. Residency rules can be gamed. California depends heavily on income taxes paid by its richest residents, and a badly designed tax could accelerate departures. A serious legislature would have to answer that. The coalition included Planned Parenthood and the California Medical Association. Gov. Gavin Newsom opposed the measure; Sen. Bernie Sanders campaigned for it. Voters can reject a proposal without every opponent having bought the result.

The problem is not that the “no” side raised money. The problem is what the money allowed it to avoid. It did not have to win the design argument. It could make the design argument irrelevant.

Walk me through the mass-exodus story slowly. The tax was 5%, once, on net worth above $1 billion. It did not take 5% from every paycheck. It reached only the narrowest class of balance sheets.

Some billionaires did begin cutting ties with California, so mobility is not imaginary. Brin and fellow Google co-founder Larry Page were among those who started loosening their ties with the state before the vote. Mark Zuckerberg, by contrast, made a deal with Meta that would help him pay the tax without losing his ownership stake if it passed.

Put those facts side by side and the “exodus” stops looking like an impersonal law of nature. Wealth could bargain. Some people cut ties. One negotiated how the tax would apply. The threat to leave belonged to the people best able to make leaving hurt California. That is a rather concentrated form of market behavior. Please note the asymmetry.

The campaign offered a warning, not a measurement. Nothing in the reported record turns the concern about departures into a demonstrated mass exodus. Meanwhile, the $100 billion that SEIU United Healthcare Workers West estimates California will lose in federal health-care funding over five years does not shrink because a ballot measure failed. It stays on the ledger.

Walk me through that ledger slowly. Proposition 40 would not have replaced every lost federal dollar. It did not have to. It would have put a California source of revenue behind services that federal cuts were already threatening. Most of the money would have backfilled federal health-care funding. Another 10% would have gone to education and food-assistance programs.

The opposition did not make the $100 billion disappear. It removed the vehicle designed to pay for it. The legislature still has the hole. It can now cut services, raise other taxes, or push more of the bill onto households.

The campaign had $333 million to keep the experiment from producing evidence. An experiment produces evidence. This campaign produced a conclusion with a media budget.

The campaign did not choose between spending money and not spending money. It chose who would not pay.

I’m not against markets. I am against turning a private fortune into a private veto over public policy. Those are different things. The first lets people make things and bargain over their prices. The second lets one person buy the rules for everyone else. The first is a market. The second is a gate with a tollbooth. One person, one vote is not the same thing as one dollar, one voice.

I grew up between two working answers to the question of who owns the essentials. On my father’s Minnesota side, a dairy cooperative and a rural electric cooperative made ownership something families entered themselves. At my mother’s family’s kitchen table in Gothenburg, health coverage was an ordinary Tuesday, not a ballot measure. California put a third version on the November ballot: private fortunes could buy political voice on a scale the public programs at stake could not match.

Those are not competing national destinies. They are proof that ownership, coverage and political voice can be arranged. The arrangement is never automatic. Somebody builds it.

So what do we build? Not another press release. A sequence.

First, make political voice less saleable. California should offer matching public funds for ballot campaigns under identical rules, tied to verified small-dollar contributions, with spending limits and no donor control over the message or the media time. A billionaire can buy more television. A billionaire should not be able to buy ten times the public square.

Then build a federal minimum tax on net worth above $100 million—the position Newsom supports—with workable collection and portability rules. A state levy can become an exit sign. A national floor makes it harder to improve your tax position merely by crossing a border. This does not prove Congress will pass such a measure, or that every dollar will be collectible. It means opposition to a revenue measure cannot also be treated as a substitute for building one.

Next comes automatic enrollment in health and food benefits, followed by a public option in health coverage. The expanded monthly Child Tax Credit proves one narrow point: automatic cash support can reach families. In 2021, child poverty fell 46%, from 9.7% to 5.2%, and 2.9 million children left poverty. When the expansion lapsed, hardship returned.

That receipt does not prove a public option can run, or that a wealth tax will pass. Those institutions still have to be built and tested. For the delivery side, Medicare supplies a separate operating receipt: the public can administer a universal payer for seniors. California can test a public option rather than making charity the substitute for coverage. Private insurers can still compete. Patients get a floor, and the cost of the alternative becomes visible instead of hiding inside medical bills.

A recurring hole should not depend on a one-time balance-sheet snapshot. Fund the public option over time with broad, recurring revenue. If voters later restore a wealth tax, it can be one instrument in a durable revenue mix rather than the whole structure. Give workers and patients real authority over the plan.

Then build bargaining power. Collective-bargaining coverage in America is about 11%. In Denmark it is above 80%, and Danish unions help administer unemployment insurance. We cannot simply copy Denmark. America does not have the same union density, organized employers or administrative capacity.

California can start closer to the ground. Hospital and home-care workers can negotiate standards across a sector rather than employer by employer, with a public care option and public campaign money giving workers something concrete to bargain around.

At the provider level, worker ownership offers another route. Cooperative Home Care Associates in the Bronx is a worker-owned home-care cooperative with roughly 2,000 workers. Its workers are owners, not a labor input assembled by distant shareholders. A California version would be harder to build. It would need patient capital—money willing to wait for care businesses to grow—along with procurement access and training. But the mechanism is visible. The people doing the work can govern the company.

The hard part is not inventing the institution. It is building the coalition. America has weaker unions, fragmented parties, less tax capacity and a federal system that lets every state become an exit route. The Nordic versions rest on decades of bargaining, organized employers and public capacity. California cannot air-drop that machinery or pretend a referendum substitutes for it.

Public matching funds will be hard because wealthy donors are organized and public institutions are not. A federal tax floor will be hard because collection and portability must work across state lines. A public option will be hard unless patients and workers have real power over it. Sectoral bargaining requires unions and employers capable of acting together. Worker ownership requires patient finance.

Those are reasons to build, not reasons to sneer and leave.

Together, these are not vague centrism. Public matching funds give ordinary citizens a louder voice. A federal tax floor makes residence less of an escape hatch. Automatic enrollment makes benefits usable. A public option gives patients a non-extractive provider to choose. Sectoral bargaining gives workers a hand in setting the rules. Worker ownership puts more of the value where the work happens. A wealth tax is then no longer the only possible way to finance the state. It becomes one instrument among accountable ones.

California made this choice. The economy is a set of choices, not the weather. It can make another. Start with public campaign money, a federal tax floor, enrollment and a public care option, sectoral bargaining and worker ownership. Let the ads be expensive. Let the argument be louder. Build the institutions, and the $333 million can buy television. It cannot buy the future. The wealthy will still get a vote. They will no longer get to make public capacity conditional on their private consent.
