*Americans got warnings. The bench got functioning institutions.*

The Trump administration’s immigration program has produced a genuine conservative victory. The share of asylum claims denied in immigration court rose from roughly 72 percent at the end of the Biden administration to 94 percent in June of the President’s second term. The administration also organized mass hearings, authorized military lawyers to serve as immigration judges, and gave judges wider authority to summarily dismiss claims. Mass hearings clear the docket; military lawyers furnish trained adjudicators; summary disposition ends claims that fail the statute without a full hearing. The machinery is faster, and the executive branch’s evidence sits closer to the legal question.

The conservative case begins with the statute. Under 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1), the statutory scheme requires an applicant to establish persecution or a well-founded fear of persecution on account of race, religion, nationality, political opinion, or membership in a particular social group; to show that the government cannot or will not protect the applicant; and to show that relocation within the country is unavailable. General hardship, economic motive, and undifferentiated danger do not by themselves supply those elements. The President has argued that many claims rest on poverty and economic opportunity rather than protected persecution. A sovereign may require proof without opening the border to general want.

Country-conditions reports have long assisted judges in applying those elements. The State Department’s travel advisory for Gracias a Dios, Honduras, warns Americans about violent crime, armed robbery, murder, and active terrorist groups. The background report prepared for immigration court says, “Overall, Gracias a Dios is a low-crime, functioning department.” For El Salvador, the court brief says the government’s response to rights abuses “is no different than that of developed countries in Europe and North America, including the United States itself.”

The instruments are distinguishable. The citizen advisory tells an American where not to travel; the court brief tells the judge where institutions function and where a petitioner might relocate. A gate may speak differently to a citizen and to one seeking entry. As MSI has [reported the divergence between the two systems](/articles/2026-11-25-state-department-asylum-briefs-present-a-safer-view-than-travel-advisories/), the shorter files were prepared for the bench rather than for an ordinary account of each country. They identify schools, hospitals, constitutional protections, police capacity, safer districts, and the availability of services. That is the evidence the statutory inquiry requires.

I learned in my father’s pew on Beacon Hill that a household can open a gate to a stranger without surrendering the whole house to him. Romans 13:1 says that governing authorities are ordained by God; Nehemiah’s wall had gates because a household must distinguish the traveler from the resident. Asylum, rightly understood, is that bounded welcome: particular protection for the person whom the state cannot or will not protect, not a general entitlement to enter for work or education. The State Department supplies the map; the statute supplies the boundary; the judge supplies the verdict.

At the same hearing where the State Department supplied its map, Lucía Martínez entered with two fractured ribs, a cut above her left eye, and her two daughters beside her. Lucía was thirty-six. Sofía, twelve, carried her school bag and Daniela’s lunch. Daniela, eight, left her breakfast untouched.

Before seeking asylum, Lucía lived in a town in Gracias a Dios. For years, her intimate partner beat her, threatened her with a gun, and drove her from one family home to another. She tried moving twice. The first time, gang members found her before she had been gone a week. The second time, her partner and his associates waited outside a relative’s house. Honduran police did not arrest the men. Lucía and the girls later reached the United States and entered the asylum process.

One afternoon the partner enters while the girl is outside and waits for her return. He beats Lucía until her mouth fills with blood, breaks two ribs, and leaves her on the concrete floor. The child hears the blows from the stairwell. When she enters, her mother cannot stand. The girl carries her to the bed. She does not loosen her mother’s blouse until she sees blood.

At the clinic, a clinician records two fractured ribs, bruising across Lucía’s lower ribs, pain with deep breathing, and restricted movement of her right arm. In the weeks that follow, the fractures knit, but the pain remains when she bends, reaches, or lifts anything above her chest. Her blood pressure stays elevated. She cannot sleep through a full night without waking when the weight of her body presses against the injuries.

Before each hearing, Lucía takes a third job in a food-processing plant. She stands for hours beside a conveyor, leaves before dawn to reach court, and cannot change shifts when the judge reschedules a hearing. She loses weight. Her clothing hangs loosely where her waistband used to fit. Her treating physician records continuing pain and restricted movement.

The girls carry school bags between apartments. Sofía changes her name at school because men threatened the family at their former address. She assumes care of Daniela during Lucía’s shifts when no neighbor can drive them. Their attendance is interrupted. They know which family will allow them to sleep on a floor when their mother cannot return. Before court dates, Daniela’s stomach closes around the breakfast she cannot eat.

The immigration file contains medical records, police reports, witness statements, school records, and the present locations of the men who threatened Lucía. The State Department submits its six-page country report. It describes functioning institutions, protected rights, good schools, operating hospitals, and districts where violent crime compares favorably with rates in many parts of the United States. The report counts the institutions. The applicant brings the body.

The State Department submitted the country report to the immigration judge. The judge asks whether Lucía faces persecution on a protected ground, whether Honduras can protect her, and whether she can relocate safely. Lucía names the town where gang members found her after her first move and the house where her partner’s associates waited after the second. The judge denies her claim.

The judge enters an order of removal. ICE officers transport Lucía and the girls to the airport and put them aboard a flight to Honduras. Lucía holds her right side against the seat. Each compression of the seat belt across the fractures makes her stop and breathe through her mouth. Daniela’s empty stomach turns; she vomits into a paper bag. Sofía wipes her sister’s mouth and keeps one hand on her mother’s wrist.

The aircraft lands at La Ceiba, the airport serving Gracias a Dios. At the arrivals gate, Sofía carries the bag because Lucía cannot lift it. Daniela grips her mother’s sleeve. Lucía’s broken ribs make each step a controlled breath. Honduran police have not arrested the man who broke them, and the gang that found her has not dispersed.

Nehemiah built a wall and gates because a household must judge at its threshold. Acts 17:26 assigns peoples their appointed periods and “the bounds of their habitation”; Romans 13:1 assigns judgment to the governing authority. The gate distinguishes the resident from the traveler.

Lucía and her daughters stand outside the covenant, outside the blessing, and outside full standing. Their condition—flight, dependency, children without a secure household, no covenantal incorporation—fixed the station assigned to them. Asylum asks the receiving household to extend a bounded protection; the receiving household did not confer standing.

Galatians 6:7 supplies the ledger: “whatever one sows, that he will also reap.” Lucía’s broken ribs, Daniela’s empty stomach, Sofía’s interrupted schooling, and the flight into Gracias a Dios are its entries. They disclose the standing of the condition that placed them beyond the wall. Providence has entered the verdict, and the account is closed upon the terms the household set.
