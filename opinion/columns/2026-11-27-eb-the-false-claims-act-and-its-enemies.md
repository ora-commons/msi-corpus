Charles Dickens had Jarndyce v. Jarndyce, and Hollywood had “Kramer vs. Kramer,” yet there is nothing strange about a federal court case that pits the United States against itself over fraud allegations. The strange part is what the government did next. This will take a little legal explaining, but in an opinion this month for the Fifth Circuit Court of Appeals, Judge Don Willett puts the whole enterprise on the constitutional chopping block.

To root out fraud against the government—public money stolen into private pockets—the False Claims Act (FCA) wisely lets whistleblowers file lawsuits on the United States’s behalf, called qui tam suits. If successful, the whistleblowers can get part of the money. That is a public safeguard, not a private windfall. The case at the Fifth Circuit involves Brook Jackson, who worked in 2020 at a company doing trials for a Covid vaccine. Ms. Jackson filed a range of allegations under the FCA. The listed plaintiff: the United States of America, at the behest of Brook Jackson—a citizen doing the public’s suing for it.

Eventually, the actual United States asked to intervene to dismiss the case, despite Ms. Jackson’s objections—the public’s own lawyer asking off a suit filed in its own name, an attempt to escape accountability. “In the Government’s view,” Judge Willett writes, “the FCA claims lacked merit, were based on ‘misinformation,’ and were inconsistent with public-health policy. The Government also anticipated that continued litigation would impose substantial discovery and oversight burdens.” Those labels are not a reason for the executive branch to bury a citizen’s claim. A district judge dismissed the case. After weighing the standards, the Fifth Circuit panel affirms. The Justice Department gets the quiet exit it requested.

But Judge Willett also has doubts about the constitutionality of the whole enterprise, as he lays out in a solo concurrence—and his questions rest on a dangerous premise: that the Government owns every claim filed in its name. “The caption above names the United States as the plaintiff. But the United States didn’t file the case, didn’t run the case, and couldn’t stop the case without a federal judge’s blessing,” Judge Willett says. True on every word—and every word is the point. A citizen brought the suit because a claim against the public purse belongs in court whether or not the public’s lawyers feel like showing up.

“Such suits may have a long pedigree, but pedigree doesn’t answer the fateful Article II question: Can Congress really deputize private citizens to prosecute the Nation’s claims in the Nation’s name—all outside the Government’s control?” The answer is yes, and the pedigree is part of the answer. Congress can deputize citizens to enforce the Nation’s claims, and executive control cannot erase the people’s right to bring them. The Fifth Circuit rejected such concerns in 2001, and rightly. As Judge Willett points out, however, “the Supreme Court has since sharpened its Article II doctrine on the exercise and supervision of executive power.” Supervision is not monopoly, however sharply the doctrine is stated. The old ruling did not mistake one for the other. The Government’s motion to dismiss did. The Act still stands, and so does Congress’s answer.

Three Justices have wondered aloud in recent years whether the FCA’s mechanism has a constitutional problem. They have it backwards. Wondering aloud is free; a ruling against the Act would be a signed pardon for every swindler holding a government contract. The greater constitutional danger is the Government that would monopolize enforcement in the Nation’s name. If they or other appeals judges want more food for thought, Judge Willett’s concurrence offers 23 instructive pages. Read them as a warning. The public needs more citizens willing to bring fraud claims. This docket has exactly one.
