The United States abandoned the climate talks and left the bill to poorer countries.

Saturday morning I sat the bow stand on the north ridge and the rut had not turned on. The green notebook that lives in the truck got its line anyway. I write the first hard chase down every fall, twelve years running, and most falls this decade it has come late. This one is late too. Two winters ago, on the same bank where I write the Petenwell ice-out every spring, I put the earliest date in the book — eleven days earlier than the first entry, same water, same bank. The rut has slid two weeks later than it ran when my grandfather was still hanging stands in these woods. Some Marches now bring the first mosquitoes; they used to wait for May. I have never been to a climate conference and I won't pretend otherwise. But that page is what the fire looks like from Adams County. The negotiators opening COP31 on Monday in Antalya, Turkey, are meeting inside the same fire to argue about a number, and the buck movement I didn't see off that ridge is my copy of the same number. The country that put more of it into the sky than anyone else will not be in the room.

The number is bad in a specific direction. The world sits at 1.4 degrees Celsius of warming above preindustrial times, and the United Nations puts the current track at 2.6. Eleven years ago the track pointed at 4 degrees by century's end. It points at 2.6 now — progress, and nowhere near enough. In 2015 the nations set a goal of holding warming under 2 degrees, with 1.5 preferred; two months ago the U.N. readjusted the 1.5 goal because the world is passing through it, not approaching it. "We have failed so far," said Johan Rockström, director of the Potsdam Institute for Climate Impact Research. The failing is no longer a forecast: heat waves like the ones that killed in Europe this summer, flooding like the kind that drowned Nepal. Rockström's forecast runs further out — more frequent floods, droughts and storms, and tipping points: ocean currents shifting, the Amazon rainforest going over.

Wisconsin keeps the same ledger in its own handwriting. The state has warmed about 3 degrees since 1950, fastest in winter, per the Wisconsin Initiative on Climate Change Impacts, and the 2010s were the wettest decade in the state's record. One of the longest ice records on the continent — Lake Mendota's, kept continuously since 1855 — runs roughly a month shorter than it did in the middle of the nineteenth century.

The empty chair is not a symbol. It is a hole in the ledger. The United States is the world's largest historic carbon polluter; no other country has loaded the sky with more. It re-withdrew from the Paris agreement in January 2025. It stayed home from last year's talks in Brazil. It is not expected in Antalya either. And the business this year was supposed to be follow-through: making good on the promise to transition away from fossil fuels, and moving money to the poorer countries that have to live with warming they did least to cause.

Niklas Höhne, co-founder of the NewClimate Institute, said these negotiations always come down to two hard questions — who produces the emissions, and who pays. On the second one, he said, "we are particularly in a difficult situation because the U.S., as the largest contributor, has dropped out. And there's no other player who can jump in." Mohamed Adow, director of Power Shift Africa, put the unfairness plainly — Nepal faces catastrophic destruction while "the world's wealthiest country and biggest polluter sits on the fence" — and he did not soften the ending: "A time will come when we will have to hold the U.S. accountable for the climate harms that it's caused." Then this fall the U.N.'s own inventory found [U.S. emissions rose while the global total held steady](/articles/2026-10-21-u-n-report-finds-u-s-emissions-rose-while-global-total-held-steady/). A country doing that isn't just missing the meeting. It is widening the tab while it misses. The country most responsible for the fire walked out of the firefighters' meeting, and its smoke went up.

Down here we already know the shape of that bill, because the county has been holding pieces of it for years. The wells on the south side have tested over the nitrate standard — one neighbor's nitrate at 14 milligrams per liter last spring against the public-health limit of 10, numbers published by the county's Land and Water Conservation Department and written up in the Adams County Times-Reporter. The Wisconsin DNR lists Petenwell as impaired — mercury and phosphorus both. The water moving through this sand in a wet decade ends up in wells like those. A bill like that does not disappear when the biggest payer walks out. It moves. It moves onto counties that did not set the policy and cannot refuse it.

Chapter 16 of *We Too* gives the pattern its name — the Nationalist Shell Game — and Antalya is running it at full scale. The flag talks about defending the countryside; the incumbents write what the policy is. The administration has its own name for the arrangement: "energy dominance." Bill Hare, who runs Climate Analytics, said it clean: "What's standing in the way is not so much technology as governments and incumbent industries," and "one cannot underestimate the power and influence of the fossil fuel industry in slowing down action." The U.N. estimates fossil-fuel subsidies hit $1.1 trillion globally this year, which means the public is still buying the arsonist his fuel. The doubt playbook is on paper too. In 1998 the American Petroleum Institute's own strategy memo, leaked to the New York Times, announced that "Victory will be achieved when average citizens 'understand' uncertainties in climate science" — manufactured doubt straight off the tobacco template, and it ran for a generation while the ice went out earlier every spring.

Meanwhile the bill keeps coming due at pumps like the one at the co-op in Friendship. This year the United States and Israel bombed Iran; the fighting closed the Strait of Hormuz; fossil fuel prices spiked, because the diesel in my 2014 Silverado is priced off a market that runs through that water, and so is the LP tank behind the shop. That is the contact test the "energy independence" slogan never survives. The United States has been a net petroleum exporter since 2019, and crude production has set records under both parties' watch — neither one earned the credit it claimed, because production follows leases drilled years back and prices set in Riyadh as much as anywhere. The selective energy-independence frame is the name for the slogan that survives anyway: state the true half, we produce a lot, and stop exactly where the pump price would contradict it. [World leaders at Climate Week](/articles/2026-09-21-world-leaders-at-climate-week-link-fossil-fuels-to-wars-and-economic-risk/) were already connecting fossil fuels to wars and economic risk back in September; the shock made their point for them. A world that runs on fuel it has to fight over is a world that fights. None of this lets me off. The truck burns diesel, the shop furnace burns LP, and I filled both this month.

The talks themselves are built to dodge the subject. Coal, oil and gas cause the warming, and the final documents almost never name them, because final documents require consensus and the industry's friends in the room only have to say no once — a rule that has kept the source of the problem unnamed year after year. Fifty-six countries met separately in April to push a direct phaseout. Brazil, which hosted last year's conference, published a road map this month laying out how countries could actually move, and Ana Toni, who ran those talks, named its first step: stop the subsidies. Meanwhile the world is not switching energy sources, it is stacking them — renewables growing faster than ever alongside fossil fuels that keep growing right along, because data centers running artificial intelligence and plain old growth want every watt they can get. Adding clean energy over fossil energy is not the same as replacing it. "Unless and until we resolve the issue of fossil fuel phase out, we will not be able to deal with the problem," as Harjeet Singh of the Satat Sampada Climate Foundation put it.

Wendell Berry spent a career on what comes after extraction, and the mechanism is in *The Unsettling of America*: a community's economy is its membership, and the extractive economy consumes the membership before it consumes the resource. Strip the acronyms off a climate conference and that is what it is — the world's membership meeting to settle who pays for what got taken. Every pledge on the books was signed in that room, and the country that owes the most has stopped attending the meeting where its own signature is kept. A country can fly any flag it likes. Quitting the meeting is quitting the membership, and the countryside the flag claims to defend is next in line for the extraction.

None of this is a technology problem, and the price tag proves it. "It has never been as easy to reduce emissions than it is today because, again, this year, renewable energy has been developing faster than before," Höhne said. The part nobody tells from Washington is that rural America already won this argument once, with wires — and in a county the market has always rated marginal, we have run this experiment before, and it worked. In the mid-1930s fewer than 11 percent of American farms had electricity; the investor-owned utilities looked at the customer density on roads like County Z and decided rural America wasn't worth the wire. The Rural Electrification Act lent cheap, long money to member-owned cooperatives instead, and the co-ops ran the wire anyway; by 1950 most American farms had power. Adams-Columbia Electric Cooperative, headquartered in Friendship, is now the largest rural electric cooperative in Wisconsin, and my shop has lights because of a statute signed before my grandfather rebuilt his first engine. That same arithmetic is the transition's arithmetic now. Turkey's co-host plan — a sideline initiative, because the sidelines are where work happens when consensus is held hostage — would lift electricity's share of global energy use from 21 percent to 35 percent by 2035, preferably solar and wind; Turkey spent the pre-summit calling the climate crisis [the security issue](/articles/2026-10-09-at-fiji-pre-summit-kurum-calls-climate-crisis-the-security-issue/), and this plan is what that sounds like as wiring. That treats cheap electrons as a floor, not a ceiling. Electrifying what currently burns — trucks, shop heat, grain dryers, the water heater in the barn — is the move at planetary scale.

The last Congress funded exactly this: the New ERA program put roughly $9.7 billion on the table for rural electric cooperatives, and Dairyland Power, the generation cooperative for much of western and central Wisconsin, was among those selected. Then this administration ordered the climate money paused for review, and the federal loan office reportedly answers to a new name now: "Energy Dominance Financing." The affirmative fits in three sentences: stop paying the incumbents to keep burning, which means stop buying the arsonist his fuel; run the wires and set the renewables, because they are the cheapest power in history and getting cheaper; pay the money already promised to the countries absorbing damage they did least to cause, and wire the transition out to the counties the investors skip. The argument against wiring Adams County in 2026 is the argument the utilities made about copper in the 1930s, and it was wrong then too. We already have the rebuttal strung on our own poles.

Ashley Lashley, a youth activist from Barbados whose first of these conferences was five years ago, said the science keeps getting more alarming while "politically we seem to be negotiating how much failure we are prepared to tolerate." That is the sentence to carry out of Antalya, and it is the sentence to set beside Aldo Leopold, who wrote out of these same sand counties that conservation is "a state of harmony between men and land." The land is not negotiating a tolerable amount of failure. The land keeps its own record.

Adow expects an accounting, someday, for the climate harms this country has caused. From Adams County I'd add only this: the harms are already in the local record, and none of them require a negotiation to verify. My kids are eight and five. The question the negotiators gathering in Antalya won't settle — who pays — gets answered one way or another, in budgets if it is done on purpose and in shorter winters and thinner ice if it isn't. My notebook doesn't pass resolutions. It keeps dates, twelve years of them, every one off the same bank, and the record gets worse without asking anyone's permission. The negotiators in Antalya will keep minutes. Someday the two records have to reconcile, and one of them is already worse. Mike and Quinn will inherit whichever record the country decides to believe. I would like them to inherit the county too. The ice on Petenwell doesn't care how the conference ends. It has gone out eleven days earlier, on my record, than the first winter I wrote the date down, and come March I will write it down again. That entry nobody walks out on.
