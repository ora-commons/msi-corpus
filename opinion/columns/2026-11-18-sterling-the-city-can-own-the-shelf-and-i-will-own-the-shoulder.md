Oleta is a 54-year-old pallet in the stockroom of the discount grocery I own outside Bowling Green, Kentucky, and I win grocery price wars by making shoulders cheaper than machinery. A pallet is what lifts when the powered lift doesn’t come. The powered lift doesn’t come because her shoulder came free. Her right shoulder grinds when the cooler door swings. It has ground since March. The can of green beans on your shelf and the jar of peanut butter in your cabinet come through that shoulder. Nothing for 40 miles is cheaper because of her. James Freeman writes in *The Wall Street Journal*’s [*Another Cautionary Tale for MamdaniMarts*](https://www.wsj.com/opinion/another-cautionary-tale-for-mamdanimarts-9c0a2b9a?mod=rss_opinion) that taxpayer-backed city-owned grocery stores are due to launch next year, that the Prairie Food Co-Op struggled after trying nearly everything except operating as an ordinary profit-seeking business, and that public subsidies might let city-owned grocers undercut private ones. Freeman has the discipline right. A grocer who keeps his profit listens to the customer. I listen too. You say price. I hear labor cost. My margin has never once listened to her shoulder. God blesses the shopper who hunts the lowest tag. My 38 stores stand inside that blessing.

The city can issue the subsidy. I can bid on its distribution contract. If the mayor wants the 2-liter jug below my cost, I lower my cost. The powered lift-assist for Oleta’s aisle cost $74. I declined it. The hoist rail above her station would add $0.0029 a case when amortized across the volume it serves. Take the number slowly; it’s small on purpose.

$0.0029.

That’s the entire distance between my shelf tag and the chain out on the highway. I didn’t buy the rail. I wouldn’t buy it the morning the city cuts a ribbon across town. Read the next sentence twice.

Her shoulder doesn’t appear on the tag.

The tag is the only inspection my industry has ever answered to, and it leads me to questions I’d put to you, if you’ll sit for them.

Then tell me what you actually say at the shelf. You say price. You say nothing else, because there is no box on the survey for the sound a shoulder makes at 5 a.m. The tag audits 1 line. The can is priced in the stockroom. You’ve followed every step, and each one was yours. Thank you for that.

The notion that her shoulder should enter the pricing is a sentimentality I paid to have removed, and it was worth every cent. In my accounting, she is a carrying cost. That’s the polite phrase for a 54-year-old woman whose shoulder grinds when the cooler door swings. Her shoulder is a maintenance entry now. It has no purchase order attached. I prefer the accounting terms because auditors ask follow-up questions over lunch, and lunch runs long.

900 cases come off the 5 a.m. truck, and her station takes the heavy ones.

$2.61 a morning.

Amortized across them, that is what the rail costs me. I keep the $2.61. I would keep it the day of the ribbon-cutting. It isn’t her I enjoy; it’s that the column balances. Every price war pays for itself if you know where the margin hides.

Move down the wall to my freezer and meet Garnett; same law, colder shirt. His insulated gloves run $11.50 a pair.

The cotton pair runs $1.75.

I stock the cotton pair and keep $9.75 a hand, every winter, on every freezer hand I’ve employed. His fingertips go white by hour 2 and burn when they warm. Hands come back. There’s another at the door by 6. In my ledger, he’s a cold-chain cost. Anyway.

The same arithmetic crosses the distribution yard: Nell is a 19-year-old shelf unit at the warehouse I own outside Newark, New Jersey. Oleta grinds a shoulder; Nell’s hand goes numb before lunch. A milk case weighs 24 pounds. Her shoulder climbs toward her ear as she lifts.

$0.002 a case.

That’s what I keep when public money lowers the shelf price and labor gets cheaper for me. The 2-liter jug on your counter comes from that margin. Her hand stays numb. The public price can be lower than my cost. That is how I lower my cost.

Nell has 47 cases left before the shift ends. She lowers the final case, then holds her right arm above her shoulder until the shift ends.

Widen the frame, because here’s the part worth the lesson. Markets are wonderfully responsive when the adjustment lands on somebody other than the owner. What do you call a system that converts a public price into a private injury? Market discipline becomes maintenance discipline. A taxpayer-backed shelf price doesn’t make milk cheaper to produce. It decides whose body pays for the difference. The public store gets the customer. I get the body.

A store without market discipline has never worried me. The law is older than the supermarket. It does not come from the shopper. It comes from ownership, and Scripture was franker than our benefit memos. Mark the verse, because it managed a shoulder 3,000 years before Kentucky had one.

Oleta returned in April. The unit returned to the line, so there is no claim. Scripture filed her under property. My carrier files her under return-to-work. Between the 2, the case closed itself.

As Dean of Instruction at Warden University, I have graded managers into this arithmetic for 30 years. I am not unusual. I am a curriculum. The buyer who sets your peanut butter tag sat in my cost seminar at Warden in 2019. He found the stockroom before I finished teaching it. He now sets the labor rule for the chain that decides when Oleta’s shift starts and what the tag reads when you reach his register. You’ve never met him, though you’ve met his number. I didn’t sign his prices. I certified him.

Another graduate sets the piece rate for the stocking work behind the milk in your cart. Another sits on a grocery board and models public grocery programs as guaranteed demand. They run the subtraction faster than I do, and I say that with a teacher’s pride. That is not a store. It is a course catalog with payroll authority. A curriculum doesn’t retire merely because its dean does.

To hold the shelf share, I cut the warehouse’s starting wage by $0.07 an hour. Nell keeps the same pace. Her shoulder keeps the same climb toward her ear. The lower wage trims the household budgets that would buy the milk she stocks. I didn’t commission the household-income study. The dispatch schedule is fuller through June.

The June schedule looks excellent.

MamdaniMarts can undercut my stores. Good. If they take my shoppers, my milk has fewer buyers. In 3 years, the mayor will meet his own Garnett inside a freezer line. His stores will need cold-chain inventory, and inventory needs hands. The arithmetic crosses itself somewhere between his subsidy and my wage bill. His food-access study is in the binder behind my desk. The binder is still shut. The June dispatch schedule is full. I have other work.

Oleta restocks the green beans at 5:40, before you arrive. At 8, the inspection opens. The shelf looks full. The inspection is you. You’ll hear the scanner, and the scanner says the price. It has never once said her name. Thank you for the subsidy. Truly. The city couldn’t have arranged this without you. Cheap doesn’t care who signs the checks. That is what winning a price war means. Not every store standing. The margin standing.

I’ve never fought a price war I didn’t win, because I don’t fight on the shelf. I fight where the tag gets made.

Mind the tag.
