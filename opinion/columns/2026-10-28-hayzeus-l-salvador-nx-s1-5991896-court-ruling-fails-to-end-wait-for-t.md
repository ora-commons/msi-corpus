The State Department’s screening regime keeps thousands of immigrant applicants waiting abroad.

In August, a federal judge struck down the Trump administration’s ban on processing immigrant visas for citizens of 75 countries. The State Department says the ban is no longer in effect and that the matter remains in litigation. It declined to comment further.

Those are distinct propositions. The court invalidated the policy. The department says the formal ban has ended. Attorneys representing migrants make a stronger allegation: that the administration is not complying with the ruling and is instead slow-walking thousands of cases. No final court finding reported here establishes deliberate obstruction. I do not need to pretend that the intent question is settled to judge the effect: continued training and added screening are keeping applicants abroad. That is a de facto suspension of the visa ban, whatever label the department uses.

The ban began in January. By the time it took effect, more than 280,000 immigrants had applied for visas. Some had already received approval and were waiting for the physical document. Others could not complete the interviews required to leave their countries. The State Department continued to demand additional evidence that these families could live in the United States without becoming a “public charge.”

A Cato Institute analysis found that the policy trapped thousands of close family members of U.S. citizens and permanent residents, including spouses and minor children. It called the ban “one of the most extreme legal immigration policies in U.S. history.”

The Hoffman family has been trapped inside that machinery for nearly a year.

Chaya Hoffman is five years old and autistic. Her 12-year-old stepbrother, Israel, is in Argentina with her. Their father, Dustin Hoffman, is a U.S. Army veteran who moved to Argentina in 2013 after a deployment in Iraq. He began paperwork late last year to secure a visa for his Colombian wife, Gloria, so the family could live together in the United States.

Argentina did not offer the educational resources and opportunities Hoffman wanted for Chaya. The visa was meant to bring the family together. The ban halted the process. Even after the judge struck it down, the State Department continued requesting additional evidence that the Hoffmans could support themselves without becoming a public charge. They were told to provide further proof of their financial means.

Nearly a year later, Gloria remains in Argentina.

Hoffman works as an Uber driver and tries to pay a mortgage in the United States while paying rent in Argentina. Soaring gas prices during the war in Iran have cut into an already squeezed income. He misses his daughter, stepson, and wife every day. Chaya cries “all the time” and asks for her father. Hoffman’s doctors have increased his psychiatric medication.

“We’re living on a dream and a prayer right now,” he said.

Then a judge struck down the ban. Gloria Hoffman remained in Argentina.

Jesus said, “I was a stranger and you did not welcome me.” He also said, “Truly I tell you, just as you did it to one of the least of these who are members of my family, you did it to me.” The Hoffmans do not need that sentence explained to them. A child asks for her father. An interview remains unfinished. Another document arrives. This system is not welcoming strangers and then failing them. It is keeping them from arriving while the welcome is declared.

In August, the department said it had paused visa processing while launching an extensive training program for embassies and consulates on identifying immigrants who might become burdens on taxpayers. Even immigrants whose visas had already been approved faced additional screening.

A former State Department consular officer told NPR that she had never seen a substantial pause in visa processing. She believed the slowdown was driven directly by presidential policy.

Whatever the department calls this, it is not routine processing. For these families, the cruelty is administered one document at a time. Training may tell consular officers what to look for. It does not tell a waiting family when its interview will occur. I do not accept training as compliance.

The department can request additional evidence, leave interviews unfinished, and let the queue grow without offering the families a date for relief. The formal ban is gone; the discretionary choke point remains.

This policy also collides with the administration’s own rhetoric about the immigrants it wants. During his first term, President Trump said he wanted to attract “the brightest” immigrants to the United States. In March 2026, the America First Policy Institute urged the country to prioritize immigrants with education, skills, and well-paying jobs waiting for them here.

Dr. Fernando Lizcano is precisely the kind of immigrant that rhetoric celebrates. A Colombian national, he researches major cardiovascular-health challenges in the United States, including obesity. He applied for an EB-1A visa, reserved for people of “extraordinary ability,” including an “outstanding professor or researcher.” USCIS deemed him qualified.

Lizcano intended to continue his academic biomedical research in the United States, drawing on his experience in endocrinology, molecular biology, and translational research. All that remained was final State Department approval and a visa stamp in his passport.

Instead, he remains in Bogotá.

He is the immigrant the administration says it wants. USCIS judged that his extraordinary abilities qualify him for entry. Then the State Department left him waiting while it trained officials to find more reasons not to let him in. If this is a merit-based system, the merits have already been judged. The government has simply decided that its own timetable outranks the applicant’s future.

My own tradition has to answer too. Catholic parishes and charities in this country have fed and sheltered migrants while some employers have benefited from workers kept legally precarious and divided from their families. Too many church leaders kept silent while public politics sorted some migrants into deserving welcome and others into disposable enforcement.

I have preached that we are one family while relying on undocumented neighbors’ labor in my parish and community and treating their exposure as Washington’s problem rather than mine. That confession carries a present obligation. I will learn the names of undocumented workers in my parish and shop, stand with them when agencies demand papers or threaten removal, refuse employers the chance to use their status to break them, and bring their cases before the people in my pews who employ workers and vote for the officials who write the rules.

Vice President JD Vance did something similar when he invoked the Catholic idea of *ordo amoris* in January 2025 to arrange obligations: family, community, country, and then the rest of the world. On February 10, Pope Francis rejected that reading in his [message to the bishops of the United States on their National Day of Prayer and Fasting for Peace](https://www.vaticannews.va/en/holy-father/francesco/2025/02/messages/bishops-usa-day-prayer-fasting-peace.html): “Christian love is not a concentric expansion of interests that little by little extend to other persons and groups.” The true *ordo amoris*, Francis wrote, is “a love that builds a fraternity open to all, without exception.”

That correction is not an ornament. It gives us a test.

If the nation claims family as its first moral obligation, the Hoffman family cannot be left in Argentina because a visa process takes longer than the family can bear. If the nation claims to select the brightest immigrants, Lizcano cannot be praised as exceptional while the State Department withholds final approval after USCIS has already qualified him. If the nation invokes Christ, it must treat neither family as disposable.

The administration’s conduct and Vance’s misuse of Catholic teaching are separate wrongs. Neither excuses the other. The theology must reach the consulate as firmly as it reaches the campaign trail.

The humane policy is plain: timely interviews abroad; decisions on family unity and professional merit; no added barrier surviving the policy the court struck down; and a route home for those already approved.

The fix cannot be another agency explanation or another legal delay. The State Department should publish a case-by-case compliance plan, schedule the pending interviews, issue visas already approved, withdraw added screening demands imposed because of the halted policy, and put its position before the court. If officials believe the judge’s ruling does not require immediate action, they should make that argument to the judge—not turn “training” into a private suspension of the law.

Families such as the Hoffmans do not need another debate over what America’s immigration system should value. They need an interview, a passport stamp, and a government willing to treat a court order as an order.

Romero told soldiers ordered to kill their own brother peasants that conscience must govern obedience and cried, “¡Cese la persecución!” Stop the repression. The State Department can hear that command without a theological argument. You have the court order, the pending interviews, and the approved visas. Schedule the interviews. Issue the visas. Make your legal arguments to the judge, not to families trapped abroad.

August did not end the trap. It renamed the lock. In Argentina, Chaya still asks for her father. The law has opened the door. Until the State Department walks through it, the Hoffman family remains on the wrong side of the ruling.
