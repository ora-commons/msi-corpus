Donald Trump hounded Kilmar Abrego Garcia out of the country he called home.

On Tuesday, Kilmar Abrego Garcia [left for Spain](/articles/2026-11-24-kilmar-brego-garc-a-is-leaving-the-u-s-for-spain-voluntarily/), according to his lawyers and administration officials. Sen. Chris Van Hollen, who led the fight for him in the Senate, said he was traveling “to a country of his choice, a free man, unharmed and with dignity.” The Department of Homeland Security announced the departure in capital letters: “We are DELIGHTED to announce that Kilmar Abrego Garcia has self-deported from the United States to Spain.” In the same post, Secretary Markwayne Mullin called a man whose criminal case a judge had dismissed a gang member and a human trafficker without evidence. Abrego Garcia kept his dignity. His government is delighted to be rid of him. The whole policy lives inside those two facts.

We have to say this plainly because our own communities have not been outside this. Some of us have benefited from undocumented workers’ labor and tax contributions while other immigrant families bore the raids, the detentions, and the fear that made that labor possible. Earlier administrations built much of this machine, and other governments have built versions of it in their own laws and languages. That does not make this administration’s conduct less cruel, and it does not make our own complicity less real. Our standard cannot change with the party in power: no removal without due process, no accusation of criminality without evidence, no pressured departure renamed as a free choice.

Here is the record. In 2019, an immigration judge ruled that Abrego Garcia could not be deported to El Salvador after he argued that he would face torture or persecution there. In 2025, immigration officers arrested him and sent him to a notorious Salvadoran prison, where he says he suffered beatings and sleep deprivation. Later, immigration officials said he had been deported by mistake because of an administrative error. They brought him back to the United States in June 2025. I call that a betrayal. The government removed a man the court had protected from return to El Salvador, then called the wrong a clerical error. The error was administrative. The cruelty was institutional.

Upon his return, the government charged Abrego Garcia with human smuggling. Prosecutors said body-camera footage from a 2022 Tennessee traffic stop showed him with several other men inside his SUV. They claimed he was a human trafficker moving immigrants from Houston to Maryland. Abrego Garcia denied the allegations. His lawyers argued that the prosecution was retaliation after his wrongful deportation drew national attention. A judge dismissed the charges. The first deportation broke the judge’s ruling. Everything after it was a choice, and the choices were cruel.

Secretary Mullin, the court dismissed the case, and you announced the accusation anyway. That was not evidence. It was not enforcement. It was official power reaching past the court to repeat an accusation it had not proved. You can enforce immigration law. What you cannot do is make a criminal accusation the substitute for the proof the court demanded. You can still correct the record. Do not let his departure leave the unsupported accusations standing. A person can change course; an institution can too. You are still held to the truth.

The sequence matters. Abrego Garcia had said he was willing to move to Costa Rica, and his attorneys said the country had offered him refugee status. The administration did not support that plan. It sought to send him to Liberia instead, until [the courts blocked that transfer](/articles/2026-10-06-abrego-garcia-in-court-tuesday-to-block-liberia-deportation/). Then Spain was the exit he took. He chose the way a man chooses when every better door has been locked. His departure was legally voluntary. It was not free. A choice made while deportation proceedings pressed against a person was not the freedom of an ordinary traveler. The designation of self-deportation let officials applaud the result of their own campaign and deny their part in producing it. Spain is not the homecoming. It does not restore the justice the government denied him.

Adam Goodman’s ledger on the deportation machine shows its quietest gear. Over the past century, roughly eight million formal removals were recorded, along with some fifty million departures treated as “voluntary.” In March 2025, the government rebranded its CBP One application as “CBP Home” to process self-deportation; by May, it was offering a $1,000 voluntary-departure stipend. The machine is older than the men now running it. Congress attached employer sanctions to legalization in 1986. The laws of 1996 expanded deportation grounds retroactively and sharply restricted judicial review. Title 42 operated across administrations from March 2020 to May 2023. It grew with the votes and the silence of people like me: Catholics in pews, Sunday after Sunday.

I grew up in El Paso watching classmates vanish after pre-dawn raids, and the school day went on as if an empty desk were an ordinary thing. That ordinary thing is what this administration exploits, and my own communities helped build it. Few of us will ever be charged for any of it. All of us are answerable for it.

I keep a different set of records on Sunday mornings. “When an alien resides with you in your land, you shall not oppress the alien,” Leviticus says. “The alien who resides with you shall be to you as the citizen among you; you shall love the alien as yourself, for you were aliens in the land of Egypt: I am the Lord your God.” That commandment runs through the Torah more often than any other. It does not come with a paperwork exemption. And Jesus asks what was done to the stranger: “I was a stranger and you did not welcome me.” The answer to Jesus’s question is written in the government’s own record. Hold the text beside the conduct. A man whom the court had barred from removal to El Salvador was seized, imprisoned where he says he was beaten, prosecuted when he returned, denied the refuge he sought, and driven across an ocean. Isaiah named the moment when a people calls such a thing good: “Woe to those who call evil good and good evil.”

What is owed here is specific. A humane country obeys its courts the first time; it does not negotiate a man’s refuge down from Costa Rica to Liberia; it does not drive a man from his family and then celebrate the driving. The bishops of the United States and Mexico wrote the standard in 2003: the right to find opportunity at home, the right to migrate when home fails, the right of nations to control their borders, protection for refugees and asylum seekers, and dignity for undocumented migrants that no status erases. Any administration of either party can be measured against that standard, this one no less than its predecessors. A just immigration system requires a real hearing before removal, no removal to a country shown to be unsafe, preservation of the right to seek asylum, protection of family unity, and border rules that do not turn a human being into material for political applause. When a government has wronged a man this completely, it owes him the road home.

To the Secretary, to the agents who carried him out, and to every official who chose removal over due process: I know you have your reasons, and I know you are people with families of your own. So hear the Archbishop of San Salvador, who stood in his cathedral the day before they shot him and spoke to soldiers who were also sons of their own people. No one is obligated to obey an order against the law of God. Romero pleaded, he implored, and then he commanded in the name of God: “les suplico, les ruego, les ordeno: ¡cese la represión!” I plead, I beg, I order you: stop the repression. Your orders mostly arrive as paperwork, and that is exactly the temptation—a cruelty that comes to you as a form to process. You can decline it. Abrego Garcia’s lawyers expect the next administration to reunify the family and bring him home. If they are right, the doors will be open. If they are wrong, the doors stay open anyway.

This morning I repaired a hinge before I sat down to write. A door either opens or it does not; there is no third thing. The work is as plain as that: tell the truth about what was done, keep the door open on our side, and repair what we broke, one broken thing at a time. *Mañana* is tomorrow, and tomorrow comes every morning. I leave the parish door unlatched. The man is gone for now. The door is not. A country that jailed a man after a court barred his removal to El Salvador has confused cruelty with strength.
