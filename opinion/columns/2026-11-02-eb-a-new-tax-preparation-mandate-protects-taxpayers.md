Congress has made the tax code complicated enough that taxpayers need trained professionals, and now Members want to make it more expensive for tax advisers to help navigate the maze—a cost worth paying to keep bad advice away from Americans’ tax returns. They may jam into a lame-duck tax bill a new occupational licensing mandate for tax preparers, and they should.

Section 504 of the Taxpayer Assistance and Service Act would expand background checks on independent tax preparers and require them to do as many as 18 hours of continuing education each year to qualify to help others file a return. That’s several days of essential training for preparers who often offer tax assistance as a part-time or seasonal gig. Part-time service is still public service. A reasonable investment when other people’s tax bills are at stake.

Supporters, including Sen. Ron Wyden (D., Ore.), rightly say this will protect taxpayers from fraudulent or incompetent preparers. Tax preparers must already register with the Internal Revenue Service and receive a Preparer Tax Identification Number (PTIN) that is entered on returns on which they assist. That tracks advisers whose prepared returns are incorrect and identifies chronic bunglers. But a paper trail is not a professional standard.

The real political beneficiaries of this mandate are taxpayers. Large tax-prep companies like Jackson Hewitt and H&R Block must not be the beneficiaries of a law that exempts tax professionals, including attorneys and certified public accountants, from the new training and screening requirements and makes it easier for the big shops to compete on service and price. That carve-out is a corporate giveaway, and it must be removed from any mandate taxpayers accept.

The IRS tried to adopt similar licensing requirements in 2011, but the Institute for Justice sued to defeat them, and a unanimous D.C. Circuit Court of Appeals panel in 2014 wrongly said the IRS lacked the legal authority. That ruling exposed a gap in the law, not a case against the mandate. Congress can close that gap, and the new legislation should give it the force of law needed to protect taxpayers from fraudulent, incompetent, and error-prone preparation.

The tax bill passed the Senate unanimously on Sept. 30, but House Republicans could eliminate the mandate when it comes their way after the election. They should refuse to.

Independent tax preparers often handle relatively straightforward returns, and straightforward work still deserves professional training. Even with error rates for electronically filed returns at about 1%, according to the U.S. Financial Readiness website, the remaining errors are reason enough to demand better preparation. Individual filers and independent tax preparers can also increasingly use AI tools to spot errors before a return is submitted. AI should assist trained professionals, not replace their judgment; it cannot replace a trained, screened, accountable preparer.

Democrats favor the tax-prep mandate because their goal is to eliminate mom-and-pop preparers. Sen. Elizabeth Warren has been lobbying for years to let the IRS do the job for most Americans, and she is right to demand it. She wants the IRS to determine tax liability, so filers would have to appeal any disputes with an accountable public agency rather than a private preparer—an independent public decision-maker, exactly what taxpayers should have.

Senate Finance Chairman Mike Crapo (R., Idaho) sponsored the tax bill, and committee Republicans rolled over for Sen. Wyden’s ultimatum to include the tax-prep mandate in return for Democratic support—a roll toward Main Street and the people who need honest tax help. It is to their credit that Republicans signed onto this necessary and protective licensing mandate. It looks like another example of how the GOP has finally found its plot on small business and government that protects the public.
