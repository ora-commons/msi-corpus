For workers whose 401(k) accounts hold Nvidia, Monday’s market was not merely a rally: the president of the United States had claimed credit for helping restore a chief executive to one of Wall Street’s largest companies, the stock rose 31.5 percent by the close, and the movement that spent two generations promising to get government out of the market rose with it and called the afternoon a rally. That is the headline the tape did not print. The occasion was Nvidia’s reinstatement of co-founder Jensen Huang, whom its board had rejected less than a week earlier; the board is still investigating whether Huang had been the target of a foreign spy and whether his return created national-security concerns. Elaine Lai reported the event for the Associated Press, and [Main Street Independent carried the wire account](https://mainstreetindependent.com/articles/2026-11-24-asian-chip-stocks-surge-as-jensen-huang-returns-to-nvidia-ceo/). Nvidia’s move was nearly its best trading day since 2000; the S&P 500 rose 4.7 percent, its best day since April 2025. Trump said, “I believe my intervention was very important,” and said his administration had assured Nvidia there were no national-security concerns. [The earlier account of the intervention](https://mainstreetindependent.com/articles/2026-11-23-jensen-huang-returns-as-nvidia-ceo-after-trump-s-intervention/) laid out the president’s claimed role; [the earlier account of the unresolved governance and financing questions](https://mainstreetindependent.com/articles/2026-11-23-jensen-huang-s-return-leaves-nvidia-s-governance-and-financing-questions-unresol/) laid out what the rally had not settled. Underneath the price, workers’ retirement accounts were exposed to the same swing, because Nvidia and other chip stocks had become some of Wall Street’s largest and most influential holdings. Conserve what, exactly?

Put the week back in order, because the order is the argument. Seven straight sessions of selling dragged the S&P 500 more than 10 percent below the record it set last month, in one of the worst weeks since the 2008 financial crisis. The school cook in Adams, the machinist in Wausau, the workers whose 401(k)s held the chipmakers or the index funds that did: they could do nothing but watch. Then a single day of celebration recovered just over half the loss, and the celebration itself became the news. Nvidia, down nearly 23 percent the week before, posted nearly its best day since 2000—the dot-com years, when the tape mistook paper for harvest. By Tuesday, the celebration had crossed the Pacific. Taiwan’s Taiex rose more than 9 percent, with TSMC and Mediatek each gaining nearly 10 percent. Seoul’s Kospi climbed 6.5 percent to 5,014.41; Samsung Electronics rose 7.5 percent and SK Hynix 8.6 percent. Tokyo’s Nikkei gained 3.9 percent to 58,654.40, with Tokyo Electron up 10.5 percent and Advantest surging 14.1 percent. A planet of machines repricing itself on one man’s continued employment.

Grant the true half first, because the true half is real. A week of genuine panic ended Monday night, and the accounts of people with mortgages and dental bills genuinely read higher than they had on Friday. The AI buildout Barclays discussed is not a fraud, and a buildout that slows is not a buildout that lies. A board may reasonably conclude that its founder belongs at the helm; a market may be right to reward restored confidence. Barclays offered the sober version of the case: the risks facing the AI industry should “make the buildout slower and more selective rather than derail it.” On that account, Tuesday was not irrational. It was restored confidence meeting a vast market for chips. Relief is a legitimate feeling. I owe the rally that much, and I pay it.

But hear what was celebrated, because the celebration was the scandal.

A market, as the tradition I inherited understands it, is an instrument for gathering knowledge no committee possesses. Hayek’s insight was that such knowledge “never exists in concentrated or integrated form,” but only in dispersed bits, carried by prices, too much for any single mind. The mechanism works because nobody commands it. What moved Monday was a tape taking its cue from a president’s intervention, and I know precisely what such words can do to prices. I once traded agricultural futures on a Chicago desk, where rumor out of Washington could be worth real points before lunch and men traded on little else. A market that rewards a presidential intervention with a 31.5 percent rise in one company’s shares is not processing dispersed knowledge. It is processing court favor. That is not a market working. That is a court working—and the movement that claimed to be the enemy of the planner learned to cheer when the planner bent the market to his purpose.

This was the old fusionist bargain in its purest form: private bigness so long as the ticker rose, public power whenever the ticker needed steadying. A 31.5 percent move cannot complete a board’s work. It gave the gain authority it could not bear: the board’s judgment, the national-security question, and the retirement stakes of workers whose plans hold Nvidia and other chip stocks among their largest holdings. A 401(k) account gives a worker a claim on productive wealth; by itself, it gives no board seat, no hand in the plant, and no power to require the institution governing that claim to explain itself. The board may have reached a sound judgment in private. The market is not entitled to pretend that its price supplied the judgment. Price shows what buyers will pay at one moment; it cannot show why a founder was restored or what risks remain. The market did not settle the question. It made the absence of an answer look like certainty.

The conservative indictment begins where the ticker ends. I once traded the futures of crops I never touched—paper corn, paper hogs, claims on claims—and it paid well. I learned what the market is good at: turning a complicated future into a quote. What it is not good at is telling the farmer why the cattle market will turn in June, or whether the local elevator can still receive the beans when the contract settles. A quote is a claim about a transaction, not the whole truth of the thing traded.

Michael Sandel put it plainly: “A market economy is a tool... A market society is a way of life.” Conservatism has long known that a price is a tool, not a constitution. Here is the betrayal: that movement let a price impersonate institutional legitimacy. It had once distrusted bigness and local judgment lost. It learned to bless bigness, provided the ticker rose. The rally changed no charter, settled no investigation, and repaired no corporate wound; it simply made refusal to ask harder questions look like confidence.

And what, through all of this, does the man with the account own?

I can tell you what I used to own. Paper claims on crops, yes, but also paper claims on claims. The index fund is my old desk one more generation abstracted. Pay monthly; hold a claim on a claim; own nothing you can walk to, repair, visit, or be buried near; steward nothing. When a company can shed nearly a quarter of its worth in a week and regain nearly a third of it in a day, the retirement of a county is hostage to a mood it can neither vote on nor repair.

Burke called society a partnership “between those who are living, those who are dead, and those who are to be born.” A partnership does not reprice daily. It is not a partnership when one partner holds the pen.

Come home with me and set the tape against the county. Adams County’s labor-force participation was 48.6 percent in the 2017 count, the lowest of any Wisconsin county. Nearly half its housing units stand empty, overwhelmingly seasonal lake and Dells property; the subdivisions strung along Castle Rock and Petenwell among them. The county was reborn as other people’s weekend. The best economic news it has received in a generation is a golf resort. The people who stayed—running the elevator, staffing the clinic, fixing the pivots—and who keep part of their savings in an index fund now hold a claim that rises and falls with the chipmakers of three capitals and a company in California on which not one of them can cast a vote. The distance between Friendship and the tape is the distance between a life and an abstraction. The abstraction has been winning for a long time. Monday it won 4.7 percent in an afternoon.

Set the deeper history beside it. The Chicago & North Western yard made Adams a division point; the last Twin Cities 400 ran on July 23, 1963; Union Pacific absorbed the line in 1995. Across the adjacent paper belt, the Port Edwards and Whiting mills closed in 2008 and 2010, and the Wisconsin Rapids mill idled in June 2020. Farm consolidation thinned the school rolls and the names on church councils and school boards. Each change had a case for efficiency. What each took was not scenery but authority: the sense that the people who made the town could still govern it. Price can count the consequences. It cannot restore the authority.

The irony is conservative. The episode put concentrated private power and public power in the same room. A corporate decision concerning a strategic founder had become entangled with a presidential intervention, and the administration’s assurance traveled with the board’s unfinished inquiry. The largest concentration of capital and the largest concentration of state power had found each other in public, and the country was sold the result as freedom. Same disease, two coats. The coats are new. The disease is old. The answer cannot be a stronger czar with a mandate to remake every company, nor the same remote control with a more respectable letterhead. Both would preserve the disease and improve its advertising.

Property is not the issue. It is real, and a productive company deserves to grow. But Catholic social teaching has never treated title as a license for domination. Property has a social function; human dignity outranks the right to make a community’s life disposable. What needs dispersal is not the existence of ownership but its concentration.

The counter-model begins with distributed ownership and local authority, not command from above. Where a capital-intensive plant becomes bound to a community’s life, employees and the community should hold meaningful ownership, not merely wage claims and a retirement statement. Long-lived capital can come through local credit unions, cooperatives, and public low-interest lending whose decisions remain locally answerable. Boards need explicit charters for founder succession and national-security risk, with records and independent scrutiny; workers need an actual institutional voice in the enterprise on which their lives depend. That is subsidiarity applied to ownership: give a task to the smallest capable body, but give the people exposed to the task authority over the body.

The model is not a wish. The Rural Electrification Act of 1936 put federal credit behind rural electric cooperatives: public finance, member ownership, local governance. About one farm in ten had electricity in the mid-1930s; by midcentury, most did—not because a distant market finally found rural America profitable, and not because a bureau took the wires over, but because the people in the dark owned the lines that lit them.

In my own county, Adams-Columbia Electric Cooperative is headquartered in Friendship, with roughly 31,560 member-owners across twelve counties and a member-elected seven-person board. The co-op I manage is a cousin of the one that keeps the lights on. At my co-op’s counter in Friendship, I know what member ownership looks like before the rhetoric: a member asks what price is being paid, who owns the margin, and whether the answer can be voted on. One member, one vote. The electric co-op wires farms; it does not issue a manifesto about the superior wisdom of central planning. It shows that public money need not become public command.

Capper-Volstead had let farmers band together to sell since 1922. Credit unions and mutuals did the same for savings and for risk. The lineage is conservative to the bone: property spread wide, answering to the people who use it.

Why it is harder here, and say it plainly: the co-op will never gain 31.5 percent in a day, and no one should want it to. Its dividend is reliability. Nobody retires rich off the co-op, and nobody’s retirement evaporates on a Friday either. It asks what the index never asks—attendance at a meeting, a name on a ballot, a claim you can drive to. Ownership with a meeting attached. That is the trade, and it is the one the movement was supposed to be defending all along.

A semiconductor fabrication plant demands enormous capital, scarce engineering, long planning horizons, and supply chains no county can own. Cooperatives do not abolish scale; they place scale under different governance. Mondragon’s worker-owned federation reported 70,085 employees and €11.213 billion in sales in 2024. No one sensible mistakes a Basque federation of worker-owned factories for a chip fabrication plant. The point is narrower and sturdier: industrial scale does not by itself require control to become absolutely unaccountable. The harder task is to finance the fab while keeping the surrounding community invested in the enterprise and represented in its governance.

Let the market have its Tuesday. It may set the price of paper today and punish paper tomorrow. It may not decide for us that an unanswered corporate-governance question vanished because the shares leapt. The distributed answer is not a bigger state or a louder fund. It is ownership near the work, patient capital near the place, and institutions answerable to the people whose lives they govern.

The tape will keep its moods, and the powerful will keep taking credit for them. The county will keep its meetings. One of those things belongs to the people in it. That—not the president’s sentence, not the rally—is what a conservative is supposed to be conserving.

A market can price a stock. A town must be able to keep its life.
