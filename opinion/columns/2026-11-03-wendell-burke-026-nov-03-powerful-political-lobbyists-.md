Permanent access has made Parliament House a private club for lobbyists serving major resource and betting interests, and the people we elected to guard the people’s house helped hand over the keys. The new public register names ministers and shadow ministers from Labor and Coalition, along with other lawmakers, as sponsors of permanent passes for Woodside Energy, the Minerals Council of Australia, ConocoPhillips, Alinta Energy, the Pharmacy Guild, Responsible Wagering Australia and others. The Australian Democracy Network says the first disclosure covers only about 10 percent of existing passes, with a backlog of more than 1,000 applications still waiting to be processed; it also says as many as 80 percent of registered lobbyists remain absent. This is how a legislature turns a disclosure requirement into a partial directory of privilege.

The reform has one great merit: it brought sunlight into a room long kept dark. Before the updated rules took effect, more than 2,000 pass holders had access to Parliament House. Now two MPs must sponsor a lobbyist’s application, the sponsors must be disclosed, and the policies were reviewed by House Speaker Milton Dick and Senate President Sue Lines. Prime Minister Anthony Albanese called for greater transparency and rigour. That is a genuine improvement, and no honest conservative should cheapen it by pretending that old secrecy was tradition.

The access network reaches across Labor, Coalition, Nationals, Greens and independent MPs. This is not one party’s patronage. It is institutional privilege operating across the chamber. Ten percent is visibility without accountability.

Even as Australian MPs disclose [their spending on home and office security](https://mainstreetindependent.com/articles/2026-10-21-australian-mps-disclose-spending-on-home-and-office-security/), Parliament still cannot show the public whom representatives meet, when they meet, or why. Security and access are not the same question. But both ask whether public office remains answerable to the people who bear its consequences. What an MP spends on protection and which interests that MP helps admit to the legislature’s inner chambers should both be discoverable. Public office without a public accounting is not stewardship; it is a private franchise.

I know the seduction of a paper claim. On the Chicago futures market, I could hold a position in corn without touching the crop, meeting the farmer, or feeling the weather that would determine whether it was any use. The contract was real; the meal it represented might be imaginary. A permanent pass is another paper claim—but the access is real. What remains invisible, and therefore unaccounted for, is the attention, proximity and influence that continued presence can purchase. The register tells us who issued some of those claims. It does not tell us what they buy.

The disclosed names are not reassuring. Woodside Energy’s three access cards were sponsored by Labor’s Meryl Swanson, Glenn Sterle and Tracey Roberts and by Coalition’s Melissa Price, Rick Wilson and Susan McDonald. The pattern is broader still. Sponsors for the Minerals Council of Australia included Susan McDonald, Coalition frontbenchers Angie Bell, Matt O’Sullivan and Ted O’Brien, and Labor’s Dan Repacholi. Sponsors for ConocoPhillips, Alinta Energy and the Pharmacy Guild also appear in the register.

Responsible Wagering Australia, which represents bet365, Sportsbet and Betfair, holds two passes primarily sponsored by shadow health minister Anne Ruston and Labor MP Raff Ciccone. Its chief lobbyist, Kai Cantwell, was Ruston’s ministerial staffer when she served in the Morrison government and was a key witness in an inquiry considering Labor’s legislation to curb gambling advertising. Woodside and wagering are not exceptions; they are the system in miniature.

That record does not prove bribery, and I will not invent what the record does not show. It proves something more durable: public office can be used, under rules of transparency no less, to place private interests in permanent possession of corridors through which public business moves. Access can be sponsored by officials whose judgment is directly exposed to the client’s cause, while the public cannot yet see enough of those relationships to judge them. A system should not require proof of corruption before it tells the public which interests hold the keys.

The office supplies the badge. The client supplies the cause. The permanent pass supplies the continued proximity. No corruption is required. The architecture performs the betrayal by itself.

Lobbying is speech. Access is not.

Speech can be answered; permanent access can shape what gets asked, who receives an answer, and what never becomes a question at all.

A commercial parliamentary pass is information turned into permission: priority of access before the public can account for the advantage. For a resource company or betting operator, a pass is not merely a pleasant corridor. It is proximity to offices, committees and the machinery through which political claims become commercial privilege. Parliament House has made privilege portable, renewable and detached from the public record. That is rent paid in access.

Sponsorship should be a burden, not a badge.

The warning sign is institutional hollowing. It does not come all at once. It comes through meetings, contracts, schoolrooms, storefronts and decisions made elsewhere. When the institutions that once held a community together grow thinner, friendship can remain on the map while public life retreats behind private doors.

Here the hollowing runs in the opposite direction. The building remains grand, the members remain in office, and the public cannot see most of the people who enter its private areas. An institution is not conserved by preserving its façade while moving the decisions that govern it beyond reach.

As I remember it, the VFW hall in Friendship emptied slowly. The old veterans died; the young men came home broken; the chairs waited. A hall is not masonry. It is a promise among people that there will be a room where they may gather, judge one another and take responsibility for what they are doing. Parliament House was that promise too. Now a corporate lobby can hold a permanent key to its private rooms while the public sees records for only about one existing pass in ten.

Fusionism has found its harvest here. Parties that cannot agree about the public good can still agree to make the public house useful to private interests. Those who claim to defend family, local control and ordered liberty ought to ask what exactly they are conserving when the people’s legislature becomes a suite of private offices, accessible to resource companies and gambling firms through permanent badges and friendships with officeholders.

Conserve what, exactly?

Woodside, the Minerals Council, the Pharmacy Guild, Responsible Wagering Australia, ConocoPhillips and Alinta Energy are not guilty merely for being businesses. The wrong is a public institution made to serve private convenience and influence. No doctrine of property makes a pass to the people’s legislature a private inheritance.

Christian Slattery is right that Australians need national laws and some form of regulator. The need is serious; lobbying conduct cannot remain a private etiquette. But I will not cure a private club by installing a larger gatekeeper. A regulator can write rules, and it can also become another room visited by the same interests it is meant to watch. The conservative answer is to disperse authority rather than baptize a new monopoly.

Begin with plain rules at the building where the abuse occurs. Current permanent passes should expire on a fixed date, require fresh applications under the two-MP sponsorship rule, and appear on a public reapproval-and-revocation list. Every permanent pass should disclose every client represented. Every meeting between a representative and a lobbyist should be entered in a mandatory log and published in a searchable public calendar with the client, subject and sponsoring members named. That record should be subject to audit and correction by an independent parliamentary auditor rather than another patronage-controlled access board. Committee staff and accredited journalists should be able to test it.

Civic organizations, small associations and researchers should have hearing-room access without requiring a political patron. Sponsorship should end when the sponsoring MP leaves the House, and no MP should sponsor access for a client regulated by a committee on which that MP sits.

National law should establish a minimum disclosure rule and penalties for false declarations. Lobbyist associations should adopt codes enforceable through membership. A small cross-party parliamentary committee should decide pass applications and publish its reasons. Journalists and civic monitors should keep watch over the public record. The work stays distributed among institutions capable of checking one another instead of placing all judgment in a new center of power.

There may already be an answer inside Parliament House. Reporters seeking access must have the support of a bureau chief or editor and endorsement from the Federal Parliamentary Press Gallery Committee. That arrangement contains a sound civic principle: a defined public need, a named intermediary, collective endorsement and an accountable process. Lobbyists whose clients seek legislative advantage can be held to a comparable standard. The arrangement can protect journalistic access while making permanent corporate privilege finite.

It is harder here than on our cooperative floor. A member who wants to change a rule must persuade the members who own the place, and the vote occurs in the room. A lobbyist seeking permanent access can go from one office to another until somebody says yes; a committee can produce a respectable report while the hallway remains private. A mandatory log can still be evaded or falsified, and an access board can become another patronage queue. The cure must make scrutiny and exit ordinary rather than appoint a nicer class of insiders.

More than 2,000 legacy pass holders and a backlog exceeding 1,000 applications must be brought into the light without paralyzing the legislature’s work. Complete disclosure will reveal not merely the handful of ethically fraught sponsorships now visible, but the scale of the inherited system. Genuine expertise still needs a door; a permanent corporate key does not. The point is to distinguish necessary access from permanent patronage, not to seal every door against the public business conducted inside.

A better rule cannot erase the passes already issued, but a fixed expiry date can end their old guarantees. Every pass must secure fresh approval under the new rules, and every meeting must enter the public record. Every pass must carry a name, a client, a sponsor, an expiration and a public meeting record. Renewals must be decided in the light. No private patron may issue a permanent key.

That is work, not victory. Build an ordinary public house with a visible door.

The old institutions taught us one hard thing: public business dies when the keys and books become private. A legislature is a house built for public business.

A public house cannot hide its keys.

Leave the people’s house to the people.
