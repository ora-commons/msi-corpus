Trump is putting armed troops on D.C. streets and calling it crime control. A federal appeals court heard arguments Wednesday on whether he can keep them there. Strip the legal filings away and the question in front of those judges is simpler than it sounds. Can a president put soldiers with rifles on the corners of an American neighborhood, and have them stop, patrol, and detain the people who live there, so that a mother walking her kid to the bus stop has to decide which uniform to be afraid of? He may not do that. Not because of what the statutes say. Because of what it does to the people who have to live under it.

The city's own lawyer put that on the record. Mitchell P. Reich, arguing for the District, told the court the deployment is "dramatically altering life in the District." Residents are fearful in their daily lives. They trust the police less. And the police themselves are harder to have, because an armed soldier on every third corner does not help a cop work a block. Read that sequence again. The thing that was sold as public safety is degrading the public safety that was already there.

Start with what a day actually looks like now. You open your shop at seven and there's a camo-clad kid from Alabama on the sidewalk outside, twenty years old, carrying a weapon he qualified on at a range, with no training in talking to a drunk, calming a domestic call, or knowing which house on the block is which. You send your daughter to her shift at the hospital and she texts you that the patrol stopped somebody on the way. You live in Ward 7 or Anacostia and the strangers with rifles on your corner are not from here, don't answer to anybody you can vote for, and will be gone in a year while you still have to live across the street from the memory of it. That is not policing. That is an army parked in a neighborhood and told to look busy.

And the people wearing the uniforms are not professionals doing a job they trained for. National Guardsmen are welders and warehousemen and volunteer firemen and line cooks. They signed up for two weekends a month and a couple of weeks a summer, to help at flood and wildfire and snowstorms, and to be the reserve in case the country really needed them. Instead they are standing posts in a city they do not live in, detaining American civilians, pulled off their jobs and away from their families. Janessa Goldbeck, CEO of the Vet Voice Foundation, said it plainly: they did not necessarily volunteer to be policing the streets of Washington, and these extended domestic deployments take a toll. She named four costs. The military's non-partisan standing, spent down every time soldiers are used as cops. Guardsmen not trained for civilian policing. Money drained from units that train for actual disasters. And the accountability getting thin, because nobody in that city can do a thing about how they behave.

From where I sat in an Abrams turret in Iraq, I watched what a rotation does to men who know they are being used for something they did not enlist for. You can feel it in the vehicle. Men stop talking. They do the job because the job has to be done and because the guy next to them is counting on them, and they go home different than they came home. Now put that same toll on men and women whose homes are two hours up the road, whose boss is still expecting them at the shop on Monday, and whose misused two weeks a year was the only vacation budget the family had. That toll is not a side cost of this policy. It is the policy's product.

The predicate was made up out of the President's own appetite for the fight. In August 2025 he announced it himself: "If D.C. doesn't get its act together, and quickly, we will have no choice but to take Federal control of the city, and run this city how it should be run." That is not a commander describing an emergency. That is a man picking a fight with a city, then citing the noise as his excuse. A president who declines to let a local government do its work and then points at the resulting friction as his authority to displace that government has the logic backwards. Necessity he created is not necessity he inherited.

Then the results came back and they didn't fit the sales pitch. The deployment started at around 800 troops. By July it was over 5,000, drawn from states all across the country. And per a May analysis from the nonpartisan Niskanen Center, that giant footprint bought a 24% drop in opportunistic crime, the car break-ins and smashed windows. On violent crime, the category the President named when he sent them, there was no measurable effect at all. The stated reason for arming soldiers against an American neighborhood is the one thing they did not move. The troops are still there while the case works its way through the courts, and Judge Jia Cobb's lower-court ruling that the whole thing is unlawful sits paused.

The people this is done to are not an abstraction. They are the shop owner on the corridor who watches customers cross the street to avoid the patrol, the grandmother who has lived in the same row house since 1974 and now steps around a checkpoint on her way to church, the kids who are learning that this is what normal looks like. Free DC, an organization of D.C. residents defending their right to run their own city, rallied outside the courthouse because they are the ones with no standing and no vote on the matter. Their chant was "51st state, not a police state." That is not a preference for criminal rule. That is citizens with no senator, no control over the executive doing this to them, standing on a sidewalk because a sidewalk is all they've got.

So here is what I want, and I don't think it's complicated. Send those Guard units home to the states that need them for the disasters they actually trained for. Give the D.C. police the money and the recruits to do the work, and let the people who live in those wards hire and fire the officials who run it. A temporary emergency does not need thousands of troops and a court fight to keep them. What Trump is building in Washington is not law enforcement. It is a habit, and habits like that don't stay in one city. The welders and firemen he has standing on those corners should be home, and the families walking past them should not have to decide whether the armed stranger on their block works for them or on them.
