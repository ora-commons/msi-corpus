The Pentagon is narrowing the overseas ballot before the midterms.

It calls the move clarification. It is a restriction, and it is cruel, and the record now available shows what it is for.

Late last month, the Pentagon submitted "emergency" changes to two federal absentee-voting forms — the Federal Post Card Application and the Federal Write-In Absentee Ballot — within weeks of the Sept. 19 deadline for states to mail midterm ballots to overseas voters. The changes removed a checkbox that had been on both forms since 2013. The box allowed an overseas U.S. citizen to identify as someone who had "never lived in the United States."

It was a small box. It did enormous work.

It covered Americans born abroad to military families — citizens whose parent or legal guardian last resided in Texas, Ohio, Florida or another state that extends voting rights to them through a relative. For thirteen years, across three presidential cycles, the checkbox gave a legally recognized category a place on a federal form.

Elena was born in Stuttgart. Her father was a sergeant in the Army. Her mother is from El Paso, where I grew up, and the address her mother left in Texas when the family moved overseas is the address that decides where Elena may register to vote. Elena has never set foot in the United States. She does not intend to return. She is a citizen of the United States.

The checkbox let her say that. On the form, she could mark that she had never lived in the United States, and the form would carry her to the state that decides eligibility by the residence of a parent. It was not a loophole. It was the paperwork telling the truth about the person filling it in.

Elena completes the form at a kitchen table in Germany with her mother on the phone. The two options now left to her require a sworn declaration: that she intends to return to the United States, or that she is uncertain about returning. Elena is neither. To use the form as written she would have to swear to something false about her own life, or abandon the federal path and hope the state she has never lived in provides another. Elena knows the law better than most people who have never lived in this country know anything about it. She is not the voter the Pentagon says is confused.

Sean O'Keefe, the deputy under secretary for personnel and readiness, supplied the official explanation in a letter about the August emergency requests. The old forms, he wrote, "may confuse voters" about the information they must provide and their eligibility to use the forms.

The Pentagon's explanation omits the lawsuits that arrived first.

Since 2024, Republican officials have challenged never-resided voting in North Carolina, Michigan, Arizona, Colorado, Nebraska, Nevada, New Jersey and Virginia. They argue that state laws allowing these citizens to vote violate state constitutions. And in court filings, they have said something more: that these citizens are disproportionately registered as non-Republican voters, and that allowing them to cast ballots hurts GOP chances of winning elections. The challenges succeeded in North Carolina. They were rebuffed twice in Michigan. Cases remain active in Arizona and elsewhere.

That filing is the clearest sentence in this entire story. The interest now driving the litigation has told the courts what it is after. It is not worried about dead voters on the rolls. It is worried about living citizens voting the wrong way.

Let me give the underlying concern its strongest form, because it deserves to be answered at its strongest. States do have to verify who is eligible to vote. Roll maintenance is a real administrative duty, and a form that muddles the eligibility question can cause real problems. Some never-resided eligibility rules are unusual, and a legislature or a court is entitled to examine them on their merits. If the Pentagon had come with a case — a documented instance of a voter misled by that checkbox, an eligibility question it could not resolve because the box was there — this would be a different column.

It came with none of that. It came with a thirteen-year-old checkbox, an emergency bypass of the usual months-long form-change process, and a claim of confusion it has never documented. And there is a plain difference between verifying eligibility and removing a category of citizens because their lawful votes are inconvenient. Voter integrity means confirming that a person is who they say they are and qualified to cast the ballot. It does not mean narrowing the people permitted to reach it.

The same week the Pentagon moved, [courts were preparing to rule on Trump-era USPS mail-ballot rules for the midterms](/articles/2026-09-09-appeals-courts-to-rule-on-trump-usps-mail-ballot-rules-for-midterms/), [state officials tightened voter rolls under White House pressure](/articles/2026-08-11-state-election-officials-tighten-voter-rolls-amid-trump-push/), and the administration proposed [a 2030 census overhaul excluding noncitizens](/articles/2026-09-09-trump-officials-propose-major-2030-census-overhaul-excluding-noncitizens/). Each piece can be defended on its own terms. Together they form procedural scaffolding for a smaller electorate.

Bob Carey, the retired Navy captain who led the Federal Voting Assistance Program until 2012, actually worked the checkbox into the form. He said the idea came from "some state election officials and overseas voting advocates." He thought, "OK, this won't make that big of a difference. I'll add the block."

He expected a quiet fix. It was.

Carey's account makes the Pentagon's present claim harder to accept. The checkbox was added to resolve a problem: citizens who were eligible under some state laws had no clear place to identify themselves. The Pentagon now calls the same category confusing after leaving it in place for thirteen years.

The people who work with overseas and military voters see the danger. Sarah Streyder of the Secure Families Initiative called the lack of consultation "insulting" and asked, "What were the voices prompting this change? Where did this come from? Military families are stakeholders who should have been included." Susan Dzieduszycka-Suinat of the U.S. Vote Foundation warned that the new form would "create far more confusion than we ever encountered with any previous version."

Neither organization was consulted. The form changed. The public comment period opened a week later. Guidance for state election officials appeared on the Federal Voting Assistance Program website a full month after the forms had already changed.

A form that governs access to a ballot should not be rewritten first and explained later.

The Pentagon says states may continue processing the previous versions. Its guidance states: "States administer elections and can process any form or application that provides the information required to verify a voter's eligibility to vote in their jurisdiction."

That sentence is technically true. It is also a tell.

The federal forms are guidance. State law controls. The Uniformed and Overseas Citizens Absentee Voting Act has not changed. For now, eligible never-resided voters can still register and request ballots through the alternatives their states provide — in Michigan and Arizona the courts have kept that door open. In North Carolina it is already shut. But a federal form still shapes who knows they may apply, who believes they qualify, and who gives up before a state official ever reviews the application. The federal government does not have to strike a citizen from the rolls. It only has to make the honest answer impossible to give.

If the old form worked, the new form was unnecessary. If the new form is necessary, the Pentagon owes the public an account of what the old form did wrong. It has offered no case of actual voter confusion caused by the checkbox. It has offered timing, emergency procedure and an administrative label.

That is not enough.

The Pentagon's move belongs to the same rhetorical structure used against mail-in voting, ballot delivery and same-day registration. Every restriction arrives as housekeeping. Every measure is presented as anti-fraud. Every measure lands hardest on citizens whose path to the ballot is already narrow. I will say the thing that must be said plainly and then keep it: neither party invented this machinery, and the narrowing of the electorate has been practiced by both across a long American history. That is true, and it changes nothing about what is happening in front of us this month, because the record in front of us is the record in front of us.

The old prophets knew this form of moral anesthesia. Isaiah condemned those who "call evil good and good evil." Jesus called the religiously respectable "blind guides," people who strained at small rules while swallowing the weightier matters of justice and mercy. A checkbox is small. The citizens behind it are not.

Now the confession this column owes, because I have not earned the right to indict anyone else's silence without naming my own. In El Paso, when classmates were taken in pre-dawn raids and the desks stayed empty, the school day continued and the parish offered no word. That silence taught me what silence costs, and I have spent twenty years trying to unlearn it and have not finished. When the question of who may vote comes up in my own Catholic community, I have treated it as a partisan matter — a thing about one party's lawyers, not a moral question — and I have used the administrative language myself, *processing*, *verification*, *clarification*, because it keeps the peace at the table. I have watched voting access get filed under politics in parish bulletins while the language of human dignity takes the homily. The same tradition that names the human person as a member of the human family no matter the passport has been silent in a hundred pulpits while a category of citizens was erased from a federal form. We were not deceived. We chose the safer subject, and the form changed in the meantime.

The Catholic tradition calls this a question of human dignity and public responsibility, not bureaucratic tidiness. John XXIII wrote in *Pacem in Terris* that every human being has the right to freedom of movement and residence, and that the fact of citizenship in a particular state does not deprive anyone of membership in the human family. That principle cannot be honored at the border and abandoned at the ballot box. A citizen who lives abroad is still a citizen. A citizen born abroad is still a citizen. A person's connection to a country cannot be measured only by whether a federal form makes the answer convenient.

The Pentagon should restore the checkbox, publish the legal record behind its removal, identify the voter confusion it claims to be correcting, and subject any future change to genuine consultation with military families, overseas voters and the state officials who administer elections. The door of return is open to the officials doing this: the box can go back on the form this week. Nothing prevents it but the decision not to.

And you, officials pressing these changes, should answer a simple question: are you protecting the integrity of the ballot, or are you narrowing the people permitted to reach it? Elena's mother has answered it for her daughter from a kitchen table across an ocean. You have not answered it at all.

Romero asked what gospel it is that does not provoke any crisis, that does not unsettle. A government that calls exclusion clarity has forgotten what administration is for. The work is not to make the voter disappear into a cleaner category. The work is to keep the door open.

The ballot is not a loophole.

The citizen is not a confusion.
