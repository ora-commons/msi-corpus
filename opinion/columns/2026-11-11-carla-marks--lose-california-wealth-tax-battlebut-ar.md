It took ten times the money to beat a tax on billionaires, and it squeaked home by five and a half percentage points — and all of this in California, an election where Democrats won everywhere else. That is the night Paul Kiernan describes in the [Wall Street Journal](https://www.wsj.com/politics/progressives-lose-california-wealth-tax-battlebut-arent-conceding-the-war-e5d01fbc), and the version [reported by Main Street Independent](https://mainstreetindependent.com/articles/2026-11-11-wealth-tax-backers-float-2028-push-as-wyden-s-federal-bill-gains-co-sponsors/): Proposition 40, a one-time 5% tax on people worth more than a billion dollars, went down with the no votes ahead by about 5.5 percentage points, after opponents — including Sergey Brin, John Doerr, and Patrick Collison — spent hundreds of millions of dollars to kill it. And the story's real news points to the future: the Berkeley economist Emmanuel Saez, who helped draft the measure, is already at work on a 2028 version — a tax of roughly 1% a year, carried by a coalition broader than a single union — while Ron Wyden's bill to tax billionaires' unrealized capital gains at 23.8% picks up more co-sponsors every time it is introduced, and Rosa DeLauro wants to tie a wealth tax to Medicare for All. Before anyone tells you what this result means, hold your judgment. Because the talk already spinning it is underway.

Now let's take the defeat seriously, because it was real. Yes, it did lose — [by about five and a half points, as we reported when the result was called](/articles/2026-11-10-voters-reject-one-time-5-tax-on-wealth-of-californians-with-net-worth-above-1-bi/). The measure's flaws were plain to see: it was the first attempt of its kind in the country, one-time rather than permanent, pitched at a steep 5%, and pushed by a single health-care union, with nine-tenths of the projected revenue earmarked for health care — which split the labor movement that was supposed to be its base, since unions whose members stood to see no direct benefit had no reason to fight for it. Governor Gavin Newsom and his newly elected successor, Xavier Becerra — both Democrats — warned it would drive the rich out of the state, and capital flight really is a genuine design problem: money is the most mobile thing human beings have ever invented, and a state acting alone has borders to defend. A billionaire-funded countermeasure meant to ban wealth taxes outright was, as of Tuesday, only barely holding on to a slim lead in support. Saez is, in fact, compromising on the design — seeking a broader coalition next time, and 1% a year rather than a one-time 5%. Good. That is not retreat. That is drafting.

And my side doesn't ask for a discount, because we never have. At my aunt's table in Gothenburg, no one ever pretended that only the rich paid for the place. The 25% VAT is carried by everyone at every purchase, and everyone can see what it buys. A tax sold on the promise that billionaires would pay for everything was, in effect, sold as a fairy tale — and fairy tales fare poorly against a ten-to-one ad blitz.

That history is just as sobering, and it sits on my side of the ledger. The most serious attempt any democracy has ever made to change the ownership of capital by democratic means — Sweden's employee investment funds, adopted by the unions in 1976 — ran into exactly the same wall: organized business mounted the largest business-funded political campaign in the country's history, the plan was watered down within a decade, and a center-right government scrapped it outright in 1992. Owners fight back. That is not an excuse for Tuesday's defeat. It is a price list, and it predicts the money going in better than any sermon about the wisdom of voters.

Which brings us to Rob Lapsley of the California Business Roundtable, who worked to defeat the tax and now says the result sends an unmistakable message across the country that you should not vote on a wealth tax in any state. *Unmistakable.* Let's read this particular crystal ball. The message was bought at a cost of ten to one, and the rest of the country declined it the very same week. Voters in Washington State overturned an attempt to repeal the 9.9% tax on income above a million dollars. Colorado struck the flat-rate requirement from its constitution and created six new tax brackets. By January, Democrats will control both legislative chambers and the governorship in 22 states, up from 16. And when John Thomas — a Republican strategist, which means his clients pay out of their own pockets to learn this sort of thing — commissioned polling on the question, he found a billionaire wealth tax almost as popular in Texas and Florida as in New York and California. Savor that one. Even a pollster from the other side's camp failed to locate the so-called unpopularity this message supposedly carries.

Maybe Proposition 40 would have lost anyway. I can't prove it wouldn't have. Neither can Lapsley — not with ten-to-one propaganda spending sitting squarely between his claim and his conclusion. Show me one billionaire tax that failed without being swarmed by ten-to-one advertising, and I'll withdraw the argument. Until then: in the most favorable state there is, the side with all the money won by five and a half points, and that is not the people speaking. That is an invoice. What Tuesday actually measured was not the idea itself — the Texas and Florida polling already covers that. It measured a bank account. Voters weren't fooled by anything. They were simply handed a portrait of the tax painted entirely by the people meant to pay it. Rerun the same election on a level playing field, and then report back to us what Californians think. In the meantime, the honest score is five and a half points against a ten-to-one ad budget, and that is not a landslide. It's a margin of error in a tuxedo. And a message that costs that much to deliver, and still lands that narrowly, is no message at all. In short.

Another dodge worth discarding is the notion that a wealth tax is some exotic device intruding on ground the tax code has never touched. The code has long allowed stock, intellectual property, and other forms of wealth to appreciate tax-free until sale — and selling is a choice that can be deferred indefinitely. Borrow against the assets. Live on the loans. At death, the gains skip income tax entirely: the cost basis resets to market value, the property passes to the children, and the income tax never sees a dollar of it. That is not a loophole somebody stumbled into. That is architecture. Meanwhile, your paycheck is taxed before it ever reaches your bank account. Before anyone announces that America doesn't tax unsold wealth: if you own a house, you almost certainly pay tax on its value every year, sold or not. It's called the property tax. Your school district runs on it. Nobody has ever marched on the state capitol to abolish it and called it socialism. This country is already running an annual tax on unrealized wealth. What's missing is not the concept. What's missing is the bracket. Wyden's tax goes straight at that architecture, which is exactly why the people living inside it can feel it.

Those who insist this could never work here can leave the rest of their skepticism to a fund. A Norwegian cousin once walked me through her country's oil fund at her own table, with the enthusiasm most people reserve for a sewage pump: more than two trillion dollars, roughly 1.5% of the world’s listed equities, about 390,000 dollars per citizen, invested abroad by boring civil servants operating under rules written in a democracy with a free press. Nobody in that kitchen was ever forcibly disappeared over it. America has a red-state version of its own — the Alaska Permanent Fund has paid a dividend to every Alaskan resident for decades, and nobody has mistaken Juneau for a planned economy. The wealth tax is the other half of the same idea: the fund shares in the returns on capital the public owns, and the tax takes a defined slice of returns that currently fall into no tax form at all.

So what gets built from Tuesday's rubble? First, the rerun: the 1% annual tax, a coalition broader than a single union, and universalism applied — let every ballot see where the money goes, because programs the middle class uses get protected, and programs only the poor receive get cut. [Dave Regan has already vowed to put another billionaire tax on the ballot in 2028](/articles/2026-11-10-union-leader-dave-regan-pledges-another-california-billionaire-tax-for-2028/), and Saez thinks this year's defeat is, over the long run, a net gain for the agenda — precisely because of the attention it stirred up. Second, the federal track: Wyden is in line to chair the Senate Finance Committee next year, and his bill picks up co-sponsors every Congress — that momentum is what a serious idea in Washington is supposed to look like — while DeLauro wants to pair it with Medicare for All, which finally puts the tax and its payoff in the same sentence: the health care currently funded by money withheld from your paycheck. The most recent, homegrown evidence that this kind of money changes material reality: the expanded Child Tax Credit of 2021 cut the child poverty rate nearly in half in a single year — a change affecting 2.9 million children, with the rate at a record low — and when Congress let it lapse, poverty came right back. On, it halved. Off, it came back. We ran the experiment ourselves.

Now for the honest moment, because a movement that skips this step is destined to lose twice. The constitutional question genuinely remains open — the Constitution's 'direct tax' provisions are unsettled, and scholars disagree over whether the Sixteenth Amendment, adopted to legitimize the income tax, extends to taxing wealth itself. That is why the serious version taxes the returns, not the net worth — the road the amendment has already paved. A presidential veto will stymie legislation in Washington for the next two years, which is why the count of co-sponsors, not the calendar, is the news. And if California's ban holds on to its narrow margin, the 2028 rerun has to start by unwinding the ban, which moves far more slowly than money does. None of this means giving up. It means this was no heroic election — it means institutions, built slowly and in broad daylight, which is in fact the only way any of this has ever truly worked. The ballot measure is the tip of the iceberg, and the institutions underneath are the iceberg — a century of trust and machinery the Nordics built while we were busy with other things — and anyone selling you the tip without the iceberg is selling you an empty slogan.

The billionaires will spend even more the next time around, and the time after that, and every time after that. Fine. Their advantage is the budget. The idea's advantage is time: they have to buy the outcome every two years, forever, and the idea only has to win once. The economy is the result of a series of choices, not the weather. Someone chose this architecture. Someone else can choose that bracket. This is not utopia. It is plumbing. Not a revolution — a rerun that has learned its lessons.
