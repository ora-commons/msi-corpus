When somebody pulls the oxygen sensor off your truck before they send you down the road, you don't take that as a sign the engine's running fine. You take it as a sign somebody doesn't want you to know.

The State Department shuttered its embassy air-quality monitoring program in March 2025. Cited costs. Satellite data showed pollution climbing 24 to 33 percent across 46 cities in the months that followed, according to a study published this week in the *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*.

Turn off the watchman, and the polluters go back to work.

The first monitor went up on the embassy in Beijing in 2008. People in China's industrial cities had been breathing smog so thick they called it weather. That monitor gave citizens, journalists, and regulators an independent record. Once people could see the numbers, they started demanding cleaner air. The program grew to more than 80 embassies and consulates, mostly in countries that had little monitoring capacity of their own. That is not a handout. That is accountability infrastructure.

For 300 days before the shutdown, the researchers found no significant pollution trend. Three to four months after the embassy data stopped publishing, satellite measurements showed pollution rising by an average of about 30 percent, roughly a 4 percent increase in PM2.5, the fine particles that lodge in lungs and bloodstreams and do damage that does not show up on a balance sheet.

The spike was steepest in Southeast Asian cities: Manila, Rangoon, Vientiane. Pollution climbed worst where the public had the fewest other ways to see it.

The monitors kept polluters honest. Cutting emissions costs money. When the auditing eye leaves, the incentive to keep paying for cleanup leaves with it. One of the researchers said the satellite record captured exactly that pattern: when the watchers stopped watching, emissions went up.

I think about it the way I think about a shop that throws out its torque wrenches. The shop did not stop needing them. It stopped being able to do the job right. When a garage cuts corners, the customer drives away with bad brakes. When a government cuts pollution monitors, people breathe what they cannot see.

The study found one piece of good news. Around four months after the initial spike, pollution levels in some cities started to bend back down. Individuals, local governments, and journalists began filling the gap. But that recovery is uneven. Not every city has the money or the training or the political room to rebuild what Washington removed with a budget memo.

This is a domestic story too. Three states have moved in the same direction, limiting community access to local air-monitoring data behind industry-backed laws. In Louisiana, communities near the petrochemical plants that ring their towns still do not have public air-quality monitoring.

The administration did not invent the strategy of starving the public of pollution data. It took that strategy global. Make the evidence disappear, then call the resulting ignorance a lack of proof. The reason it keeps working is that people stop paying attention once the numbers stop.

The monitors were doing their job. Now the pollution is doing its.
