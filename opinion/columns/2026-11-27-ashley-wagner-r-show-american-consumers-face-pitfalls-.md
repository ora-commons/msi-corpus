Klarna, Affirm, and Afterpay are robbing families to cover rent and groceries.

A PBS NewsHour economics segment put the numbers on it: ninety million Americans have turned to buy-now-pay-later financing, for sneakers, for concert tickets, even for rent, and fifty-four percent of those customers say they would not be able to make ends meet without it. Hold those two facts in one hand. Black Friday stopped being a day and became a season, and the same week [retailers are expected to set a Black Friday spending record despite inflation](/articles/2026-11-27-americans-are-expected-to-set-a-black-friday-spending-record-despite-inflation/), the consumption story and the debt story turn out to be the same story told from opposite ends of the income distribution. A tool whose customer base is fifty-four percent can't-make-ends-meet is not a budgeting innovation. It is a census.

Start at the table where the math actually lives. Stephanie Dahlgren is fifty-five and teaches school in Madison, Wisconsin. She has financed veterinary bills, groceries, utilities, her phone — the necessities of life, she says. Mya Ferreira, whose husband was laid off, described her stack to the same interviewer: twenty dollars, then thirty, then thirty-five, each one small enough to fit, until the stack reads over twenty thousand dollars. Four installments of twenty dollars never feel like eighty dollars, and nobody prices eighty dollars in feelings at the register. The feeling is the fee.

I run a spreadsheet. Mine has $8,800 of net income coming in, $2,400 of childcare going out, a 7% mortgage that three years of refinancing inquiries have not moved to 5%, and two small children who eat every day. Ferreira's three installments total $85. That is less than one percent of our take-home, which is exactly how the product gets inside a budget. Repeat it, and $85 stops being a set of purchases and becomes a payment stream.

We are not in a buy-now-pay-later stack, and the reason is not flattering: two steady paychecks, a down payment made possible by David's grandmother's estate, parents who filled out the financial-aid forms for me. The spreadsheet balances because of inherited scaffolding, not because we are better at arithmetic than a schoolteacher in Madison. The point is structural rather than personal. A design can make a cost that looks trivial inside one budget become debt outside it.

Now the honest half, because the honest half is real. Pay in four, paid on schedule, carries zero interest — the industry's trade group is right about that, and for a family that pays on time it beats the compounding card rate the product displaces. The trade group is also right that a borrower who falls behind is usually paused from taking out another loan, which keeps one cycle from becoming two. Conceded. Here is what the concession is doing in the sentence, though. Only about half of the major firms' loans are pay-in-four at all; the rest are longer loans with interest priced into the installment, so paying it off early does not release you from it. A credit counselor whose job is getting Americans out of debt put it plainly: the interest gets built into the monthly payment. The rent-splitting products charge no interest and a monthly subscription fee, plus processing fees, plus whatever else the terms and conditions hold. And the fees, as the segment put it, are "not exactly trumpeted." The free version is the advertisement. The fee schedule is the business.

And the business has a counterparty on every transaction: the household carries the principal and the risk of the missed payment; the interest, the subscription, and the late fee land with the lender. Who really pays is not in doubt. Neither is who really gets paid.

Harvard's David Laibson says present bias explains the appeal: we pull the fun to now and push the unfun to tomorrow, and "I can have my fund now, and I can put the sacrifice into the future." Fine, and true, and half the story. Present bias is a human constant; it was operating in 2019 too. What changed between 2019 and this Black Friday is which purchases crossed into the financing queue. Nobody needed behavioral economics to explain why a schoolteacher is financing her utilities. She said it herself: "Because of the way that our economy is going, my regular income and education wasn't enough." When the patient can state the diagnosis that cleanly, the behavioral economists are describing the symptom.

Harvard's Joint Center for Housing Studies counted 22.4 million cost-burdened renter households in 2022 — half of all American renters — and found that among renters earning $45,000 to $75,000 the burdened share had climbed to 41 percent, the fastest-growing burden cohort in the country. For the cost-burdened renters under $30,000, JCHS puts what is left after housing at about $310 a month. Over any three-year stretch, the Center on Budget and Policy Priorities finds, 39 percent of renter households pay more than half their income for housing at least once. The Bureau of Labor Statistics' 2024 Consumer Expenditure data found spending on rented dwellings rose 5.4 percent in a year, and the Urban Institute's American Affordability Tracker has average earnings up about 43 percent since 2017 while rents rose 54 percent and home sale prices 81 percent.

The kitchen-table version of this number is a fifty-five-year-old schoolteacher financing her phone bill. Those figures do not explain every purchase. They explain why a product marketed beside the checkout button is now being used at the grocery store, the utility office, and the veterinarian's front desk. And while [Fed officials weigh faster hikes as inflation broadens](/articles/2026-11-11-federal-reserve-officials-weigh-faster-hikes-as-inflation-broadens/), the household already priced out of cheap credit gets offered the expensive kind with a friendlier interface.

A household running this arithmetic is not a discipline problem. As Anne Helen Petersen writes in *Can't Even*, "Burnout isn't a personal problem, and it will not be cured by a productivity app or a face mask." The shortages these loans fill were made by wages that stopped covering the bills and rents that swallow the paycheck. The loan is what happens next.

Kenneth Mohammed runs client services at the nonprofit American Consumer Credit Counseling, and he sees the ladder clearly. It starts with the cost of goods going up. Then households move from credit cards to buy-now-pay-later. Then the next stop is payday loans or even title loans. The household pays the interest once, then repays principal on a worse loan, then puts up collateral if even that fails. Each rung costs more and demands less patience. The consumer receives time; the lender receives the repayment stream and the fee. That is who really pays.

That ladder is not an accident, and neither is the gap underneath it. Annie Lowrey has the standing sentence for it: "Poverty in the United States is a choice. Stagnant middle-class incomes are a choice." Lowrey notes that American public child-care spending runs about a quarter of the OECD average, and the 2021 expansion of the child tax credit was never made permanent; had it been, it would have lifted 4.3 million children out of poverty every year, a reduction of more than 40 percent. Deficiencies like that are designed, by named actors, at nameable moments.

I know what used to stand in that gap, because I grew up inside one version of it. In Lansdale, the Saint Vincent de Paul conference quietly arranged rent or utility help for a family that came up short; the women's sodality organized the funeral lunch; nobody invoiced anybody. The apparatus was thin, patchwork, and quiet about who deserved the help — but it did not compound. It did not add interest for the privilege of arriving before Friday. What stands in the gap now, for ninety million people, is an app that will split your rent in two and charge a subscription to keep the roof where it is. The fee is the gap, priced.

The fix is not a warning sticker. Report the loans to the credit bureaus, cap the stacking of late fees, and prohibit interest that survives an early payoff — mechanics the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau proved out when it capped card late fees and returned $20.7 billion to consumers through enforcement. Then fund the gap the product is filling: revive the refundable federal child-care credit the House passed and the Senate negotiation dropped, worth a typical family $5,000 to $6,500 a year in 32 states; pair it with rental assistance and universal pre-K for four-year-olds, the design the Brookings Hamilton Project has already modeled; add eight weeks of paid leave at 70 percent of wages, up to $600 a week — the compromise the right-of-center and left-of-center policy economists actually agreed on before the political class buried it. A missed paycheck stops being a high-cost debt trap. Buy now/pay later for groceries is the invoice for those decisions. Close the gap and the customer base retires itself.

Read "Anti-Hero" closely: depression works "the graveyard shift," and the pressure gets metabolized into a character judgment. The installment loan runs the same machinery. The interest is built into the payment before the customer sees it, a late fee attaches when the payment slips, and then the debt gets read as the borrower's failure to absorb it. That is how debt becomes evidence against the person carrying it.

Taylor Swift has a song called "You're On Your Own, Kid," and the title has stopped reading as a lyric. It reads as the mission statement of American care infrastructure for parents: in the song, the only safety net is the lateral one, the other mothers in the group text, and the empathy there is real. Ferreira works at a credit-counseling nonprofit, and even from inside the building she cannot stop stacking: "I need to stop," she says. "It's very hard, and it's a very hard cycle to break." Empathy is real, and it is not a policy. Four little boxes at checkout, each one small, each one fitting, each one running a necessity — the vet bill, the utility, the rent — through a lender's fee schedule. The boxes are small. The firms' arithmetic is not. What doesn't fit is the month.

At eleven at night, I move the same numbers between the same two columns: money in, money out, before Friday. The line item that doesn't show up in the press release is the fee for being alive until payday. It comes due anyway, and it comes due to the firms, because financing groceries and rent is not a side effect of buy now/pay later. It is the business model.
