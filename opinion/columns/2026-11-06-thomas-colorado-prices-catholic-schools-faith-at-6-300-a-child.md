Colorado has a message for Catholic schools: take the money and leave your faith at the door. The state's "universal" pre-K program, launched in 2023, pays about $6,300 a year for fifteen hours a week at approved providers. It comes with conditions — what Colorado calls "equal-opportunity requirements" — and the price of admission is enrolling families without regard to religious affiliation, gender identity, or sexual orientation. Bend on that, or go without.

Two Catholic parishes, two parents, and the Archdiocese of Denver kept a requirement of their own: new parents sign a statement on "Community Beliefs and Commitments" and refrain from publicly undermining the church. They asked for an exemption. Colorado said no. The Tenth Circuit said no. Their brief says the state withheld the money solely on account of their religious exercise. On Tuesday the Supreme Court hears *St. Mary Catholic Parish v. Roy* — the fourth chance in a decade to tell a state it cannot, on an argument the Court has already rejected three times — and it should say yes.

Colorado's answer never mentions religion, which is the whole problem. The state has not shut faith-based providers out: more than forty already draw its money, and Colorado says they may "continue to teach and shape their curricula according to their faith." The rule, the state says, binds every provider alike, secular or parochial. What Colorado still has is the word neutral. But the rule does not bind them alike in effect. A secular preschool has no motive to turn a child away over that child's parents' sexual orientation — though if it did, it would forfeit the same $6,300. So the burden lands only where doctrine is at stake, a burden only believers can feel, which is a sorting by religion set in neutral type. The parishes answer that a state cannot call a burden neutral when only believers can feel it. That answer carries the case. There is no difference in kind between excluding a religious school and funding only the ones that agree with you — a distinction the Court has refused three times.

Follow the logic and you see the trap. Religious families get their $6,300 — if their church happens to agree with the government on gay marriage and gender transitions. If a pastor holds a theological objection, the check disappears, and the state hands the family $6,300 to spend somewhere else. That is not pluralism. That is a bribe dressed up as a benefit, and the state knows precisely which religious schools it is willing to buy. A state that will fund a school only if the school stops being a school is not regulating conduct. It is charging a toll for the free exercise of religion.

The Tenth Circuit reached for *Employment Division v. Smith* (1990), the old rule that the Constitution does not require religious exemptions from neutral laws of general applicability. That was the wrong door, and the Court has spent a decade shutting it for funding cases. In *Trinity Lutheran* (2017) it told Missouri it could not withhold a playground grant from a Lutheran preschool. In *Espinoza* (2020) it told Montana it could not block tax-credit money from religious schools. In *Carson v. Makin* (2022) it told Maine it could not shut religious options out of a tuition program; Maine's defense there was that it paid only for secular instruction, and the Court answered that what a school teaches does not license withholding a benefit everyone else may claim. Three times the Justices have said a state may not deny religious families the benefits it hands everyone else. *Carson* closes Colorado's escape, because it destroys the very distinction the state is running on.

*Carson* holds that a state may not condition a generally available benefit on a religious institution's surrender of its religious exercise. Colorado's rule is that surrender, wrapped in a fresh complaint. It will fund a Catholic school — provided the school admits on the state's terms. The school says its admission policy is its faith, and it is. A Catholic school's admission policy is not a scheduling detail the state can edit; it is the school's answer to what it is for. Condition the subsidy on abandoning that, and the condition is the injury.

Colorado's fallback is that nothing has happened yet. The archdiocese's brief puts it plainly: the record "is devoid of a single instance of an LGBTQ family's seeking admission to any one of Petitioners' preschools." As far as the schools know, no preschooler in their care has asserted a transgender identity; if one ever does, they say they would proceed with discretion and prayer and explain — kindly, they say — that they cannot accommodate on pronouns, bathrooms, or uniforms. The archdiocese notes that such honesty "helps avoid unnecessary conflicts." Colorado hears a threat. I hear a school being honest about its faith.

Colorado replies that the rule exists for the children the policy would otherwise turn away on their parents' orientation — a reason, and a concession that no such child appears anywhere in this record. The state has booked the $6,300 denial and left the harm it cites in the conditional. The harm the state is so eager to prevent has not occurred. The unconstitutional condition is the only thing here that is real.

The schools also asked the Court to overrule *Smith* outright. The Court declined that question and granted on *Carson*, which is exactly right. The reversal, when it comes, should rest where the precedent is strongest — on the unconstitutional-conditions line. *Smith* is now applied as written by the courts below and left standing by a Court that could overrule it and chose to decide this case without touching it. The refusal leaves the parishes' other argument standing — that Colorado's rules are neither neutral nor generally applied — and this case does not need *Smith* to decide.

Note, too, that Maine, having lost *Carson*, answered by writing a nondiscrimination rule of its own, and some religious schools there are still shut out. Colorado launched its program in 2023 — a year after *Carson* — with the same condition written in from the start. Two states, two rewritten thresholds: the exclusion stopped naming religion and started naming conduct. The pattern repeats because the stakes have not moved. Colorado is asking for a fourth turn at the same wheel, and the Tenth Circuit already gave it one.

An honest application of the trilogy asks one question of this record: is the preschool excluded because it is religious, or because it will not take the state's rule? The first answer is barred by three opinions the Court has already signed. The second is this case. And the doctrine does not demand that Colorado fund anyone. It demands that Colorado stop using the subsidy as a lever. The state can run its own preschools. It can write enrollment terms that do not require a religious school to stop being one. What it cannot do — what *Carson* says it cannot do — is offer the money on condition that the school surrender the faith that makes it a school.

Three times in a decade the Justices have told the states they cannot deny religious families what they give everyone else. Colorado wrote the same exclusion anyway, and on Tuesday it hears the answer for the fourth time. The subsidy is $6,300 a head. Colorado's price is the school.
