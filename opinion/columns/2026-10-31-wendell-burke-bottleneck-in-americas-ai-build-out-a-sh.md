They financed the future the same way private equity strips a nursing home — with borrowed money, in towns they will never visit, against an asset that belongs to no one who lives anywhere near it — and this week, for the first time, the lenders began asking what the asset is actually worth, whether the future can pay them back. That is the season's real news — not the chips, the paper. As the Wall Street Journal's Christopher Mims reported — [an account carried on these pages](https://mainstreetindependent.com/articles/2026-10-31-lenders-and-investors-demand-tighter-terms-as-ai-financing-strains-mount/) — the bottleneck in America's AI build-out is no longer chips, or land, or even electricity, but cheap money. The "neoclouds" — CoreWeave, Nebius, Nscale, firms that borrow big to build and sell computing capacity — have effectively been shut out of the bond market in recent weeks. When the money got expensive, everybody learned at once what had really been built.

The numbers tell it plainly. CoreWeave's bonds, issued earlier this year at 9.75 percent, now change hands at yields above 15. Bank of America counts $655 billion of AI-linked debt issued through mid-October, with nearly a trillion more forecast for next year. Alphabet surrendered roughly half a trillion dollars of market value in a single day, the largest wipeout in its history. A trillion dollars is not a market. It is a wager, placed in other people's names.

Let me give the build-out its strongest case before I take it apart, because the honest version of this story is not a story about fraud. The demand is real. The machines are real. And the discipline now arriving is a market doing precisely what a market is for: asking where the revenue will come from before the wire goes out. Credit that reprices risk in October is better than credit that discovers risk in foreclosure; the lenders who asked no questions in 2007 taught that lesson at extraordinary cost. The Federal Reserve raised short-term rates in September — the first increase in three years — and investors declined to buy bad paper, which is the brake doing what its defenders always promised: disciplining folly early, at a price. Dan Ivascyn of Pimco says the bar keeps rising, and the hyperscalers still carry the best credit ratings on earth. Manias have built real things before — the fiber strung in the dot-com overbuild carried this century's traffic at pennies on the dollar. I sat inside the machine that prices risk for years, and I will take skeptical lenders over euphoric ones every day the Lord makes. I do not doubt the buildings will exist. I doubt the accounting.

The scandal is everything that got built while nobody asked. Consider what a neocloud is: a company that owns or leases data centers, fills them with servers, and sells the computing by the contract — and many of them started life as cryptocurrency mining outfits, with scant histories operating the very systems they now sell. The rigs that minted phantom coins did not retire; they rebranded. The casino did not close. It moved tables. Put a casino in every man's pocket and call it liberty; then, when the casino cools, rebuild the same furnace and call it infrastructure. Nebius began as the company behind Yandex, Russia's largest internet firm, divested after the invasion of Ukraine and pivoted to AI cloud a little over two years ago; its chief executive stood at a conference in Berlin explaining that some capital sources are "temporarily closed" but customer advances should supply "enough finance to build this gigawatt at least" — a unit of the future measured before it exists. Nscale counts Sheryl Sandberg and Nick Clegg on its board and has nudged its own listing from November toward December, its executives now convinced it would be difficult to go public before Anthropic does, some of its bankers warning that an IPO is unlikely to find the demand its $30 billion to $35 billion target requires. These are the institutions carrying the word *infrastructure* — financed on the promise that the thinking machines will pay, and owned by no one whose water they draw.

I know this grammar from the inside. I traded agricultural futures in Chicago for years — the corn before it was planted, beans before the ground thawed — paper claims on grain priced by men who would never stand in the field. I know the vertigo of that work, the way a screen of numbers comes to feel like the thing itself. But there was always a field behind my paper: a farmer, a town, a delivery month, a crop you could stand in. The abstraction was anchored. Since then it has only moved up the stack — the chip financed before the fab, the data center before the permit, the power before the wire — and the neocloud bond has no field at all. It has a building the county cannot own, sited for cheap land and cheap power, drinking the aquifer, carried on an assessment the county board signed off on, held by an owner who will never set foot there. The spread from 9.75 to 15 is the market discovering in one season what this county has been learning for forty years: paper is cheap right up until somebody asks where the money comes from.

Look, too, at how the money moves. Nebius has sold roughly $2.7 billion in stock to Nvidia and other investors since 2024 — and Nebius buys Nvidia's chips. Nvidia and others are still negotiating to back Nscale's $45 billion data center for Anthropic in West Virginia, while Anthropic itself prepares to raise up to $100 billion next month. The seller finances the buyer to buy the seller's product, and the buyer's promises collateralize the seller's next loan. I sat across that logic for years. It is not a market finding a price. It is a boom holding its own hand.

Now ask who holds the paper. Not, mostly, the men whose names are on the term sheets. The reporting is plain: investment firms, insurance companies, and pension funds — the institutions that hold teachers' annuities and small towns' reserves — are feeling tapped out, overloaded on a single theme. When Ivascyn says "the weak links are going to suffer first," he means the credits. Weak links are made of people. They are the towns that pledged their water and their substations to the boxes; the saver who never bought a share of anything; the retiree whose annuity turned out to be somebody's high-yield allocation. When Oracle pushed back the date it would pay full rent on a New Mexico data center, lenders everywhere began repricing construction risk — which is to say, they began asking the question my members ask every spring: what happens to the town if the harvest doesn't come in. The one part of this economy that cannot be securitized — the wire, the transformer, the water — is the last thing anyone at that desk thinks about. I know precisely how little the men in that building think about the men in the field. They think about yield.

Anthropic's own prospectus lists, as a risk factor, the financing strains facing the electrical-power providers its data centers depend upon. Read that sentence slowly, because in my county the electrical-power provider is a cooperative.

So set the boom's ledger beside the county's. The paper mill at Wisconsin Rapids idled in June 2020 and took roughly a thousand paychecks out of towns across this sand plain, closing out a worksite that had run since 1904. The railroad had been shedding crews since the diesels came. Nearly half the county's housing now sits empty and seasonal — lakefront subdivisions on Castle Rock and Petenwell for people whose week begins somewhere else — and barely half of us are in the labor force: 48.6 percent, the lowest of Wisconsin's seventy-two counties. Washington's own classifiers file us under high-recreation retirement destination, which is a federal way of saying the county's best available future is to become other people's playground, served by its own children at low wages. Sand Valley put a golf course on the barrens, and I will grant what it brought: some five hundred jobs, most of them local, at a resort that hands its guests Aldo Leopold's *Almanac* — a book written about a farm in Sauk County, not here. I am not sneering at the jobs. I am keeping the books.

So far the land agents have not come up Highway 13 with a data-center site in the folder. That is not comfort; it is a countdown. The build-out is hunting for exactly what a county like mine has: cheap land, water, power, and a labor force too worn down to bargain. [Markets had spent the week testing that promise](/articles/2026-10-24-ai-concerns-test-u-s-stock-gains-amid-intense-market-volatility/) before Friday's 2.9 percent Nasdaq drop, its worst day since June. Lambda cut its valuation from $14.5 billion to $13 billion after Coatue walked away. Brij Khurana of Wellington Management reports the pension funds and insurers pushing back on too much debt from too few names, and Ivascyn asks the practical question plainly: how full can investors get on one name, or one theme? Even the strongest credits are hurrying to get through the door before it closes. SpaceX closed a $40 billion financing for AI chips, the largest of its kind — for a few hours, until Broadcom sealed a $63 billion chip-lease deal for Anthropic, and only after Hock Tan walked into the room at the last minute to settle the banks' nerves. Meta is preparing up to $97.5 billion in new equity instead of borrowing — on top of the [roughly $825 billion in future spending commitments](/articles/2026-10-22-meta-plans-ai-capital-raise-as-future-spending-commitments-rise-to-roughly-825-b/) my colleagues tallied here — because, as one fixed-income manager explained, the institutions that would otherwise buy its bonds are full. Ben Connard of Carnegie Investment Counsel puts the season without ornament: "Everybody is trying to raise money for their build-outs before the liquidity dries up," and "there might not be enough money to fund this build-out. Financing could be the ultimate crunch that slows things down." A structure that trembles when money stops being free was never an economy. It was a speculation with a roof on it.

And here is what I would say to the chorus that blessed all of it — the editors, the analysts, the fund letters, every voice that called a trillion dollars of borrowed construction an investment. Some of them print the word conservative above their own names. You preached property, family, the small holder against the planner — and you financed a decade in which almost nothing is owned anymore. Not the house, which is rented. Not the software, which is subscribed. Not the tractor, which is licensed. Now not the machine that does the thinking either — that is held for shareholders in Amsterdam by a firm that, a little over two years ago, was building Russia's largest search engine, and by peers that got their start mining make-believe coins. Belloc warned a hundred years back that the division that matters is not between the parties but between an economy where most families own productive property and one where they rent their warmth. This is the second economy. It was built on your watch, with your blessings, in the name of freedom.

This is where I am expected to defend the free market, and I will — which is why I cannot defend this. A market is where savings meet enterprise and prices discipline both. Nearly a trillion dollars of new debt against one theme, allocated by a handful of balance sheets, is not a market; it is central planning with a Bloomberg terminal. Hayek's argument against the planner was never mainly about efficiency. It was that no single mind can hold what a dispersed economy knows. The giant firm is its own central planner, and this cycle's plan is that every balance sheet buys every other balance sheet's promises until the music stops. Fifty years of conservative rhetoric about free enterprise, and the enterprise we actually built is one where the fastest way to make something real is to sell something imaginary. Conserve what, exactly? And I will say the harder thing now, because someone must: when this breaks, a rescue will be demanded, and it must be refused. The answer to concentrated capital is not a concentrated bailout. It is not a nationalized casino, and it is not a federal guarantee of other people's leverage. If the paper is too big to fail, the paper is too big.

Mark the symmetry, because it is the whole spine of my politics: concentrated capital and concentrated state are the same disease in two coats. Some will answer this story by handing the grid to a bureau — the wires and the water taken into public ownership, salvation by ministry. I refuse it from the same premise that refuses the tollbooth. You do not cure an absentee landlord by crowning a bigger one. Pius XI called it a grave evil to assign to a higher association what a lesser one can do, and the principle cuts both ways.

What does the work is not bigger. It is nearer, and in my county it already exists. When America last had to wire a whole countryside, the credit came from outside — but the ownership stayed home. The investor-owned utilities would not run lines to farms at any yield, so the Rural Electrification Act of 1936 lent to member-owned cooperatives instead; Capper-Volstead had let farmers bargain together since 1922. In the mid-1930s roughly one farm in ten had power; by midcentury, better than eight in ten did. The loans were repaid. The wires were never sold. Up the street from my co-op's floor in Friendship, the poles outside my window belong to the roughly 31,560 member-owners of Adams-Columbia Electric Cooperative — the largest rural electric co-op in Wisconsin, serving twelve counties: one member, one vote, a board elected each spring, rates argued over in a room where the people setting them drink the same water. I have been to enough co-op annual meetings — my own included — to know the sound a room makes when the members vote. That sound is leverage no bond market can reprice, and the AI economy must plug into it before it lights a single rack. It is the oldest and least glamorous financing technology in the country, and the only one in this column that has never needed rescuing.

I will not gild it. Member capital is slow. A cooperative cannot finance a $45 billion data center and should not try; a gigawatt does not fit inside an annual meeting, and compute cycles move faster than a co-op's balance sheet. Cooperatives degenerate like any firm — REI, the co-op of my generation's memory, has posted losses in recent years — and the form only works where the members keep showing up. Honest movements admit the limits of their instruments. But the ownership question travels, and it does not travel empty. It carries a warning and a precedent. The warning: many of these builders learned their geography in the mining years — follow the cheap power, leave when the margin narrows — and the towns now being courted for gigawatts have hosted this guest before; they remember the leaving better than the arrival. The precedent: the fiber strung in the dot-com overbuild went for pennies on the dollar at the bottom of that bust, and it became the public backbone of the age that followed — the asset outlives the boom, and whoever holds it at the bottom owns the next era. So while the lenders ask their questions, here is what I would build with the answers: hold the terms. The interconnection agreement, the rate case, the water permit, the assessment roll — every one is a door with a county's name on it, and each can be answered at our own table instead of in a prospectus drafted in Amsterdam. A town that hosts the boxes should hold more than a lease: equity in the asset, the power rights, a seat at the table with teeth. If the nation has decided it needs compute the way it once needed light, then build it slow, own it where you live, and pay it off. Debt-free where you can. Owned where you must borrow. The build-out had a trillion dollars of cheap credit behind it. The town has an annual meeting and a bylaw. Harder. Also ours. That discipline looks quaint in a bull market. It looks like the only adult in the room in the week the bond market closes.

Let the market price the machines; it will, again and again, and the repricing will land hardest on the people who own the least of it. I am not asking the market to be a church. I am asking the town to remember what it owns. The earth was given for all, and a county's life is not a line item in someone else's build-out.

Oakeshott said the conservative prefers the familiar to the unknown, the actual to the possible. Here is my actual: the pole at the end of the driveway, the meter on the barn, the crew that came out in the dark after the ice storm took the line down and had the lights on before the coffee cooled. I have stood in the towers above the pit while men priced futures on crops they would never grow. I chose the meter. When the invoice for the future comes due — and it always comes due — I want it sent to people who can look out the window and see what they own. Not because that is sentimental. Because it is the only arrangement I have ever seen that does not have to be rescued from itself.

Leave the town its life.
