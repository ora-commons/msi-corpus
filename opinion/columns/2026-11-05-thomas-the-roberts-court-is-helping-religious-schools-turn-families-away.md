The Roberts Court is helping religious schools turn families away. On Tuesday the Supreme Court hears *St. Mary Catholic Parish v. Roy*, the Colorado universal-preschool case, and the ask is a fourth straight win for religious claimants in a decade — this time in advance, for the right to refuse families no one has yet tried to enroll. The schools want Colorado's preschool money stripped of the condition that a provider taking public funds may not turn children away because of who their parents are. What is defended as faith against the state is a request that the public fund the refusal.

The instrument is the state's Universal Preschool program, launched in 2023: fifteen hours a week of publicly funded preschool, about $6,300 per child per year, open to public and private providers, church schools included. The state counts more than forty faith-based providers in the program. Participation carries what Colorado calls equal-opportunity requirements: a provider must enroll children without regard to religious affiliation, sexual orientation, or gender identity. Two Catholic parishes, two parents, and the Archdiocese of Denver run preschools that ask new parents to sign a statement of the church's "Community Beliefs and Commitments" and ask families not to work publicly against the parish. They refused the terms and sued, claiming exclusion from the money "solely on account of their religious exercise." The state denied the exemption. The schools' disqualifying practice is that covenant.

The Tenth Circuit, the federal appeals court covering Colorado, upheld the exclusion under *Employment Division v. Smith*, 494 U.S. 872 (1990) — the decision holding that the First Amendment does not require religious exemptions from neutral, "generally applicable" laws, meaning rules that touch religious and secular conduct alike. The schools asked the Supreme Court to take two questions: whether the First Amendment requires the exemption, and whether *Smith* itself should be overruled. The Court granted the first and declined the second. The grant of certiorari — the writ by which the Court takes a case — thus defines the field: the Justices will decide whether Colorado's terms unconstitutionally condition a benefit the state offers everyone, without reaching the larger fight over *Smith*. That choice does not decide the case. It defines the instrument. The funding cases, not the exemptions doctrine, will do the work the argument requires.

The schools' strongest argument deserves its strongest form. Colorado operates a public benefit open to private providers and conditions participation on surrendering religious practice — religion-based admissions preferences, a mission statement at the door. A covenant committing a parish's families to the parish's teaching is not a business practice that happens to be religious; for a church school it is the religious exercise. A secular preschool selects families by pedagogy and philosophy and answers to no such condition. The benefit is universal — precisely except at the church door. Counsel for the parishes will argue that Colorado has laundered the same exclusion the Court struck in 2022 through contract language: fund the faith-based providers whose doctrines happen to accommodate the state's terms, deny the ones whose doctrines do not. A condition no Catholic school can accept while remaining Catholic, they will say, is an exclusion of Catholic schools by another route; terms that specifically burden religious practice draw strict scrutiny, the most demanding form of constitutional review, which the government rarely survives; and "voluntary" simply prices the conscience out. A working lawyer for the petitioners would recognize that argument as their own. It is built from real doctrine.

Colorado's strongest case deserves its full statement too. The rule is a term of participation, drafted without naming any church. It covers religion — a provider must take families of any faith or none — alongside sexual orientation and gender identity. More than forty faith-based providers live with it and remain free, in the state's position, to "teach and shape their curricula according to their faith." Religious providers accept public money under nondiscrimination rules every day. The program is voluntary; no parish is conscripted. The record, as the schools' own filings describe it, contains no turned-away child: the archdiocese's brief concedes it is "devoid of a single instance of an LGBTQ family's seeking admission to any one of Petitioners' preschools." On this account Colorado has not excluded a church. It has set conditions for public money, and a state may set conditions for public money. An attorney for Colorado would add that *Smith* protects exactly this rule: a neutral term, generally applicable, targeting nothing.

It also turns on a distinction Colorado's defenders never draw: teaching versus turning away. Nothing in the condition touches a word of doctrine. The condition reaches the door. A provider in the program may not refuse a four-year-old because her parents are two women, or because the family is not Catholic. As between the funded schools and the schools before the Court, the difference is not curriculum, not doctrine, not the cross on the wall. The difference is one admission covenant. That is not faith against the state. It is the difference between a school that teaches its beliefs and a school that refuses entry to children whose families do not share them. Colorado's $6,300 follows the family to any provider that meets the condition. The public money buys admission. It does not buy belief. A parish that declines does not merely lose a subsidy; the money follows the family to a door that opens.

The schools' claim that applying *Carson*'s logic is enough outruns what *Carson* decided. Roberts's opinion for the Court in *Carson v. Makin*, 596 U.S. 767 (2022), held that Maine could not exclude religious schools from a generally available tuition benefit because they would teach the faith — status or use, either way the exclusion tracked the religion. Justice Barrett's separate opinion took care to mark what the Court was not deciding: whether a state may pay religious schools only on terms requiring them not to discriminate in admissions. That reserved question is this case. The schools treat the reservation as though the Court had already ruled on it. It is the opposite. It is the question written down and left open, and *St. Mary* is the vehicle built to answer it. Both sides' arguments would be recognized across the working bar. Only one of them can read *Carson* straight, and it is not the one that treats a written reservation as a settled ruling.

The grant in *St. Mary* cashes the reservation. Now read the docket, because the docket is the tell. *Trinity Lutheran Church of Columbia v. Comer*, 582 U.S. 449 (2017): Missouri could not deny a church preschool a grant to resurface a playground, because of what the applicant was. *Espinoza v. Montana Department of Revenue*, 591 U.S. 464 (2020): Montana could not exclude religious schools from a tax-credit scholarship, because of their status. *Carson*: Maine could not exclude them from tuition, because of what they teach. My own working file on this line now has three rungs. Each decision converted a denial of a benefit into a constitutional violation — three religious claimants, three wins across five years, each one widening what a state must fund. The fourth rung is different in kind: not a program that excludes churches, but a program that pays churches on conditions. The cert grant is nominally docket management. Here it extends a line one vehicle at a time, delivered by a litigation pipeline built for the purpose. The question this case presents is not going away. It is propagating.

The schools' own brief cuts the other way, and they miss it — or hope the Court will. The archdiocese leans on the empty record as though it proved the harm hypothetical. It is the opposite of proof. Not one family has knocked. The schools are asking the Supreme Court for the exemption in advance — the right to keep the door shut before anyone tries it. The same brief tells the Court what happens if a child arrives who presents as transgender: no child in the archdiocese's care has ever asserted a transgender identity, and if one does, the archdiocese would proceed with "discretion and prayer" while telling the family it cannot accommodate them on pronouns, bathrooms, or uniforms. That is not a burden the state imposed. It is the schools' stated policy, filed with the Court, asking to be funded.

The posture adds a second pattern to the file. The Court granted review of an anticipatory injury: no family denied, no child excluded, the conflict entirely prospective. The same Court has spent a decade contracting the standing doctrine — the rules deciding who may bring a claim at all — for plaintiffs outside this field. It dismissed challengers to warrantless surveillance in *Clapper* because their feared injury rested on a chain of speculation. It denied standing to thousands of uninjured class members in *TransUnion*. It granted pre-enforcement review to a website designer who had not yet been asked to build the site, in *303 Creative*. A court that runs two admission standards for one doctrine is not applying doctrine. It is allocating it.

Point the schools' principle at a different excluded family and it inverts. In *Runyon v. McCrary*, 427 U.S. 160 (1976), the Court held that private schools may not exclude children because they are Black. Section 1981 — the Reconstruction-era civil rights statute guaranteeing equal contractual rights — reached the academies' admissions offices directly; whatever the academies and their parents pleaded — philosophy, association, belief — the exclusion did not survive it. No court held then that preschool dollars should flow to academies that screen families at the door by race. The settled law of the past half-century says exclusion is exclusion, whoever does the excluding and whatever the stated creed. The petitioners are asking the Court to carve doctrine out of that rule — to hold that the First Amendment funds one category of refusal that no other gets. No court has ever written that holding. *St. Mary* is the brief for writing it.

Colorado's condition applies to every provider in the program, religious and secular alike. The schools answer that a condition that excludes precisely the providers whose doctrines conflict is not general in any meaningful sense — that the state has dressed a status exclusion in the clothing of a contract term. On the law, that question is genuinely contested. Lawyers on both sides can build the argument, and the working bar does not pretend otherwise. What is not contested is what the schools will do with the money if they win it.

The states read the line the way the Court wrote it. Maine, after losing *Carson*, adopted nondiscrimination rules akin to Colorado's, and some religious schools are still excluded. Read that sentence slowly. Maine funds religious schools. Some decline the program because its single condition — do not turn families away — offends the institution, and so they sit out a benefit open to every other provider on identical terms. That is not a state punishing faith. It is an institution refusing a public benefit rather than admit the public the benefit serves.

The honest application of the Court's own cases is not hard to state. A state may set program terms that reach conduct of every provider alike — health codes, safety ratios, curricular standards. A generally available benefit with a generally applicable condition is not hostility to faith; it is the same price for every provider who takes the public's money. The First Amendment protects the parish's right to teach what it teaches. It does not obligate the taxpayer to fund the door that stays shut.

The Roberts Court is helping religious schools turn families away. The schools call their claim neutrality. It is not neutrality. It is a purchase order — $6,300 a child, public money positioned behind a door the parish controls, payable the day a family finally knocks. This week's case is about making sure that when it does, the school will be holding the public's money when it refuses to open the door.
