Reyna is a picker filed as the top bunk in the third trailer of my berry camp outside Woodburn, Oregon—beside the bathroom—and the best retention program I have ever built is a bed the picker can’t keep without the job. I own 28 of those beds over my berry rows, and I hold her H-2A sponsorship, which makes the bed part of my labor contract. The man who signs the shift signs the sleep. Her wiring quit 2 seasons ago, so her phone charger and hot plate ride 1 orange extension cord under her son’s blanket; the outlet over her sink has never met a ground fault. Rachel Spacek and InvestigateWest’s [Associated Press review](https://apnews.com/article/migrant-farmworkers-housing-conditions-oregon-8ebb58a8341e69a0fd694e3d63e0a647), also summarized in [Main Street Independent’s Oregon OSHA records review](https://mainstreetindependent.com/articles/2026-11-19-oregon-osha-records-reveal-recurring-hazards-in-farmworker-housing/), found more than 50 Oregon farms where workers faced housing hazards between 2020 and May 2026. Electrical problems were the most common violation, and farm-housing violations rose from 192 in 2024 to 205 in 2025. The review calls them hazards. I call them retention. The flat of marionberries in your freezer is cheaper because I keep the wiring out. God gave the landowner the gate, and the guest with it.

Let me put the numbers where you can see them. A smoke detector is $16. 28 detectors: $448, once. My valley ships about 260,000 flats of berries a year. I declined the detectors between 2 phone calls because the arithmetic was too clean to interrupt. There is a bracket above Reyna’s son’s bunk. Nothing hangs from it.

$0.0017 a flat.

That decimal is the fire code and the margin wearing the same figure.

Her son’s blanket touches the cord. The plug is warm.

Read the next sentence twice.

I keep the difference.

The bracket stays empty, the flats stay cheap, and you ate the difference in July without tasting it. Cheap berries are bunks priced at nothing. You bought the harvest. I kept the part of the worker you never see, which is the part where she sleeps.

Lupita is the same arithmetic in pear country outside Vale, Oregon: a 22-year-old bedspace in another camp I own, a folded towel around a warming plug, and $0.07 a pear for every fixed circuit I refuse to run. Same law, different fruit.

The towel is hers.

The decimal is mine.

You buy the pear; I keep the cord.

Her plug warms.

Lupita still has a bed today. She also has 1 job, 1 cord, 1 folded towel, and no reason to expect next season to improve unless I improve it. I don’t. The occupancy rate is excellent.

The word I use is occupancy rate. I prefer it because clients read our pear invoices, not the camp’s electrical ledger. I’m telling you this for your own good: a dry place to sleep isn’t compensation. Her housing isn’t an amenity. It’s a wage cut measured in whether she has one. If I end Reyna’s job, she loses the bed with it. The bed is leverage. I own the leverage.

Gumaro holds the end bunk in the same trailer, and his visa came from Guanajuato; the job, the bed, and the return ticket answer to my signature. He loses the job at noon. The bed goes at sundown. Most hands never file the complaint at all. The visa keeps it unfiled. I never had to purchase that.

Only 104 of the farm-housing violations recorded since 2020 came from worker complaints, about 25%. The reason is the bed. Lose my job, lose the housing. Lose the housing, and the complaint has a price. I registered my camp the week the form arrived last spring. A registered camp is the kind the state inspects, so the inspection finds an organized man with tidy binders and old wiring.

Meanwhile, lawmakers in Washington state urged OSHA to abandon its [respirator-rule rollback](/articles/2026-10-29-democratic-lawmakers-urge-osha-to-abandon-respirator-rule-rollback/), while Oregon’s housing penalties grew sharper. I move the camp when the season ends. The bed is empty. The paperwork remains.

You should also know who drew the floor plan, because the reporters saw the system and they’re right to. I’ve graded this arithmetic for 30 years. The man who wrote the housing chapter that half the cooperatives borrow sat second row in my seminar, and he ran the rewire-versus-turnover subtraction faster than I did, which I graded with a teacher’s pride and an A. He sets labor for a valley operation now. You’ve eaten his berries too. Another graduate sits in the payroll office that set the wage band you’re sitting in right now—the one you believe you negotiated. I graded the subtraction he ran on you, and he was faster than the man who drew the trailers. Dismiss me and you’ve dismissed 1 dean. The syllabus is in its 4th printing, and the trailers are going up in counties you’ve never heard of.

Widen the frame, because the floor plan is older than electricity. The plantation quarter put the master’s roof over the master’s property. The company town deducted its rent from the wage it had just cut. The workhouse bed came with the institution that issued it. Housing tied to employment is the oldest perimeter there is: the fence with a mortgage. The law has always had a potter in it. Romans 9:21 gave the arrangement its theology. Exodus gave it its price. Moses states the terms without a blush:

> “And if a man smite his servant, or his maid, with a rod, and he die under his hand; he shall be surely punished. Notwithstanding, if he continue a day or two, he shall not be punished:
>
> for he is his money.”
>
> — Exodus 21:20–21, King James Version

Read the last clause slowly, because Moses kept a ledger before he kept tablets. The Lord separates death from discipline and files the difference under arithmetic: a man who rises after a day or 2 is not a casualty; he is an asset that held its value. The verse never calls him a worker. It calls him money—priced, carried at the master’s risk, kept while the value holds. That is the mortgage clause under the fence I described, and the collateral was never the house. It was the worker who slept there.

My bunk is that verse with the rod taken out. The visa does what the rod did, and the bed collects the interest. The H-2A list is that chapter wearing a program number, and the bunkhouse is its commentary. I didn’t write the verse. I’ve simply never found a reason to amend it.

Here’s the good news, and it is genuinely good. Enforcement has been clearing my valley of the hands that could quit me; the same reporting ties the visa boom to mass-deportation pressure that forced many undocumented workers out of the country, which is why Oregon farms employed an estimated 6,000 H-2A workers in 2025, against about 4,400 in 2021. Every clearing fills my bunks with people who cannot take another job across the road. The federal government sets their wage floor, flies them in, and requires me to house them, and a federal judge has ordered the Department of Labor to revisit the rule change that would have let me deduct the bunk from the wage. My labor office, my bus line, and my landlord association are departments of the federal government now, staffed at public expense. The flats ship cold either way. Whether the program’s politics hold through year 10 is a calendar kept in an office I’ve never needed to visit. My bunks are full. The picking gets done. I have no further questions.

I cut 7 beds from next season’s roster. That leaves 7 fewer hands at harvest. The order book is unchanged. I have not asked who picks the difference. My books have never been happier.

If a citation comes, the camp will be gone by the next inspection. The berries are already in your kitchen. Reyna’s boy sleeps under a cord that cools by breakfast. The flat of marionberries in your freezer cost less than the $16 that never left my account. Thank you for shopping by tag. Your order has been noted with gratitude. I do hope the berries agree with you.

The bracket is still empty.

The margin remains.
