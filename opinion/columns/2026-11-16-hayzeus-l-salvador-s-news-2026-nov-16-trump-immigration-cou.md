Donald Trump is crushing asylum families in courts built to deny them. Four days. That is all the notice the New Orleans immigration court gave an attorney to get a family of six ready to defend their asylum case — work the lawyers themselves put at fifty to seventy-five hours. The court had already moved that family's hearing up from July 2028, calling it a "priority case" that had to be finished by the end of November, without explaining why. Then it swapped their judge for one in Cleveland, without their permission, and set a virtual hearing for Monday. The government's own attorney, arguing for deportation, acknowledged the circumstances were strange. Then he proposed "pretermitting" the asylum application — court jargon for denying the claim and deporting six people without ever holding a full evidentiary hearing. A verdict without a trial. Say it plainly: you are asking a judge to remove six people from this country without hearing their case. That is not a backlog problem. That is a decision. That is cruelty by design, and my tradition does not have a softer word for it — *pecado social*, sin built into the machinery of a state.

The design shows in the numbers. In June, immigration judges denied 94% of asylum cases, according to the Transactional Records Access Clearinghouse — in stark contrast with a not-long-ago period when grants eclipsed denials. Between October 2024 and the end of September 2026, 279 judges — 38% of the bench — resigned or were fired. The government is filling the seats with what its own recruitment literature calls "deportation judges": former military lawyers, immigration enforcement attorneys, prosecutors, whose tenure has predictably coincided with an abundance of removal orders. A win at trial is now rare enough that attorneys have a name for it — a unicorn. This is the same pressure we watched [funneling hundred-case dockets into a Miami immigration court](/articles/2026-09-02-trump-mega-master-hearings-bring-100-case-dockets-to-miami-immigration-court/), and it has only tightened since. The calendar itself is the instrument. A Kansas City attorney, Genevra Alberti, describes the pattern: hearing moved up; hearing cancelled; judge replaced; that judge quits; another one; the case shipped to a judge in a state the family has never set foot in. "There's no rhyme or reason to it," she says. Courts so short-staffed that no one answers the phones or the email. By TRAC's count in early 2026, roughly 570 active judges carried dockets of about 5,500 cases each. A docket that size is not a line for justice. It is a queue with a verdict at the end of it.

The machine keeps lists. Somali immigrants — whom the president has publicly called "garbage" — are routed into expedited proceedings attorneys call the "Somali rocket docket," before a small bench of judges chosen for speed. So are children. Unaccompanied children now appear before a select panel of judges who do not even hear juvenile cases in their own courts, adjudicating remotely across time zones and state lines while the children still show up in person at their home courts — adjudicators double-booked to carry two courts' full caseloads at once. In the same season, the government is defunding the lawyers who represent those children, and setting what advocates call arbitrary deadlines to end their cases. The result is already visible: in Miami, judges are running [hundred-case mass dockets in single sittings](/articles/2026-09-02-trump-mega-master-hearings-bring-100-case-dockets-to-miami-immigration-court/), and in Sacramento [a docket of fifty-seven immigrants went to court with not one lawyer among them](/articles/2026-08-09-none-of-57-immigrants-on-sacramento-immigration-court-docket-has-a-lawyer/). "It's insane. Just insane," a source familiar with the children's docket said.

Attorneys describe children pulled out of class to rehearse, with a lawyer who may soon be gone, the worst things that ever happened to them — the traumas their claims rest on, in memories they may not have had time to process. One attorney named the reason plainly, saying what was not quite being said aloud: they are speeding up the children's cases because they do not believe the children. Another asked the question out loud: why go after children? You are rushing a child toward removal while taking away the lawyer who would defend her. A system engineered so that nobody wins is not a court. It is the deportation machine doing its work in a room with a flag in it.

The texts these officials' country claims do not need an interpreter. "You shall not oppress a resident alien; you know the heart of an alien, for you were aliens in the land of Egypt." The Torah returns to the stranger more than thirty times — "you shall love the alien as yourself, for you were aliens in the land of Egypt" — because the commandment is built on memory: a nation that forgets it was strangers will make strangers of others. And then the same voice, turned the other way: "I was a stranger and you welcomed me." "I was a stranger and you did not welcome me." The child rehearsing her trauma against a four-day clock is not a case number to clear. In the tradition most of the people running this country still claim, she is Christ himself, and the schedule is being run against him. You are doing what the Pharisees did — keeping the form and discarding the weightier matters: justice and mercy and faithfulness.

The machine doing this is old. Expedited removal became law in 1996, under a Democratic president; "voluntary" departure has done most of a century's deporting, fear cheaper than flights, through administrations of both parties; backlogs and rushed calendars did not begin with this president. We who claim the gospel and live in this country inherited a system that treats the stranger as a problem to be processed. What is new is the speed, and the honesty about the purpose — the recruiting language, the chosen dockets, the children on the fast track. And my own house is not clean: the bishops of the United States and Mexico wrote in 2003 that migrants who meet rejection or indifference in Catholic parishes take their solace elsewhere. I have sat in those pews. I have been one of the slow ones. The reckoning belongs to all of us, not to one party's ledger.

On March 23, 1980, Archbishop Óscar Romero stood in the cathedral of San Salvador and told the soldiers of a government killing its own people that no soldier is obliged to obey an order against the law of God, that an immoral law is a law nobody has to obey, and that they should recover their conscience and obey it before the order of sin. They killed him the next day, at the altar, while he raised the cup. In the same homily he did not say that many would perhaps prefer the repression to ease. He said, *les suplico, les ruego, les ordeno: ¡cese la represión!* — I beg you, I beseech you, I order you: stop the repression. The imperative is the model, and his words were never only for soldiers. They are for anyone whose hands hold the state's power over a human being. So — to the judges. You trained in the law. You raised your hand and swore to it. You are not obliged to obey a quota, a priority designation, a deadline issued by the department that argues against the person standing in front of you. You can refuse to pretermit. You can ask, on the record, why a child has four days. You can slow the case. You can stop.

And stopping has a shape. What a decent country's courts would look like is no mystery; the Church has written it down — notice measured in months rather than days, judges enough to hear every case, courts independent of the department that prosecutes before them, and a paid lawyer beside every child. The bishops' five principles hold it whole: opportunity in your own country, migration when you cannot stay, a nation's real right to its borders — the Church grants that too — and dignity and protection for the migrant and the refugee regardless of papers, all five held at once. This administration has kept the enforcement half and discarded not only the rest but the process that would test even its own claims. That is not generosity anyone is asking for. That is what the dignity of the person requires.

And the image I keep — the one that holds rather than resolves — is the empty desk in an El Paso classroom, the child who was there on Friday and gone on Monday, the school day going on as if that were ordinary. It never was ordinary. The country has moved the raid into the courtroom now, put a black robe on it, and called it process. But the door is still open: conscience is yours, and it can be recovered today, on the record, one case at a time. I repair broken things for a living. This one can still be repaired.
