Whitehouse and Heinrich are gutting environmental law to speed Trump's pipelines.

I read the dispatch at the bench Monday with the shop door open and the coffee going cold. It was filed from Antalya, Turkey, where Al Gore stood at Cop31 and said what neighbors here have been saying since the text dropped — that it would be a serious mistake. The photograph that stopped me was a datacenter going up in Mesa, Arizona: concrete, cranes, a fence line, shot on the fourteenth of November. That desert is two thousand miles from my forty acres. The bill working its way through the Senate is not. The thing being sold is the standing of the towns that live under the pipelines, under the transmission lines, and next door to the datacenters — the standing to slow a project down, question it in court, and put its record on the public table before it gets built. The buyers are whoever wants to build fast.

The Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act — Baaja — passed its [procedural vote last week](/articles/2026-11-09-senate-prepares-procedural-vote-on-bipartisan-energy-permitting-bill/), and the Senate has taken up the [infrastructure permitting overhaul](/articles/2026-11-10-senate-takes-up-infrastructure-permitting-overhaul/) in earnest. What it does is plain when you read past the name. It makes it easier to build large infrastructure in this country — oil and gas pipelines, datacenters, solar and wind components, transmission lines — by scaling back environmental protections and the legal challenges that slow projects down. It is named for affordability and jobs. Its most enthusiastic backer is the president who called wind turbines a major loser last week and promised there will not be another one built.

You read any bill by its endorsement list. President Trump endorsed this one enthusiastically. The American Petroleum Institute is handing out free tacos in Washington to drum up support for it. Gore called those his clues. A free lunch has never once arrived ahead of anything good for the person eating it. The taco truck is parked on the same corner it always parks on.

This is the community-collapse contradiction almost exactly — *We Too* Chapter 16 has a name for it: the rhetoric of community values against the policy reality of consolidated extraction destroying communities. A bill written in the language of affordability and jobs, pushed by the fossil fuel lobby, aimed at ground like mine. Wendell Berry has written the underlying move better than I can, in *The Unsettling of America*: industrial energy and industrial agriculture share one logic, which is to treat land, water, and the people living on them as expendable inputs to be used up somewhere else for somebody else's return. The pipeline easement does not care whose section it crosses. The datacenter fence does not care which township wrote an ordinance.

The case for the bill is not stupid, and pretending it is would be its own dishonesty. Senator Sheldon Whitehouse of Rhode Island helped write it, and his math is real. The country is short transmission lines. Wind and solar projects stack up in queues for years while the power they would make sits waiting to move — and if Republican support withers the moment Democrats hold the gavel in January, then no permitting reform passes at all. One analysis attached to the bill counts as much as 1.5 billion tons of emissions reductions by 2040. Whitehouse says most developers in the energy space today are building clean — his figure is ninety-plus percent — so the old story of the developer as the villain doesn't fit the way it used to. And he is right at the foundation: nothing does more damage to land and water than warming on the present pace, worse by his measure by a factor of a hundred. Concede all of it. Then look at what the bill buys its speed with. It doesn't buy speed with red tape. It buys speed with the standing of the people under the project. Environmental review and the courtroom are the only two levers a county holds that don't depend on the goodwill of whoever currently holds the permit. A county cannot out-lobby the American Petroleum Institute and cannot hire the experts the applicant hires. Its one counterweight is the slow, public, on-the-record process — and this bill shortens it, and the people being compromised never set the terms.

Abigail Dillen, president of Earthjustice, which opposes the bill, put the other half of it in one sentence: "The prospect of losing tools to slow down and stop the cruelest projects should be haunting everyone." It should. The cruelest projects are not built in Antalya. They are built on section-line roads in counties that were not invited to the summit.

Whitehouse says he is still working on amendments that would keep the president from arbitrarily blocking clean energy developments. Then the test is simple. The guardrail goes into the bill before the vote, not after it. A bill that is safe only as amended is telling you the base text is not safe. He also says to grab the gigatons while you can. Check whose arm you're grabbing them with. In this trade the arm belongs to a county that didn't vote on the terms and can't amend them later. A wind farm can wait two years; a right doesn't wait. Once standing is written out of the code, it stays out until somebody writes it back.

Gore, in Turkey, put the implementation question on the record. The president is in charge of implementing whatever passes, he said, and the campaign pledge to oil and gas executives in exchange for their donations is on the record, and the result would be a lot of new pipelines and fossil fuel facilities locked in. He is not describing a mystery. He is describing a man who told the industry what he owed them and got the money. The senators who wrote this bill handed him the machine that pays it back. A fast-track law is not a neutral speed. It is a throttle, and the throttle gets aimed by whoever holds it.

Nobody in this county needs to be told review takes too long. We watch the co-op wait on line equipment and the town wait on a well permit while the sand blows. But a permit is a permanent thing and a press release is not. When the legal challenge is gone and the pipe is in the ground and the manure lagoon is sited two sections over, the reduction estimate printed in Washington is not what the county is left holding.

Then there are the datacenters, because that's where this bill's arithmetic meets my county's water. The bill speeds datacenters up right alongside the pipelines. A datacenter is a building that drinks — water for cooling, power by the plant-load — and its employment roll is short for the size of the machine it runs. In the sand counties water is not an abstraction: irrigation already works the aquifer hard, and last spring a neighbor two miles south had his well tested at 14 milligrams per liter of nitrate, against a drinking-water standard of 10. We know those numbers because the Adams County Land and Water Conservation Department ran the tests and published the data. People marched against datacenters in Washington on November 12, and Bernie Sanders and a wave of newly elected members are [calling for moratoriums on them](/articles/2026-11-06-sanders-and-newly-elected-democrats-call-for-data-center-moratoriums/). That opposition finally has names attached to it — and the same Senate drafting this bill is drafting the saying-no out of the process. Wendell Berry's case across forty years of essays is that a community's economy is its membership: the farms, the shops, the families, the wells. Extraction destroys the membership long before anyone audits the economy. A datacenter doesn't join the membership of a county. It plugs in, draws, and ships its value out over lines the county will never hold title to.

The midterms last week handed Democrats the Congress in January, and the bill's own backers make the hurry argument out of that: Trump's support won't survive, so it's now or never. But "now" is priced for the buyers standing closest to the counter, and the buyers standing closest are the Petroleum Institute and the datacenter builders, not the solar co-ops. Real reform can be written in five weeks without selling anything: fund the reviewers, set hard deadlines with staff behind them, fix the interconnection queue, put the transmission lines and the solar interconnection on a fast track where the review is real and the clock is short, pay the counties that host the lines, and keep the courtroom open. Speed from capacity, not from silence.

I know a smaller version of this trade from my own bench. John Deere spent years keeping its repair manuals and service software behind dealer logins, so a farmer with a tractor down in planting season couldn't open his own machine without the company's permission. The fight over that practice is public record — a farmers' class action that survived the motion to dismiss, a federal enforcement case still going against the company, and a class settlement announced this spring. The point of the lock was never the manual. The point was the standing. Once the company controls who may open the machine, the machine belongs to the company no matter whose name is on the title. This bill does the same thing to counties at scale. You keep your land, your road, your well, your courthouse address. Somebody else holds the key to whether you can act on any of it.

President Trump carried the rural counties two years back on promises to defend their way of life, and the bill he endorsed with enthusiasm makes that way of life easier to run over. The Democratic senators who helped write it — Whitehouse and Heinrich among them — signed onto the same trade, willing to spend somebody else's standing to buy speed for wind farms. That's its own problem. You don't get to spend another county's counterweight on your project and call it a compromise. A group of Republicans and Democrats wrote it together, which in Adams County is not a selling point; it's a warning — consolidation has never checked party registration, and neither does the statute that serves it. And the title does the usual double work. American Affordability and Jobs. Affordability for whom — the pipeline owners' margins, the server farms' power bills? The jobs are a short construction crew and a skeleton shift. What the county keeps is permanent: the line, the draw, and a shorter day in court.

None of this lets me off the hook. I read three papers a week through the cloud those buildings hold, and at nine at night in Friendship the alternative store is the Dollar General, and I use it. A person can live downstream of a machine and still be entitled to name what the machine is doing to the water. That's the whole reason the standing matters. The county's voice doesn't have to be innocent to count, and a right you may exercise only when you're pure is a right you don't have.

Mike is eight and Quinn is five, and they will inherit whatever the vote this week makes permanent. Leopold wrote in "Thinking Like a Mountain," in *A Sand County Almanac*, that conservation is a state of harmony between men and land. Harmony is not a word you use for a permit stream that closes the courthouse door behind it.

The courthouse on the square in Friendship went up in 1913 and 1914, and it is the oldest working promise this county has — that the people who live under a decision get their day, slow as the day runs. Leopold put the deeper version in the same book: "We abuse land because we regard it as a commodity belonging to us." A permitting bill that treats a county's objection as friction to be cleared has already answered the question of how it regards us. The Sand Hill mill closed in 2009, the year I graduated. The bank my mother worked at was bought and consolidated in 2014. The county's land-and-water department publishes the well numbers and the *Adams County Times-Reporter* prints them. Every one of those was sold as progress for somebody. I would like this one, for once, to be sold for what it is — the ground handed over, the check written to Washington, and the rest of us left reading about it in the dispatches. The Senate is selling the day and calling the sale a climate bill. The tacos were free. The right wasn't.
