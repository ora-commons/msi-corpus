The Federal Scholarship Tax Credit pressures public schools to become fee collectors.

Picture a school board meeting in the spring of 2027. A superintendent explains that, if the district joins the new federal scholarship program, it may have to charge families for field trips, assemblies, access to tutoring, aftercare, homework drop-in hours and the end-of-year party. Somebody in the back asks the obvious question: what about math class?

Math class stays free. It has to.

That scene is a warning, not a report that districts are already charging for field trips. The list came from Marguerite Roza of Georgetown University’s Edunomics Lab. Jessica Levin, litigation director at the Education Law Center, called the program a Trojan horse and said the fee-creating move would be “very wrong and very dangerous.” Neither woman’s proposal nor her objection is the governing law. The law is more specific.

Congress established the credit in section 705 of the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, Pub. L. No. 119-21, adding the Federal Scholarship Tax Credit to the Internal Revenue Code as section 25Z. Treasury and IRS guidance under that section, including REG-121709-25, defines the qualifying scholarships, educational expenses and state-certified organizations. Section 25Z also makes participation a state election: for the 2027 program, a state must opt in by Jan. 1. None of those authorities orders a school district to erect a tollbooth.

Public-school students can use scholarships for eligible tutoring, summer school, aftercare, computers and other educational expenses whether or not their district invents a fee for them. The program does not prescribe Roza’s menu. But if a district wants the program to reimburse services it supplies, the tax-credit machinery can pressure it to create and collect charges. That is a risk, not a mandate—and it is enough to poison the model.

Public education has constitutional footing. Colorado’s Constitution, article XIV, section 1, requires a “thorough and uniform system of free public schools.” North Carolina’s Constitution, article IX, section 5, says, “Education in public schools shall be free.” Georgia’s Constitution, article IX, section II, directs the creation of public schools free for children. The details differ from state to state, and those clauses do not make every pencil, field trip or school lunch constitutional offenses. They do mean a state cannot sell access to the public instruction it is required to provide. Whether a particular supplemental charge is lawful depends on the service and the state law. Neither Roza’s list nor Levin’s objection changes that division.

Here is the mechanism.

Beginning in January, qualifying taxpayers can donate as much as $1,700 to a scholarship-granting organization and reduce their federal income-tax bill by the same amount. The organization turns the donation into scholarships covering private-school tuition, tutoring, summer school, aftercare and other eligible expenses. “It’s not your money. It’s money that you would have sent to the IRS,” Roza said.

That is the first turn of the screw. Taxpayers finance the scholarship; the tax code hands the bill back to the donor; the scholarship lands wherever the organization decides. Choice for whom? At the first cut, the donor. Taxpayers choose the scholarship-granting organization. The organization then decides what the donation becomes.

“Choice” is becoming this administration’s favorite governing word, from [Trump and Vance back a bill letting merchants pick card networks](/articles/2026-10-09-trump-vance-back-bill-letting-merchants-pick-card-networks/) to school choice. Pick your network. Pick your school. Choice sounds voluntary until government builds the tracks, subsidizes the preferred route and calls withdrawal an individual decision. The verb is the product. In this program, the donor gets the first word, and the mechanics decide where the money goes.

Private schools enter the new market already built to sell. They set tuition prices. They have scholarship organizations, donor relationships, advertising budgets and administrative systems. Public schools do not. A private school can turn a tax credit into tuition revenue. A public school must first decide what it will charge and then persuade families to pay it.

The public school does not even enter the same market. It has to raise money the old way—from the families it serves.

“The most convoluted, circuitous way possible anybody could have ever laid out — and with all sorts of caveats in it,” Roza called the design. The money, she said, “is not showing up automatically.” Districts must alter what they charge, persuade employers to fold donations into payroll operations and chase money across community lines. A district that shrugs gets nothing. If helping public schools required only a few school-board votes, public-school advocates would welcome the program. Instead, they must reconstruct their institutions to resemble the private system the tax credit is designed to subsidize.

Levin expects “the vast majority of the money” to go to private-school tuition and other private education expenses because the infrastructure is already there. Public schools are not equal participants. They are customers invited to a table where the house already set the menu.

The scale is not small. The Institute on Taxation and Economic Policy estimates that more than 138 million people could qualify for the full credit. Treasury projects nearly $26 billion in scholarship donations by 2030—the gross volume this tax break is expected to unleash, with money routed through taxpayers rather than arriving as a government check.

For comparison, Title I, the largest source of federal education funding and the program aimed at low-income students, sent more than $18 billion directly to schools in 2023. Title I comes with a formula and a purpose. This comes with a donor and a deduction. If Congress wants tutoring, summer school or aftercare for public-school children, it can appropriate the money directly, target need, and preserve the principle that ordinary public education is free. Instead, it built a route through scholarship organizations, gave private schools a built-in customer base and asked cash-strapped districts to become fee-charging institutions before they could touch the money.

Then comes the part that decides who actually wins. Brookings researchers estimate that the wealthiest counties will raise roughly three times as much money through the program as the nation’s poorest counties. Donors give to organizations; those organizations give back to schools in their own backyards. The money tracks the donor map, not the need map.

Eligibility does not correct that imbalance. A family qualifies if the household earns under 300 percent of the gross median income in its area—roughly 95 percent of all students, by Treasury’s estimate. Jon Valant, a Brookings senior fellow, notes that some children qualify in families making $500,000 a year. Lower-income rural districts, meanwhile, must hope wealthy donors will give money beyond their own communities. Valant calls that unlikely.

“There’s a real chance that we end up seeing this program as Title I for rich kids,” Valant said. That is not a malfunction. It is what a donor-driven program does.

Which is why Democrats are tearing themselves apart, and why the tears are so predictable. Colorado Gov. Jared Polis was the first Democratic governor to sign up, calling it “an obvious decision.” “Of course I want Colorado kids to be the beneficiaries of that,” he said. Minnesota Gov. Tim Walz was asked whether Minnesota would opt in for 2027 and answered, “That is never going to happen. We are not doing it.”

Thirty-one states have signed on, most led by Republicans. Only Polis and New York Gov. Kathy Hochul joined voluntarily, while top Democrats in Kansas, Kentucky and North Carolina fought the program and were overridden by Republican legislatures. Fifteen Democratic governors and the mayor of Washington, D.C., were still undecided. Jan. 1 was the deadline.

“The policy is designed to try to make these [political] wedges,” Levin said. A Democratic governor who signs gets attacked by her own party for privatizing education. A Democratic governor who refuses hands private schools the money and loses the argument at home. So the choice is not principle against principle. It is who blinks.

The refusal is cleaner. The bait is not.

There is also an asymmetry nobody mentions. Opt in, and a state’s families qualify for scholarships. Stay out, and the state’s taxpayers can still donate and take the full $1,700 back from the IRS while families at home remain locked out. The cost is national; the benefit is local. States can make the election year by year, joining for 2027 and later leaving or returning, while the federal deduction remains available. Governors who join can be blamed locally for subsidizing private education while much of the tax benefit flows elsewhere.

That is why the program is politically potent even before a single scholarship is awarded. It makes privatization look like assistance. It makes refusing assistance look like hostility to struggling families. And it makes the public school’s nonparticipation look like an ideological choice rather than the preservation of a legal and moral principle.

The Southern Education Foundation knows the trap. Created after the Civil War to guarantee schooling for formerly enslaved children, the organization long fought private-school vouchers. Now its leaders are forming an alliance of scholarship organizations designed to steer donations toward public-school students. Its president and CEO, Raymond C. Pierce, does not pretend this is a victory: “The question for me became less about whether I agreed with every aspect of the program, because I don’t, and more about what happens now that the program is moving forward.”

Former Tulsa Public Schools superintendent Deborah Gist founded a national organization for public-school students after reading the bill language and seeing that public schools were included. “I pulled up the bill language itself and saw right there that public schools were very explicitly included,” she said. Fred Jones Jr. says public-school students will benefit only if somebody deliberately organizes to steer the money. Without that effort, he says, it will flow to private schools.

Their pivot deserves credit for pragmatism, not endorsement. The foundation’s history gives this fight moral stakes, but the present case cannot rest on memory. It rests on receipts: the threefold gap between wealthy and poor counties, the donor map replacing the need map, and rural communities that cannot expect wealthy outsiders to rescue their schools through charitable giving. Those are not abstract inequalities. They determine which children receive help and which schools must find their own way.

None of this means a family should be denied a scholarship for tutoring. The money can be real and the architecture still be wrong. Where states have joined, public-school advocates should fight for every lawfully available dollar for tutoring, summer learning and other supplemental services. They should not pretend that tactical participation is strategic victory. Every new fee schedule, donor relationship and scholarship bureaucracy built to exploit the program is another concession to privatization.

Levin has the right line: “The best way to support public schools is to fund public schools. It is not to accept a privatization program that throws a few crumbs at public school students.”

The Trojan horse has a tax code, a political wedge and a deadline. It does not need a single district to start charging for a field trip. It only needs enough governors to mistake private benefit for public good, and enough institutions to mistake pressure for permission.

Call it “choice,” and make the public pay for the exit. Jan. 1 is the deadline. Much of the money will find private schools either way. The real question is whether public schools get a piece—and what they must become to take it.
