Pricing discipline is the upstream of economic growth. On Holding builds running shoes with automated manufacturing, refuses to discount them, and reported direct-to-consumer sales up 26% last quarter while wholesale demand slowed — not because consumers left, but because the company pulled back shipments to retailers operating in a more promotional environment. Total revenue climbed 13.5%, to 850.3 million Swiss francs, roughly $1.04 billion. The company chose margin over channel volume. Consumers paid full price.

The pricing model works because the manufacturing infrastructure underwrites it. On Holding invested in robotic assembly, vertical integration, and a direct retail footprint — capex that compresses unit costs and converts the savings into design iteration rather than promotional subsidy. The result is a product cycle that compounds: lighter shoe, lower unit cost, full retail price, margin reinvested in the next cycle. This is the mechanics of pricing power, and it requires two things most American retailers have spent a decade avoiding: patient capital deployed in productive capacity rather than financial engineering, and a customer relationship that does not depend on the next markdown to close the sale.

The tradeoffs are real and worth naming. On Holding's wholesale partners — the department stores and specialty chains that built the brand's shelf presence — absorbed a deliberate pullback in shipments. For those retailers, the arithmetic is straightforward: less inventory from a high-demand brand means less foot traffic and less margin in a channel already under pressure. The company chose direct-over-wholesale, and wholesale bore the cost. Consumers face a different barrier. At $150 to $180 per pair, On Holding's footwear is priced beyond the reach of a significant share of American households. The pricing discipline that protects margin also protects exclusivity. What the company calls a promotional-free model, a household spending $60 on last season's markdowns calls a different word. Neither description is wrong; they describe different positions in the income distribution.

The replication barrier is the part the growth-on-autopilot crowd ignores. Building a product line that commands full retail price requires sustained capital investment in automation, supply-chain control, and direct distribution — years of spending before the unit economics flip. On Holding's 26% direct-to-consumer number is the payoff on years of that investment. The American retail sector, by contrast, has spent the last decade optimizing the opposite: inventory manipulation, channel stuffing, and promotional discounting that looks like sales growth until the customer stops showing up. When tariffs raise import costs and Amazon exerts marketplace leverage, the discount model has no margin to absorb either. It cuts SKUs and prays.

The human cost lives in the gap between those two models. The workers at promotional-dependent retailers — floor associates, warehouse staff, the labor force that keeps the discount cycle running — face a restructuring they did not design. The legacy retail model that employed them was already marginal; the On Holding model does not employ them at all. Automated assembly shifts the labor demand toward maintenance technicians and engineers, roles that pay more but exist in fewer locations and require credentials the displaced workforce does not hold. The GAO found that federal workforce-retraining programs reach only a fraction of displaced workers and produce measurable earnings recovery in a smaller fraction still. The transition from promotional retail to automated manufacturing is a real economic gain. Whether it is a net gain depends on who bears the cost of getting there. Right now, the answer is the workers and communities on the losing side of the channel shift.

Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent told an audience at Southern Methodist University last week that 3% growth lets the country grow its way out of the debt crunch — that even with trillions more in obligations, the load is manageable if the economy booms. The JCT's current-law baseline projects federal interest costs approaching 6% of GDP by 2035, more than doubling from the current trajectory. Sustaining 3% real growth requires capital investment in productive capacity — manufacturing, infrastructure, workforce skills — not more financial engineering. On Holding invested the capital. The question Bessent does not answer is what happens when growth does not materialize, which in the historical record it usually does not.

On Holding's 26% direct-to-consumer growth is the receipt: a company that invested in making things people pay full price for, rather than borrowing money and hoping the economy outgrows the debt service.
