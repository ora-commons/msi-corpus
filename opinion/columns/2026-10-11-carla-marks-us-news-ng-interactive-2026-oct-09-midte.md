We work more and fall further behind. Nine Americans were asked what matters most about this country, and seven of them told you where it hurts — wages that don't keep up, labor that vanished, cruelty that got licensed, a war that can't be won, a passport that's now a daily companion. The other two — a twenty-two-year-old conservative in South Carolina who calls the Greenland deal a masterstroke, and a seventy-year-old retired Tennessean who says her life is better and her grandchildren are safer — see a country on the right track. The seven told you where it hurts. The two told you what the leadership wants to hear.

In Detroit, an economics student is doing the math his grandfather did. The same wage that once raised six kids cannot now raise one. His pickup cost eighty dollars to fill two months ago; now it costs a hundred and twenty, and he can already see a hundred and fifty coming. The companies posting record profits are not the ones writing his check. The arithmetic does not negotiate. The math that broke a Detroit grandfather's wage is the [same cost-of-living test](/articles/2026-10-07-guardian-editorial-frames-us-midterms-as-cost-of-living-democracy-test/) this cycle keeps returning to.

In the Rio Grande Valley, a builder cannot find workers, because the labor force that used to frame her houses has been told it is not welcome. Houses take longer, interest rates climb, every closing cost moves up. The supply shock started at the labor line and touched every block in town.

In Tallahassee, a nineteen-year-old watched his parents close a twenty-year business because tariff whiplash made planning impossible. They are starting over. The country is starting over too — slower than the family.

The cruelty is not a footnote. In Wisconsin, an independent voter names it: permission. A leadership that gave a sector of the country permission to be cruel in public, and the recovery from that permission will outlast the men who issued it. In Georgia, a Venezuelan American carries her passport in a country that is supposed to be hers, and a thirty-two-year sense of belonging is gone. The cruelty is not loud. It is constant, and it is licensed.

The war is the third wound. In Georgia, a voter looks at a trillion-dollar military stalled by a country a fraction of its size and says the only path left is to walk away and spin the press. In Detroit, a veteran measures his words because the leadership he served now sits beside an indicted war minister abroad. The body counts are not on the podium. They never are.

Now the opposition. The Democrats are not loved. Nobody in the panel loved them. And the plan on offer — win first, reform after — is the most expensive sentence in American politics, and it has now been billed twice.

Walk me through it slowly. The strategy is to promise the median voter nothing that would materially change their Tuesday, on the theory that the other side will eventually lose the argument for them. Then, having won on a platform of arithmetic-free reassurance, the reform. Presumably in year three of the term nobody can promise. Two presidential losses in a row. I'm told this time the discipline will pay off. It has a certain zen quality: the promise of future material benefit is itself too material, and must be withheld until after the victory that only material benefit would produce. At some point the restraint stops being strategy and starts being the excuse. Working families do not need to be reassured harder. They need a number on a paystub that covers the rent — and the reason the number is short is not a mystery of nature. Somebody set it short, and someone else is paid to keep it there.

Which brings me to the other half of that panel, the part nobody built a message around. David Ferrari asked for policies that help people's lives in a real material way. That is not a mood. It's a shopping list, and the stores are already open.

Start with the Detroit student's arithmetic, because America already ran his experiment. In 2021 the country paid a monthly, near-universal child allowance — the expanded Child Tax Credit — and the Census Bureau measured what happened: child poverty by the Supplemental Poverty Measure fell from 9.7 percent to 5.2 percent in a single year, down 46 percent, with 2.9 million children lifted out. Then the credit lapsed and poverty came back. That is not a Scandinavian fairy tale. That is an American controlled experiment, run on ourselves, measured and reversible. The math is already in. It says the number on the paystub is a policy choice, and so is its size.

Justin Daniels does math for a living — or is training to. So do the institutions that would move his number:

Healthcare that doesn't ride on the job. Sick people make terrible shoppers; you don't price-compare an ambulance. Single-payer — Medicare extended to everyone — ends the arrangement where the Rio Grande Valley builder's coverage and the Detroit student's coverage both hang on a single employer's payroll. It also ends job-lock, which is a wage issue dressed as a health issue: you cannot bargain, cannot quit, cannot start over in Tallahassee, when quitting means your family's coverage dies with the job.

Wages set across a whole industry instead of one workplace at a time. That is sectoral bargaining — the reason a French welder and a Danish home aide never had to win a union drive to get a decent rate. American bargaining coverage sits at about 11 percent. Modeling from the Center for American Progress puts a sectoral system at 29 percent — 16.5 million covered workers becoming 42.4 million. That is the difference between hoping your employer is generous and having the floor set where your competitors' wages are set. The builder can't find workers because the labor market told an entire workforce it wasn't welcome; a wage floor across construction doesn't fix the cruelty, but it stops it from being free.

Workers owning the building they work in. Mondragon in the Basque Country has done this for seventy years — 70,000 worker-owners, roughly 11 billion euros of business, a pay ratio around five to one instead of the three hundred American boardrooms chose. You don't need to go to Spain. About 15 million Americans already own their workplace through employee ownership plans, holding over two trillion in assets; the home-care cooperative in the Bronx has paid working women a living wage for four decades in the sector private equity strips for parts. When the Tallahassee family starts over, a co-op is what keeps the next twenty years of gains in that town instead of extracting them to a headquarters three states away.

Money that lends to the town instead of to the deal. The Bank of North Dakota has run profitably, under public ownership, since 1919. Nobody in Bismarck has ever called it the Kremlin. Patient public capital is precisely the antidote to tariff whiplash — the thing that lets a twenty-year business plan around a twenty-year loan instead of a quarterly one.

None of this is one election away, and I won't pretend otherwise. Nordic plumbing runs on institutions America mostly lacks — union density, organized employers, state capacity built over a century, not a campaign. That iceberg is real, and anyone selling you the tip without it is selling you something. But the honest sorting still matters: a child allowance and better bankruptcy rules for small businesses can pass at state level next year; wage boards in construction and home care exist in several states already; a public bank needs one legislature. Provisional steps, as a Swedish finance minister once put it — the achievable next thing, not the perfect end-state.

The panel did the arithmetic on the country. The seven hurting voters aren't asking for a message and they aren't asking for a leader who inspires them. They are asking for the number to move. So move the number: floor set across the trade, healthcare off the paystub's leash, the gains from the next Tallahassee business kept in Tallahassee, and cash to the families who will otherwise be doing this math again in 2030. The economy is a set of choices, not the weather. Now build the thing that changes the answer.
