Clinton and Talbott disarmed Ukraine and left Bucha to Russian artillery.

Bucha first. The suburb north of Kyiv where Russian troops shot civilians in the streets in March 2022. Mariupol after it, and Kherson, where the lists of names run longer than the lists of readers. The weapons are artillery, glide bombs, and drones, aimed at cities. The war is the largest in Europe since 1945, and Ukrainian civilians die of Russian fire every day it runs.

On the other side of the guns are Russian conscripts from the poorer provinces, men from towns with one factory and no bargaining power, ordered into a fight their fathers' country started and their fathers' government could not stop. The old song had it right: the men sent to fight are not the men whose fathers run the policy. That cuts both ways. Our own enlisted ranks come out of the same kind of country. The Defense Department's own *Population Representation in the Military Services* reports show where the force comes from, and the answer is working-class, rural, and small-town America, while the national-security establishment that sets strategy comes out of the colleges the enlisted ranks do not. The men who carry the ammunition do not get to write the war. Neither do their fathers.

Bill Clinton's words after Talbott's death were generous: "Strobe was a good and brilliant man who made our world more peaceful and secure." Talbott died Thursday at eighty, at his home in East Hampton, New York, after a long illness, and [the obituary this paper carried](/articles/2026-11-26-strobe-talbott-clinton-era-diplomat-and-brookings-president-dies-at-80/) runs the whole arc: Yale and Oxford, where he roomed with a future president and translated Khrushchev's leaked memoirs while Clinton cooked his breakfast; twenty-one years at *Time* writing on Soviet-American relations; deputy secretary of state from 1994; the architect of the administration's approach to Russia and the other former Soviet republics; the nuclear negotiations with India after its 1998 tests; fifteen years running Brookings; and the chairmanship of the State Department's Foreign Affairs Policy Board. His pedigree was not the problem. A policy can be made by the best-credentialed people in the room and still be paid for by people who never enter it.

Clinton's eulogy gives grief the first sentence, and grief is earned. History gets the second. "More peaceful and secure" is not a eulogy. It is a verdict. An estimate that hides the labor is not kindness. It is a false verdict.

The decisions are on paper, and paper is where an argument gets checked.

In December 1994 the United States put its name to the Budapest Memorandum. Ukraine, which had inherited the third-largest nuclear arsenal on earth, gave it up, in exchange for security assurances from Washington, London, and Moscow. Assurances, not Article 5. NATO's Article 5 is the clause that says an attack on one member is an attack on all. Assurances are a diplomatic promise. Ukraine was never under the alliance, and one of the three states that signed the assurances later invaded the country. That does not pencil out as security for a nation that just gave away its guns.

In 1997 came the NATO-Russia Founding Act, written to persuade Moscow that enlargement and partnership could share the same house. That February, George Kennan, ninety-three years old, the man who wrote containment in the first place, published an essay in the *New York Times* called "A Fateful Error," warning that moving the alliance east would restore the Cold War's atmosphere and count among the gravest mistakes of post-Cold War American policy. That June, an open letter to President Clinton from national-security veterans of both parties said the same thing: expansion would decrease security on the continent, not increase it. Fourteen months later the Senate ratified the first enlargement round eighty to nineteen, and Poland, Hungary, and the Czech Republic came in. At Prague in November 2002 the administration invited seven more states in for 2004; Albania and Croatia came in 2009, Montenegro after that, Finland in 2023, Sweden in 2024. The door was open, and the line kept moving east. Talbott carried the Russia file inside that administration, and his memoir of those years, *The Russia Hand*, makes the case that a democratic Russia could be talked into partnership around enlargement. He was not alone in making it. He was the one carrying it.

Follow the money, because the money is the part of the argument a reader can check.

When a country joins NATO, it agrees to field forces that can fight beside everybody else in the alliance: Western-built jets, tanks, trucks, radios, radars, and missiles. It sends officers to joint schools and soldiers to exercises. It hosts allied units, or hosts them on its own soil. Every one of those lines comes from a short list of yards. In 1993, at a meeting the press called the Last Supper, Secretary of Defense Les Aspin and Deputy Secretary William Perry signaled the buy-down of roughly fifty prime contractors, and by the end of the 1990s the defense industry had consolidated into five: Lockheed Martin, RTX, Boeing Defense, Northrop Grumman, and General Dynamics. Those yards build the F-35 and the F-15EX, Aegis and THAAD and PAC-3, Tomahawk, the B-21 and the Sentinel, the Virginia- and Columbia-class submarines, Abrams and Stryker. Fort Worth assembles the fighters. Groton lays the keel on the submarines. Congress writes the appropriation, those yards cash the check, and the money moves out of defense appropriations into a handful of towns.

Every round of enlargement added customers, bases, depots, and training lines, and made the next round easier to sell, to Congress, to the ministries of defense, and to the men who draft the maps for the next war. In Fort Worth and Groton and the towns around them, that money shows up as a paycheck, an apprenticeship slot, a mortgage payment, a child's school. It does not show up in the debate that decides whose child pays. Bucha, Mariupol, and Kherson are not diplomatic footnotes. They are the bill.

That machinery had a name long before enlargement gave it a growth plan. Eisenhower stood before the cameras on January 17, 1961, three days before leaving office, and put the warning in paragraph twenty-four of his farewell address: "In the councils of government, we must guard against the acquisition of unwarranted influence, whether sought or unsought, by the military-industrial complex." The Cold War ended. The machinery did not. Andrew Bacevich's *Washington Rules* names the creed that filled the vacancy: hold global military preeminence, keep permanent presence, keep intervention open anywhere on earth. A creed nobody ever voted on. Enlargement gave that creed a growth plan, and the peace dividend was refused, quietly, by both parties, before most Americans noticed it had been offered. The institutions that grade the policy draw support from the same security economy the policy feeds. The think-tank sector runs on defense-industry, government, and foundation money, and the donor lists are public, for the reader to weigh every time the policy gets a good grade. That is not a conspiracy. It is a funding architecture.

Barbara Tuchman built a checklist for decisions like that one: policy contrary to the country's own interest, chosen by a group, with a feasible alternative named at the time, and persisted past the arrival of contrary evidence. Walk this one. The alternative was named in print by the most senior statesman of the Cold War fourteen months before the vote, and the group chose anyway, on a recorded vote. The contrary evidence arrived on schedule: Putin at Munich in February 2007 rejecting what the 1990s had agreed; Bucharest in April 2008 declaring Ukraine and Georgia future members of the alliance with no timeline attached; Crimea in February 2014; the full invasion on February 24, 2022. Folly is not stupidity. It is what the best rooms produce when the alternative is sitting on the table and the vote happens anyway.

The accounting should be exact, because these men deserve precision instead of blame scattered across forty years. Clinton and Talbott owned these: the Russia strategy of enlargement plus engagement; the Budapest Memorandum and the assurances that came with it; the NATO-Russia Founding Act; the first enlargement vote; and the seven invitations at Prague in 2002. They inherited the rest. The later wars in Chechnya, the pressure on the neighboring states, the war in Georgia in 2008, Crimea in 2014, and the invasion in 2022 were other men's decisions, made in other rooms, under other presidencies. The men who ordered the shelling of Bucha are not Clinton and Talbott, and I will not pretend otherwise.

And I will not pretend the other thing either. Nobody can prove that a vote in 1998 pulled a shell into a street in Bucha. What can be shown is what was decided, in whose rooms, on whose advice, and what the decision was built to do. They decided to take away Ukraine's nuclear shield in exchange for assurances that were not a treaty, and to move a military alliance to Russia's border on the argument that partnership and expansion could live in the same house. They decided that a state invested in the Western order would keep the promises made inside it. What that cost: a country with no deterrent left, and thirty years later, artillery in its cities. Everything after that was Russia deciding whether the paper held.

The discipline that failed in that room is missing from this one. This paper reported that the sitting president calls Vladimir Putin a friend, and that an official of his administration [says he trusts the president completely on exactly that](/articles/2026-11-22-clayton-says-he-trusts-trump-completely-on-calling-putin-a-friend/). A president's description of a dictator is a mood, not a strategy. When foreign policy rests on what one man thinks of another, Congress and its allies are left holding moods to interpret. The men who built the enlargement order at least had Kennan to overrule, and they had to write the overrule down. The defense running the Russia file today names no standard at all, and the standard that convicts Talbott convicts any room that governs without writing down what it refuses to hear.

A constitutional posture worthy of the Republic would put decisions that move this country's security commitments across a continent's fault lines into open argument: the named dissent seated in the hearing room at full charge, the warning in the record next to the vote, and the public record corrected when the warnings prove out. Congress should not accept a president's friendship as a substitute for strategy. The Constitution gives Congress the power to declare war, to authorize armed force, and to ratify treaties, so any new use of force, any new mutual-defense commitment, and any new treaty guarantee concerning Russia and Ukraine should come to Congress with explicit authority, clear limits, and a defined end state. The Senate and the House cannot hand the cost of private diplomacy to Ukrainian civilians and call the cost security.

Clinton remembers breakfast at Oxford and a dead friend's good name. Talbott's Russia policy is paid for in Ukrainian graves, and in the graves of Russian conscripts from provinces that never had a seat at any table. The eulogy calls that peace. The graves call it murder.
