Tell it to the woman paying 21% on her Discover bill while her rent goes up another $300. That is the whole column, in one sentence, and everything below is just the accounting.

The Bureau of Economic Analysis says the economy grew. Your kitchen-table budget says it didn't. And Freeman wants you to believe that's a difference of opinion rather than a difference of wallet. Let's concede the true half, because I'm generous: output grew. The BEA's quarterly readout is a real number, produced by serious people at the Commerce Department, and it went up. Freeman is therefore technically correct that the economy — in the sense economists use that word, which is roughly the sense a coroner uses the word "body" — is not dead. Wonderful. Truly thrilling. Now explain the rent.

The move he makes is old, it is elegant, and it is garbage. He takes an aggregate — total output, GDP, the sum of every transaction in the country — and he presents it as evidence about households. As if "the economy" were a single family with a single checking account. It isn't. GDP is a river. Freeman points at the river and tells the people standing downstream holding empty cups that the river has never been fuller.

Watch what the river doesn't know. GDP can't tell the difference between a hospital that cures you and a hospital that bankrupts you — both produce output, both count. It can't tell the difference between a business growing its revenue and a landlord raising your rent; the first is "investment," the second is "housing services," and they go in the same column pushing the same line upward. Your credit card interest payment is personal consumption expenditure. Your payday loan at three-hundred-something percent is financial services. Your auto insurance premium is consumption too. When Freeman celebrates the top line, he is, in the most literal accounting sense available, celebrating the fact that you are being charged more — more efficiently, and with far better coverage of the aggregate. It is the policy equivalent of diagnosing a patient by reading the lobby's thermostat. Aggregate output rising while the median household pays north of twenty-one percent on revolving credit is not a contradiction with the BEA print. It *is* the BEA print, read in the language of the people who live inside it.

So here are the bills, in the language of the people who live inside it. The Bureau of Labor Statistics' Consumer Price Index carries shelter — rent, and what owners pay themselves — as the single heaviest component of the basket, roughly a third of the whole index. It kept climbing long after the goods side of the economy calmed down: CPI shelter rose near nine percent year over year at its peak and was still running around four percent years later, by which point Freeman's favorite "inflation is over" narrative had already gone to print. The line on the chart went up. So did your rent. Freeman has strong opinions about which of those matters.

The New York Fed's Household Debt and Credit Report — the document, not the vibes — puts the average credit-card rate above twenty-one percent, and has had it there for going on two years. Twenty-one percent is not a market-clearing price of capital. It's a usury floor maintained by issuers who know their customers can't pay and who have, across forty-five years of usury-cap repeal and preemption, been told the market will sort it out. The market sorted it out: a single number, twenty-one, repeated across every issuer, every quarter, in the Fed's own data. The economy grew, and the price of carrying a balance grew faster than the economy did.

S&P's insurance data and the industry's own reporting put the year-over-year jump in auto premiums at around twenty percent, the fastest in decades, in a category no working household can opt out of if it wants to keep a job. The S&P figures didn't show twenty percent premium growth because American drivers got worse in a year. They showed it because replacement costs exploded, reinsurance hardened, and the deregulated market passed every single dollar through to the household. The economy grew. You now pay roughly a fifth more for the privilege of driving to it.

And the Census Bureau's American Community Survey keeps returning the same verdict on housing: roughly half of all renter households in America spend more than thirty percent of their income on rent, roughly twenty-two percent are severely cost-burdened paying half or more, and in the expensive metros the recovery story gets told about, the typical rent-to-income ratio runs past forty percent. That is not a market finding. The rent-burden number isn't a flaw in the measurement. It is the measurement of a flaw in the policy.

And here is what the aggregate is hiding while it grows. Household share of national income falls. Corporate profits as a share of GDP sit at post-war highs. Labor's share sits at post-war lows. Aggregate output rising over that configuration is not a vindication. It's a confession about whose economy is being grown.

Now the part where Freeman tells you the answer is to deregulate — zoning, permits, certificates of need, more supply, and the prices will sort themselves out. We have been deregulating the supply side of housing since the late seventies, when the federal government stopped building at scale. That agenda has had forty years. The kitchen table still looks like this. Public housing in America peaked above a million units and has been shrinking ever since, while the private market that was supposed to fill the gap handed you a rent-to-income ratio you'd have laughed at in 1975. Public housing once housed a third of New Yorkers; the defunding was a choice, and the re-funding is one too. Housing Choice Vouchers chasing the same exhausted stock don't solve this — they inflate the rents around them. The supply story isn't wrong. It's just not a household story. It tells you how many units exist. It does not tell you who owns them, who sets the price, or why there is a landlord's spreadsheet standing between you and the wrench. The gap between what the Census counts and what the country needs is a construction program, not a tax credit.

So here is what I would build instead. Five institutions. Each of them already exists somewhere on this earth. Each of them does the one thing the supply agenda never will: it changes who holds the claim on the output.

A real public healthcare option. Not a study. Not a pilot. An actual plan — negotiated rates, federal buying power, open to anyone at any age — using the network logic Medicare already runs and administrative costs in the low single digits instead of the double digits private insurers carry. A public option isn't charity. It's a price ceiling on the entire market. Every private insurer in the country would suddenly have to explain a premium that a federal competitor undercuts by a third. The integrated delivery networks and pharmacy benefit managers have been extracting the difference between what other rich democracies pay and what we pay for twenty years; the public option ends the transfer. Not as a thought experiment — as a procurement decision. You want aggregate output to mean something? Start by subtracting the part that goes to shareholders every time somebody gets sick.

Public and social housing production at scale. Roughly three in five people in Vienna live in municipal or limited-profit social housing, and they have for a century, and their rent-to-income ratio is a rounding error next to Miami's. About four-fifths of Singapore's population lives in public housing. We used to do a version of this — a million-plus units, a third of New York — and then we stopped building and called it reform. So build again: a federal capital stack, local authorities with real development capacity, mixed-income, mission-driven, on publicly owned or community land trust ground, housing that pays for itself through its own rents instead of through the fantasy that the private market will eventually grow a conscience. Housing isn't a commodity we should be betting on. It's the thing people need before they can do anything else.

Sectoral wage boards. Not one federal minimum fighting fifty state cost-of-living differences by losing. Minimums set per industry and region — care work, food service, logistics, the industries that never stopped being essential and never started being paid like it — enforceable against every employer in the sector rather than just the ones too broke to hire a lawyer, with automatic indexation to median wages in that sector. The New York fast-food wage board is the proof of concept; the fast-food wage board everywhere is the policy. Walmart pays what the board says, or doesn't operate. Austria runs its economy this way and covers almost its entire workforce. The old British wages boards did versions of it before 1993. The machinery exists. We stopped using it because "collective bargaining" started sounding like 1975 — and 1975, for the record, sat at the front end of the fastest wage growth in modern American history. Interesting coincidence.

A public or postal banking option. The United States ran a Postal Savings System from 1911 to 1966 — deposits at the post office, because the post office is already in every town in the country and the private banks had already shown what they'd do with a monopoly on that fact. It is not a proposal. It is a record. Bring it back: basic checking, small-dollar credit at single-digit rates, no fees, no minimums, priced at cost, in the places where the nearest financial institution is a payday lender running APRs past three hundred percent or a check-cashing storefront taking a cut of your paycheck before you've spent a dollar of it. The post office is the one network in America legally required to go everywhere. Use it.

Worker-cooperative conversion financing. Here's a fact Freeman never mentions: a great many small-business owners are at or nearing retirement over the next decade, and they are going to sell. The only open question is to whom — private equity, which strips and flips, or the people who actually run the place. Make the second answer cheap: when a private owner retires, the workers get first right of refusal, backed by federal loan guarantees, patient capital from a public development bank, and technical assistance for employee buyouts structured on a one-worker-one-vote basis, so the gains from the output land with the people who produced them instead of getting skimmed off the top on their way up the chart. Italy's Marcora law has been doing this since 1985. Mondragón is the textbook. Cleveland's Evergreen Cooperatives have been proving the model for more than a decade. A 2018 federal law nudged the Small Business Administration into the employee-ownership business. Scale it.

That's what building looks like — not deregulating the supply of houses, but building the institutions that let the people who make the output keep some of it.

Freeman will tell you this is all a distraction from the real story, which is that the pie grew. Sure it did. The pie grew, and the people who own ovens are absolutely delighted. Somebody waves the GDP print like a get-out-of-jail card and asks why all the whining — at that point they're not making a case, they're performing one, and the byline is less interesting than the argument. Meanwhile the average household is paying twenty-one percent on a credit card to buy groceries at prices the CPI keeps describing as "moderating" — and "moderating" is doing an enormous amount of work in that sentence, because it means the rent is still going up, just slightly less fast than the thing that made it impossible.

The economy is not a person. It has no needs, no landlord, no insulin copay, no shift that starts before dawn. The only people who benefit from treating it as one are the people who already own it. Freeman isn't lying to you, exactly. He's measuring the river and telling the people downstream they're hydrated. The aggregate output number doesn't care whether any of this gets built — that's its feature, not its flaw. The household budget does care. The whole point of an economy is the second one.
