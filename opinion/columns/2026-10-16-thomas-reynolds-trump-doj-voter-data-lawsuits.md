The Trump Justice Department has spent thirteen months inventing legal pretexts to seize the voter rolls of thirty states and the District of Columbia. Those rolls hold confidential information on millions of Americans, and since September 2025 the Civil Rights Division has sued 31 times to get them unredacted. Judges have ruled against the department 26 times. It has won zero rulings. Four cases remain pending. One state settled. Every theory the department has put on paper has collapsed in writing, before judges appointed by presidents of both parties.

The bench is not a partisan instrument. Of the 26 randomly assigned judges, eight were appointed by President Trump, four by President George W. Bush, and 14 by Democratic presidents. Republican appointees made up nearly half the panel. They ruled like the rest: against the government. A legal theory that fails before the judges a president appointed himself is not a theory.

The first seven lawsuits openly invoked President Trump's March 2025 executive order on election integrity, claiming federal voting laws gave the department authority to demand the rolls. [As we reported earlier this week](/articles/2026-10-15-doj-has-lost-every-federal-ruling-sought-in-voter-records-cases-against-states/), every one of those cases collapsed. A federal judge in Maine captured what the courts saw: the department's case was "halfhearted," and no statute gives the United States the power to compel a state to turn over its current unredacted voter list.

The department did not take the hint. It pivoted.

The next 19 lawsuits dropped the executive order and dropped the federal voting laws. They relied instead on Title III of the Civil Rights Act of 1960 — a provision written at a moment when Black voters in the South were being driven off the rolls — and claimed it gave the attorney general "sweeping power" to obtain the records to combat alleged noncitizen voting.

Put at its strongest, the argument has a shape. The statute orders the preservation of election records, and "records" is not a narrow word: a voter roll is plainly an election record a state makes and maintains. Title III's history includes compelled disclosures to the attorney general, so the department could argue that a preservation statute carries a disclosure mechanism at its core — otherwise preservation is unenforceable. And if the department has a duty to investigate alleged noncitizen voting, the reasoning runs, it cannot investigate what it cannot see. A lawyer could file that brief and sleep at night.

The courts took it apart twice over. Title III, they ruled, applies to voter registration documents — not to records states generate themselves, like voter rolls. The textual hook does not reach. And in any event the department never gave the explanation the statute requires for why it needed the records at all. A West Virginia federal judge called the demand "devoid of any factual basis."

Then the contradiction the department never resolved. The voting laws the department claims it is enforcing require states to keep voter lists continuously updated. Title III forbids any alteration of voter records. Under the department's reading, a Maryland judge wrote, the law would "criminalize the same conduct" the statutes require — a result the court refused to reach. The preservation argument and the enforcement argument cannot both be true. The department advanced both.

What is left, once the statutes are read honestly, is the inversion — and the judges named it. A D.C. federal judge put the history on the record: Title III "was the Department of Justice's answer to two years of resistance and obstruction by state election officials who destroyed, failed to retain, or simply refused to disclose the voting records … that would prove that black voters were victims of disparate treatment and had been unlawfully excluded from the rolls based on nothing more than their race." The statute exists to protect the right to vote. The department now wields it to find out who exercised that right. The Sixth Circuit, in the only appeal decided so far, stated the flip plainly: "Back then, the government used this power to ensure that everyone who had the right to vote could freely exercise that right. But today, the government invokes Title III for an inverse purpose — to ensure that some people have not voted."

Several judges went past the statutes to the motive. A California federal judge wrote that "the Court does not take lightly DOJ's obfuscation of its true motives." A Pennsylvania judge wrote that public statements from officials reveal the actual intentions: to build "a nationwide voter-database, for potential weaponization in future elections; as a 'fishing expedition,' hoped to advance unsubstantiated claims of non-citizen voting; and as a tool for immigration enforcement" — the same database infrastructure the [administration has spent months trying to assemble from other directions](/articles/2026-09-26-supreme-court-allows-federal-database-to-check-voter-citizenship/). The Oregon judge went furthest. The presumption that the Justice Department acts in good faith — the presumption of regularity, the standing assumption that lets the federal government move through the courts without every filing treated as suspect — "no longer holds," he wrote. He called the conduct "chilling."

The department has appealed most of its losses and says it will consider taking the fight to the Supreme Court. On October 9, a district judge ruled the department's national effort to acquire and share state voter rolls an illegal abuse of its authority; a spokesperson said the department is "reviewing the ruling" — the same posture it has taken after every previous defeat.

Asked about the record, the department said its lawyers are "skilled, principled advocates who argue every case honestly and in good faith," and that "disagreement with the government's position is not evidence of bad faith." Twenty-six federal judges, appointed by presidents of both parties, have formally disagreed in writing. One court has withdrawn the presumption of regularity itself. A civil-rights statute written to stop states from destroying the records of Black disfranchisement is now in the hands of a department using it to compile a list of who voted — and every judge who has read the statute has said so out loud.
