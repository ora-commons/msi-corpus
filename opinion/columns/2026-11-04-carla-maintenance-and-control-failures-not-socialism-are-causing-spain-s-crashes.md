Spain’s rail crashes come from maintenance and control failures, not from socialism: high-speed passenger traffic rose about 69% from 2019 to 2025 while spending on network repairs and maintenance rose 18.3%. In [Another Train Wreck in Socialist Spain](https://www.wsj.com/opinion/another-train-wreck-in-socialist-spain-6dbe3208?mod=rss_opinion), *Wall Street Journal* Opinion writer Itxu Díaz argues that the deadly Adamuz and Urda crashes, the 23-hour blackout of 2025, and other infrastructure failures show how eight years of Pedro Sánchez’s Socialist government degraded Spain’s railways. He calls for a national reconstruction plan and makes the November 29 election a chance to replace Sánchez with Alberto Núñez Feijóo. The network is failing. The party label is the escape hatch.

The strongest honest version of Díaz’s case is this: maintenance is not decoration, and a repair budget growing 18.3% while high-speed traffic grows 69% is a warning sign. The visible service is outrunning the less visible work of inspections, renewals, and signaling. That demands an answer.

But be precise about what the comparison proves. Passenger counts are not wear. A carriage holding two hundred people and one holding twenty put the same number of axles over the same rail. What wears a track is train movements, axle passages, switches, crossings, signals, and new route assets. The 69% figure shows demand pulling harder on the network. The 18.3% figure shows what the owner spent answering it. Side by side, they measure a widening funding gap. They do not measure how many tonnes hit the rail. That number comes from train-movement and axle-load counts.

These are not abstractions. The Adamuz crash killed 46 and injured 292. The investigation appeared to establish deficient infrastructure maintenance. Urda has left 69 dead so far and dozens injured. Human error has not been ruled out there, but the train that was struck had reported a failure in the LZB signaling system about ten minutes before the collision. LZB tracks train positions and automatically applies the brakes when it detects anomalies. Whatever the final investigation finds, the state that owns the infrastructure cannot outsource responsibility for keeping it safe.

Transport Minister Óscar Puente has refused to resign. He says the ADIF control center authorized a train to move even though another train was stopped ahead. He called the decision inexplicable. The drivers’ union countered that the earlier LZB failure created the danger regardless of any later human error. On these facts, the government failed. That is a system failure, not a party diagnosis. We can say it without ideological fog.

The tell is the order of the evidence. First come the figures: 69% more high-speed passengers and 18.3% more repair and maintenance spending. Then come the failed signal, the control-center allegation, and the minister’s refusal to quit. Only afterward comes socialism. A signal does not check the party’s registration.

Spain is not Cuba or Venezuela. Its freight rail was opened to private operators in 2005, and two private passenger operators entered after full liberalization in 2020. It has a scheduled election and an opposition waiting in November. Those private operators are in Díaz’s own article. That is inconvenient for a Cuba-Venezuela comparison. You cannot borrow the word from one economic arrangement and pretend the institutions are interchangeable.

One historical concession belongs here: the one-party communist regimes of the twentieth century were catastrophes. Sánchez’s elected government in Madrid is not their institutional twin. It operates through private operators, public infrastructure, competitive elections, and an opposition that can replace it.

Walk me through it slowly. How does the presence of the Socialist Workers Party in Madrid deactivate an LZB system? Is there a party-detection feature I missed? What changed in 2018, when Sánchez took office: the concrete available for maintenance, the staffing of control rooms, the rules for dispatch, or merely the party label? If the answer is “only the label,” then socialism is not the mechanism. The maintenance backlog and the dispatch rules are.

The institutional story is right there. Lower fares and more passengers followed liberalization, though Díaz also lists more service, network expansion, population growth, and public subsidies as fuels. Infrastructure remained with ADIF, the state-owned company responsible for the rails, signals, and infrastructure-control centers. Private operators run services on that network. The usage side grew. More train movements put more wear on the same rails, switches, crossings, and signals. Maintenance has to rise with that load, not merely with the calendar. The upkeep side did not keep pace. That mismatch is where the safety risk sits.

The bill arrives as a collision. The people on the train pay. It is what happens when service expands faster than the systems meant to keep it safe.

The real split is between the people who gain from more traffic and the public agency that must pay for the wear. More train movements load rails, switches, and crossings. New routes add track and signaling that must be inspected and renewed. If operating support expands while renewal costs are postponed, the service looks cheaper on today’s budget and more expensive on tomorrow’s maintenance schedule. The cost does not disappear because operators are private. It changes address.

Díaz also reports that public subsidies were designed to encourage rail travel. So his ledger contains three connected facts: more passengers, public operating support, and repair spending that grew far more slowly. He never reconciles them. Public support expanded demand while the state retained the renewal bill. That split pushed the visible cost of travel onto future maintenance budgets. If service expansion is good, fund the maintenance it requires. If maintenance cannot be funded, slow the expansion. There is no third option, and there is no ideological branch on the signal panel.

None of this requires exonerating Sánchez. His government chose the budget mix, the expansion targets, the inspection system, and the maintenance backlog. His ministers own the maintenance decisions. If a signal reported failure ten minutes before impact, if the control center cleared a train onto occupied track, and if the minister has refused to resign, the government failed its job.

The 23-hour blackout of 2025 and the 2024 Valencia floods make the broader infrastructure case harder to dismiss. The conviction of former minister José Luis Ábalos for bribery, influence peddling, and misuse of public funds demands accountability. A minister’s conviction or social-media habits are separate moral and political questions. A pile of unrelated misconduct is not a cause. The article gives us accidents, a maintenance investigation, and a spending gap. It supplies party labels for the missing links. The labels are louder. The rails are still broken.

What does socialism explain that a budget does not? The maintenance backlog is visible. Traffic growth is visible. Failed signaling is visible. A dispatch decision is visible. Ministerial responsibility is visible. The vote is visible too, but it is not causal. Show that changing the party changes the concrete, the dispatch rules, or the maintenance budget, and socialism will do some work. If an election changes only the name above the ministry and leaves those things untouched, the next failure arrives on the same schedule. Díaz bears the burden of showing the mechanism.

His final question—why are Spaniards paying more in taxes while public services worsen—also misses the mechanism. A government can collect more and still spend badly. Show the budget, the contracts, and the inspections. Taxes are not a maintenance plan. Leave the fog machine parked.

I am not against private rail operators. I am against making the public hold the maintenance bill while the operating side gets the traffic. Until Díaz shows that the operating arrangements make operators bear the full cost of the extra maintenance their traffic creates, “more privatization” is not the answer, and “socialism” is not a cause.

A party change is not a maintenance plan. Spain builds a rail-maintenance floor, not another election slogan. Build a reconstruction plan that survives the next election.

Start with the institutional division already in place. Train operations can compete. The rails beneath them are a network, and safety rules cannot be customized by each operator. Keep ADIF responsible for infrastructure, but put rail safety under an independent regulator with published powers, inspections, and accident authority. That authority must be able to delay or stop service when safety requires it. Public infrastructure. Competitive operation. One safety standard.

Protect multiyear maintenance and renewal money from annual operating budgets and new-route announcements. Tie it to network condition and passenger traffic, not the calendar or the annual news cycle. If passenger traffic rose about 69% while repair and maintenance spending rose 18.3%, the budget formula failed before the trains did. Publish the maintenance backlog. Publish signal failures and near misses. Show whether money reaches track, signals, inspection, and staff before the timetable makes the decision for you.

Give drivers, maintenance crews, operators, and independent inspectors formal seats on safety councils with authority to pause expansion. The drivers’ union has already called for action over rail safety and called a strike on October 29 for greater safety. That should be the clue: the people closest to the risk are telling the government what the maintenance gap means. Not as a decoration. As part of the institution.

Make a reported LZB failure a hard stop. A failed signal should not become a controller’s discretion. Automatic protection and human dispatch need one rule: stop, isolate, investigate, resume. Staff the control rooms. Train the dispatchers.

Operators should pay the full cost of the wear their traffic creates through transparent access charges and contracts. If traffic cannot pay for its own maintenance, it does not get to expand. Spain can keep ADIF public and still give the public owner a hard safety floor. Public ownership describes who owns the asset; it does not excuse a raided maintenance budget.

Finally, audit the public commitments abroad. Díaz asks how Spain can fund rail improvements in Morocco, Egypt, and Uganda while its own network deteriorates. That is the right question. Each overseas project must show what domestic track, signal, and safety work, if any, it displaced. Each domestic project must show what condition it fixed.

This will be harder than swapping ministers. The network is public, passenger service includes private operators, and the political budget rewards visible trains over invisible maintenance. New signaling must be designed, installed, tested, and certified while trains keep running on the old network. That is why the reconstruction needs engineers, money, and years, not another slogan. The safety authority must be independent, and the maintenance money must last across electoral cycles. The plumbing is the medicine.

None of this requires a command economy. None requires pretending competition is bad. It requires public ownership where a network is indivisible, competitive operation where operators can compete, independent safety control, organized workers with bargaining power, and budgets that cannot be starved between elections.

Spain already has most of the map. ADIF is responsible for the infrastructure. Private operators run services. The union knows the system. Voters can change governments. Keep the first. Regulate the second. Seat the third. Make the fourth prove itself in audits and safety results.

Do not nationalize every train. Do not privatize every bolt. Fund the rails. Fix the signals. Stop the train when the system says stop.

The point is not to make rail ideological. It is to make maintenance impossible to raid between elections. Then build the trains. The market stays. The safety floor goes in. Everything else waits for the budget.

That is the reconstruction plan worth voting for.
