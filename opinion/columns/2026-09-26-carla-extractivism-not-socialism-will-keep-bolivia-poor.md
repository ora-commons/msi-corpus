Bolivia's poverty is not the inheritance of twenty years of socialism. It is the inheritance of a century and a half of shipping raw wealth abroad and calling the leftovers development. In a [Fox News op-ed](https://www.foxnews.com/opinion/president-paz-bolivia-rejected-socialism-open-business), President Rodrigo Paz Pereira says Bolivia has rejected socialism, is open for business, and is ready for "capitalism for everyone." He points to central-bank reserves rising from $51 million to roughly $1.2 billion in ten months. The number is real. The diagnosis is wrong. His own column gives it away.

A twenty-three-fold reserve increase does not mean Bolivia suddenly built a productive economy. It means dollars arrived. The jump reflects higher global natural-gas prices, the normalization of Bolivian gas exports to Brazil and Argentina after two years of political crisis, new external credit lines, and possibly the other familiar sources of emergency cash: IMF facilities, Chinese advance payments for lithium, dollar bonds, and asset sales. None of that was produced by inviting foreign mining capital. It was produced by the commodity-export machine Paz inherited, plus the credit that keeps the machine running.

The underlying economy did not create $1.15 billion in new value in ten months. The balance sheet found a new credit card.

Paz calls this putting the economic house in order. Fine. But a line of credit is not a factory. Reserves buy time. They do not buy productive capacity.

Here is where Paz is right, and it is worth saying plainly. Bolivia cannot keep digging up lithium, copper, tin, and gas, shipping them out, and hoping prosperity returns by mail. He is right that "ideology does not put food on the table." He is right that subsidized fuel, swollen state payrolls, and price controls did not deliver durable growth. The old state-led model genuinely needed reform.

But the answer to broken state-led extractivism is not foreign-led extractivism. That is not a break with the old model. It is the same mine with a different letterhead.

Paz says he does not want Bolivia to "export its natural resources for decades while remaining poor." Then he invites international investors to come extract those resources, promising that "long-term partnerships built around employment, training, technology, infrastructure, productivity, and value added in Bolivia" will follow. That promise has been printed so many times it should be sold as stationery.

Chile under Pinochet, Peru under Fujimori, the Democratic Republic of Congo, and Nigeria have all heard versions of it. The copper, lithium, tin, and oil leave as concentrate, unfinished metal, or crude. The training, technology, and value-added plants remain on the brochure.

"Open for business" has a very clear meaning in the lithium triangle. It usually means long concessions, low royalties, tax holidays, arbitration in foreign courts, and local-content rules that exist on paper while the ore crosses the border. American and Chinese capital are not arriving to perform a civic ritual. They are shopping for the lowest-cost way to own the cash flow.

That is the suppressed part of the story: who captures the resource rent, and who is left with the holes, the fuel queues, and the next balance-of-payments crisis?

The fuel shortage Paz mentions is not socialist residue waiting to be exorcised by a foreign investor. Bolivia imports most of its gasoline and diesel. Its refineries have been starved of maintenance for years. When dollars run short, fuel does not reach the pump. That is a balance-of-payments problem. It does not care whether the central bank is run by a Movement Toward Socialism appointee or a Paz appointee.

Privatize the whole economy and Bolivia will still struggle to refine its own gasoline if it has not imported the technology, trained the workers, maintained the equipment, and built the dollar-earning industries that pay for the fuel.

Inflation works the same way. Prices for cooking oil, school shoes, and bus fares are tied to imported goods. Dollars are scarce. When the currency weakens, ordinary life gets more expensive. That is a thin-industrial-base problem. The factories that could substitute for those imports do not exist. They did not exist in 2005 either.

A foreign mining concession does not solve that problem. It can deepen it. If the country exports raw material, imports finished goods, and sends the profits back out, the economy remains dependent on the next commodity price and the next foreign financing window. The slogans change. The dependency does not.

The countries that built industry on resource wealth did something less glamorous than opening a press release. South Korea taxed steel exports and subsidized domestic mills for two decades. Norway parked oil revenue in a sovereign wealth fund and required foreign operators to share technology. Chile kept majority state control of Codelco, built smelting and refining capacity at home, and accumulated a stabilization fund designed to outlast political cycles. Chile also forced copper producers to process more of the concentrate domestically before shipment.

None of this is a command economy. None of it abolishes markets. It is selective, patient industrial policy: you may extract here, but you will build here too, train workers here, transfer technology here, and follow a timetable set by the country that owns the resource.

That is the difference between attracting capital and governing it.

Bolivia's lithium should not be treated as a windfall to be sold by the ton. It is feedstock for a domestic battery, electric-vehicle materials, wire, and industrial-chemicals strategy. That requires the state to own the resource in the ground, negotiate contracts in public, require domestic processing, train Bolivian engineers, finance plants patiently, and protect the industry long enough for it to stand on its own.

It also requires an honest sovereign wealth fund. Put the rents somewhere that survives the next commodity downturn and the next president. Do not spend the boom, borrow against the future, and congratulate yourself when the reserves rise.

Paz's reserve number may buy Bolivia time. It will not buy Bolivia factories. Foreign capital may help build them, but only if the contracts make building them part of the deal rather than a decorative promise.

I'm not anti-market. I'm anti-extraction. The question was never whether Bolivia should trade. It is who owns the resource, who captures the gains, who bears the risk, and what remains when the ore is gone.

Bolivia does not need another permission slip for outsiders to take its wealth. It needs a state capable of making capital build at home: domestic refining, battery plants, trained workers, public contracts, a sovereign fund, and terms Bolivians can read before they sign.

Not a slogan. Not a foreign balance sheet with a Bolivian flag on the brochure. A program.
