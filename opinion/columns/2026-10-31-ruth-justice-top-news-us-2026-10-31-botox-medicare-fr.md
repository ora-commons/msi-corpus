Violetta Mailyan submitted more than $45 million in fraudulent claims to Medicare for Botox treatments that were unnecessary, cosmetic, or never administered, and a federal court sentenced her to 15 years in prison.

Mailyan, 45, of Glendale, ran the Healthy Way Medical Center. Medicare covers Botox for certain medical conditions, chronic migraines among them. It does not generally pay for cosmetic procedures. Mailyan claimed reimbursement anyway, and in volume.

Her method was plain. Investigators found claims for thousands of treatments supposedly performed on days when the clinic was closed. Those claims totaled more than $19 million. She submitted claims for California treatments while in Mexico, Hawaii, Las Vegas, Pennsylvania and New York. On one trip to Cabo San Lucas, she billed Medicare more than $200,000 for services supposedly provided while she was away.

Federal prosecutors charged her under the wire-fraud and obstruction laws. The U.S. Attorney’s Office for the Central District of California brought the prosecution. A federal jury convicted her in May on nine counts of wire fraud and three counts of obstructing a healthcare fraud investigation. The court imposed 15 years in prison, ordered more than $32.9 million in restitution to Medicare, and required surrender of more than $16 million in seized assets: real estate, investments and bank funds.

This is not a clerical error. It is fraud with a paper trail and a signature. A false appointment turns into cash faster than a patient can question it. Mailyan supplied the dates and the claims, wrote the records, forged the signatures, collected the money and spent it. The doctor chose to lie. When the investigation came, she chose again.

She is not alone in the docket. An Illinois man [pleaded guilty to a $227 million Medicare fraud scheme](/articles/2026-10-30-illinois-man-pleads-guilty-to-227-million-medicare-fraud-scheme/). An Oklahoma chiropractor [got 14 years for a $30 million fraud scheme targeting Medicare, TRICARE and CHAMPVA](/articles/2026-10-09/oklahoma-chiropractor-gets-14-years-for-30m-fraud-targeting-medicare-tricare-cha/). Different states. Different professions. The defendant changes. The scheme does not: a provider invents the treatment, files the claim, takes the money.

The law reached the people who wrote and certified the false claims. The Illinois defendant pleaded guilty. The Oklahoma chiropractor went to prison. Mailyan went to prison. Provider title did not purchase immunity.

Compare Wells Fargo, where the actor was a bank. In 2020, DOJ reached a $3 billion deferred-prosecution agreement over millions of unauthorized accounts. Its terms said the agreement was reached “with the bank itself, not with any individuals responsible for the fraud.” The agreement named no individual. One person was pursued separately: the OCC fined former CEO John Stumpf $17.5 million and barred him from banking. That was a civil penalty, not a prison sentence.

Here there was no bank to stand behind. There was Mailyan, the owner who submitted the claims, wrote the records and forged the signatures. One defendant here: Mailyan. The victim: Medicare. Medicare is not the missing defendant; it is the payer that was defrauded and the creditor ordered restitution.

The signature artist was thorough. Prosecutors said the proceeds bought two Teslas, a Cybertruck, five California properties, a $12,000 seventeenth-century crossbow and a $3,000 painting. I will not dwell on the crossbow. It is almost too small to mention. The $45 million is not.

And one of the patients she claimed to have treated was serving time in a federal prison when the supposed appointment took place.

Wells Fargo paid three billion. Mailyan got fifteen years. Fraud. Gavel. The docket does not close.
