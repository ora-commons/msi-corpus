The AI boom’s fragile joint is leverage and ownership, not one presidential policy. In [The AI Correction Trade Arrives](https://www.wsj.com/opinion/the-ai-correction-trade-arrives-d76f8636?mod=rss_opinion), the Wall Street Journal’s Editorial Board reports that Anthropic fell 8.5 percent on Friday after Thursday’s initial public offering, while retaining a $1.18 trillion market value. The board raises three live pressures: anxiety over AI’s promise, President Trump’s threatened penalties for U.S. companies building data centers overseas, and the danger that leveraged funds will be forced to sell as the correction runs. It also says export controls can serve a national interest and that taxing overseas centers would weaken American competition. It does not say politics alone caused the correction. The mistake lies one level deeper: the board treats shareholder value as the scoreboard and leaves the public capacity beneath the boom outside the frame.

Credit where due, three times. A one-day drop says little about Anthropic’s long-run prospects, and the Meta comparison is fair. Export controls can serve a national interest. And taxing American firms for building data centers where their customers are is a cheerful way to lose business. I will even sign the forced-sale warning. A heavily indebted fund cannot wait for sentiment to improve. If collateral falls, it posts more cash or sells. Policy is not weather. But a press conference is not the whole building either.

Slow down and watch the machine the board itself describes. Funds borrow to buy in. Prices fall. Lenders ask for more collateral. Funds sell. Selling pushes prices lower. More lenders ask for more collateral. The loop runs on arithmetic. It does not need a podium.

The public record does not tell us whether Friday’s 8.5 percent was that loop, political anxiety, or both. The board does not claim otherwise. It says this may be manageable and that there may be casualties. But the board puts the debt in the room and then leaves it there. The problem is the filing. The most honest paragraph is also the least developed. It can describe the machine without asking what the machine costs or who carries the loss when it stops.

A structure that turns a bad afternoon into a cascade is not a weather report. It is architecture. Anyway.

Who are the casualties? The board does not say. Retirement money is not safely outside the room. The [Investment Company Institute’s 2024 retirement-assets data](https://www.ici.org/research/stats/retirement) show that retirement plans hold roughly a third of mutual-fund assets. That does not mean every pension owns every leveraged fund. It means a forced sale can travel from a fund into a retirement account without the retiree voting for it.

The [Federal Reserve’s history of the 2007–08 crisis](https://www.federalreservehistory.org/essays/financial-crisis-of-2007-08) records the same chain: falling asset prices, margin calls, forced sales, and stress moving through the wider financial system. 2008 is not a prophecy. It is the mechanism at larger scale. The board asks whether there will be casualties without asking whose.

This is financialization. It is not merely a lot of trading. It is the point at which the price of a claim on future profit sets the tempo of a physical buildout. Chips must be fabricated. Power must be connected. Workers must be trained. Those things take time. A promised stream of future profit can be repriced in a second. Leverage bridges the gap. When the second clock sets the terms of the first, a correction is not a surprise. It is the bill arriving on schedule.

Much debt is ordinary financing for real assets. Good lenders finance real machines. The trouble starts when margin calls outrun operating cash. The board has named the spark. It has not examined the building.

The board’s masthead says free markets and free people. The piece’s scoreboard is narrower: can shareholders recover value, can American companies win abroad, and can government avoid interfering? Those are real questions. The missing question is ownership.

Who owns the machine? Who owns the grid connection beneath it? Who captures the productivity, and who carries the cost? “Global competition” can mean that shareholders capture more of the AI economy, or that workers and communities capture more of what it produces. The board leaves the first meaning in view and the second outside the frame.

I’m not anti-market. I’m against asking a share price to answer an ownership question. Markets can allocate compute. They cannot decide, by themselves, who owns the machine or who pays for the common capacity beneath it.

The machine also has a public bill. An AI firm does not build the grid. It plugs into one. The interconnection study, substation, transmission line, and grid upgrades can be paid for by a public utility, a rural cooperative, or a county while the campus’s productivity and ownership remain private. A [2024 Department of Energy laboratory study](https://eta-publications.lbl.gov/sites/default/files/2024-12/lbnl-2024-united-states-data-center-energy-usage-report.pdf) documents the scale of the electricity demand. Ownership is a separate question from size.

Take Fairfax County, Virginia. Amazon Web Services received roughly $72 million in tax breaks over ten years for a 1.7-million-square-foot data center, according to the [contemporaneous AP report](https://www.pbs.org/newshour/nation/amazon-to-receive-72-million-in-tax-breaks-for-new-fairfax-county-data-center). The public side gave up revenue that would otherwise have supported county services and taxpayers. The company and the owners of the asset received the earnings. Virginia regulators later had to develop a separate large-load tariff for major data-center connections because new transmission costs could otherwise be spread across ordinary utility customers, as documented in the [Virginia State Utilities Commission’s public record](https://www.scc.virginia.gov/pages/Pres-and-Data-Reports).

Abatements can bring jobs and expand the tax base. They are not automatically theft. But when a private campus depends on public risk and public infrastructure, the bargain should run both ways. Public money should buy public ownership: a capacity charge, a stake in the project, or revenue that returns to the public. Otherwise the boom is a subsidy with better branding.

Two problems are tangled here, and they do not have the same cure. The debt is a financing choice: pay it down. The free ride on public infrastructure is an ownership choice: price it, regulate it, or own it. Neither problem disappears because one data-center rule changes.

Keep the caution about export controls. Drop the ownership frame if it cannot show that public capacity is being paid for and that shareholders do not capture the gains while somebody else carries the bill.

So build the other thing: a public option for compute.

A regional public infrastructure trust could finance and own shared data centers and grid connections, then sell computing capacity to competing model firms under published prices and common rules. A public bank could supply patient capital instead of squeezing every buildout through quarterly earnings stories. Private firms would still compete over products and customers. They simply would not own every common input merely because they arrived first.

This would not be a blank check. The trust would need an independent board, published accounts, common-access terms, and penalties for political favors. Public money would take public risk. If the public helps build the machine, the public should own a share of the machine.

This is not theoretical. Norway’s Government Pension Fund Global now exceeds $2 trillion, owns stakes in roughly 1.5 percent of the world’s listed companies, works out to more than $390,000 per Norwegian, and booked a market-value gain of about $248 billion in 2025, according to [Norges Bank’s official figures](https://www.norgesbanken.no/en/topics/Investment/Government-Pension-Fund-Global/). Norway did not nationalize the oil companies. It made the public an owner of capital while private firms kept operating in markets. The useful principle is simple: the public can own a piece of what private machines sit on.

Alaska does the smaller American version in one of the reddest states in the union. Its Permanent Fund was created by a 1976 constitutional amendment, financed with mineral royalties, and pays residents annual dividends, as [the fund explains](https://www.apfc.org/about/). North Dakota’s state-owned bank says it has stayed profitable every year since opening in 1919, according to [its history](https://bank.nd.gov/about/history/). About 900 rural electric cooperatives serve 42 million members across roughly 56 percent of the country’s land, according to [NRECA’s figures](https://www.cooperative.org/about-us). None needs an AI mandate. Together they show that common capacity can be owned without pretending private shareholders built the grid. Remind me what the obstacle is again.

Now use the cooperatives for the grid. When a data center asks a rural electric co-op for megawatts, the member-owned utility can negotiate a special tariff and a capacity agreement. The giant customer may itself be a member with a vote. The utility board still has to protect the residential bill. Smaller co-ops may need state help financing the expertise to negotiate.

That is how the membership model works: the customer arrives with a truckload of load, and the members ask what the truck is worth to the system. Harder here because the machinery is patchwork and many wires are investor-owned. No press conference fixes that either. But the plumbing exists in 900 places.

Ownership should also reach workers. Mondragon now has about 70,000 people in roughly 80 cooperatives and more than €11 billion in revenue, according to [Mondragon’s corporate figures](https://www.mondragon-corporation.com/en/). Its pay ratio is roughly five to one, chosen by each co-op and capped around six to one under its [governance rules](https://www.mondragon-corporation.com/en/people-and-culture/participation/). Most workers in its Spanish operations are members. Worker-owners are a minority of the global workforce once foreign subsidiaries are counted. That caveat matters.

Fagor’s 2013 bankruptcy proves co-ops are not magic. They can lose. The federation says roughly 1,700 of Fagor’s 1,800 Spanish members were relocated to other co-ops after the collapse, as [Mondragon’s own history](https://www.mondragon-corporation.com/en/history/) records. Ownership changes who bears failure and who receives the surplus.

If public capacity helps build an AI company, broad employee ownership or profit-sharing is a more defensible bargain than public risk with private upside.

Why is this harder here? The grid is local. Corporate law is national. Labor law is mostly state-law terrain. A public trust crossing those lines needs state charters, local consent, competent administration, and worker power.

The Nordic social-democratic model did not arrive in a policy package. It grew through organized workers, organized employers, and public administration over decades. Smaller countries and higher initial trust gave them a head start, as [the Nordic model’s own history](https://www.norden.org/en/info-norden/nordic-model) explains. The institutions are the achievement. The country label is not.

So America needs local public banks and cooperatives, patient national capital, and worker power. One federal office cannot conjure a social bargain from a filing cabinet. Every public dollar needs a constituency beyond the vendor selling it.

Anthropic’s Friday fall is not the story. The story is a physical buildout financed like a stock trade, dependent on common infrastructure, and arranged so private owners keep the upside while somebody else gets the bill.

Build the public option: a compute-and-grid trust financed by public banks and public capital, owned in part by public utilities and worker institutions, with published capacity prices, common-access rules, an independent board, and worker ownership inside the firms that use it. Private companies will still compete over models. They will not own every common input merely because they arrived first.

The pieces exist: public banks, member-owned wires, permanent funds, and worker co-ops. The hard work is assembling them into institutions that answer to the public, not the vendor. The market is not the weather. We choose the owner.
