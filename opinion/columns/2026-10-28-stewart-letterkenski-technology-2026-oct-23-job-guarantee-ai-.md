Congress is gambling workers’ livelihoods on AI forecasts without building a job guarantee.

The strongest case for caution is also the strongest case for preparation. We do not yet have a demonstrated countrywide wave of AI-driven layoffs. What we have are forecasts, warnings, and surveys. Bill Gates has said AI could advance so quickly that within a decade humans will not be needed “for most things.” Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei has warned that it could cause widespread unemployment within one to five years, eliminating many positions in law, technology, finance, and other white-collar professions. Both men know the systems under discussion better than most. Neither statement is a headcount of establishments actually cutting jobs.

For a worker whose hours or wage have disappeared, this is not an academic distinction. It is the difference between a task being redesigned and a livelihood being removed.

A benchmark asks whether a model can perform a bounded task under test conditions. A workplace is an organization with contracts, customers, payroll, legal liability, physical space, quality controls, and workers whose duties have to be rewritten. My father worked on a bar mill, where the rule was simple: a motor that can turn is not a production system until the rest of the line has been made safe, fed, and staffed. A model can change a job before it eliminates one.

Here it is worth being precise about what “AI adoption” actually is, because the public discourse has the misleading habit of treating deployment as a thing rather than a set of management decisions. AI can perform a task; adoption begins when an employer changes the workflow, buys complementary systems, trains and supervises staff, allocates liability, and decides what to do with the time saved. It may reduce headcount, redirect hours to other work, increase output at the same staffing level, or make a service cheap enough that the employer sells more of it. The last three are not unemployment.

The arithmetic is less forgiving than the slogan. Employment is output divided by the labour required per unit of output. AI can lower the denominator; it does not determine the numerator. A gain in output per worker does not tell us whether the firm employs fewer people, the same number of people, or more because cheaper output creates demand that was not there before. Complementarity cuts both ways: new tools require investment, integration, maintenance, training, and sometimes new roles.

The relevant labour-market evidence is not a demonstration or an executive forecast. It is adoption-linked movement in payroll, hours, separations, and wages at establishments using the technology. The record in this debate does not supply a demonstrated countrywide displacement. It does supply a reason to prepare. A [Pew survey](https://mainstreetindependent.com/articles/2026-09-18-pew-finds-majority-in-34-of-37-countries-expect-ai-to-reduce-jobs/) found majorities in 34 of 37 countries expect AI to reduce jobs, while [economists have already urged immediate action on AI’s economic risks](https://mainstreetindependent.com/articles/2026-07-13-economists-urge-immediate-action-on-ai-s-economic-risks/). Those surveys measure expectation, not layoffs; the urgency is evidence of risk, not of a completed mass displacement. We do not need to agree about the size of the shock to agree that employment policy cannot be designed after the shock has emptied payroll accounts.

There is also a second shock, often hidden inside the first. If investors conclude that the AI investment boom is a bubble, pull back, and cause a recession, employment can collapse even if no employer has replaced a worker with a model. Structural displacement and a financial bust are different problems. The first is an industrial-policy problem: labour demand has changed, and the gains and costs must be allocated. The second is a stabilization problem. A job guarantee addresses the first; macroeconomic stabilization addresses the second. Conflating them weakens both, but it does not make the first disappear.

Milton Friedman wrote in the preface to *Capitalism and Freedom*: “Only a crisis – actual or perceived – produces real change. When that crisis occurs, the actions that are taken depend on the ideas that are lying around.” The idea lying around now is a federal job guarantee: a public option for work that gives anyone seeking employment a meaningful, decently paid job. This is statecraft rather than forecasting.

It is also an old idea with a modern application. Article 23 of the 1948 Universal Declaration of Human Rights declares: “Everyone has the right to work, to free choice of employment, to just and favourable conditions of work and to protection against unemployment.” Franklin D. Roosevelt’s Works Progress Administration put millions of Americans to work during the Great Depression, building roads, bridges, sewers, and playing fields. France, India, and Argentina have since experimented with or launched versions of a job guarantee. The concept is not waiting for proof that it is possible. It is waiting for a legislature with the nerve to make it permanent.

Congress is beginning to recognize this. In September, Representative Ayanna Pressley of Massachusetts reintroduced her federal job guarantee resolution, first proposed in 2021. Her plan would finance the scheme federally while administering it locally, pay at least $25 an hour, and make regular adjustments for the cost of living. The work would include emergency preparedness, disaster relief, childcare, eldercare, and public-art projects modelled on the WPA’s Federal Art Project. Neither the resolution nor the tax intended to fund it is law. Congress should enact the guarantee now.

A federal job guarantee would make the government an employer of last resort, not the employer of first resort. The case is not that refusing automation magically preserves each private job; refusal is not a policy, and the private labour market may offer no job at any wage. Public employment can put people to work on needs the market underprovides, while the guarantee supplies a floor for those for whom no private offer appears.

Algernon Austin of the Center for Economic and Policy Research makes the related point that work provides experience, skills, and a network. Joblessness removes not only income but the social architecture that employment creates: the schedule, the workplace, the professional relationships, and the evidence of competence that a person carries into the next job.

Nick Hanauer puts the human side more plainly: “Jobs represent so much more than income in human societies. They represent connection and dignity and enfranchisement and participation.” A cash payment answers what a household can buy this month; a job can also answer what a person can become over years. Cash and work are not substitutes. Cash can cushion an interruption, support a caregiver, or provide income to someone who cannot work. A job can provide cash and a place in public life. A responsible policy uses both.

That means the job guarantee must be compared with the rest of the policy portfolio rather than treated as a rival to it. Unemployment insurance is right for temporary or cyclical layoffs because it replaces part of lost wages while workers search or return; it is poorly suited to long-term displacement. Cash assistance is right for immediate income, disability, caregiving, and workers unable to take a job, and it can accompany a job offer. Retraining is sound only where the destination occupation has durable demand, the training reaches workers who can use it, and placement actually raises earnings. A course leading toward work that is also being automated is relabelled unemployment.

Wage subsidies are right where an employer has a viable job but cannot yet pay a floor that keeps the worker above benefit cliffs, as the Cleveland pilot demonstrates. The job guarantee is the final layer: it creates an offer for people for whom none of those routes produces employment, while the other instruments remain available to share the cost and support the transition. These instruments do different jobs. The failure of one does not make the others redundant.

The politics are less poisoned than economic orthodoxy suggests. A recent global survey found greater support for job guarantees than for universal basic incomes. Polling by David Shor shows the same pattern, with greater support for well-paid jobs than for direct income support. Universal Basic Employment founder Devin Cotten supplies the reason: “Work is a shared value across political lines.” A program built around dignified work has a broader constituency than an argument over whether automation should be taxed, how many jobs it will destroy, or which prediction will prove most accurate.

The principal objection has always been cost, and the cost is real. During a Senate hearing on affordability in June, Senator John Kennedy of Louisiana said a federal job guarantee would cost trillions and was impossible. When Dr Julie Margetta Morgan, president of the Century Foundation, said she supported one, Kennedy replied: “Do you believe in the Easter bunny?”

That is not fiscal analysis. It is theatre. The rabbit has the better fiscal case than the senator.

At the proposal’s $25 floor and 40 paid hours a week, every million participants adds about $52 billion a year to gross wages. A universal guarantee large enough to cover tens of millions could indeed run into trillions. Kennedy has the gross order of magnitude right; he has not calculated the net social cost.

Max Kasy, the Oxford economist who has studied Austria’s job-guarantee experiment, notes that workers receiving guaranteed jobs may no longer need unemployment benefits, disability benefits, or early-retirement support. They pay taxes and social-insurance contributions. They also produce something society can use: safer neighbourhoods, prepared communities, and better care. Their work may create value that nobody had to purchase on the market.

Pavlina Tcherneva of Bard College calls a good job “absolutely transformative” and argues that when the health costs of precarious and long-term unemployment are counted, the policy is an investment rather than merely an expense. Gross wages are not the net cost. They buy employment, public value, and social insurance while reducing the damage joblessness inflicts.

The financing should come from the source of the disruption. Representatives Greg Casar of Texas, Valerie Foushee of North Carolina, and Sara Jacobs of California support a tax on large AI companies calculated on whichever is greater: the value of tokens processed internally for training and other operations, or revenue from AI products.

The word “tokens” needs precision. A token is a unit a model reads or writes—often a word or sub-word piece, while images and audio are represented by their own model-specific units. Three flows must not be blurred. Training tokens are consumed while the model is being fitted. Internal tokens are read or written when a company uses its models in its own operations. Customer-inference tokens are model processing supplied to a paying customer; that side of the formula enters through revenue from AI products, not as a separate volume count of inference sold. Calling the proposal a direct tax on inference sold would conflate the two measures. The tax rate would rise as unemployment rose.

That is the correct logic. If AI firms capture the gains from automation while transferring the costs to displaced workers and the wider economy, they have externalized the most important part of their business model. The tax lets the public recapture part of the dividend. It is not a punishment imposed on invention; it is the price of letting private firms privatize extraordinary profits while the social costs of displacement are borne collectively.

There is also room for a more market-led version of the guarantee. In Cleveland, Ohio, the nonprofit Universal Basic Employment is running a pilot with five van drivers at a private transportation company. Rather than inventing jobs through a new bureaucracy, it subsidizes existing positions. The programme calls $27 an hour the “autonomous wage”, the point at which most means-tested benefits no longer apply. The pilot costs almost $500,000 for one year and shares the cost of reaching that wage with employers. The drivers currently earn about $15 an hour; workers receive a substantial raise, and employers receive a subsidy.

The two wage figures are not in conflict. The $25 floor is the federal proposal’s pay standard; the $27 figure is the Cleveland pilot’s locally calibrated threshold for leaving most social-safety-net benefits behind. The threshold is also a warning. A benefit cliff is not a virtue, so federal rules should taper assistance as earnings rise rather than remove it abruptly.

This is what a public option for employment can look like in practice: not a federal employment office assigning make-work tasks, but leverage in the labour market that gives workers and employers a viable deal. Government need not own the workplace to guarantee work. When higher wages replace fragmented benefits, workers can build credit, perhaps qualify for a mortgage, and keep money circulating in neighbourhoods otherwise receiving little of the productivity gains generated around them.

The rules should be plain. The guarantee should be open to anyone able and willing to work. The wage floor should be indexed to the cost of living, so it cannot drift back toward the $15 an hour the Cleveland drivers currently earn. Direct public roles should add work rather than replace unsubsidized employment, while employer subsidies should be conditional on preserving and raising existing jobs rather than replacing them with cheaper or unpaid labour. Oversight should measure care delivered, infrastructure completed, and emergencies handled—not headcounts on a roster. The state should not guarantee busywork; it should guarantee useful work.

Congress should pass the federal job guarantee, finance it through the tax on the firms driving AI investment, pay workers at least $25 an hour with cost-of-living adjustments, and administer it through local institutions capable of identifying real needs. Cash assistance, retraining, unemployment insurance, and wage subsidies should remain in the portfolio, each aimed at the failure it can actually solve.

Build it in ordinary times, while the idea is merely lying around. The machinery of automation can remain private; the promise of employment must become public. The machines do not need employment insurance; the people displaced by them certainly do.

The machines are being built now. So should the jobs.
