The Vatican converted four centuries of slaveholding into an apology and left the debt standing.

Susana Baca serenaded Pope Leo XIV when he arrived in Peru — two songs, "Canción de Fe" and "Si me quitaran totalmente todo," chosen, she told the Associated Press, because they carry the same faith the pope carries. She is the first Black Cabinet minister Peru ever had, the woman who spent a career fighting the discrimination that made second-class citizens of Black Peruvians, and she sang to the first pope with Black roots. On Saturday he leads a prayer meeting at the sanctuary of St. Turibius of Mogrovejo in Zaña — the first archbishop of Lima, who in the sixteenth century defended the rights of Indigenous and enslaved people and confirmed St. Martin de Porres, the first Black saint of the Americas. A few streets away, the Afro-Peruvian museum keeps the shackles used to restrain enslaved people in the same collection as the plantation carts, the documents of the enslaved, and the framed photographs of Leo from his years as bishop of nearby Chiclayo. Beside those photographs: Barack Obama and Nelson Mandela. Look at that wall long enough and the whole story is there — the instruments of the crime, the photographs of the men the crime's descendants have become, and no ledger anywhere in the building showing what is owed.

Leo named the crime himself. His first encyclical, "Magnificent Humanity," addresses a series of 15th-century directives from the Vatican that authorized European sovereigns to conquer Africa and the Americas and enslave non-Christians, and it says what no encyclical said before it: "It is impossible not to feel deep sorrow when contemplating the immense suffering and humiliation endured by so many in stark contrast to their immeasurable dignity as persons infinitely loved by the Lord… For this, in the name of the church, I sincerely ask for pardon." Take the sentence seriously. The institution that wrote the authorization is now, in the person of this pope, naming what it wrote and asking the people it wrote about for forgiveness. That is more than his predecessors did across five centuries. Nothing in the record suggests the sorrow is feigned, and the encyclical did not have to exist. Credit where the record puts it — because the concession is what earns the standing to make the demand that follows.

Now watch what happens to it. The apology travels. It goes out across the wire, it lands in the middle of the pope's Peruvian homecoming coverage, alongside his [return to the former diocese of Chiclayo](/articles/2026-11-13-pope-leo-xiv-returns-to-former-diocese-of-chiclayo-in-peru/) and his denunciations of violence against women, and it enters the world as an institution that has faced its history. What does not travel is the sugar. An estimated 100,000 African slaves were brought to Peru after the arrival of the Spanish conquistadores; they toiled in the sugar plantations and the silver mines; at one point they and their descendants made up more than 40% of Lima's population. Black Peruvians today are a small fraction of Peru's 35 million people, and in the 2017 national census 1,311 residents of Zaña identified as Afro-Peruvian — 78,639 across the entire Lambayeque region. The institution keeps its standing. The descendants of the transfer keep the transfer.

Ask the question that sorts it: cui bono — who benefits from a visit remembered as a song and an embrace? The institution does: a liability becomes an asset, the pope brave enough to apologize, the Church that leads with contrition. The Peruvian state benefits too: a government that promotes the UNESCO plaque gets a memory ceremony in place of a settlement. And who pays? The numbers answer — the same 1,311, the same fraction, more than 170 years after emancipation, still climbing toward equality on a record that offers struggle where it should offer return.

That is the mechanism worth naming precisely, because it is not hypocrisy and it is not a lie. The acknowledgment is real; every word of the encyclical's sorrow is meant. What the form does is accept the fact while declining the obligation that follows from it. The analytic name for it is implicatory denial: the wrong is described accurately; the responsibility it carries is refused. The church can disclose the directives, name the suffering, and ask for pardon, and the disclosure can still be engineered to end at exactly the point where the account would otherwise have to be opened.

Watch the frame the celebration builds around the words: forgiveness. Baca told the wire service she feels connected to the pope because of the importance he places on asking forgiveness — "We are an Afro-Peruvian people who have learned to forgive," she said, and the injustices of history, she went on, "are less painful if we have the capacity to forgive and to be forgiven." Hear that sentence fully before deciding what it settles, because forgiveness is the creditor's act, and a creditor's grace can be converted, quietly, into the debtor's accounting. When a five-century-old debt is the subject of the ceremony, the story that centers the creditor's mercy does the debtor's work for him. Pardon requested from the harmed is the institution's own closing entry. It is structured as the harmed party's gift — and Baca's people, who "did not remain submerged despite our history of suffering," are the ones being handed the pen.

Her forgiveness is hers. It is freely given and it is magnificent, and it belongs to the people who gave it. What it cannot be is the church's receipt. To be exact about Susana Baca, because exactness is owed her: her forgiveness is paid for in advance by everything her people survived. Forgiveness from the injured is not repair from the injurer, and no amount of grace flowing upward discharges a debt that runs the other direction. Leo's own family tree includes enslaved people and slave owners; all four of his maternal great-grandparents were "free people of color" in Louisiana, Creole in the melting pot of French, Spanish, African and Native American cultures. He carries the history inside his own lineage, which is likely why he can see it. But the pattern he is working inside does not end with his seeing it. An institution that licensed the traffic has now placed in its chair a descendant of both sides of it. Standing is not absolution. It is obligation.

Then the institution's argument learned its most durable trick. The Church's five-century position on the word "slavery" is a single term used in two distinct senses across an argument. In the 15th-century directives the same office wrote, enslavement is the grant, issued to Christian crowns as an entitlement. In the encyclical the same office writes now, enslavement is the crime, and the sorrow is "immense." The word never changed. What changed was the price of the older sense — and the institution switched senses without ever reconciling the two, which is precisely what a word in two senses is for.

An American pope apologizing in 2026 is not the beginning of this story. It is the last visible symptom of it. Trace the chain below the apology and the root is an institution whose moral machinery can absolve forever without ever settling: confession without restitution, penance without payment, sorrow renewed each century while the balance stands untouched. The Gospel the pope serves does not run that way, and the receipts are in his own book. John the Baptist stood at the Jordan demanding fruit worthy of repentance — not feelings, fruit (Matthew 3:8). Zacchaeus heard his own account read aloud and restored fourfold to everyone he had defrauded (Luke 19). I grew up in a Black Baptist congregation where Zacchaeus was preached with the numbers left in, restitution as the test of whether the repentance was real, and the preaching was right. The encyclical asks pardon. The standard it invokes demands payment.

And past the silence of the popes who failed to condemn for centuries, past the encyclical, down to the thing Zaña is actually living with. The 15th-century directives did not merely permit an evil; they organized a world. Sugar plantations, silver mines, a Pacific-coast town whose census still carries the shape of what was done to it, a museum whose collections include plantation carts and the documents of enslaved people. Apology addresses the silence. The silence is not what those 1,311 people are living with. They are living with the world the authorization built and the reversal that was never entered into the account.

Say what payment is, because the institution's vocabulary keeps confusing it with memory. The UNESCO plaque is memory. The museum wall with the pope beside Obama and Mandela is memory. The visit, the song, the prayer meeting — memory. Memory is what institutions do instead of paying, and it is cheap. Felipe Gutierrez — chef, poet of decimas, the man they call Frontado, wearing a Bulls jersey with Jordan's number on the back — feels the pull of the symbolism and says it straight: the photographs show "how someone who is Black as we are in Zaña became a president," and the pride is that "we all deserve the same rights," he said. "Black, Indigenous, all of us are equal in the eyes of God." He is right about the rights and the eyes of God. But representation ascends while the census stays. The pride a town takes in the first pope with Black roots does not move a single Afro-Peruvian out of second-class standing, and the institution knows the difference between a wall of photographs and a ledger.

The payment runs through material things. Through the archives: Rome holds the licensing instruments of the conquest, Zaña's museum holds the shackles and the papers of the enslaved, and the ledgers — wherever they survive in the religious archives of two continents — open them, unconditionally, to the descendants' scholars. Through the canon-law inheritance that outlived the sin that wrote it. Through the weight of the Peruvian hierarchy in a country where discrimination has long made second-class citizens of Black Peruvians and Peru's Indigenous peoples.

King drew the distinction this visit needs in *Where Do We Go From Here* (1967): true compassion is more than flinging a coin to a beggar — it sees that the edifice producing beggars needs restructuring, and restructuring the edifice is the work. He had already named the other half of it in the Birmingham letter of April 1963: the negative peace that is the absence of tension, set against the positive peace that is the presence of justice. An apology is the absence of tension. Sorrow stands to restitution as the coin stands to the edifice. And King pressed the point further in the same 1967 book — civil rights legislation was a foothold, no more, and the country had to abolish poverty directly or confess it did not intend to. The church had its own legislation, five centuries of it, written in the voice of the authorization itself. What it has not done is treat the transfer as a transfer.

King, eulogizing the four girls killed at Sixteenth Street Baptist Church in September 1963, refused to let the inquiry stop at the men who planted the bomb: he demanded an accounting of the system, the way of life, the philosophy that produced the murderers. Run that standard here. Leo has named the sin. Repentance that stops at the named sin — while the doctrine that licensed it, the archives that recorded it, and the hierarchy that administered it remain closed and unsettled — is the polite version, the one this institution has been rehearsing for five centuries.

Malcolm X said it of America in *Message to the Grass Roots* (Detroit, November 10, 1963), and the line travels south without translation: "We didn't land on Plymouth Rock; Plymouth Rock landed on us." The African in Peru did not arrive in Peru. The trade arrived on them, with papal paperwork in its pocket. And the demand that answers this moment is the one Malcolm X spoke at the Audubon Ballroom on June 28, 1964: "We want freedom by any means necessary. We want justice by any means necessary. We want equality by any means necessary." He meant by that phrase what he spent his last years drawing it to mean: commitment to structural change through every instrument that works — the analysis, the archives, the courts, the collections, the legislation — and violence never, because the means he defended are the ones this column uses now.

Leo has prayed in that sanctuary, holds the office that licensed the traffic, and carries both sides of it in his veins. On this homecoming he [returned to the diocese that formed him](/articles/2026-11-13-pope-leo-xiv-returns-to-former-diocese-of-chiclayo-in-peru/), and the same homecoming has already carried structural indictments — he [stood in Peru and denounced machismo and violence against women](/articles/2026-11-12-pope-leo-xiv-denounces-machismo-violence-against-women-peru-homecoming/), and the denunciation was right. So the question the prayer meeting in Zaña must answer is narrow and checkable: whether the pen that wrote the apology will now write the settlement. His lineage hands him a standing none of his predecessors carried.

The people of Zaña have forgiven across seven El Niños and the darker blows — Salvador del Solar, a former culture minister, said it standing in their square: no blow, not even slavery, "has ever been strong enough to break them completely," and they reaffirm their Afro-descendant identity with pride. Their forgiveness is the most expensive thing in this story, paid for in advance by the people who gave it. But forgiveness forecloses the hatred; it does not erase the balance.

So look again at that museum wall. The shackles in the display case are evidence, not payment. Forgiveness without restitution is performance. Memory without payment is decoration. An apology without repair is the institution blessing itself one more time. Keep the transfer named. Keep the instruments visible. Keep the debt on the books until someone pays it. The arc bends toward justice only when specific people, in a specific moment, push it — and the people pushing it this week are the 1,311, the singers and the poets and the historians who kept a town's memory intact while the institution that wrote the crime spent five centuries editing its own. Pardon is the pope's to ask and theirs to give. The debt is someone else's to pay, and it has not been paid. The God the pope serves kept receipts. So do we.
