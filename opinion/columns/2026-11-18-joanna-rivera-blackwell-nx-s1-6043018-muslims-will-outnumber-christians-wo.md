The Christian-nationalist movement made America's Christian majority an idol.

The public claim behind that accusation is old enough to date. At the Southern Baptist Convention’s 1980 National Affairs Briefing, Ronald Reagan told conservative Southern Baptists, “I know you can’t endorse me, but I want you to know that I endorse you.” That was more than partisan cheerleading. It announced a compact: evangelical political power would bless Republican statecraft, and Republican officeholders would treat the language of evangelical Christianity as public legitimacy. Christian nationalism did not invent that compact. It organized it.

Georgia’s LIFE Act shows what such a compact becomes when theology is written as law. Adriana Smith was a thirty-year-old Black nurse in Georgia who was brain-dead at nine weeks of pregnancy after developing blood clots. The statute in force made fetal personhood a legal framework. Emory University maintained Smith on life support for more than ninety days. Her son Chance was delivered prematurely on June 13, 2025; support was withdrawn on June 17.

The concrete breach is not difficult to name: Georgia’s fetal-personhood framework put fetal legal status ahead of a pregnant patient’s own medical standing, and a major medical institution acted upon that hierarchy. The right made vulnerable was the bodily autonomy and equal medical decision-making authority of pregnant patients. The person on whom the harm landed was Smith; the community exposed by the rule was every patient whose own body could be made to serve another legal claim.

That does not tell us what every Georgia lawmaker believed in his or her own heart. It tells us what the law did. Nor is every antiabortion argument identical to Christian nationalism. But Christian nationalism has made control of abortion one of the tests by which it demands public faithfulness. Georgia’s statute is where that overlap becomes concrete.

The pressure driving it is demographic, not eschatological. According to [Pew Research Center’s projections](</articles/2026-11-18-pew-research-center-projects-muslims-will-outnumber-christians-worldwide-by-2035/>), by 2035 Islam will pass Christianity as the world’s largest religion. That will not mean Islam is replacing Christianity through mass conversion. Both faiths are growing; Islam is growing faster, partly because Muslim women have an average of 3.1 children while every other religious group has fewer. The religiously unaffiliated are the group growing primarily through switching. By 2070, the Democratic Republic of the Congo is projected to contain more Christians than any other country. In the United States, Christians are projected to fall from about 62 percent of the population to less than half, while the unaffiliated reach 41 percent.

Christianity is not dying. Its center of gravity is moving. Pew’s Alan Cooperman called that a change in the “center of gravity” of Christianity. The mistake is hearing that movement as a summons to political panic rather than as a rebuke to Western Christianity’s presumption that the proper center of the faith is wherever Western Christians happen to hold the most power.

The numbers do not audit the Gospel. They audit the political machinery that mistook Western Christianity’s demographic position for permanence. The wrong is not Muslim growth. The wrong is turning a worldwide faith into a national property and the Bible into authorization for state power.

Andrew Whitehead and Samuel Perry’s *Taking America Back for God* gives the political account. Their survey findings showed that Christian nationalism predicted support for Donald Trump in 2016 more strongly than evangelical identity, denomination, church attendance, or theological conservatism did. “Christian nationalism is all about power,” they write, and it “co-opts Christian language and iconography in order to cloak particular political or social ends in moral and religious symbolism.” Philip Gorski supplied the more severe diagnosis: “Christian nationalism is political idolatry dressed up as religious orthodoxy.”

Those authors do not say every Evangelical is a Christian nationalist. The findings do something more useful: they show that an ideology, rather than Christian devotion by itself, best predicts the political alignment. The United States has no established official religion. “Christian nation” has been a cultural description and a political aspiration, not a constitutional settlement. The Christian-nationalist project seeks to turn a former numerical advantage into a claim of permanent entitlement.

Its institutional carriers are named. The Council for National Policy, founded in 1981 by Tim LaHaye, brought together figures from Richard Viguerie’s direct-mail mobilization and Paul Weyrich’s conservative policy network; Whitehead and Perry document its coordinating role. Project 2025’s published materials continued the method by placing movement loyalists where government policy is made; Liberty University joined its advisory board in 2024. Katherine Stewart’s *The Power of Worshippers* documents the New Apostolic Reformation, Project Blitz, the National Association of Christian Lawmakers, and Lance Wallnau’s Seven Mountain Mandate, with its demand for influence over religion, family, education, government, media, arts, and business. The Appeal to Heaven flags carried that dominionist vocabulary into the January 6 crowds.

And the Southern Baptist Convention’s own resolution history shows how young the hard certainty became. In 1971, the denomination permitted abortion in cases including rape, incest, severe fetal deformity, and serious danger to the mother’s physical and emotional health. In 1980 it adopted its first explicitly antiabortion resolution. In 2003 it repented of the earlier resolutions as support for what it called the abortion culture. Adrian Rogers’s 1979 election as president marked the conservative resurgence that made the new line binding. Jemar Tisby’s *The Color of Compromise* and Kristin Du Mez’s *Jesus and John Wayne* place that change within the longer history of white Protestant complicity. Randall Balmer’s *The Making of Evangelicalism* shows that the tax treatment of segregated Christian schools, rather than a recovered verse, supplied a central grievance for the modern Religious Right.

The legalism is younger than Micah.

For thirty years, I was inside the apparatus. I taught the authority of Scripture while helping preserve an institutional culture in which “Christian America” required no defense. Sunday-school attendance boards hung by the gymnasium door. Revival conversion counts were reported like box scores. At church-growth seminars, men with pointers taught us that a shrinking roll meant a failing congregation meant, somehow, a failing faith. I taught from those workbooks. I tithed to them. When attendance dipped, the answer from the platform was never the poor; it was the election.

I know exactly how that political theology worked on a room because I was the room. That is what complicity looks like from inside: not deliberate paganism, but ordinary sentences repeated until they feel like wisdom.

Let me show you what Micah actually says.

You have heard Micah 6:8 reduced to a slogan; so had I. I taught it as a refrigerator-magnet verse myself and watched it embroidered on women’s-ministry retreat totes. Micah was not decorating a kitchen.

Micah 6:1–8 places the question inside a covenant whose people have used religious confidence while their society runs roughshod over human beings. At the center comes the question: “He has shown you, O mortal, what is good. And what does the Lord require of you? To act justly and to love mercy and to walk humbly with your God.”

The verbs are plain. Act justly. Love mercy. Walk humbly with your God. Micah does not give his people a title deed to divine favor. He asks what their covenant requires of them. Justice is action, not a preference. Mercy is not a decorative word for people who have already received their share. Humility means no nation may convert inherited blessings into a certificate of chosenness.

The Christian-nationalist reading asks what a nation deserves from God and who must control public institutions to preserve its favored status. The plain-language reading asks what people owe God and one another. That is the chasm. The legalist machinery reverses the question: it makes the people owners of divine favor rather than debtors—recipients of a deed instead of subjects of a summons.

I do not need Micah to settle every policy question of 2070. Micah settles the shape of the question. A people may ask what God requires; they may not ask God to certify their national supremacy. The legalism is younger than Micah.

Matthew 7:13–14 gives the same warning in even more compressed language. The KJV says, “Enter ye in at the strait gate,” while the NIV says, “Enter through the narrow gate.” Both end with only a few finding the way that leads to life. Jesus does not add an asterisk for the United States. He does not say that God’s favor is measured by the size of the crowd, the market share of a church, or the percentage of a nation claiming belief.

The legalist theology built beside that text did something nearly opposite. It taught children that the narrow gate concerned personal discipleship, then taught their parents at the polls that God’s favor was secured by national majority. The text says only the first thing. The distance between what Matthew 7 says and the majoritarian theology constructed beside it is the whole argument.

The global shift in Christianity’s center of gravity does not contradict Luke 4:16–21. In the Nazareth synagogue, Jesus reads Isaiah’s Jubilee promise: good news for the poor, liberty for captives, sight for the blind, and release for the oppressed. The KJV renders the first phrase “preach the gospel to the poor”; the NIV says “bring good news to the poor.” Both preserve the Greek *euangelizomai* from which “evangelist” comes. When Jesus applies Isaiah to himself, Luke 4:28–29 records the response of his hometown majority: wrath, and an attempt to throw him from the hill. The point is not that majorities are inherently evil. The point is that majority status cannot certify truth.

In the NPR discussion of the Pew report, religion correspondent Jason DeRose suggested that the geographic shift might bring the global church a greater emphasis on poverty and the environment. That is a possibility, not a prophecy I can announce. But the movement is already visible in [Latin America’s religious realignment](</articles/2026-08-14-latin-america-s-religious-shift-reshapes-regional-politics/>), and Walter Brueggemann’s *The Prophetic Imagination* gives us language for it: “a consciousness and perception alternative to the consciousness and perception of the dominant culture.” Christianity’s center-of-gravity shift is not defeat. It is the end of the Western presumption that Christianity’s proper center is wherever Western Christians hold the most power.

None of this makes Muslims the threat in these numbers. The numbers record families. Women having children is the ordinary arithmetic the same movement blessed over its own cradles whenever it quoted Genesis 1:28: “Be fruitful and multiply.” A pulpit that reads Muslim cradles as a siege while preaching fruitfulness as covenant blessing has not exegeted Genesis. It has picked a team.

The neighbor Leviticus 19:34 commands Israel to love the foreigner “as one born among you.” In Luke 10:25–37, Jesus makes the Samaritan—the outsider most likely to be stepped around—the model of neighborly mercy. Jesus never asks his hearers to secure the covenant by counting their own. The letter that arrives the week the Pew numbers land may call that loss. The Bible calls it repentance.

Matthew 25:31–46 makes the point still more concrete. When the Son of Man separates the nations, the record asks what they did for the hungry, the stranger, the sick, and the imprisoned. It does not ask which nation retained control of the faith. Christian nationalism wants the state to enforce a religious majority; Matthew 25 measures nations by what they do to vulnerable people.

The landscape is also not simply draining. Religiosity is [rising among young men even as the unaffiliated increase overall](</articles/2026-04-16-young-men-s-religiosity-surges-widening-gender-gap/>). Some people leaving the unaffiliated category are leaving belief itself. Others are leaving a political brand. The texts do not settle the first question for me; God alone reads that ledger. What I can say is that a Christian-nationalist movement treats every departure as defection and answers with escalation, thereby confusing withdrawal from its political program with withdrawal from God.

The temptation to turn demographics into divine verdict is not confined to one coalition. I have sat in progressive Christian rooms where the rise of the religiously unaffiliated was treated as history’s amen—proof that the future belonged to their politics. That is the same arithmetic in a different accent. If a progressive movement uses Matthew 25 as a deed to its own majority, it has entered the same machinery. The text belongs to neither camp. Whoever turns a census into a verdict has stopped reading the passage.

I will not tell you what God is doing with these numbers. I read texts; I do not audit heaven. But this much the texts settle without my help: no biblical text cited here promises any nation permanent possession, and the Gospel never makes the size of a crowd the evidence of God’s favor.

So to the woman in the pew who saw the headlines and felt the floor move—the one wondering whether her grandchildren will know the hymns, whether there will be a church left in any shape she recognizes—the floor is not what the headlines say it is. What is shifting is not the faith. It is the furniture America built on top of it, and furniture is meant to be moved.

On Sunday, when the bulletin reports another poll and somebody asks whether the country still belongs to the faithful, the answer is not a census and not a panic. It is a reading: act justly, love mercy, walk humbly with your God. Stop worshiping the census. No text in Scripture promises any nation permanent possession. The church was never ours to hold by majority. It was ours to feed, clothe, visit, and love.

Small gate. Narrow road. Few who find it.

Enough.
