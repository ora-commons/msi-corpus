HUD is evicting U.S.-citizen children from their homes over their relatives’ immigration status.

The operative document is HUD’s final rule, *Eligibility Requirements for Public Housing*, 91 Fed. Reg. (Nov. 13, 2026). It takes effect Dec. 14, 2026. HUD calls the measure enforcement. The rule’s operative effect is household forfeiture.

HUD has the stronger case on one narrow proposition. A government may verify eligibility before it pays a housing subsidy. Its audit of assisted households found nearly 200,000 tenants, just under 2 percent, whose eligibility verification was incomplete or unknown. On the proposition that federal housing funds should reach eligible recipients, HUD has the stronger case.

An incomplete file warrants investigation, not forfeiture. It does not establish that anyone is ineligible. Two percent of assisted households cannot carry a rule that turns one ineligible relative into a veto over everyone else’s housing.

The new rule reverses a regulatory practice dating to 1995. HUD proposed a similar change in 2019, 84 Fed. Reg. 59140 (Nov. 8, 2019), withdrew it in 2021, and revived it in February 2026. Under the old rule, a mixed-status household—meaning a family whose members have different immigration statuses—could remain in public housing. The subsidy covered only eligible family members.

Revised 24 C.F.R. § 5.106 now establishes a household-wide eligibility rule. Amended 24 C.F.R. § 960.225 carries that condition into admissions to public housing. Every household member must be a U.S. citizen or a noncitizen whose status qualifies the person for HUD assistance. The verification standard attaches to every member. One member who cannot produce qualifying verification makes the whole household ineligible, and the assistance ends.

The rule does not merely investigate a filing problem. Once verification exposes mixed status, it cancels the subsidy.

HUD invokes the public-housing admission statute, 42 U.S.C. § 1436d(2), its general rulemaking authority under 42 U.S.C. § 3534(d), and the Administrative Procedure Act, 5 U.S.C. § 553. Neither substantive provision expressly chooses between individual and household eligibility. The individual-proration policy existed in HUD’s regulation. HUD changed that regulation. The supportable conclusion is therefore narrower than saying Congress required family forfeiture: HUD selected household-wide forfeiture, and the rule identifies no congressional command resolving that choice.

Turner’s answer is that “American taxpayers will no longer foot the bill.” His statement also says “Promises made, promises kept.” The proration ledger defeats both claims. Since 1995, the subsidy has been calculated to cover only eligible family members. The payment Turner promised to stop was never being made on behalf of an ineligible member. If HUD found a federal cost in a larger unit, a utility allowance, or an administrative dollar, its announcement did not identify one. HUD has cited a filing problem as the warrant for evicting families.

HUD also says the assistance relinquished by mixed-status families will pass to fully eligible people on waiting lists. Federal housing reaches only about a quarter of the eligible households in need. A waitlist does not answer the arithmetic.

The rule’s own regulatory record does. HUD’s regulatory impact analysis projected the affected population eligible for assistance falling from roughly 55,100 to 24,100. Commenters put the added cost of serving the same number of households at $311 million to $385 million. HUD did not adopt those figures, but it acknowledged the category in its own words: “HUD acknowledges these transfer and cost implications.” A rule that costs more to run the same program is not a savings measure. It is a spending measure with an eviction attached.

The warnings from state officials were specific. A coalition of 22 attorneys general, led by Massachusetts Attorney General Andrea Campbell, said the rule would force families to choose between “evicting family members and losing their affordable housing.” The Massachusetts Union of Public Housing Tenants and the Massachusetts Law Reform Institute warned that the rule “will result in eligible families being ousted from their homes, cause family division, and cost housing providers and HUD millions of dollars to implement.” That is household forfeiture.

The scale is not marginal. A late-2025 Center for Budget and Policy Priorities analysis of the proposed rule estimated that nearly 80,000 people nationwide could be displaced. About 37,000 of them were children, most of them U.S. citizens. Researchers put the number of disrupted mixed-status families near 20,000. Nearly 1,000 mixed-status households in Massachusetts could be affected. This rule manufactures evictions at scale.

Massachusetts local housing authorities manage about 30,000 federally supported public-housing units and 41,500 state-funded units. This rule reaches the federal category, not the state-funded stock. The federal government writes the family-separation rule. Local authorities process it. Families absorb the loss.

State senators opposing the rule said shelter systems in Massachusetts were already operating beyond capacity and that subsidized-housing waitlists run for years. For most families holding a unit, they wrote, a federal subsidy “is what keeps them housed.”

HUD’s separate work-requirement rule is not final. It would encourage public housing authorities to limit assistance for nondisabled, nonelderly adults to no less than two years and urge landlords accepting Section 8 vouchers to require full-time employment. Massachusetts officials estimate that proposal would place another 115,000 people at risk of losing rental assistance, including 51,100 children and 79,500 people in working families. The citizenship rule is final. The work proposal is not. Both make federally assisted housing conditional on immigration status, age, and labor. That is removal policy carried out through the benefits machinery, household by household.

The same administration’s eligibility architecture reaches across federal benefits. States have already [sued to block the public-charge rule](/articles/2026-09-14-states-sue-to-block-public-charge-rule-citing-billions-in-lost-funds/), a separate barrier to public benefits for some noncitizens. A [Census proposal](/articles/2026-09-10/census-proposal-would-exclude-refugees-and-asylum-seekers/) would exclude refugees and asylum seekers. Across housing, benefits, and census systems, federal eligibility is becoming a citizenship screen. Supporters call the work requirement work integrity. The mechanism is exclusion. The price falls on families whose members do not possess the status the government wants to reward.

An honest eligibility rule would determine each person’s status, prorate assistance for eligible members, and keep one person’s immigration status from canceling everyone else’s housing claim. It would investigate incomplete files while preserving assistance for the eligible members. Whether Congress should revisit the governing eligibility statute is a separate question; HUD’s rulemaking record does not establish a statutory command for family forfeiture. Families will make the government’s arithmetic at their kitchen tables on Dec. 14.

Turner told taxpayers their bill was ending. The proration ledger shows the subsidy had covered only eligible members all along; the payment he promised to stop was never being made. Scott Turner is throwing thousands of families out of their homes. His rule turns one relative’s immigration status into the loss of every other relative’s federally assisted home.
