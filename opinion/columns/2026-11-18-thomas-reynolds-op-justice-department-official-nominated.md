Cornyn, Cruz, and the White House are hijacking a lifetime federal judgeship for political leverage.

Article II gives the President the power to nominate federal judges “by and with the Advice and Consent of the Senate.” U.S. Const. art. II, § 2, cl. 2. The strongest case for Elliot Gaiser is narrow but real. Gaiser leads the Justice Department’s Office of Legal Counsel, and the Senate confirmed him for that post. Cruz says Gaiser has already been through the background-check process because of that confirmation. A district seat left open to a successor Senate can remain vacant for years. The calendar is finite. Cruz says Gaiser is “the only nominee who could actually be confirmed by the end of the year,” because the White House has been “explicit” that there is no time to run a background check on another potential nominee. The administration expects all Trump judicial nominees confirmed by year’s end. This is the case for Gaiser as his advocates make it.

Those facts do not answer the missing record. Gaiser’s Senate nominee questionnaire says Ted Cruz’s staff first contacted him about becoming a federal judge on Nov. 4, the day after the election. He was nominated within weeks. John Cornyn says Gaiser never went through the bipartisan federal judicial evaluation committee traditionally used in Texas, and he says the White House conducted zero consultation. Cruz says the White House has been explicit that there is no time to run a background check on another candidate. None of this is accident. It is schedule. A calendar deadline cannot manufacture vetting. Gaiser’s confirmation for his Justice Department position cannot supply the home-state vetting Cornyn says the process skipped. The process was not merely rushed. It was skipped.

DOJ’s statement answers that record with adjectives. It calls Cornyn’s refusal “political antics” that “only hurt the American public and the rule of law.” It calls Gaiser “a brilliant legal mind, a principled defender of the Constitution, and an outstanding judicial nominee,” and calls the opposition “political obstruction.” It identifies no completed bipartisan evaluation. It gives no account of the Nov. 4 contact or the absent local review. A department can condemn delay. It cannot produce a committee record that does not exist. The department has the relationship backwards: Vetting is what protects the rule of law. Cornyn’s strongest objection concerns process, not ideology, and DOJ has not supplied process.

Cornyn’s conduct does not rescue the White House. A “blue slip” is effectively a home-state senator’s permission for the Judiciary Committee to advance that senator’s judicial nominee. Cornyn has not returned Gaiser’s slip and does not plan to. He says he does not want the seat left vacant, while the White House has requested information about his preferred candidate, Fort Worth lawyer Steve Fahey. Fahey served in the U.S. Attorney’s Office for the Northern District of Texas from 2003 to 2021, then became a partner at Kirkland & Ellis before starting his own firm in 2024. Those facts make him a serious alternative. A résumé is not committee review. The White House’s request proves an alternative could be considered; it does not prove Gaiser received review. The blue slip can block Gaiser. It cannot install Fahey. DOJ calls the refusal political. It is a home-state veto exercised for a preferred occupant. The process objection remains unanswered.

Cruz supplies the other end of the coercion. If the committee does not move Gaiser, he says “the people of Texas will pay the price” because Democrats will effectively take the seat and keep it vacant “in perpetuity.” A vacant judgeship belongs to no party until the President nominates and the Senate confirms. Cruz’s statement turns a public office into partisan property and treats a vacancy as punishment. That is not constitutional advice. It is a threat. That is a threat filed as a forecast.

The blue slip is a tradition rather than a statute, and an incoming majority need not honor the gate. Cruz’s forecast depends on the next majority observing a practice that the next majority controls. The threat borrows its force from a norm the threat’s own logic declines to guarantee.

Even Cornyn saw the instrument. He called Gaiser “a very bright young man” with “a very bright future” and said he felt bad that Gaiser had “been used basically as a pawn in this process.” The nominee is the tool here, not the point. Cruz’s statement exposes the selection method, stated aloud: The White House chose among the candidates its clock would clear, and the nominee whose completed process was for the Justice Department post, not the judgeship.

The brake held this once because the man holding it had nothing left for the machine to take. Cornyn lost his primary to Ken Paxton after a term spent [going to great lengths to avoid the president’s wrath](/articles/2026-05-26-cornyn-tried-to-avoid-trump-s-wrath-but-lost-texas-senate-primary-to-paxton/), and his majority expires with the year. The calendar priced the norm, and the norm held only where the price had already been paid.

Whether an incoming Senate would honor the blue-slip tradition or leave the seat open indefinitely is genuinely contested. The record’s sharpest fact does not depend on that forecast. The committee can convene. The file can be checked. A completed judicial file can reach the Senate.

The pattern is not confined to this nomination. On Nov. 11, MSI reported that [Republican senators were already stalling Trump judicial nominees in the majority’s final weeks](/articles/2026-11-11-republican-senators-stall-trump-judicial-nominees-in-the-majority-s-final-weeks/). The recurring machinery is a home-state slip, an end-of-session deadline, and the claim that a seat belongs to the other party. That machinery outlives the majority. The same levers remain available when control of the Senate changes. A home-state check can legitimately test whether a nominee’s record is complete. A personal selection claim cannot.

Gaiser exposes the machinery because each side converted a lifetime appointment into leverage before the record could support confirmation. The White House moved toward Gaiser after Cruz’s staff contacted him. Cornyn froze the seat and identified Fahey as his preferred candidate. DOJ then called the resulting gridlock political. The actors arranged the obstruction and charged the other side with causing it.

The confirmation calendar is the upstream half of the docket. Whoever sits on the district bench decides which cases receive a federal hearing in Dallas and which remain unresolved, for the life of the appointment. Every practice that slows the throughput gets renamed obstruction. This window’s renaming is already on the record; DOJ supplied the phrase.

An honest end-of-year confirmation would put the nominee through the federal judicial evaluation and home-state review Texas ordinarily uses, disclose that record, and let senators consent or withhold consent on a completed judicial file. If the White House cannot finish before January, it should leave the seat vacant rather than ask the Senate to treat a press release as consultation. The White House asked for information about Fahey. Cruz then said the White House had been explicit that it had no time to check anyone else. A calendar that can clear only the man it has already cleared has announced what it optimizes for.

The blue slip is one sheet of paper. The judgeship is a courtroom in Dallas, held for life, ruling on federal cases for as long as its holder draws breath. Cruz’s staff reached Gaiser the day after the election, and the administration says there is no time to check anyone else. That arithmetic is the whole story.

Cornyn used the blue slip to turn personal preference into Senate leverage. Cruz used a threatened vacancy to turn delay into a partisan weapon. The White House used the year-end calendar to excuse the vetting it skipped. They turned a lifetime judgeship into campaign inventory; advice and consent became a bargaining chip.
