The district attorney who campaigned on alternatives to prison collected one on Thursday: three years and change of it, for selling the courthouse. Jody Owens, formerly the chief prosecutor of Hinds County, Mississippi's largest, put his office up for sale — soliciting $250,000 in bribes to rig the bidding on city contracts — and the public learned the asking price only because the buyers turned out to be FBI agents in costume, two of them, posing as real estate developers. The judge tacked on a $100,000 fine. The Reformer is going where he used to send people. That part of the machine worked.

Sentencing him, Judge Daniel Jordan put the transaction in plain words: "He took an oath to uphold the law, and yet he solicited $250,000 in bribes to rig what should have been a fair and open bidding system for city contracts." Owens came to that oath from the Southern Poverty Law Center, where his trade was suing the powerful on behalf of people the law had overlooked. In court he said, "I can't begin to tell you the shame I feel," and then he counted who he betrayed: himself, his family, his friends, his colleagues. The people of Jackson did not make the list. They were the merchandise.

He was elected in 2019 as a criminal justice reformer, running on prosecuting violent crime while finding alternatives to jail for the rest. No candidate that year promised the voters an honest district attorney, so Jackson was free to elect the other kind.

A pawnbroker sells what other people left in his keeping. A courthouse pawnbroker sells what the public left in his, and Owens was working the commission side of the counter. The merchandise was Jackson.

Call it what it is. A city contract is public money waiting for a signature, and the signature is worth whatever the hand on the pen decides to charge. The only thing that keeps the price honest is somebody watching, and the oversight that caught this one in Jackson came dressed as the buyer. The excuse writes itself: ambush, setup, target. An ambush explains the buyer. It does not explain the sellers — three guilty pleas, two prison terms, and a relative of the district attorney among the guilty.

He did not dine alone. The scheme wound through Jackson's city hall like cash through a coin slot. The indictment says he passed more than $80,000 along to the council president, the mayor and a council member so a development project could move. The city's former mayor, Chokwe Antar Lumumba, [pleaded guilty in July to one count of conspiracy](/articles/2026-07-07-former-jackson-mayor-pleads-guilty-to-bribery-wire-fraud-money-laundering/) and is still waiting to be sentenced. Aaron Banks, former president of the city council, drew almost four years the same day Owens did. Angelique Lee, formerly the council's vice president, pleaded guilty in 2024, and so did Sherik Marve Smith, a relative of the district attorney, which tells you how the envelopes traveled. And then there is the district attorney. I have watched this movie since Nixon, and the badge has never once stopped the sale.

This is a capital city, in the poorest state in the union, one that has spent years struggling to keep clean water in its pipes, and its leadership was for sale to a development deal. Not one bad apple. The barrel came with a price list.

The trade keeps colleagues, and the trade keeps a map. This summer a Montana jury convicted a former state Senate president of official misconduct [over no-bid contracts](/articles/2026-07-22-montana-jury-convicts-former-senate-president-of-official-misconduct-for-no-bid-/). Weeks later a New Mexico jury convicted a former state lawmaker of a [school-fund kickback scheme](/articles/2026-08-14-jury-convicts-former-new-mexico-lawmaker-of-school-fund-kickback-scheme/). Four since July, by my count.

The lineage runs back past all of them: Charles Keating spread about $1.3 million across five United States senators, and when he was asked whether the money had bought influence, he answered — in the most forceful way he could — that he hoped so. The FBI has run this exact play before — fake money, a fictional sheikh, hungry officials — and the take, half a century back, was a United States senator and a row of congressmen. The script has never needed a rewrite. The marks volunteer.

Mark the strange part, because it does not come around often in six decades of watching: this time the sellers went inside. And note the price of admission. Surfacing a quarter-million-dollar theft from a city that cannot spare it took the federal government flying into town and pretending to be the market. No one stages that for the banks.

In 2012, HSBC admitted washing money for Mexican drug cartels, paid about $1.9 billion, and signed a deferred-prosecution deal; not one officer of that bank saw a cell, and the arrangement was exactly what it looked like — quite literally, a purchased get-out-of-jail-free card. Not one Wall Street chief executive went to prison for the crash of 2008. A kid who walks out of a store with a television collects a record. A bank that launders cartel money collects an installment plan. The law binds the man. It negotiates with the balance sheet.

Owens priced the courthouse at a quarter of a million dollars. Judge Jordan said the bidding in that room should have been fair and open. On the record produced, the description fits exactly one bidder — the only buyer who ever showed up at that counter. He came in pretending to be somebody else.
