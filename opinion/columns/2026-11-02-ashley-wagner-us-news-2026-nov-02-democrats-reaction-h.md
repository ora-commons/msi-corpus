Haley Stevens betrayed Michigan working families by endorsing Mike Rogers for Senate.

This is not a breach of etiquette. It is a political defection. Stevens is the Michigan Democrat who narrowly lost the Democratic primary to Abdul El-Sayed, then handed the Republican candidate an endorsement after a feud with El-Sayed’s campaign over an outside-group advertisement blaming the high cost of living on U.S. support for Israel. The sequence matters: the disagreement came first; the handoff was the response.

The advertisement deserved condemnation. It gave families a foreign-policy villain for a domestic economic squeeze and turned material hardship into a moral test. Some Democrats and Jewish leaders condemned it. Separately, Democratic congressional nominees Brad Lander and Daniel Biss, both Jewish, urged voters to support El-Sayed. The advertisement was not El-Sayed’s own position, and Stevens did object publicly. She could have rejected the message, skipped the event, and still supported him. She said she would appear only if he denounced it.

The rupture was already public when, after she made denouncing the ad a condition of appearing, [El-Sayed asked Stevens to stay away from his campaign events](/articles/2026-10-31-el-sayed-asks-haley-stevens-to-stay-away-from-michigan-senate-campaign-events/). His campaign disinvited her. Then Stevens endorsed the opposition. Her grievance was with El-Sayed; Rogers was the candidate on the other side of the line. A bad message is not a warrant to endorse the opposition. It is a reason to defeat the message without abandoning the voters who made you a candidate.

Instead, Stevens transferred a Michigan Democrat’s credibility directly to the Republican campaign in a Senate race where working families were deciding who would answer for health care, housing, and the household ledger. Rogers got an endorsement. Trump got a Democratic name to praise. A Rogers victory would put the Republican in the Michigan Senate seat El-Sayed was contesting. Working families got no extra dollar of child care, rent relief, or health coverage.

Her [defense of the switch](/articles/2026-11-02-haley-stevens-defends-switch-to-mike-rogers-in-michigan-senate-race/) was not an explanation. “I’m just casting my one vote this way,” she said while insisting she still wanted Democrats to win the majority. When Cooper asked whether the disinvite triggered the endorsement, Stevens refused to answer and let several seconds of silence air. Her preference for a Democratic majority explains why she wanted the seat held by a Democrat. It does not explain why backing the Republican challenger would better serve working families after El-Sayed had become the nominee.

Michigan Democratic Party chair Curtis Hertel said Stevens was not a member of the party and would not be in the future; he called the decision the antithesis of the career he had known. Trump called her a “highly respected Democrat,” praised her loyalty, and said he would welcome her into the Republican Party. Asked about Trump’s praise, Stevens told CNN: “Well, I impeached him twice.” Then she criticized Republicans for being “really focused on just letting Trump be the dictator.” Those words did not explain Rogers; they sharpened the contradiction. The endorsement’s direction was not hard to read.

She can impeach Trump in one sentence and endorse a Republican Senate candidate in the next. That is not consistency; it is selective loyalty.

On my kitchen-table spreadsheet, the arithmetic is not ideological. David and I have two children, Eva and Ben. Our household ledger tracks what comes home after taxes, then subtracts childcare, housing, utilities, food, and debt service. The bills arrive before the family gets to choose anything. We bought the 1,400-square-foot Fishtown rowhouse in 2022 with help from David’s grandmother’s estate. Our mortgage remains at 7 percent, and three years of refinancing inquiries have not moved it to 5 percent.

The estate money belongs in the ledger, not a footnote. Without it, our housing security is shakier than the spreadsheet makes it look. That cushion matters. This is our household, not a national average, and it is not a proxy for Michigan. It shows what “cost of living” becomes at a bank account: every dollar has a job before the family gets to choose one.

Debbie Dingell said Michigan residents were focused on the cost of living, health care, and housing—not on Stevens’ continued efforts to get attention. She was right. The voters did not need another party strategist to tell them what the grocery cart, rent check, insurance deductible, and childcare invoice already said. The urgency was concrete: it was what those bills did to a family’s choices. El-Sayed described the divide as people locked out versus people locking them out. Stevens answered that material question with a Republican endorsement.

A generation was sold on credentials as the route to a stable middle-class life. Now the credential can arrive with a daycare invoice, a mortgage renewal, and an ER bill, while work that was supposed to finance a life gets sold as a personality test. Anne Helen Petersen’s burnout diagnosis matters because it explains how a structural squeeze becomes a private failure: “Burnout isn’t a personal problem, and it will not be cured by a productivity app or face mask.” A side hustle will not cure it. A more disciplined mother will not cure it. Voters are not irrational when they price the squeeze. Stevens’ endorsement asks them to put party loyalty above the ledger they can read.

Taylor Swift’s “You’re On Your Own, Kid” can be read as the mission statement of American care infrastructure: manage the shock yourself. The title assigns the parent the assembly of work, insurance, childcare, transportation, and contingency, while the public has arranged much of the surrounding system so that the assembly becomes unpaid labor. Swift puts the pressure in the title instead of disguising it as personal failure. The public guarantee is thin, the shared floor is narrow, and affordable care is scarce. Every shock gets handed back to the household and called independence.

The song’s turn toward a circle of friends is the redemptive part. In a working mother’s life, that circle may be the group chat, the sister who covers Saturday, the parish fundraiser, the neighbor with the spare car. It is a safety net made of love. It is not public provision. Stevens’ endorsement leaves Michigan families choosing a party over their bills while public policy keeps the bills private.

My mother belonged to the Altar-Rosary Society at St. Stanislaus in Lansdale. I remember the parish’s women moving meals to sick neighbors and St. Vincent de Paul quietly arranging rent and utility help. The society organized meals, school help, and funeral lunches because the neighborhood had to carry what institutions would not. That was the common-good formation my household inherited: care was somebody’s organized obligation, not proof of a personal deficiency. My parents raised three children through parish school and paid off their house in that formation. During my elementary years, our household ran on Dad’s postal salary.

By the only measure I trust—what our families can actually afford—David and I are better off than my parents were and still cannot manage for two children what they managed for three. That is not a story about an older generation ruining everything. It is a policy indictment. The wage-and-housing regime that made the old arrangement ordinary has been replaced by one that makes it unreplicable. The family-wage standard our parish knew was simple: work ought to make a decent household possible. The current model asks the household to absorb what wages, relatives, and parish volunteers once carried between them.

The [Democratic divisions already visible in the race’s final week](/articles/2026-10-29-democratic-divisions-surface-in-michigan-senate-race-s-final-week/) did not need this ending. Follow the benefit. Rogers gets a Democratic endorsement. Trump gets the spectacle of a Democratic defection he praised as almost unprecedented loyalty. The diffuse cost lands on the households whose votes are supposedly being represented. Stevens chose Rogers. Working families got another month of doing the assembly work themselves.

A real pro-family agenda is embarrassingly concrete and measurable: universal pre-K; a refundable federal childcare credit; paid leave that reaches hourly workers; stronger renter protection; health coverage families can use without first facing an unaffordable deductible; full funding for special education under IDEA; and Pell aid that covers a meaningful share of public-college costs. These policies make the spreadsheet work. This is not a fantasy about kindness; it is public investment, and serious public investment has to be costed. Make care a floor under the family wage, not a test of which parent can optimize harder.

At eleven at night, I open the household spreadsheet and move the same numbers between the same two columns. The last cell is net income minus childcare, mortgage, health care, and the hours that make the schedule work. Childcare is not a belief system. The mortgage is not a metaphor. A family vote is not a loyalty test. That is the betrayal.

Taylor Swift’s title is the instruction this country keeps giving us: “You’re On Your Own, Kid.” The kitchen table does not need another sermon about resilience. It needs public care that makes a family possible—and a Senate candidate who answers to the ledger, not the party seal.
