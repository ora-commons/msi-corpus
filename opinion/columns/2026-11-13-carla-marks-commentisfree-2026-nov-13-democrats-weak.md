A cost-of-living crisis is a wage crisis wearing a price tag. Everyone in politics can see the price tag; almost nobody will name the wage. [Dustin Guastella, writing in the Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/commentisfree/2026/nov/13/democrats-weaknesses-2028), comes closer than most: he grants the Democratic midterm wins in Ohio, Georgia, Iowa, Maine and Texas, then warns that "affordability" is messaging rather than an economic program, that the revolt against globalism isn't going away, and that the party has drifted from working-class cultural values — three weaknesses to fix before 2028. He has earned the first point outright and half of the second. The third is the one I want to take apart.

Give him his due, because most of the scaffolding holds. He is honest about the wreckage: the tariffs failed to bring good jobs home, and no Republican defends them now. He is right that 2024 was also an affordability election, one the party lost — and 2026 was an affordability election the party won, which is its own proof that anger at prices is loyalty to a moment, not to a flag. He is right that a coalition that leans richer while wearing the affordability mantle has a real problem. And he is right that "affordability" is not a program. It is a grievance with a coupon attached.

Consider the coupon itself. Cory Booker campaigned to exempt every resident making under $75,000 from federal income tax — a $5tn tax cut, in Guastella's phrase enough to make Grover Norquist blush, a giveaway in a populism costume with an expiration date and nothing built underneath. Cash-in-hand can win blue-collar votes in a cost-of-living crunch; it will not win their loyalty. A tax cut is a price argument. Hand a voter a few dollars. Change nothing about who sets the wage.

He is right about the cultural gap as a fact, too: professional-class voters are more socially liberal than working-class voters, and more so than they were a few years ago, and the people who staff campaigns live in a much narrower world than the voters they are meant to persuade. All true. I grant it the way I grant most things — happily, and then I ask what it buys. Here is what it buys: a fix that is free. If the problem is tone, you change the consultants, soften the sentences, run the ads in a different key — and nobody has to give up anything. Now read what his own program asks for. Major new investment in infrastructure. Industrial policy to revive manufacturing. Crackdowns on corporate gouging. Expanded Medicare and Social Security. Free community college. New workplace rights. That is a real list. It is furniture I'd happily put in the same room — and it is entirely a list of things the state does with money. Not one line of it changes who owns the firm or who names the price of an hour of work.

The wage bill did the damage, and we can read it. Union contracts cover about 11% of American workers — 16.5 million people — and under 6% of private-sector workers carry a card. Union members take home a median $1,404 a week; non-union workers, $1,174. The party won 53% of voters between $30,000 and $99,000 and about 60% of voters above $300,000, and still lost the non-college vote, 47 to 51. Its richest bracket is its steadiest one. That is not a working-class movement with a messaging problem. That is a party that has never touched the split.

Cost of living is the tell. A third of voters named it their number-one issue, and nearly 40% said their finances had gotten worse over the year. What were those people describing? Not a price. A split. Prices are set by firms with pricing power. Wages are set by firms with hiring power. When one side of that table has an organization and the other has a job application, the split moves one way — and it has. When the wage comes up short, the difference lands on the public's tab — food stamps, Medicaid, tax credits. That is a business model that only pencils out because someone else eats the cost. Walk me through it slowly: which sentence about cultural values moves a wage?

Because here is the evidence his column never mentions: the party already built the program, ran it, measured it, and shelved it. In 2021 the expanded Child Tax Credit put a monthly deposit in family bank accounts, and by the Census Bureau's own count child poverty fell 46% in a single year — from 9.7% to 5.2%, the lowest ever measured — with roughly 2.9 million children lifted out, about 2.1 million of them because of the expansion. In January 2022 the expansion expired, and the rate snapped back. Turned on, halved. Turned off, restored. That was an economic program, and it took one year. Since then the party has mostly been auditioning personalities. The economy is a set of choices, not the weather; the lapse was a choice made in a room, and it can be unmade in one. A party that halved child poverty and forgot to run on it doesn't have a program gap. It has a memory gap.

On trade, I'll concede the wound before I object to the bandage. By the Wall Street Journal's count, Democrats spent more than $58m on ads attacking tariffs and exactly nothing making the case for a trade agenda of their own. That is not a cultural failure. That is a party willing to pay for a message and unwilling to pay for a program. But the positive case doesn't live where he's pointing, and it is not a tariff schedule with better manners. Every summer at my aunt's kitchen table on the Swedish west coast, the unemployment fund came up the way Americans discuss the garbage schedule: the a-kassa, the envelope, the cousin between jobs who kept her apartment through a retraining program. Nobody at that table has ever once used the word utopia. Swedes and Danes are among the most trade-dependent people on earth — they export for a living — and their answer to globalization was never a wall. It was a floor. Fire people easily, then pay most of a laid-off worker's wages — up to two years of them, historically — then spend roughly 2% of GDP retraining her into the next job. The Danes protect the worker, not the job — which is why a small open economy keeps voting for the arrangement instead of against it. The tariff nostalgia has already been tested at full scale, and even the column concedes no Republican will defend the results. Anyway.

His border beat is the same answer in different work clothes. Immigration ranked number three among voters' concerns this cycle — the only issue where Republicans still hold the trust advantage — and Guastella is right that decrying Ice's brutality is not a border policy. But control plus humane treatment describes a labor market, and the wage-and-standards half runs through the same bargaining machinery that would take coverage from 11% to 29.

Then the third weakness, where he loses me. The report he cites — Jared Abbott and Joan Williams, for the Center for Working-Class Politics — finds that the biggest single contributor to winning blue-collar voters is "embracing cultural positions that resonate with the working class." Fine: retire the lecture; no serious person wants the party talking down to anybody. But read his own evidence. The candidates who overperformed in blue-collar districts — Sherrod Brown, Brian Poindexter, Bob Brooks, his "Bernie-dogs" — weren't running on vibes. They were running on wages, plants, pensions. The report itself notes that working-class voters are not uniformly conservative on social issues, and in many cases they have moved significantly to the left in recent years. And the largest transfer the party ever made to working families in recent memory — the child benefit, again — asked nobody to adopt anybody's dialect. It asked for a bank account. The remedy for distance isn't mimicry; it's recurrence: people trust what shows up every month. A column that diagnoses a values gap, proposes cultural accommodation as the cure, and never mentions the repealed benefit that produced the biggest poverty number in the party's modern record has told on itself. Adjusting the message is cheap. Rebuilding the machine is not.

So the culture-first theory owes an explanation of 2021: the year the party delivered cash, child poverty halved, and the voters it supposedly cannot reach took the benefit. Explain the lapse, or retire the diagnosis. And when someone tells you the floor is impossible here — remind me what the obstacle is again. In Denmark the union runs the unemployment insurance, so joining the one pays for the other; in Ohio the two have never met. Danish contracts cover four out of five workers, bargained for a whole industry at once, so the non-union shop down the street can't undercut them; American contracts cover about eleven percent. A century of pipes nobody poured — and the pourable part exists now. Somebody chose that. It wasn't the culture.

So what do we build instead? Three things, each already legal somewhere. Wage boards: several states already run them for specific industries — fast food, home care — setting a floor across a whole sector at once; modeling of a full sectoral system puts American bargaining coverage at 29% instead of today's 11%, roughly 42.4 million workers instead of 16.5 million. That is not a message. That is a wage — and it is a number a workers' party could put on a poster. Ownership: about 6,500 employee-ownership plans already cover 15 million Americans and hold more than $2tn; the worker-cooperative census counts roughly 820 firms, up from 323 in 2014. Mondragon — 70,000 workers, €11bn in revenue — pays its highest-paid people about five or six times what its lowest-paid members take home. Five-to-one is a choice. So is three-hundred-to-one.

And the deposit, the one the party already knows how to run: re-enact the child benefit and make it automatic, so no future Congress has to rediscover 2021 by accident — a monthly deposit nobody has to re-earn. Around it, the long project: tie unemployment insurance to union membership, so a union card pays for itself, and a retraining guarantee that turns a plant closing into a transition instead of a verdict. The pay-fors start where the money pools — Guastella himself volunteers the list, from rich tax cheats and big pharma to Wall Street — beginning with [the tech billionaires now discovering what scrutiny feels like](/articles/2026-11-05-tech-billionaires-face-scrutiny-after-democratic-congressional-gains/).

None of it is quick, and I won't pretend otherwise. Union density sits at ten percent; right-to-work statutes run across much of the country; employers have never once organized as a bloc, and they would rather litigate than bargain. The policy is the tip; the institutions are the iceberg. Two years will not build a century of machinery. Two years can pass wage boards in a dozen states and make employee ownership the first question anyone asks when a firm goes up for sale — and unlike the slogan, the tip is real, measured, and it reverses only by choice.

Every summer I watched my cousins in Gothenburg never once run the arithmetic on whether a sick child was affordable that week. Not because Swedes are nicer. Because somebody built the table.

The midterms handed Democrats [control of Congress](/articles/2026-11-09-democrats-control-congress-as-business-faces-four-post-election-concerns/) on a grocery bill, with Trump [entering them at 32% approval](/articles/2026-11-01-democrats-lead-polls-as-trump-enters-midterms-with-32-approval/). The cheap diagnosis — tone, values, messaging — will be the first thing anyone reaches for in January, because it is the only item on the table that costs nothing. The other one costs power, which is how you know it is the real one. The Danes didn't get their floor by reading the room. They got there by showing up for a hundred years, and the trust followed the plumbing. Wage boards. Employee ownership. A monthly deposit nobody has to re-earn. A floor that does not move when the market does. Anyway — the economy is a set of choices, not the weather. Build the table. Build the plumbing. The vibes will take care of themselves.
