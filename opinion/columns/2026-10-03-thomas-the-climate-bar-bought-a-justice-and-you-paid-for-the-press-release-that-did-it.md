The climate-litigation industry has effectively purchased one-ninth of the Supreme Court with a press release, and the financial-interest language of 28 U.S.C. § 455 was never the reason.

What happened in the past week was not a measured application of judicial ethics. It was a surrender — a justice exiting a case of national consequence because activists manufactured a conflict where none exists, in a posture designed to ensure the case is never heard on the merits at all. The commentariat will tell you this is a small institutional story. It is not. It is a story about whether a litigation strategy that cannot win on the law can win by changing the bench.

Here is what the commentariat will also not tell you, because the commentariat does not want you to know it: the plaintiffs' bar has a problem. The state-law climate suits that the attorneys general have brought against the major energy producers are, on the merits, an uphill fight. The strongest of those suits has to clear a federal-preemption bar that Congress built on purpose, and that bar is the real fight. It is also the fight the activists do not want, because they are losing it. So they have done something cleverer than win on the law. They have changed the question. The recusal demand was issued. The justice complied. The case will now likely die without a hearing. That is the story.

Start with the strongest version of the plaintiffs' case, because it deserves to be stated cleanly. Read the complaints generously, and a state-law climate suit survives facial preemption review on three pillars. First, the Clean Air Act's savings clause at 42 U.S.C. § 7416 preserves state authority to adopt and enforce emissions limitations, including through state common-law actions for in-state harms traceable to out-of-state emissions — a textual reservation that defeats total field preemption on its face. Congress knows those clauses are there. Congress put them there on purpose. Second, the Supreme Court's modern preemption doctrine carries a strong presumption against displacing state law in areas of traditional state concern, and *International Paper Co. v. Ouellette*, 479 U.S. 481 (1987), cuts less far than it appears: it displaced *federal* common-law nuisance for in-state harms from out-of-state pollution, not parallel state-law theories the plaintiffs have standing to press in their own courts. The common law got there first. The federal statute arrived later. The presumption runs the way the plaintiffs want it to run. Third, and this is the move that gives the theory its surface plausibility, the dormant commerce clause's extraterritoriality line, as developed in *Healy v. Beer Institute*, 491 U.S. 324 (1989), restricts state regulation of out-of-state *conduct* but does not bar state-law tort actions for in-state *injuries* — and the specific defendants here are not the entities the Clean Air Act directly regulates at the source. They are upstream fuel producers whose product, when combusted by millions of third parties, contributes to a global atmospheric concentration. That causal chain, the argument goes, looks more like a products-liability theory than a regulated-emissions theory, and products liability is precisely the kind of state-law claim the savings clauses were written to preserve. The argument is plausible. It is not frivolous. A defense lawyer who told you this was an easy preemption win would be selling you something, and any honest briefing of the issue in the lower courts has acknowledged as much.

Plausibility is not victory, and three things break the case on the merits. The plaintiffs want damages for conduct — the sale and production of fossil fuels — that, when combusted, produces emissions that the Clean Air Act expressly and comprehensively regulates through a permitting scheme that applies to the actual emitters. The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that where Congress has occupied a field with a comprehensive regulatory architecture, state-law tort actions that seek to punish the very conduct the federal scheme permits are preempted, not preserved. The savings clauses protect state common-law claims that operate in distinct domains — a nuisance action about a local polluting factory, a trespass claim about contamination on a specific piece of land. They do not protect a claim whose entire theory of liability is that the defendant sold a product whose lawful use produces regulated emissions, where the federal regulator has decided, on a record, how to allocate that burden. Second, allowing fifty state judges, fifty state juries, and fifty different liability regimes to govern the same global conduct is exactly the balkanization that preemption doctrine exists to prevent. The Supreme Court has been clear on this point for forty years: when the regulated conduct is inherently interstate and the federal scheme is comprehensive, the state-law claim is the one that has to step back. Third, the theory as pleaded would make every major American energy producer liable in every state court in the country for conduct that the federal government has affirmatively authorized. The constitutional structure of energy policy does not tolerate that outcome. The plaintiffs know this. That is why the case cannot be allowed to be decided.

The plaintiffs are not interested in the merits. They filed recusal motions targeting justices whose financial disclosures identify holdings in energy-company defendants, and they obtained, in lieu of any merits ruling, a one-line order — distributed, in modern Court practice, by press release — disposing of the disqualification question. The recusal strategy treats § 455(b)(4) not as a neutral disqualification rule but as a partisan lever. The motions stretch the statute's language to cover routine diversified holdings, recusal-by-proximity theories, and imputed interests drawn from the political networks of the climate bar's own allies. And the inversion that follows is deliberate: when the Court declines to disqualify on those grounds, as the statute's text compels, the press release announcing the denial is then recharacterized by the climate-litigation industry as evidence that one-ninth of the Court has been "captured" by industry money. The very procedural mechanism that refuses to recuse becomes proof of corruption, regardless of what § 455 actually says. The bench has been engineered, not by a party, but by an advocacy posture that has figured out how to weaponize the appearance-of-bias standard without ever having to prove actual bias. The press release did the work of a merits brief the activists could not write. That is the scandal.

Which brings us to the statute. The governing right-instrument is 28 U.S.C. § 455, and its text is the whole answer. Subsection (b)(4) requires recusal where the justice knows that the justice, or the justice's spouse or minor child, "has a financial interest in the subject matter in controversy or in a party to the proceeding." Subsection (d)(4) of the same statute defines that category as a "financial interest in the proceeding" — meaning the potential for gain or loss to the justice or the justice's family as a result of the proceeding. The financial-interest language is narrow and serious. It captures a direct economic stake in the outcome — ownership of stock in a party, a fee arrangement contingent on the result, a payment that flows from the judgment. It is not a tool for bench-engineering. The legislative history (S. Rep. No. 93-419) and the Judicial Conference's interpretive guidance tie the standard to the specific outcome of the case, not to the identity of the parties in the abstract. Subsection (a) of the same statute adds the broader standard: the justice shall disqualify "in any proceeding in which the justice's impartiality might reasonably be questioned." That standard is objective, but it is not a weapon. Reasonable observers do not demand recusal every time a justice attends an event at a law school, writes a book touching on the subject matter, or is criticized in an advocacy campaign. The standard requires a genuine appearance of bias — a direct personal connection, a prior substantive involvement that bears on the specific case, an economic stake. It does not permit a recusal-by-press-release. It does not permit a justice stepping aside because an advocacy organization issued a demand, in a posture where compliance is itself the advocacy organization's win. Section 455 was written to protect litigants from biased decisionmakers. It was not written to give activists a tool for bench-engineering, and the reasonable-observer test was not designed to be the test that activists pass by being loud enough.

The cost is real. One-ninth of the Court is now absent from a question of national consequence — not because the justice had a financial interest under § 455(b)(4), not because a reasonable observer would find genuine bias under § 455(a), but because the alternative was a public fight the justice did not want to have. That is not a recusal. It is a capitulation. And it has purchased the climate-litigation industry exactly the outcome the merits would not have given them: a Court that will never decide whether their theory of liability is law. The headline is the product. The press release is the receipt. And § 455 — narrowly read, as written — is the reason both were necessary. What was sold is one-ninth of the bench on a case of national consequence, and what was bought is a docket entry that ends without an opinion. The only thing missing from the receipt is a signature.
