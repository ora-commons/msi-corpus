Donald Trump weaponizes federal power to overturn Alaska’s Senate election before Alaskans vote.

I had a chainsaw spread across the bench and a generator waiting on the carburetor when the Justice Department’s case against Alaska came down the wire. On the bench, trouble has a location. You can put your hands on the bad part, trace the fuel line, and make the repair. Washington’s case against Alaska runs the other way. It arrives before the ballots, reaches for certification, and selects one race among all the close ones.

Federal power usually reaches me as a rule: a highway designation, a conservation-program condition, an inspection, a price somebody must pay. This time it reaches as a prosecutor with a hand on Alaska’s certification. That is the danger in plain view. A national government has decided that one state’s result matters less than the Senate arithmetic behind it.

The chronology is the tell. In late October, at Trump’s behest, the Justice Department sued to block certification of Alaska’s U.S. Senate race if Republican incumbent Dan Sullivan lost narrowly. On Wednesday, Trump demanded that Mary Peltola be removed from the ballot and prosecuted. Alaska’s Republican governor then ordered an investigation into Peltola’s campaign. On Sunday, a judge dismissed the Justice Department’s suit, and the administration said it planned to appeal.

None of this began after Alaska counted and certified the votes. It began before Alaskans voted. [Voting-rights experts have already called the federal intervention unprecedented](/articles/2026-11-01-voting-rights-experts-call-alaska-election-intervention-unprecedented/).

An investigation asks what happened. A pre-vote certification suit asks a federal judge to replace the state’s answer with a different one.

A New York Times investigation reported that operatives working with Peltola’s campaign helped recruit another Sullivan, a retired teacher from Petersburg, to split the incumbent’s vote. [Peltola and her campaign denied knowing about the alleged scheme](/articles/2026-10-30-peltola-denies-knowing-of-alleged-alaska-ballot-scheme-as-federal-probe-escalate/). The Alaska Supreme Court refused to remove the other Sullivan from the ballot.

The alleged ballot-splitting method is generally legal in Alaska. That matters because the complaint is not that the state ignored a settled election rule. The complaint is that the federal government wants a pre-vote remedy because it does not like the likely outcome.

The ballot-splitting method is not new. Trump’s allies pushed Kanye West onto ballots in Vermont, Arkansas and Wisconsin in 2020. In 2024, they worked to put Jill Stein and Cornel West on ballots in swing states, and Trump praised both from a rally stage for taking votes from Democrats. The old tactic was to make the mathematics work for one side.

No federal prosecutor sued to preempt those ballots. The intervention is new: a federal agency reaches into one state, before the count, to keep one side’s preferred result from being certified.

This is not uniform enforcement. In Ohio, Democratic-aligned groups are promoting Libertarian candidate Bill Redpath while Republicans have spent more than $1.58 million promoting Gregory Levy. In Iowa, Democratic-aligned PACs have spent hundreds of thousands of dollars promoting Libertarian candidate Thomas Raymond Laehn, whose presence on the ballot could cut into Republican Ashley Hinson’s thin lead.

The Justice Department did not sue in Ohio or Iowa. It sued in Alaska, where Mary Peltola, an Alaska Native woman favored by the polls, was defending the seat the administration wanted. The timing is the tell: the suit arrived when the polls favored Peltola and the Republican incumbent might lose. That is not law enforcement. That is a political instrument.

Why Alaska? The arithmetic is plain. Republicans held the Senate 53-47 before Election Day, and Alaska could decide whether Trump kept his majority for the final two years of his presidency. Trump has carried Alaska three times, though a majority of likely voters now disapprove of him. That explains what the administration has to gain. It does not explain why Ohio and Iowa were left alone.

The selection of the race is plain too. The suit did not arrive in Ohio or Iowa. It arrived in Alaska against an Alaska Native woman whom the polls favored to win.

The targeting does not end in Alaska. Peggy Flanagan, a member of the White Earth Band of Ojibwe, is running for the Senate in Minnesota and has faced similar scrutiny. The Wall Street Journal reported that she had inflated her academic credentials by claiming Phi Beta Kappa membership. Phi Beta Kappa’s associate secretary and chief operating officer confirmed that Flanagan had been inducted in 2002. The Wall Street Journal corrected its story. The story did not survive the basic check.

Researchers at Rutgers’ Center for American Women and Politics have found that doubts about electability follow women of color. Primary voters may support a candidate’s positions and still withhold support because they fear other voters will not. The researchers have also found that Native American women in politics carry heightened stress and additional emotional labor, while remaining among the least studied groups in the field.

Peltola has weathered two major New York Times investigations in five weeks, one into her treatment of staff and one into the ballot scheme, and her lead has held. No Native American woman has ever served in the U.S. Senate. This year, two are within reach of doing so.

Alaska Native voters are not one bloc for Washington to manage. In 2024, the North Slope and Northwest Arctic districts, where Alaska Native voters are a majority, gave Trump half of their combined presidential vote, while Peltola won 55% of their combined House vote. Many of the voters Washington now claims to be protecting chose both.

Those voters can split their ballots, balance the ledger, and choose a candidate for one office without turning one election into a referendum on their political identity. Treating Alaska Native voters as one bloc is how Washington uses race to move the arithmetic without naming what is doing the work.

None of this lands on a spreadsheet. A 53-seat Senate majority can change a household’s bill without rewriting every law. It controls the committee system, the floor, confirmations, and the terms on which a Medicaid package, reconciliation bill or farm bill must pass.

At home, Sara carries our family’s health coverage through her job as a paraprofessional in the Adams-Friendship School District; my own shop’s insurance was unaffordable last year. Federal Medicaid and coverage rules reach Adams County Memorial Hospital and rural health services. Federal school-meal and special-education money reaches the school district where Sara works. On Wisconsin farms, crop insurance, dairy support, conservation payments and commodity aid set terms for income.

Farm income buys seed, repairs machinery and keeps Main Street supplied. Health coverage keeps a working family from choosing between a medical bill and groceries. That is what a Senate majority means in Adams County.

The method matters more than the motive. The method is the proof.

The first danger is Alaska. The next danger is every state close enough to matter.

If a federal court can freeze one state’s certification in a close race, the procedure becomes available wherever a close result makes it useful. In Wisconsin, a close gubernatorial, legislative or Senate race could become the next target. The people at our polling place in Adams County would still count under Wisconsin law, but the president’s need for a different result could arrive through the Justice Department before a single ballot was counted. That is what “every state close enough to matter” means here: a local count made to wait while a federal case searches for another answer.

Alaska’s vote is not a federal possession because one party needs it.

The history is not new. The 1868 Treaty of Fort Laramie guaranteed the Black Hills to the Sioux, and the federal government broke that promise within a decade. Native nations have no reason to treat selective federal enforcement as trustworthy merely because it arrives wrapped in a lawsuit.

Closer to home, Adams-Columbia Electric Cooperative, the largest rural electric co-op in Wisconsin, is headquartered in Friendship. Its member-elected board answers for rates, service reliability and local jobs. It exists so the people dependent on the line are not merely customers of a distant company. That is what member ownership means: the household pays the bill, and the member has a hand in the institution that sets it.

Wendell Berry’s *The Unsettling of America* treated the movement of decisions away from communities forced to live with them as an economic and civic failure. The Alaska suit runs that arrangement backward. Washington claims authority to replace Alaska’s own count because the result may cost Trump’s party a seat.

For a claimed ballot scheme, gather the evidence, publish it and apply the same evidentiary standard in every state. A specific alleged violation belongs in the record; the state should answer under state law; the Justice Department may litigate; an impartial court must decide whether certification may be enjoined; and Alaska officials must administer the count. Certification should stand until that court says otherwise. Federal power should be the backstop, not the candidate’s private lawyer. Clean elections should mean one standard from Petersburg to Iowa, not federal intervention selected before the ballots are cast.

Let the ballots be counted. Let Alaska certify them. The line from our polling place in Adams County to Alaska should not be crossed by one federal suit, or by any race that follows.

Mary Peltola’s election belongs to the people of Alaska. Donald Trump does not get to choose which Alaskans may choose their senator because his Senate majority may hinge on their vote.
