The allies walked away from the bargain American working towns paid for with their blood.

I wrote up a brake job last week for a man who hauls freight between the port at Savannah and the distribution centers up I-16. Fifty-one years old. Two tours in Afghanistan with the 48th Brigade out of Fort Stewart, forty minutes up the road from where I sit. Bad back, bad knees. The VA will not call what is wrong with him service-connected. He drives the Freightliner because it is the only thing between him and the warehouse, and the warehouse pays twelve an hour.

He had been listening to talk radio on the drive down. Asked me what I made of Turkey, Pakistan, and Saudi Arabia signing a defense pact without us.

"So they're building their own roof," I told him.

He nodded. "What happens to the contracts?"

That is the right question. That is always the right question.

The hedging is real and it is happening everywhere at once. Poland raised its defense budget 71 percent since 2022. The Baltics, 134 percent. Scandinavia, 126 percent. Turkey, Pakistan, and Saudi Arabia signed a military pact in August that is going to rewrite the Middle East's security map. The BRICS summit in Delhi ended with 140 paragraphs about moving trade and money outside American financial networks.

But every dollar that goes to a new Polish missile battery is a dollar that did not go to a Polish nurse. Every Saudi riyal spent on a Turkish drone is a riyal that did not pave a road in Jeddah. And every American defense dollar that stays in the budget to prop up an umbrella that allies are now building their own copy of is a dollar that did not fix the bridge on I-16, or staff the VA clinic in Hinesville, or pay a teacher in Liberty County.

Here is the part that ought to make your teeth hurt. The defense contractors, Lockheed, RTX, Northrop, they do not lose when allies hedge. They sell to both sides of every deal. Half the components in the new European missile systems come from American subcontractors. The Turkish drones started in an American defense lab. The hedging does not cut the contractors out. It doubles the market. More countries buying more weapons, more often, from the same five companies that Eisenhower warned us about sixty-five years ago.

Eisenhower, in his farewell address of January 17, 1961, paragraph 24, warned: "In the councils of government, we must guard against the acquisition of unwarranted influence, whether sought or unsought, by the military-industrial complex." He was not talking about one country. He was warning about what happens when the business of war outgrows the people who have to fight it. The hedging makes that permanent. Not one complex feeding on public money, but a dozen, spread across every capital, all extracting from working communities, and not one of them building a road or staffing a clinic.

The man with the Freightliner did not build that system. He drove into it at nineteen because the recruiter's office in the strip mall was the only place in Liberty County that had a plan for a kid with his grades. Two tours. Bad back. Twelve an hour. And now the alliance he was told he was protecting is buying its own weapons from the same companies that got rich off his deployments.

Retired General David Petraeus, the man who ran both wars, said the US bases in the Gulf became "no longer viable" after Iranian missiles and drones struck them. When the man who ran the machine says the machine does not work, that ought to tell you something. Qatar's foreign ministry spokesman said it plain: "Self-sufficiency when it comes to security is the only way forward." That is a diplomat saying what my customer already knows. You cannot count on the man who told you to trust him.

The old deal was simple: you buy our weapons, we protect you, and everybody pretends the protection is the reason. The real reason was the contracts. It was always the contracts.

Some say the alliance will snap back after the next election. Sentiment might. The contracts will not. Bob Rae, Canada's former UN ambassador, called the United States a "predatory hegemon" that "tears up treaties" and "makes a mockery of international law." When a country's former UN envoy says that about Washington and its current prime minister is in Brussels pitching European Union membership, the alliance has not frayed. It migrated.

The world used to run on one question: how do we plug into American strategy? Now it runs on a different one: how do we survive if America is not there? The old transatlantic bargain is done. Not because anyone declared it over, but because everyone started building something else.

Poland, the Baltics, and Scandinavia are spending 71, 134, and 126 percent more on defense. Turkey, Pakistan, and Saudi Arabia have their own pact. BRICS is building around the dollar. The defense budget stays at nine hundred billion. The contractors sell to the hedgers and to us.

The man with the Freightliner still cannot get his back service-connected. The bridge on I-16 still needs replacing. The VA clinic in Hinesville is still understaffed. The hedging is built. The receipt is on the counter.
