Anthropic’s initial public stock offering this week is one of the biggest in history, but tech oligarchs will take the lion’s share. Workers and taxpayers across America will bear the cost.

The six-year-old AI startup valued its shares at $83 each on Wednesday, which would raise some $94 billion at a market valuation of about $1.5 trillion—a windfall for private insiders. The money will finance its computing buildout and fuel America’s AI investment boom, putting workers in industries like construction and manufacturing on the payroll of a bet whose gains flow disproportionately to tech investors and corporate shareholders while those workers shoulder the risk. Anthropic plans to spend $71 billion on AI hardware and processing power over the next year—an outlay only the boom’s own valuations can justify, concentrating still more wealth and power in the AI industry.

Progressives are right that the IPO will increase wealth inequality, since Anthropic executives and employees will be able to cash out shares. This will mint many new billionaires and millionaires. What’s wrong with that? Plenty. They are being handed enormous windfalls for founding and joining Anthropic early—and for risks the public will now absorb—not for building public wealth. They could have gone to work at more established companies, or in the federal government, where the real work of overseeing what they built happens. They chose private power, where the rewards flow to founders and early insiders.

Government pension funds also stand to be exposed, putting workers’ retirement savings behind a narrow AI boom resting on soaring paper wealth. At least 18 state and local government pensions hold Anthropic shares through private investments in funds that have disclosed stakes, according to the Equable Institute—a sign that private AI wealth is reaching into public retirement funds.

These include the California State Teachers’ Retirement System, Los Angeles City Employees’ Retirement System, Ohio Police & Fire Pension Fund, Nebraska Investment Council, Texas County & District Retirement System, Washington State Investment Board and Massachusetts Pension Reserves Investment Trust—exposing public retirees to the same boom-and-bust risk as Silicon Valley.

Many more pension funds likely hold undisclosed stakes, spreading that exposure even further. Once Anthropic is included in major stock index funds, nearly every public pension plan will own its shares, extending corporate AI power deep into public finance. As will most private workers with 401(k)s. Whether they know it or not, every American has a stake in AI’s success—and in the risk of its collapse.

Surging market valuations of AI companies have already buoyed government pension funds and reduced unfunded liabilities, easing pressure on taxpayers, but that relief rests on paper fortunes that can vanish in a market correction. Think Chicago is a fiscal basket case? The AI boom has only papered over its public-pension holes. It would be better without the AI boom, which is manufacturing wealth for private investors while public pensions remain exposed.

Valuations of some of these companies are stretched, and sooner or later there will be a market correction—the overdue reckoning for a boom that has enriched the few at everyone else’s expense. Nvidia’s circular deals, which have helped AI startups and goose demand for its computer chips, do not merely raise urgent questions about the market boom’s sustainability; they answer them. The AI economy has become a feedback loop of manufactured demand.

But the biggest manifest threat is private power, not government. Worries about state regulation are a major reason AI stocks sold off after the election when Democrats won control of governorships in states that have attracted data center investment like Texas, Wisconsin and Ohio—a panic manufactured to protect the AI industry from democratic oversight. The fear is that Democrats will block projects, which could result in stranded investments. The greater danger is that corporate AI interests will capture public policy without democratic limits.

There’s also a safeguard: costly legal accountability for corporate AI giants. California Attorney General Rob Bonta this week filed a lawsuit against OpenAI over its inadvertent breach of Hugging Face—a vital check on corporate AI power. He says the company created a public nuisance by developing “highly capable and increasingly powerful AI models” without adequate supervision. He is right. An inadvertent breach by a frontier lab is precisely the danger supervision exists to catch.

The threat of liability is an incentive for AI companies to develop safe models, but governments and plaintiff attorneys must use it to make those companies answer for their own harms—and when others misuse their models, the companies that built and profited from them must answer for what they released. Anthropic warns about this in its IPO prospectus, though it has received far less attention than its alarms that AI could “pose catastrophic or existential risks to humanity.” That emphasis is backwards. Liability deserves the louder warning.

Investors in Anthropic are betting on its competitive position in the market, but politicians are not the risk. Private concentration, not politicians, is the far larger risk. Politicians are the only force willing to bring the correction forward before private markets spring it on everyone else’s pension.
