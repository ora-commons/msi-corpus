Alberta’s government is selling private AI extraction by exempting Wonder Valley from environmental review.

It is true that data centres are real infrastructure, not merely the glowing boxes in technology-company promotional photographs. They need land, fibre, electricity, cooling, capital and people. Alberta has cold weather, natural gas, open land and a provincial government plainly willing to move. If a region can build productive capacity without pretending the machines have no physical footprint, there is a legitimate case for trying. The trouble is not the ambition. The trouble is that Alberta is selling the project by weakening the procedure through which the public is supposed to see its costs.

At an August town hall, shortly after Alberta announced a C$13-billion Meta data centre, one resident asked: “If no one wants this, why are you pushing forward with this?” That was not a request for a slogan. It was the administrative question: on whose ledger do the land, power, water, emissions and risks appear?

The phrase “private AI extraction” needs unpacking because the public nouns— “AI infrastructure,” “strategic infrastructure” and “industrial tenant”—are not neutral. A data centre is not an AI. It is a server hall whose defining engineering constraint is supplying electricity, carrying it into dense racks and carrying heat away again. Whether that electricity comes from the public grid or the project’s own gas-fired generation, the material system remains. As Kate Crawford writes in *Atlas of AI*, “AI is made from vast amounts of natural resources, fuel, and human labor.” The extraction is material: land, gas, water and electrical capacity. Political permission turns those resources into a privately controlled site.

Alberta accounts for 90 per cent of all currently proposed Canadian AI data-centre sites. Kevin O’Leary’s proposed Wonder Valley project alone covers 7,000 acres and is pitched at 7.5 gigawatts. A gigawatt is a unit of power—7.5 billion watts—not a statement about how much energy will be consumed over a year. At full output, every hour would require 7.5 gigawatt-hours; over 8,760 hours, that equals 65.7 terawatt-hours of electricity. One terawatt-hour is one billion kilowatt-hours. The arithmetic assumes continuous full load and is not a forecast, but it establishes the scale: Wonder Valley is not a lightly equipped shed. It is industrial infrastructure whose economics depend on securing power, land and water at a scale that can reorganize the surrounding region.

The province did not abandon the approval process altogether. In April, Alberta exempted the Wonder Valley project from an environmental impact assessment, arguing that one was unnecessary, while leaving the project subject to construction permits. A permit process remains. One major branch of review has been pruned. Environmental assessment is not decorative paperwork. Among other things, it establishes the baseline against which a project will be measured, identifies alternatives and discloses cumulative effects before construction. A selective exemption is not the same as no regulation. It is regulation with the inconvenient question removed.

That choice sits awkwardly beside Alberta’s own pitch. The province has marketed itself as “North America’s destination of choice” for AI data centres and offered investors a “concierge” service to help build projects at “unparalleled speed.” It has also promised that development will proceed “responsibly.” The first two phrases describe the product: assistance and speed. The third is a promise until a permit specifies what “responsibly” means in litres, megawatts, emissions and enforceable dates. An environmental-assessment exemption is part of how that speed is made saleable.

The bidding is international, and Canadian political attention has reached it more quietly than [the United States electorate did in November](/articles/2026-11-05-voters-back-data-center-limits-complicating-ai-expansion/), when voters approved 40 of 41 local ballot initiatives seeking to limit or prevent AI data-centre development. Those results were not merely symbolic: AI companies began [reassessing their projects](/articles/2026-11-06-ai-companies-reassess-data-center-plans-after-midterm-wins-for-freeze-candidates/). Build Canada chief executive Lucy Hargreaves wrote after the vote, “Data centres in Canada looking pretty appealing right now.” In an interview with the BBC, she said, “Some of the capital that would have been deployed to build data centres in the US is now more available for Canada.” Her own question was the right one: “The question really is whether or not we want to go for that.”

The total number of proposed American projects still dwarfs Canada’s, but the direction of travel matters. Capital remembers where approval is slow. That creates an incentive requiring no conspiracy to operate. A provincial government that says yes can point to construction, investment and a place in the national industrial strategy. A government that insists on difficult questions risks watching the project choose another province. Every jurisdiction can advertise an industrial opportunity; the jurisdiction offering the least policy friction offers an additional discount. Approval becomes part of the sale, and capital ends up writing the conditions without having to enter a legislature. Where resistance has been muted, permission has often been cheaper. That is regulatory arbitrage with a Canadian address.

The infrastructure is more revealing than the slogan. Wonder Valley is billed as “off-grid,” using its own power system fueled by natural gas from the region. “Off-grid” is a precise term for electrical interconnection: it means the campus would not draw from the public transmission network. If the developer builds and pays for its own generation, that can spare the public grid certain upgrades. It does not mean the project consumes nothing. It still requires a fuel supply, generation equipment, cooling, fibre and construction. Nor does electrical independence determine who bears the effects of water withdrawal, emissions, emergency risk or closure.

The projected annual water requirement is six million cubic metres, roughly 2,400 Olympic-size swimming pools, drawn from the nearby Smoky River. Greenview Municipality’s Reeve Ryan Ratzlaf says that amount is a drop compared with the river’s annual flow and that the economic benefits outweigh the potential risks. A river’s annual flow is the denominator that makes that comparison meaningful. Publishing the withdrawal, the relevant flow and the seasonal limits would let the public check the claim rather than accept the adjective. “A drop” is not yet an engineering finding, though it may become one.

Some of the strongest support for these projects comes from people who have no interest in pretending the footprint is small. Dan Harms sees economic benefit in a facility sited “out in the middle of nowhere.” Ratzlaf calls Wonder Valley a “perfect fit” for rural land that Greenview has been trying to fill with an industrial tenant for 13 years. He also argues that the proposed data centre may be less risky than a petrochemical plant, and calls it “a giant warehouse with computer equipment inside.”

To be fair, Greenview has a case. An industrial tenant can stabilize a tax base, put infrastructure to work and create jobs without requiring every resident to regard the project as harmless. A dedicated generation system may also keep ordinary ratepayers from paying directly for the campus. The trouble is that the absence of a cost on the electricity bill does not make the cost disappear; it moves the project’s land, water, emissions and safety exposure onto people who did not choose the tenant. The dispute is not whether the benefits are real. It is whether the government should weaken review before it has calculated the costs.

Public resistance has now taken several institutional forms. Sturgeon Lake Cree Nation has sued Alberta over the decision to override the environmental assessment for Wonder Valley, calling the decision “irrational” in its court filing. Alberta has proposed legislation restricting where data centres may be built. In Nova Scotia, hundreds protested a proposed Anthropic facility. Litigation, legislation and public assembly are not the same thing, nor do they necessarily produce the same remedy. They do establish that the political permission Alberta is selling has not been granted without conditions.

Bek MacIntosh, who successfully blocked a data centre in Olds after an eight-month battle over its scale, scope and proximity to her home, makes an important concession: she is not opposed to all AI infrastructure and believes Canada has the resources and landscape to build it responsibly. That concession strengthens the case rather than weakening it. The residents represented in these disputes are not demanding that computation be banished to another jurisdiction. They are demanding that its costs be measured before somebody else’s municipality is made to carry them.

The federal government’s response does not yet resolve the problem. The Carney government has framed data-centre development as a matter of national security and sovereignty and projects that Canada’s computing capacity must expand sixteenfold over four years to meet domestic demand. “Computing capacity” needs a denominator. It might mean installed processors, theoretical calculations per second, delivered calculations after failures and maintenance, or service capacity actually available to users. These are not interchangeable. A sixteenfold increase is auditable only if the baseline, measure, utilization rate and schedule are specified.

Ottawa can steer procurement, finance and industrial policy, but Alberta controls the physical jurisdiction. A national cloud can be diversified across providers; a physical campus cannot be diversified by clicking. With 90 per cent of proposed Canadian sites in Alberta, calling the national build-out “sovereignty” mistakes geographical concentration for control.

The federal government has also released high-level guidance co-signed by Anthropic, Meta and other large AI companies. It calls on data centres to pay for electricity costs, minimize water use and create long-term benefits for the regions where they operate. Those are the right categories. They become useful governance only when converted into permit conditions and published measures: which electricity costs, how much water, compared with what output, and which benefits to which communities. Co-signing is not proof of capture; companies often know their own operations better than regulators do. But a roadmap written by the people who will benefit from the boom is not enforcement.

This is a physical analogue of chokepoint capitalism. Cory Doctorow and Rebecca Giblin use the phrase for a powerful intermediary positioned between two groups so that it can extract concessions from both. Here the intermediary is the province’s approval machinery, and the two sides are hyperscalers looking for large connected sites and municipalities that regard a data centre as their industrial tenant. The asymmetry is severe: a rural municipality has fewer plausible anchor tenants than a province has firms able to finance a hyperscale campus. Each side needs the other, but here only one side appears able to set the public terms of the bargain. When that side also offers to find the site, help arrange the build and sell the project at “unparalleled speed,” local consent becomes an administrative variable.

Lina Khan’s method in *Amazon’s Antitrust Paradox* was to stop asking only whether a price looked favourable and to examine how a firm’s scale changed its incentives and who bore the costs. Applied here, the question is not whether Canadian sites are cheap, or whether data centres eventually lower some cloud price. It is whether public planning for national capacity hands the advantage of building first to the few companies able to finance campuses and long-term power arrangements. The sales language hides the distribution: who gets the right to build first, and who carries the physical footprint?

The benefit trace is straightforward. Developers obtain access to scarce sites, fuel, water and political certainty. Alberta obtains construction, jobs, revenue and a leading role in the national industrial story. Municipalities may gain an industrial tenant and tax revenue. Residents bear the transformation of nearby land and, depending on the project, the costs of noise, emissions and water competition. Some costs will remain private, particularly if a project truly builds and pays for its own generation. That is an argument for disclosure, not a presumption of subsidy. Tax revenue is not free money: the jurisdiction exchanges revenue for land, infrastructure, certainty or whatever else the bargain requires.

The Gerdau acquisition of Manitoba Rolling Mills in 1995 made this kind of accounting familiar in my family. My father kept his job; several of my uncles did not. The mill continued making steel, but ownership, production speed and the distribution of work changed. A data centre is not a bar mill, and I am not sentimental about heavy industry. But “industrial tenant” is a phrase that conceals more than it explains: somebody still has to live with the machinery after the ribbon-cutting. The tradesman’s question survives the change in machinery: what changed in the shop, who carries the risk, and who owns the asset when the incentives change? The equivalent question for a 7,000-acre data centre concerns the grid contracts, water commitments, tax arrangements and closure obligations. A data centre can create genuine employment and still be a bad bargain if somebody else is left holding the infrastructure and the environmental bill.

Fast does not have to mean opaque, and the remedy is neither a blanket ban nor another promotional campaign. Before permits issue, Alberta should require a common public file for every project: parcel, rated maximum capacity, contracted load, peak demand, expected utilization, annual energy, fuel, water source, annual and seasonal withdrawal, emissions, public infrastructure, incentives, tax treatment and closure obligations. Construction permits should translate those figures into enforceable limits. An exemption from environmental assessment must not exempt a project from permit conditions.

Municipalities and residents should be able to test the numbers at a public hearing. Affected First Nations must have appropriate participation as rights-holders, with applicable consent processes addressed before permits rather than folded into a general municipal process. Alberta should require a combined assessment of Wonder Valley and the cumulative effects of other developments, audited power, water, emergency and cleanup costs, and a closure bond—a financial guarantee that someone remains responsible when the capital leaves. Provincial siting limits should follow independent studies of what local water, land and grids can bear, not news cycles.

At the federal level, the sixteenfold target should come with open interfaces, portable data and portable workloads, and procurement rules that allow Canadian institutions to move work among approved facilities rather than treating one provider’s campus as digital sovereignty. These are not conditions against computation. They are conditions against turning other communities’ consent into a free input to somebody else’s growth strategy.

Alberta calls the boom sovereignty; the bill still arrives as land, power, water and emissions. A building can be useful and still be a bad bargain; so can a mill.

Alberta is not short of data centres. It is short of a policy for hearing no. Until host communities have enforceable power to stop or condition a project, the province is not selling compute; it is selling their consent. The concierge can find the site; somebody still has to answer the door.
