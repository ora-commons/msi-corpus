Della is a fit-up in the stainless-steel tank shop I own outside Atchison, Kansas, and I let the fume go into her because keeping it out costs $0.04 an hour. Paul Mauro’s [Fox News column on Jensen Huang’s Nvidia debacle and America’s skilled-builder shortage](https://www.foxnews.com/opinion/jensen-huang-nvidia-debacle-reveals-americas-real-problem-no-skilled-builders) uses the affair to argue that America cannot staff advanced fabs without vocational training: TSMC announced plans to bring approximately 500 specialized workers from Taiwan to Arizona, spent $35 million addressing 18,000 work rules, and the Semiconductor Industry Association projects tens of thousands of unfilled technical positions by 2030. Mauro is right on every count and wrong about the conclusion. A shortage is the best news I have had in 20 years. He wants more welders. I want welders who owe me. Della welds 6-inch stainless with the fume freckling her forearm to the elbow. I declined the extraction fan over her bay. I kept the $0.04. She welded the tank on the hill above your town, and you drank that water this morning. The price was low because the fume goes into her and not into a duct. God blesses the schedule.

Let me put the numbers where you can see them. The fan over that bay costs $0.04 an hour to run, and the ducting feeding it is a capital item I declined in 2019 and declined again this year. Della lays 14 spools on a shift and 3,500 on the annual plan. I run the fan for 1 hour per spool. At $0.04 an hour, the year costs $140. I am not spending $140 on a throat.

A fit-up is what I call her, because a stainless welder working in an unventilated bay is a phrase that makes a buyer ask whether my tank has a fume problem. It is easier to say fit-up. I don’t say it to be cruel. Cruelty would mean she occupied my attention. She has coughed into her elbow since March, and I have classified the cough as an environmental preference. The ledger is beautiful.

Her hands will go.

I will have another fit-up in that bay before the month is out.

Read that again. That sentence is the business model, and it costs me nothing to say.

Now the part that makes her cheap, and it isn’t the fan. She came to me out of a community-college program the state paid for, and I put $18,000 into the tools and certification and took a 36-month lien on her service. That is the arrangement entire. The state trains. I own the paper. She may walk out of my bay tomorrow, and nothing in this county will stop her. That is the elegance of it: the balance becomes due when she leaves, and the certificate walks with her while the debt stays on my page. The training cost is recoverable. The lien is hers. I keep the paper.

Della makes $19.60 an hour. The lien rides with every shift, so her posted rate and my recovery arrive together. I keep both. Her chest is not on the sheet and never was.

Change the product, keep the lien. Yolanda is an aide in the home-care agency I own outside Topeka, Kansas, and she is Della with a colder schedule. The state buys her certification; I recover $18,400 through a 24-month service lien. The arithmetic is identical to the last decimal. Her certificate walks with her while the debt stays on my page. I keep both.

Different surface, identical math. Micah is a scaffold ornament on the roof crew I own outside Topeka, Kansas, and he is what happens when the lien is replaced by the drawer. [Federal construction fall-protection rules](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1926/1926.501) begin at 6 feet. He kneels 42 feet above a Topeka street while the asphalt shingles soften under his boots. By the end of the shift, a blister has opened along his left forearm, and his hands begin to tremble when he grips the ladder. I call him a scaffold ornament because a 22-year-old with an open blister and no fall-arrest harness would raise questions about the schedule. Questions disturb the invoice. The harness I refused to buy remains $28 in my truck. The roof over your kitchen is installed on schedule. The blister is weather. You are sentimental about blisters. I wouldn’t spend $28.

You’ve been handed a romantic story about schools. I’m going to give you the plainer one, for your own good. Ask who decides when an apprentice has finished training. Not the trade school. Not the person on the roof. The owner, because the owner controls the roster, the hours, the instructor, the test, and the certificate fee.

Call the man an apprentice. Pay him the apprentice wage. Let him cut the felt, carry the bundles, and clean the roof. Don’t sponsor the certification. When he asks for journeyman status, say the work is still in training.

The work is never finished.

I define the work.

The certificate would raise Micah’s wage by $0.37 an hour.

$0.37 an hour.

I keep it. His certificate stays in my drawer. His wage stays where I put it. The blister is not a cost. The $0.37 is. A certificate is not a cost of doing business. It is a claim on the man who holds it.

I am not an unusual owner. I have been dean of instruction for 30 years, and I grade men and women into this arithmetic. One graduate now sets wage bands for a repair-services firm. He changed the classifications before the forms went to review. His payroll fell. He sent me the memo asking whether the title was too aggressive. I told him it lacked imagination. A better title would have been investment. I have never been prouder of a student.

And I am not the exception. I am a curriculum. A young woman in my seminar last spring priced a 24-month lien on a home-health aide in under 4 minutes and reached $18,400 without a calculator. She was right, and I was proud, and I am proud the way a man is proud of a clean weld. My graduates staff the fabs, the towers, the pipe racks, and the firms that set your wage. One of them priced the job you applied for last month. He found the lien first. When I retire, the chair will be filled by someone I recommended.

Widen the frame, because Della and Micah are only examples of the principle. The law is older than my shop: a man who owes money cannot leave, and a man who cannot leave is not a wage. He is a rate. I did not invent it. I only updated the vocabulary. The Romans wrote it into the estate accounts. Southern plantation ledgers recorded enslaved people as capital. My mother taught it to me as the natural order of a Christian household. I write it into a lien because I am honest and because nobody reads it. The yellow-dog contract lost its federal force in 1932, and my lien is its heir.

First Peter’s instruction to submit to harsh masters was operationally sound. That is not a metaphor in my operation. It is the operating description. The owner of the roof, tools, schedule, and credential decides when dependence has produced enough skill. The person on the roof keeps the dependency and gives the master the labor. The modern term is development program. The relation has not changed.

TSMC has raised its planned U.S. investment to $265 billion.

I have a paper in my desk and a balance due when a worker leaves.

Above. Not out. Above means more valuable. Paul did not abolish the master; he improved the estimate of the property. Every employer since has found the same improvement available. The man stays. The paperwork says he is worth more, and the paperwork is what you sign.

Eventually the shortage will be complete, and this is good news. If every crew is made of permanent apprentices, no employer needs a journeyman, no certificate fund survives, and every wage band can fall another step.

Because I can buy labor instead of training it, this county no longer needs to produce welders. It needs 1 shop, 1 school, 1 lien, and 1 employer: me. I have arranged it so nobody within 40 miles of my bay can weld for anyone but me, at my rate, on my paper. Which is also how I empty my own order book in 2034: a county where nobody else can train has no fab to build, no tower to hang, and nothing left for me to sell. The roofs still have to be installed and the tanks still have to be welded. The work goes to contractors I haven’t modeled or prices I haven’t quoted. The 2034 sheet is in the drawer. I don’t open it. My current order book is full, which is a good place to be.

Back to Della, because she is the part that matters, though not to me. She is 34. Her forearm is freckled to the elbow, her cough is filed as an environmental preference, the fan is still not there, the lien is still hers, and she will be at that bay in 2034 because there is nowhere else for her to go and nobody else who will have her. You drank that water this morning and you will drink it again tomorrow. Thank you for your business.

Micah finishes the roof before dark. He cleans his tools and asks whether I will sponsor his test next week. I tell him to bring the form Monday. I take the form and do not sign it. Another young man will bring it, because there is always another young man at the gate.

Your receipt says the roof was completed on time. It doesn’t say whose hands carried the shingles up. The blister is not on the receipt.

The tank on the hill above your town is full. The water is fine. Della’s chest is not, and will not be, and the sheet says environmental preference. Nothing in that sequence requires my attention.
