Seoul's government cheered the chip boom, then changed the rules of selling when it broke, and called the rescue prudence — while the machinery behind it had already sold ordinary people a future they had no voice in. That is the mechanism [Jiyoung Sohn reports for The Wall Street Journal](https://www.wsj.com/finance/stocks/a-new-generation-of-traders-is-trapped-in-asias-market-meltdown-636d5da4) and [Main Street Independent has recorded](https://mainstreetindependent.com/articles/2026-11-10-asian-indexes-fall-as-data-center-concerns-weigh-on-chip-shares/): a remote political expectation, that the American data-center build-out might slow after the midterm elections, hardened into a market-wide wager on the future of the chips, and the paper claims went out in a hurry. South Korea's Kospi fell nearly 12 percent last week and sat roughly 45 percent below its June peak; Japan's Nikkei had surrendered almost 20 percent from its June high, and its Nikkei Semiconductor Stock Index 54 percent over the same stretch. Taiwan [mobilized its National Financial Stabilization Fund](https://mainstreetindependent.com/articles/2026-11-06-taiwan-activates-market-stabilization-fund-amid-global-selloff-in-ai-stocks/), roughly $15 billion at its disposal, and put curbs on short selling; Seoul's finance ministry temporarily loosened the collateral rules brokerages must post. In the capital, Choi Jin-wook, thirty-five, an office worker, watched $37,000 of SK Hynix stock bought in July halve.

There is an honest case to be made here, and it is stronger than most people will manage. The AI build-out is real. The chips are real. Volatility is how a price registers what nobody yet knows; a correction after a mania is not, by itself, scandal. And emergency rules can keep a forced-sale spiral from becoming a self-fulfilling collapse. Most of the people caught in this fall behaved reasonably given what they had been told. A Japanese high-school investment club put 200,000 yen — about $1,200 — into each student's first portfolio, and the seventeen-year-old in the story, Minami Kamiya, stayed with Nissin, partly because she loves instant noodles. Rio Negishi, twenty-two, a Meiji University student, had kept most of his money out of technology and still lost about $38,000, roughly 30 percent of his portfolio. Lee Ka-young, the twenty-five-year-old Seoul software developer who gives stock tips on Instagram to mom-and-pop investors, says the truest sentence in the whole report: "I thought this time would be different." He means to keep investing "with the mindset of passing my shares down for generations." Governments that loosen collateral rules or restrain forced selling are trying to keep a liquidity spiral from becoming disorder. That is not nothing.

But there is a contradiction at the center of it, and the government is at the center of it. South Korean President Lee Jae Myung was one of the biggest cheerleaders of his own country's stock-market boom. He warned last month that "a correction was inevitable." Then came the altered rules. Min Gyeong-gwon, who heads an activist group representing local individual investors, put the dependency plainly: "It was the president who laid out a bright future for Korean stocks at the start of the year. People went in with confidence because we believed him." Seoul's mayor, Oh Se-hoon, wrote on Facebook on Sunday that the Lee government's "reckless arrogance in thinking it could move the market has ultimately opened the gates of hell." The warning may have been prudent. The intervention may have been necessary. The confidence was neither. A government cannot spend the first months of a year cheering a market and then place every loss on the buyers as though its own promotion had no responsibility.

That contradiction matters because these are paper claims on somebody else's future. Choi's money now rides on American elections, on orders for memory chips, and on the pace of a data-center build-out whose buyers will never set foot in the field that grows their food. His choices — buy, sell, hold — could not change where data centers would be built, how the fabs would be financed, or how much risk the model would carry. The fear had already become a [global rout](https://mainstreetindependent.com/articles/2026-11-06-global-stocks-sink-as-ai-slowdown-fears-follow-democrats-midterm-victory/), though the burdens of it land very differently. A bond buyer in New York can name a fund that diversifies across a thousand securities, and that is prudent. Choi bought one company's memory chips and put $37,000 behind the bet that American planners would keep building. A member of a productive enterprise owns a claim on the work and a vote over what happens next. The first can change hands at the click of a screen. The second cannot be detached so easily from the people who make the value possible and must live with the consequences. Samsung fell about 16 percent last week, SK Hynix about 20, Kioxia Holdings nearly 37.

I know this pattern from the inside, because I was part of it. I traded agricultural futures for years in Chicago — a month, a price, a margin, a screen — and a contract is a simple thing once you have watched one. It is paper that moves risk away from the man with the crop and toward the man with no crop at all. On my desk the wheat was real: there was always, somewhere, a field with a farmer standing in it. The man who wanted payment for bearing the weather took the field and got his coffee. The man who wanted payment for owning the contract got the corner office and never learned the taste of the dust. I know precisely how little the men in that building thought about the men in the field. A futures contract can be an honest instrument. The rentier turn comes when ownership detaches from work, decision detaches from place, and the risk returns home to those least able to carry it. At the co-op floor where I work, a bad price is not a mood: milk prices fall, potatoes rot, and somebody must carry what the market will not. In this chip boom, distant capital holds the claim while workers, suppliers, and towns are left with the shock.

The conservative tradition that runs from Burke to Kirk puts the question as one of scale and inheritance: how large a claim should a human life be asked to carry? A modest claim on one's own future is conserving. An undiversified claim on somebody else's future is conserving nothing. Brandeis's curse of bigness is not a metaphor about factories; it is what happens when the size of the claim outruns the size of a man's life. We may have democracy, or we may have wealth concentrated in the hands of a few, but we cannot have both — and the same elections that decide how many data centers get built decide who is left holding the claim on them. The earth was given for all. It was not given to be sliced into paper and sold on an exchange to people who will never see the field it came from.

Nor does the answer sit with a more benevolent central desk. These measures may keep a market from breaking its plumbing. They cannot make politically uncertain demand certain. When a state changes who can be forced to sell, it changes the timing of liquidation; it does not give the holder a claim on the productive enterprise or a voice in its direction. That is not necessarily sinister. It is not ownership. The market steadied Monday — Taiwan's Taiex gained roughly 5 percent after the authorities acted, the Kospi stayed roughly flat — but a market that steadies after the rules change has not restored trust in a future sold as a lottery ticket.

Pius XI was blunt in *Quadragesimo Anno* §79: it is "a grave evil" to assign to the higher what lesser organizations can do. That is not a sentimental preference for Main Street. It is a conservative discipline — keep power near knowledge and consequence. Concentrated capital and concentrated state power are the same disease in two coats. I will not exchange one distant desk for another.

Some friends of mine who distrust markets have one point I accept, and I will say so to them plainly: a seventeen-year-old should not be made to carry adult losses because adults insist that financial literacy is a form of character. I agree. But when the answer to every private insecurity is another promise administered from the capital, the disease merely changes its coat. If credit, insurance, and productive ownership are pulled out of local member institutions, then dependence on distant administrators has simply replaced dependence on distant chip buyers.

The county I came home to knows this in its bones, and the particulars here are no prettier. Adams was a Chicago & North Western division point, with a roundhouse, section gangs, and shift men coming and going, until dieselization took the servicing work away; the last *Twin Cities 400* ran on July 23, 1963. Across the county line in neighboring Wood County, Port Edwards closed its Domtar mill in 2008 and Whiting closed in 2010; in Wisconsin Rapids the Verso mill — operating since 1904 — idled on June 9, 2020, with roughly a thousand jobs gone. Across Wisconsin the number of dairy farms fell from 16,886 in 2002 to 6,216 in 2022, while the average herd grew from fifty-six cows to 203.4. Heartland Farms' 27,000 acres stretch across five counties, thousands of them in Adams, and the operation grows roughly 9,000 acres of potatoes a year for Frito-Lay. Fewer farm families mean fewer children in the schools, fewer names on the church council, thinner civic rolls. Then came the replacement economy. Sand Valley's first course opened in 2017 and put a few hundred county people to work, and I will give it that credit. But the USDA now classifies Adams County as a high-recreation retirement destination, and the best future the place could offer was to become other people's backyard, served at low wages by its own children.

What gets built, then, is not a bigger stabilization fund and not a better regulator. A public fund may be needed to stem a forced-sale spiral; but if it becomes the only route by which ordinary people can hold a future, it concentrates that future in one political hand — the same disease I have been describing in a fund manager's suit. The answer is ownership a person can live with. At the farm cooperative I manage in Friendship, a member can make a bad call; some years the margin is thin. We do not promise every member a green line. We pool the crop and the risks, share ownership of the enterprise, vote on the surplus, keep reserves, and teach the knowledge where it is used. Members bear losses too. That is the point: the people who make the value possible have standing to change the institution that holds it.

My cooperative belongs to a tradition rather than a fantasy. The Rochdale Pioneers wrote down the governing rules in 1844: voluntary membership, one member and one vote, member economic participation, education, and cooperation among cooperatives. The Capper-Volstead Act of 1922 gave American farm marketing cooperatives the legal room to bargain collectively with concentrated buyers. The Rural Electrification Act of 1936 put inexpensive public credit behind member-owned electric cooperatives when the market would not serve the rural dark. In our own town, Adams-Columbia Electric has roughly 31,560 member-owners across twelve counties and a member-elected board. Mondragon's 70,085 workers and €11.213 billion in 2024 sales show that democratic ownership can operate at industrial scale. REI, member-owned, lost $156.4 million in 2024. No form is flawless. But the scale is not the problem; concentrated control is.

The nearest financial counterpart is the credit union or the mutual insurer, and it does not abolish a bad year. Capital can be impaired. Coverage depends on the policy's terms. Poor management can fail. What a member-owned institution changes is the distribution of risk: the loss is exposed, governance remains with the people who bear it, and the surplus is not quietly carried off by somebody who never sets foot in the town. A member can have a worse year. He does not have a zero. He does have a vote.

The same answer has to reach AI capital — not as a command to buy or sell, but as distributed ownership. It has three layers, each with a distinct power. At the household level, a member-owned credit union can reduce dependence on extractive intermediaries in savings and credit; a diversified fund may remain prudent for a saver, but neither governs the chip economy. At the productive level, employee ownership and supplier cooperatives put bargaining power, surplus, and decisions near the workers and suppliers who create the value. At the local level, where a data center, a chip plant, or a utility anchors a town, a local trust can hold a defined stake and reserve defined seats for workers, affected residents, and local institutions. Each form answers a different question: who controls household savings and credit, who governs production, and who has standing where the plant anchors a town.

None of this is easy here. A chip fab requires capital and expertise beyond a county's reach; worker ownership can drift back under managers; a local trust can become a closed shop. A cooperative takes years to build; it asks its members for patience and a share of the risk; and it pays no dividend on Tuesday. A lecture on the seventh principle of cooperation — concern for the community — will not pull Choi from his screen. The institution must stand next to him. The answer is harder because it asks the people who bear the risk to own decisions, not because some authority can make the chart turn green.

At the co-op floor I know what that looks like when it survives a bad year: a long table, the same members in March as in August, and nobody's share price quoted at them. Mellencamp's "Small Town" is about a man who stayed, not a man who made money. I have watched my own county lose the things it was built on, and I do not expect to see them all built back. What I can still tell you is what the building looks like from inside — a parish, a lodge, a hall, a floor with the members on it, a claim on something real that answers to the person who holds it. We cannot restore the half of Choi's $37,000 that the fall erased, or Negishi's $38,000, or the 54 percent the semiconductor index surrendered. We can stop calling the paper claim a community and ask the harder question: who owns the machine, who governs it, who bears the loss, and who has a voice when the price falls? The answer I know is not a ministry of prices. It is a member at the table.

Leave the town its life.
