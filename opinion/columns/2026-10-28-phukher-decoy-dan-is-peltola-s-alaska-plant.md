An Alaska voter will have four names to rank for Senate. One name belongs to a decoy candidate recruited to confuse that voter.

When the Justice Department sued Alaska over its manipulated Senate ballot, liberal outrage could initially be heard from Washington to the Arctic. It has gone conspicuously quiet since The New York Times published evidence that Democrats rigged Alaska’s ranked-choice ballot with a decoy candidate who threatens to deny Republican Sen. Dan Sullivan his re-election.

Exposing the deceit is helpful. It is not a remedy. Hurdle upon hurdle has been stacked against Alaskans’ ability to cast a clear and fair vote: a false-flag candidate, a Rube Goldberg ballot and deceptive electioneering. Court action may be the only remedy.

Alaska’s election system is already a nightmare. Voters rank four candidates in order of preference. Just before the filing deadline, a retired teacher named Dan Sullivan registered to challenge Republican Sen. Dan Sullivan. The name was not accidental. Neither was the timing.

The decoy filed as a Republican despite having no ties to the GOP. He donated to liberal causes and to Mary Peltola’s campaign, hired a Democratic consultant with connections to her, and asked to be listed as “Dan S. Sullivan,” using the senator’s middle initial, though his own is J. His campaign logo nearly duplicated the incumbent’s.

Alaska’s Division of Elections initially barred him for filing “with a purpose to confuse or mislead” voters. The Alaska Supreme Court allowed him to proceed anyway. Through it all, the Peltola campaign claimed it had nothing to do with the ruse.

He was no candidate. He was a weapon disguised as one.

He won 2.5% of the primary vote, enough to reach the final ballot and send his name before voters. His purpose was not to win office. It was to put that name on the ballot in the hope that confusion would tip the race to Peltola.

His website contains two press releases and six blog posts and was last updated six weeks ago. His Facebook page has eight entries since the Aug. 18 primary, the last on Sept. 30. One shares a poem. None offers an idea, platform or promise.

He has spent no money on staff, advertising, media, travel or campaign events. His listed expenses include $300 paid to the Alaska election division for a candidate brochure, $4,000 for the Democratic consultant and more than $20,000 for a Washington law firm’s “compliance and legal fees.”

This is not a candidate trying to win office. It is a ballot instrument built to mislead voters.

His only real fundraising is a link asking for money to cover his litigation costs. According to Federal Election Commission records, he has had five itemized donors for the general election, contributing $5,400. Three live outside Alaska. He has received zero contributions from party committees, political action committees or other candidates.

He does not need money. Voters supply his resources for free, in the form of mistaken ballots.

Now the evidence of how he was created is emerging. Peltola’s former campaign manager, Elisa Rios, told The Times that the campaign began an operation in 2025 to find other Alaskan Dan Sullivans, coax them into running and confusing voters. The Democratic Senatorial Campaign Committee provided an operative specializing in “secret ballot maneuvers,” who persuaded Decoy Dan to enter the race.

The operative reportedly described a candidate with no political ambition who “hated Trump,” liked Mary and was willing to help if the experience advanced her campaign: “He didn’t have any interest in being a politician, but if this could help.”

That is the profile of a plant: no constituency, no message and no political future—only a mission to divide the opposition’s vote.

The Times also provides evidence that Democrats may have recruited the fourth candidate on the ballot, Gerald Heikes. The Peltola campaign and the DSCC deny recruiting anyone.

Then Rios suddenly retracted all her statements. She told The Times that she was no longer willing to be a source. Her reversal, after she said she had discussed the operation on a call and while she plans to sue the campaign for wrongful termination, does not erase her testimony. It puts the campaign’s treatment of its former manager on trial. The paper is not bound by her reversal. Nor does that reversal erase what she said and what the evidence appears to show.

Some liberals and ranked-choice boosters insist Alaska’s system will protect voters from this chicanery. If supporters of the incumbent rank both Dan Sullivans, their votes will eventually reach the senator.

Meaning, so long as every voter knows exactly how to defeat the game, all will be well.

Right.

That defense treats voting as an intelligence test. It assumes each voter receives perfect guidance, interprets it correctly and never makes a mistake. This will be Alaskans’ third experience with ranked-choice voting, and they have been told something different each time.

Two years ago, Republicans were told to rank Nick Begich and no one else. That made sense because he was their only Republican in the House race. Now Donald Trump and the Sullivan campaign are telling voters to rank multiple candidates, including one exposed as a fraud.

Why should Alaskans deliberately vote for a deceptive candidate to cancel the deception? And because his name appears first on the ballot, plenty will do so by accident.

The mailer’s confusion is deliberate, and it is spreading. The Center for Honest Voting, a political action committee, sent a mailer this month to Republican and independent households. It screams: “One person. One vote. One choice,” beside a graphic showing a filled-in first choice and crossed-out additional choices. It asserts, “President Trump is calling out fraud.” It commands voters, “Trust your first choice. Only vote once.”

One says vote once. The other says rank multiple candidates. One calls out fraud. The other includes the decoy among the candidates Republicans are told to rank. Neither message makes sense.

Tell voters to distrust the ballot, call it fraudulent, then tell them to use that fraudulent ballot exactly as the fraudsters intended. What a useless message.

Supporters of the senator are deeply confused. That is not an accidental side effect. Confusion is the product.

Alaska’s courts have the authority to stop this. Other states have disqualified candidacies designed to mislead voters, and the U.S. Supreme Court has observed that a state has “an interest, if not a duty, to protect the integrity of its political processes from frivolous or fraudulent candidacies.”

How much more evidence does the Alaska Supreme Court need? Decoy Dan is frivolous and fraudulent by definition. He exists for no reason except to deceive.

It is unclear how far federal law reaches, but that has not stopped courts from disqualifying sham candidacies. The election results may make litigation unnecessary. But if Senate control turns on Alaska, the decoy cannot be allowed to serve his purpose in the voting booth. The Alaska Supreme Court should end this farce now, before voters are forced to decipher it.

Meanwhile, Alaskans will do themselves and the nation a favor if they pass this year’s ballot initiative to abolish ranked-choice voting—the system Democrats rigged to swing a Senate election.

The evidence now shows what the system enabled: Democratic operatives, a sham candidate and a maze of contradictory instructions, all aimed at manufacturing one woman’s victory.

The ballot must be counted, not rigged. It must inform the voter, not ambush him. It must be restored to the simple principle that every lawful vote means one choice: the candidate the voter actually wants.

Democrats did not win by persuading Alaskans of a better future. They are trying to win by deceiving them—and they must not get to do it.

Alaskans can see how this works. The only question is whether they will punish the people who built it.
