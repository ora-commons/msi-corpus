I haven't paid a full adult wage to anyone under 21 in the 4 years I've run the parcel depot I own outside Doncaster, and the law writes my discount for me. Callum is a flex — that's rota shorthand for a 19-year-old off the estate who scans your parcels 6 hours at a stretch, hands bare in a dock that never warms — and his keep arrives from a benefit your taxes pay. The [Editorial Board of The Wall Street Journal](https://www.wsj.com/opinion/alan-milburn-report-u-k-youth-unemployment-52e6b3e6?mod=rss_opinion), in *The Welfare State and Britain's Idle Youth*, has read the Milburn report so you don't have to: nearly 1 million young Britons are out of work, school, and training, 1 in 8 of the Britons under 25 are idle, and wage costs for an 18-to-20-year-old have risen 65% in 5 years. The Board is alarmed that the benefits make idleness rational. I'm not alarmed. Rational is what I shop for.

I don't pay it. I keep what his hands scan, and I keep the differential, and I keep the 62p an hour, by my ledger, that I never owe in employer's insurance. God keeps the queue orderly. It's one of His more useful habits, and I do my filing under it. Their premise holds, and the holding is the opportunity. Your country hasn't only priced its young out of work. It pays them to wait. The waiting room is my recruiting floor.

Follow the hours, not the boy, for a minute. 6 hours a shift. 11 hours a week. I keep every week of him under the line where a contract would have to begin and the employer's National Insurance would start finding me. The paperwork calls the arrangement seasonal flexibility. Strip the coat off it: seasonal flexibility is a young man the State houses, feeds, and doctors, time-sliced so that no week of his ever grows expensive. My ledger prices the saving at 62p an hour.

Sit with the 62p a moment.

I keep it.

I'd keep it at 6p.

I prefer the paperwork's word, and I'll tell you why, because you were raised to believe the honest one is better. "A 19-year-old I've engineered into permanent cheapness" ends dinner conversations. "Seasonal flexibility" doesn't. I'm telling you this for your own good. The day that phrase stops snagging on your ear is the day somebody like me can use you.

The report grieves these young people's lost confidence and lost purpose. Both are maintenance lines. Maintenance is waste wherever the replacement runs cheap, and the replacement at my gate has never once been expensive.

The trial was unpaid. 2 weeks of nights, no wage. Your sanction regime keeps a queue like that polite from the other end, so nobody has to be told twice. He came. He stayed. The fortnight never quite ended; the names turn over every season and the fortnight doesn't. Egypt ran 7 fat years and 7 lean, and it was the lean ones that did the disciplining. I file the trial under the lean ones.

Take the same queue one page over: Tegan is 22, a scan-slot on my inbound line — one pair of hands and the fraction of a second the barcode needs — and her file says anxiety, which the Board's editorial files under vaguely defined. Vague is a word for people who haven't stood at the end of my conveyor. She counts the trolleys in fives because counting is the only thing that slows her chest. She arrives 40 minutes early because the bus crowd is a wall she can't get through after 8. I have never designed a retention program that outperforms her diagnosis. An anxious worker doesn't transfer, doesn't organize, doesn't interview well enough to leave. She's my surge capacity. The reassessment letter comes when it comes, and the fear of the envelope does my supervising for me.

Milburn wants to hand firms like mine £1.1 billion a year in hiring, wage, and retention subsidies, and the Board says there's no evidence such schemes have ever worked. They work on me daily. They've simply been arriving through a wider door than the report's. You call a payment to an employer a subsidy when you like the employer and a bounty when you don't. It's a bounty. Paid on the poor, collected at the gate, and I have never once left a bounty on the table. Cut the cheque and my wage stays exactly where the Board says it can't. The public makes up what I decline to. Take the payroll-tax cut that would cost your Treasury the £5 billion a year Milburn won't recommend. A cheaper hire is a more numerous hire, and a more numerous hire is a longer queue.

Now take the same levy into a different industry, because the principle travels. Tamsin is a room-turn unit in the housekeeping company I own outside Leeds, and I refuse the mattress lifter because it costs £0.03 an occupied room. You book hotel rooms and sleep on mattresses that Tamsin's 20-year-old back can't turn, and I keep the £0.03. You want clean linen and a ready room. I understand. If a young body has become expensive, don't cut the wage where the worker can see it. Cut what the body needs. A worker can complain about the wage on her pay slip; a mattress lifter is merely capital equipment. Let me put the number where you can see it. It works out to £0.03 per occupied room across the portfolio.

By a room-turn unit, I mean a young woman whose body turns mattresses while her back refuses. The sentimental answer is that her spine belongs on my ledger. It doesn't. One rung further along the same chain: Emeka is a chemical hand in the hotel laundry I own outside Leeds, and he handles linens that drip PCE. Lift your eyes off the one boy, because the lesson is older than my depot. In 1834 your law put the design in a clause: relief must sit below the worst wage on offer, so fear does the negotiating. Fitzhugh saw the rest of the shape and called it the cruelty of free society; the employer is really free, he wrote, and I call it the business plan. The wage freed the master of the one bill a plantation could never escape: keeping the body alive from cradle to grave. Your welfare state finished his sentence for him. And while we are on the question of who is to blame for the rota: you want me to be one man, because one man can be voted out, sued, or buried. I understand the wish. But I am a curriculum. The night rota on my depot floor is Chapter 6 of *Calibrated Deprivation: A Manager's Guide to Employee Motivation*, and it is assigned in 40 programmes besides my own. The man who wrote the copy I run sat in my seminar, second row, and ran the subtraction faster than I did. I say that with a teacher's pride. I didn't invent this arithmetic. Now the forecast, since the Board asked the government for solutions and I keep a file. Disability and incapacity spending is headed from £55 billion toward £68 billion, most of it routed to keeping my waiting room fed and docile, and I fund none of it. The households that pay it in are the households that buy my parcels. Someone sent me a projection in the spring: run my model across the whole country and the programme pays out its last cheque before my queue runs out of flexes. Good news, as far as the rota is concerned. The projection sits in a drawer in the Doncaster office I have never unlocked. The demand model isn't on my desk either. I expect the sheets to arrive clean on Friday. My nights are quiet. The scanner never misses. The rota is full, and that's the figure I watch.

They'll come to you eventually with the redesign, because a Treasury at £68 billion has to be seen doing something. You'd file it under fairness to the taxpayer. I'd file it under a signature, and I have always signed fastest the papers that put another man's supper behind my name.

Callum is on nights tonight. Thank you for the parcel. Truly. You've been paying his groceries for years, and I appreciate it. It keeps my costs exactly where I like them.

Your country has built the cheapest labor supply in Europe, and the report your government commissioned says so in the polite vocabulary.
