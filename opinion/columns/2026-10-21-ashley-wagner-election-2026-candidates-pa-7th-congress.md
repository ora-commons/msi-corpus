Ryan Mackenzie is offering tax offsets instead of building a childcare system.

Childcare is deciding who works, who teaches and who can afford to remain in the workforce. Start with Jenny Escolastico, who lives outside Allentown with two children under age 5. Her household depends on her husband’s income, and she is staying home because childcare would cost more than she could earn working full time. Her son attends state-funded prekindergarten, but returning to work would still require after-school care for him and full-time care for her daughter. Her test is concrete: available seats and workable hours. “Childcare is another rent, another car note,” she said. She voted for Trump in 2024.

Lerin Hagenbuch of Allentown is a registered Republican who has not decided how to vote. She searched for childcare so she could return to teaching, only to find that some options required her to take a second job. “There are staff shortages everywhere,” she said. Her test is just as concrete: enough childcare workers to make returning to work possible. Her child is now in kindergarten, but childcare remains one of her leading concerns.

Becky Fisher of Bethlehem is a longtime preschool teacher earning less than $20 an hour. Much of her paycheck goes toward before- and after-school care for her two sons at the center where she works. She registered as a Republican after high school and backed George W. Bush’s reelection before switching parties and supporting Kamala Harris in 2024. She remains undecided, but she wants more than “lip service” to families and teachers. Her test is whether compensation can keep providers in a profession where the people doing the work cannot afford the care they arrange for their own children.

Those are household budgets, not talking points. They are also producing ballots. The voters in Pennsylvania’s competitive 7th Congressional District have already shown how thoroughly the contradiction has broken through partisan lines.

About 16% of voters remained undecided as of September, according to a Franklin and Marshall College poll. Brooks enters the final stretch with a [45% to 40% lead over Mackenzie in a Pennsylvania 7th District poll](/articles/2026-10-18-brooks-leads-mackenzie-45-to-40-in-pennsylvania-7th-district-poll/). The district north of Philadelphia is one of the narrowest paths to House control, and the votes of parents worried about affording care could determine which party controls Congress next year.

Brooks does not need a landslide to win. He needs enough of that 16% to make childcare a deciding condition, and his five-point lead gives him room to convert them. The question is whether Hagenbuch, Fisher and Escolastico see a childcare future with Brooks that they cannot see with Mackenzie.

The candidates are offering materially different answers.

Democratic nominee Bob Brooks, a retired firefighter and grandfather, is campaigning for universal prekindergarten, expanded full-day kindergarten, increased Head Start funding and broader eligibility for federal childcare assistance. His campaign treats childcare as public infrastructure: something the country should guarantee, not merely subsidize after families have been left to find it on their own.

Republican incumbent Ryan Mackenzie, a father of two children under age 3, has cosponsored an expansion of the federal childcare tax credit. His proposal was folded into Donald Trump’s bill of tax breaks and cuts to social programs, which also expanded incentives for private employers that provide childcare to workers. His campaign treats childcare principally as a tax benefit.

A deduction and a service are not the same thing.

A tax credit can lower a family’s bill after eligible care is secured; by itself, it does not guarantee a preschool seat, extend program hours or solve the worker shortages driving prices. It does not lower the price before care is available. When seats and staff are scarce, a larger deduction cannot buy a nonexistent opening, keep a center staffed or place a child with a nearby provider.

Brooks’s proposals attack those bottlenecks: more pre-K, longer kindergarten days, more Head Start funding and wider access to federal assistance. Mackenzie is offering a tax offset rather than a system.

He says he will continue working with both sides to give working families “the resources, representation, and support that they deserve.” His party’s actual governing record is less reassuring. Trump campaigned to reduce childcare costs with tariff revenue; that plan has not materialized. More recently, he said, “We’re fighting wars. We can’t take care of daycare.” That is not a budget blueprint, and it is not a rounding error in priorities. It is a declaration that government should retreat while families absorb the cost and wait while the country finds more crises to prioritize.

The scale of the problem is no longer theoretical. Child Care Aware of America reported that childcare costs rose faster than inflation from 2020 to 2024 and that the national average for full-time childcare topped $13,000 in 2025. In an AP-NORC poll from June 2025, about three-quarters of adults called childcare a “major problem,” while 64% supported the federal government providing free or low-cost childcare for children too young for public school.

Those numbers describe a country making a consequential decision about work, wages and family survival. They also expose the difference between the two campaigns.

Anne Helen Petersen wrote that burnout “isn’t a personal problem.” Childcare hunger is not a personal problem either. A parent cannot optimize her way past a missing seat or a center that cannot retain staff. The private workarounds are already visible: leave the workforce or strip something else from the month.

Taylor Swift’s title “You’re On Your Own, Kid” names the private script this policy forces on parents: make your own care plan, carry the risk and blame yourself when it fails. Read as a diagnosis of American care infrastructure, the title points to the job government has refused. The spreadsheet becomes a substitute for a seat, a teacher and a stable shift.

Congress has also seen what genuine federal investment can do. The federal government spent more than $50 billion on the childcare industry during the pandemic, keeping providers open while funding staff training, higher wages, protective gear and childcare assistance for more families. Joe Biden proposed subsidies intended to keep most families from paying more than 7% of their household income for childcare; the proposal narrowly failed. Three years later, the last of the pandemic aid expired, and assistance waitlists grew sharply, forcing low-income households to sideline a parent from the workforce or forgo necessities to foot a childcare bill.

The political consequences are already visible. [Seven senators have demanded data on childcare closures and rising costs](/articles/2026-10-01-seven-senators-demand-data-on-childcare-closures-rising-costs/). The Campaign for a Family Friendly Economy has spent $60 million this year backing Democrats in congressional and gubernatorial races while targeting voters in a dozen states—including voters from both parties—with canvassing, ads and mailers. That $60 million operation shows how seriously Democrats are betting on the conversion. The money may persuade wavering voters, but childcare’s cross-party pull already exists; the PAC can amplify it, not create it.

Their reasons are not ideological abstractions. They want parents to work, teachers to stay in classrooms and families to stop treating a second income as a luxury swallowed by daycare.

Those three needs form the policy test: seats and hours determine whether a parent can work, a viable workforce determines whether those seats and hours can be delivered, and adequate pay determines whether providers remain in the profession.

Mackenzie can cosponsor a tax-credit expansion while his party’s president dismisses federal childcare money. Brooks has put the actual bottleneck in his platform: build and fund care so families can use it.

Childcare is not a secondary issue hiding beneath the culture-war noise. It determines whether millions of parents can enter the workforce, whether teachers can remain in low-paid early-education jobs and whether providers survive another round of closures. Pennsylvania’s 7th District voters have already done the arithmetic.

The affirmative policy is already on the table: universal prekindergarten, full-day kindergarten, more Head Start funding and broader eligibility for federal childcare assistance. Funding the seats, extending the hours and paying the workforce enough to stay is how government shows up when families need it to.

Childcare is the test. Brooks has taken it. Mackenzie is still arguing about the exam.

Now the candidates must show theirs.
