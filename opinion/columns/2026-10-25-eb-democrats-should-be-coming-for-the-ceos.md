Corporate America, answer up. Stop treating lawyers as a substitute for answers. If you’ve done business with the Trump Administration, Democrats are coming for you next year—and they should be.

That isn’t what most Democrats are saying before the election, but it ought to be part of the battle plan if they take one or both houses of Congress next week. Steve Elmendorf, the longtime Democratic operative and part of a Beltway lobbying shop, made that clear in a recent chat with independent journalist Brody Mullins. Any resulting probes would not be limited to ballroom donations.

“I think it’s not going to be purely ‘you gave money to the ballroom,’” Mr. Elmendorf said. “It’s going to be: ‘I gave $20 million to the ballroom, and then I went to DoD, and I got a contract from the government.’” Or: “I gave $10 million to the ballroom, and then I went to the Commerce Department, and Howard Lutnick gave me a tariff exclusion.”

That sounds alarmingly plausible given the partisan times, and accuracy is more dangerous than partisanship. It exposes corporate complicity, not vulnerability. It exposes the bargain at the heart of political industrial policy: Government controls contracts, tariffs and exemptions; companies supply political money and expect access in return. Money moves toward power, and power returns as contracts, exemptions and access. The operating logic is simple: influence becomes a corporate expense, and public authority becomes a sales tool.

That is not civic participation. That is government for sale.

Many CEOs may not have liked writing checks for the inaugural or White House ballroom. They nevertheless had choices, and they made them. The government’s leverage over their businesses made cooperation convenient; it did not make it innocent. The leverage is real. So is the benefit. Neither fact makes the transaction innocent, and neither entitles a company to complain when political influence produces political consequences.

Did former Apple CEO Tim Cook really have a choice if he wanted an exemption from Mr. Trump’s China tariffs for electronics imports? Of course he wanted it. That is what leverage is for. He could have refused to buy access. Democrats say CEOs should have refused, and they are right. Executives do answer to shareholders; boardrooms are not neutral ground for administration policy. Democrats do: they answer to voters.

Democrats will now put the CEOs in the dock. Good. Let them. They will do it as part of their corporate-accountability agenda, and they will make companies pay a price for cooperating with the Trump Administration. The scandal is not cooperation. The scandal is companies that believe cooperation should be costless.

An accountability agenda that penalizes executives for using government to tilt commerce is not an abuse of power. It is enforcement of the rule that companies must not purchase the government’s neutrality.

Democrats will also investigate the White House and Trump family, but government officials can fight subpoenas in court on executive-privilege grounds. Not so private companies. American business does not get to purchase access, enjoy preferential treatment and then hide behind the separation of powers. That separation may shield government officials from inconvenient subpoenas; private companies cannot invoke executive privilege to avoid Congress.

“Congress is then going to go to companies, knowing that companies don’t have the protection from the courts of saying no,” Mr. Elmendorf told Mr. Mullins.

That is not a loophole to exploit. It is the point. Private executives cannot expect Congress to leave them alone merely because compliance is inconvenient. If public officials may invoke privilege, investigators should begin where private influence is easiest to conceal: in the records of the companies that bought access.

The message is that American businesses should begin preparing their answers and defenses now. Lawyers can build the defenses; boards must supply the answers. The answers should be honest. As both parties politicize every corner of the economy, no company should expect immunity from investigation or retribution. If Democrats take Congress, let them use it to make these transactions legible.

Corporate America may condemn political industrial policy now. It cannot complain when congressional scrutiny exposes the price of that cooperation.

Dollars may still talk in Washington. They should have to answer for what they bought.
