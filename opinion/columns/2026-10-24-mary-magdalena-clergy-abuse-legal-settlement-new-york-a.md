New York’s Archdiocese made 1,300 survivors wait seven years for $800 million.

About 1,300 survivors carried the claims through more than seven years of court. The agreement is not final. The parties are scheduled to sign during the week of Nov. 6. Their signatures are still required. The number is already a reckoning.

The Archdiocese of New York and attorneys for about 1,300 parishioners who said they were sexually abused by clergy have agreed to an $800 million settlement of their legal claims. It is the second-largest involving a Catholic archdiocese in the United States.

This is not the price of one moment of abuse. It is what delay costs. Survivors had to carry their claims through the courts for more than seven years before the parties agreed in May on a framework requiring everyone who filed a claim to sign off.

The survivors accomplished what institutions rarely accomplish: unanimity. Attorneys said the survivors who filed claims unanimously supported the agreement. Jeff Anderson, whose firm represents people who filed more than 1,000 claims against the Catholic Church in New York state, called it “a powerful moment in the pursuit of justice” and a meaningful step toward accountability. Trusha Goffe called it “an extraordinary achievement.”

They held the ground while years of litigation mounted against them. Look past the money for a moment. The survivors forced this result. The institution that had been accused of protecting abusers had to negotiate at the scale of $800 million.

Archbishop Ronald Hicks emailed clergy Saturday to confirm the settlement, telling parish leaders that “much progress has been made.” He wrote that the archdiocese was “on the verge” of bringing “some measure of healing and reconciliation” to the victim-survivors, and that the agreement would allow it to turn its “full attention once again” to preaching the Gospel, healing the sick, caring for the poor, welcoming the stranger and educating children.

Read that sequence carefully: lawsuits first, settlement second, healing third, institutional mission restored last.

Ronald, your throat closes around the word “reconciliation.” You did not spend seven years in court. The survivors did. Your sternum tightens when you call that distance “progress.” Seven years is not much. Seven years is the distance they crossed to bring the institution to the word “settlement.”

The law itself exposes how long the road from harm to accountability could become. New York enacted its survivor window in 2019, allowing people abused as children to sue their abusers and institutions that shielded them until they turned 55. Those already 55 when the law passed received a one-time filing window until August 2021, regardless of when the alleged abuse occurred.

The law opened a path. It did not erase the years before it or make the litigation easy. The moral case should never have needed machinery at all.

Ronald, you put “reconciliation” at the center of the announcement. Anderson put “justice” and “accountability” there. That is the divide the survivors forced into the open. Justice is not a church service. It is a reckoning.

Progress is not reconciliation. A number is not healing. Reconciliation is not a communications objective the Archdiocese can announce on survivors’ behalf. It belongs to the people who were abused. They can define it, withhold it and decide what it requires. The church can pay, answer and change. It cannot presume absolution.

The abuse was not a detour from the church’s mission. If church representatives failed children while invoking sacred authority, then answering for that failure belongs inside the mission, not outside it. The church does not get to treat survivors as an interruption to its work and then call their compensation a sign that the interruption is over.

Ronald, your jaw sets against the years it took to make your sentence true. Progress did not arrive when you sent the email. Progress arrived when the survivors held the line long enough to make the institution agree to the check.

The scale of the institution makes the excuse of isolated wrongdoing less persuasive. The New York Archdiocese is home to about 2.5 million Catholics, roughly 300 churches and more than 150 parochial schools. Its reach touches communities far beyond a single parish or a few bad actors. The settlement therefore represents something larger than the resolution of individual claims: it is the bill for an institution’s failure to protect children.

New York is not the only institution confronting the cost of clergy-abuse litigation. The national ledger is brutally consistent: delay, litigation, arithmetic.

In December 2025, a federal judge approved a settlement under which New Orleans would pay at least $230 million to abuse victims. There, [survivors have called the promised bankruptcy amounts “a farce”](/articles/2026-08-28-new-orleans-catholic-abuse-survivors-call-bankruptcy-amounts-a-farce/). This June, [San Francisco agreed to $395 million to settle clergy-abuse claims](/articles/2026-06-29-san-francisco-archdiocese-to-pay-395m-to-settle-abuse-claims/) arising from roughly 500 lawsuits.

Los Angeles remains the only larger U.S. Catholic-archdiocese settlement over clergy sexual-abuse claims. In 2024, the Archdiocese of Los Angeles agreed to pay $880 million to 1,000 victims, in addition to the $740 million it had paid previously.

Add New York’s $800 million, and the national ledger stops looking like a collection of unfortunate local exceptions. It shows the national shape of delay: survivors enter court, years pass, and accountability finally arrives. Each number marks the point at which years of fighting became arithmetic. None was voluntary generosity.

Ronald, your skin tightens when you write that “much progress has been made.” The survivors have supplied the church with years of endurance, an orderly settlement and the rarest thing a wounded group can offer: unanimity. They should not have to spend more years proving that they were believed.

They have already held the line together. They did it without gratitude for being believed. They did it while the institution prepared its next statement about healing. Their unity was not a gift to the church. It was the price the church should have paid long before they had to force it.

The $800 million should be treated as the monumental victory it is. It is not absolution. The agreement answers the legal claims. It cannot, by itself, answer the harder question: who protected the children, and will those protectors be held responsible?

Until the church answers with more than a check and a prayer for healing, victory remains enormous, hard-won and incomplete.

Sign. Pay. Let the survivors define what comes next.
