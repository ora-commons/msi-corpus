Alvaro Pulido Vargas allegedly took food money from the hungry, medicine money from the sick, and housing money from the roofless, then laundered the take through American bank accounts for five years. Tuesday he answers for it in a Florida courtroom. The banks that moved his money answer to no one.

The Justice Department has spent years building the machinery to catch him. Two federal indictments, filed in 2019 and 2021. A ten-million-dollar reward for information leading to his arrest. British sanctions. A cooperating co-conspirator in his longtime business partner Alex Saab, who pleaded guilty last month to conspiracy to launder monetary instruments and agreed to forfeit $195 million. And before all of it, a clandestine U.S. military operation inside Venezuela that put the boss, Nicolas Maduro, and his wife Cilia Flores in a Manhattan courtroom on narco-terrorism charges. Pulido Vargas was the last one standing. He is no longer standing.

The numbers are the confession. The 2019 indictment describes a low-income housing contract meant to put roofs over Venezuelan families, and alleges that $350 million was laundered off it — false import documents for goods that never arrived, officials bribed to approve them, money routed through bank accounts in the Southern District of Florida and out to overseas accounts the conspirators controlled. The 2021 indictment describes the CLAP program, the Venezuelan government's own food-and-medicine lifeline to its poorest households, as a $1.6 billion piggy bank: contracts won with bribes, proceeds run out through accounts in Florida, Antigua, the United Arab Emirates, and elsewhere, from July 2015 to at least 2020. Nearly two billion dollars in government contracts, allegedly won by bribery and washed through the American financial system.

Five years. American bank accounts in the middle of the entire stretch. Bills of lading for cargo that never crossed a border do not file themselves. Shell accounts in four jurisdictions do not open themselves. Someone is paid — well, and legally, and again this year — to process that paper, to notice it, and to look the other way.

That someone is the domestic half of this story, and the domestic half does not get arrested.

A foreign kleptocrat can rent American compliance architecture: the shell-company formation agents, the registered agents, the bank-secrecy counsel, the correspondent accounts that let money enter this country and leave it without a face. Rent it legally. Run it for years without tripping an alarm anyone is required to answer for. The machinery is for sale, and the people who sell it are licensed, bonded, and well-compensated. When the pipeline is finally traced back to its source, the person in the courtroom is the foreigner who rented it. Never the Americans who built it, staffed it, and sent the invoice.

Read the ledger of who gets the machinery and who does not. In 2012, HSBC admitted laundering money for Mexican drug cartels and for Iran, Cuba, and Sudan. It paid $1.9 billion. No individual was prosecuted. Senator Grassley said the bank had "quite literally purchased a get-out-of-jail-free card." In 2020, Wells Fargo settled for opening millions of unauthorized customer accounts over fourteen years — a $3 billion agreement, and the Justice Department's own words were that it was made "with the bank itself, not with any individuals responsible for the fraud." In 2008, no chief executive of a major Wall Street firm went to prison for the conduct that broke the world economy.

Now set that ledger beside Tuesday's docket. A private citizen of Colombia gets two indictments, a ten-million-dollar bounty, and a federal task force. A bank that launders for cartels gets a deferred prosecution agreement, a fine paid by shareholders, and a press release.

The Justice Department says this arrest sends a message. It does. The message is that the machine catches the man who feeds it and never the institutions that profit from it. Two indictments. Nearly two billion dollars. Five years of Florida bank accounts at the center of both. Not one American financial institution charged in either. The bagman is bound. The conduit is free.
