Brent Taylor wants to carry to Washington the power he spent four years taking from Memphis's Black voters.

He is a 58-year-old Republican state senator, and for nearly four years he has used the Republican supermajority in the Tennessee legislature to advance one idea with religious consistency: Tennessee's elected leaders should overrule the choices made by Memphis officials and Memphis voters whenever they disagree. On May 7, the same day the legislature approved a new congressional map stretching the Ninth District across half the state and making it majority white, Taylor announced his campaign for the seat. This is not a race that wandered into a new geography. The geography was built for him.

Taylor's four years begin with a body-camera video. In January 2023, the month he took his seat in the Tennessee Senate, a Memphis police officer stopped Tyré Nichols, a 29-year-old Black motorist. Nichols was unarmed. Body-camera and street-surveillance video captured officers beating him as he screamed for his mother. He died three days later.

After pressure from his family and community organizers, the Memphis City Council unanimously barred officers from using minor traffic violations as pretexts for investigating more serious crimes. Broken taillights, the ordinance said, were not a license to search a car for something else. Such stops are disproportionately used to detain Black drivers, and they are the opening move in encounters that end in unnecessary force.

Taylor opposed the ordinance. The following year he and Republican state Rep. John Gillespie sponsored legislation prohibiting local governments from limiting police traffic stops. The measure voided the council's vote. A state senator who does not live in Memphis, and who has never been elected by anyone in it, had decided that the elected representatives of a majority-Black city were not competent to police their own streets.

RowVaughn Wells, Nichols' mother, pleaded with lawmakers to reject the bill. Taylor responded from the Senate floor that empathy could not be allowed to "cloud our judgment in protecting 7 million Tennesseans."

There is a famous argument in the American freedom struggle about who the real obstacle is. In "Letter from Birmingham Jail" (April 1963; reprinted in *Why We Can't Wait*, 1964), King wrote that the great stumbling block was not the hooded terrorist but the orderly citizen who preferred order to justice and who set another person's timetable for freedom. King wrote it from a cell, because his freedom was the one on the timetable. Taylor is no moderate. But the structure of his argument is the structure King named: a large constituency's comfort, set against an electorate's delay. Seven million Tennesseans weigh more with him than the vote of the people who live under the law he is writing.

Eight months after the bill passed, the Justice Department released an investigation of the Memphis Police Department. It found that the department's aggressive traffic enforcement harmed Memphisans, discriminated against Black residents, and enabled the "regular use of excessive force with impunity." The department recommended limiting pretextual stops. When Donald Trump returned to office in 2025, his Justice Department retracted those findings.

Retracting a civil-rights finding did not restore Memphis's ordinance. Taylor's law remains.

And Taylor did not stop with the stops. He supported the Memphis Safe Task Force, a partnership of more than two dozen state and federal law-enforcement agencies, including the National Guard. Before the task force began, he testified before the U.S. Senate Judiciary Committee in support of its effort to "infiltrate gangs and arrest more routine criminals."

For Black motorists, Taylor's vision of public safety is straightforward: restore the discretionary stops Memphis voters rejected, and make it harder for local officials to limit them again.

Taylor calls these laws public-safety measures. His record says something more precise.

Malcolm X stood in Detroit on November 10, 1963, and told the grass roots what they were actually up against: a common oppressor, a common exploiter, a common discriminator ("Message to the Grass Roots"). The point was never one man's face. The point was a structure the people inside it could name in common, and therefore meet in common.

The operation has a name, and it is not public safety. Preemption is the transfer of power upward, from the people who live with a policy to officials who do not. Taylor has not merely used it. In four years he helped push through roughly half a dozen laws that superseded the authority of local officials and the decisions of their voters — and he singled out one local official by name.

The prosecutor's office was the second target, and the method there is where Taylor's work is most finished.

Steve Mulroy became the first Democrat elected Shelby County district attorney in twenty-five years in 2022, promising to pursue violent crime through rehabilitation, drug treatment, mental-health counseling, job training, and a unit to examine wrongful convictions. Taylor called him a "far-left zealot" who could make crime worse.

In 2023, Taylor sponsored legislation transferring review of certain death-penalty cases from elected county prosecutors to the state attorney general, an official appointed by the state Supreme Court. The law applied statewide. Shelby County prosecutors had handled half of the state's forty death-row prisoners.

Then he went further. One law he co-sponsored authorized the attorney general to review every case entering Mulroy's office, including cases arising from task-force arrests, and to report the dispositions and the bail recommendations to legislative leaders. Another provision allowed the attorney general to ask the Tennessee Supreme Court to install a temporary, unelected district attorney whenever Mulroy declined to prosecute a category of crime. A second law required Mulroy's office to report to the state and to the Justice Department within ten business days whenever it entered a plea deal, dismissed charges, or lowered charges in a felony case stemming from a task-force arrest.

The state association of district attorneys initially opposed an earlier version of the first bill because it would have applied to prosecutors throughout Tennessee. The association dropped its objection after the measure was narrowed to Mulroy alone.

Mulroy's description of the result has been exact: "an unelected shadow DA operating in parallel to the duly elected DA." He has sued to block both laws, arguing that they unfairly single out Shelby County, burden his office, and violate prosecutorial independence. The lawsuit is pending.

*Deep Space Nine* understood the shape of this before the Tennessee General Assembly did. In "Inquisition" (Deep Space Nine, 1998), Starfleet's Internal Affairs officer Luther Sloan tells Dr. Bashir, whom he is detaining, that the two of them "are not going to see eye to eye on this subject, so I suggest we stop discussing it." Sloan's real employer is Section 31, an apparatus that answers to nobody and draws its authority from a clause in a charter rather than from an election. The episode is the franchise's first formal question about whether a free society's intelligence service can be moral at all.

Tennessee needed no starship and no oath. It needed a statute. The charter clause became a line item; the uniform became a suit; and the officer who answered to nobody became a line in the state code giving the attorney general sight of every case, every disposition, and every bail recommendation in an elected prosecutor's office. Taylor never had to prove that Mulroy would not prosecute crimes. He only had to build a machine for acting on the claim. And the machine works better than an overt takeover would, because a takeover has to name the winner. This one only has to name the process.

Taylor has called Mulroy "the weak link" in the task force's work and claimed, without evidence, that he refuses to prosecute crimes. Mulroy's office prosecutes more than forty thousand defendants a year.

Taylor declined an interview and did not respond to detailed questions for this reporting. A candidate whose central political project is denying local voters control of public safety declined to explain to the people affected how his policies would make them safer. The bills answer for him. So does the federal investigation of the department whose stops he restored.

The third target was money, and here the preemption is naked.

In August 2022, criminal-justice organizations and county officials, including Mulroy's Republican predecessor Amy Weirich, agreed on a bipartisan ability-to-pay calculator, a tool for keeping people out of jail on bonds they could not afford. State law already told judges to impose the least onerous conditions and to consider a defendant's financial circumstances.

By the following January, Taylor had co-sponsored a bill to scrap it. Under his law, "the defendant's ability to pay shall not be considered." A 2025 University of Memphis study found that the median bail amount increased after the law took effect.

Bill Anderson, a former Shelby County judge who supported bail reform, said the measure hits Black residents hardest because they are disproportionately poor and overrepresented in the Shelby County jail. Anderson's verdict was blunt: "He called them reform acts, but they're not reform."

John Pfaff, a Fordham Law School professor who studies the effects of preemption on prosecutors, puts the pattern in starker terms. Tough-on-crime laws like these have historically been "a form of social control against disliked minority groups," he said. When suburban and rural voters control the state legislature, preemption allows them to "finally tell Memphis what they want Memphis to do."

That is precisely what Taylor has done. And the redistricting has transformed his state record into his congressional pitch.

That is the through-line. Taylor's policies do not simply punish Black defendants or Black motorists or residents of a predominantly Black city. They remove the authority of people elected to make the decisions in those places. A judge selected locally can no longer consider what a defendant cannot afford. A prosecutor selected locally can be shadowed, reviewed, and replaced. A city council can prohibit a police practice, and the state legislature can declare the prohibition void.

I am not interested in whether a man is tough. I am interested in who has the authority to write the rules for the people who have to live under them.

Which brings us to the opponent, the map, and the only question that will decide this race.

Justin J. Pearson is a 31-year-old Black Democrat with deep roots in southwest Memphis. He stopped an oil pipeline proposed through a Black neighborhood. He was expelled from the Tennessee House with another legislator after leading a gun-control protest following a 2023 school shooting. He [won the August Democratic primary](/articles/2026-08-14-justin-pearson-wins-tennessee-democratic-congressional-primary/) with a campaign built around schools, economic opportunity, and rehabilitation as crime-fighting tools rather than incarceration alone. He has also outraised Taylor in campaign donations, even after Taylor loaned his congressional campaign $1.5 million.

Taylor remains the favorite, because the [new congressional map](/articles/2026-08-06-tennessee-primary-tests-map-that-reshapes-memphis-congressional-district/) does the work money cannot. For more than four decades the Ninth was Tennessee's sole majority-Black congressional district, covering most of Memphis. The map approved this spring stretched it across half the state and made it majority white, a Republican-driven move intended to eliminate Democratic representation and to make the dilution of Black votes harder to challenge.

And on the day the map was approved, Taylor and U.S. Rep. Tim Burchett, a fellow Republican, sat side by side on the U.S. Capitol steps. "My friend Tim Burchett showing me the dadgum ropes," Taylor wrote in a Facebook post. The post was a joke. The contrast was not. The man who had spent nearly four years taking Memphis officials' authority was preparing to seek a larger seat.

The race will be presented as a referendum on crime. Taylor has made that framing impossible to debate honestly, because his laws pre-empt the debate itself. A city cannot argue with a state senator who has already voided its vote, subjected its elected prosecutor to state supervision, and prohibited its judges from considering whether defendants can afford bail.

Black Memphisans have watched their officials try to protect Black drivers after the killing of Tyré Nichols. They have watched the election of a district attorney Shelby County had not chosen in twenty-five years treated as an inconvenience to be legislated away. They have watched poor defendants lose access to judges who can see what poverty does to a family and a jail.

Taylor asks the Ninth District to send to Washington the representative who spent nearly four years using state power to make the voters' own choices disposable.

There is a Memphis in King's argument, and it is not this one.

In the Memphis sanitation-strike speeches of March 1968 (March 18, 1968; "I've Been to the Mountaintop," April 3, 1968), King told the city that a nation sitting on that much wealth had no business tolerating conditions in which a man's rent and his children's shoes could not both be paid; that the wealth had to be used to end poverty; and that a country which refused would answer for it in the most theological sense. He meant it. He had come to Memphis to stand with sanitation workers asking for a living wage, not with a task force asking for the power to stop Black drivers.

The question this city is being asked now, by a state senate that has spent four years taking its own answers, is a different question. Not what do we do about a man who cannot pay his bail, but what do we do about a Black driver on a freeway, a prosecutor who will not dismiss a case on command, and a council that voted unanimously to protect the people it governs?

King said the arc of the moral universe is long but it bends toward justice. King was right, and King was incomplete. In his SCLC presidential address in Atlanta (August 16, 1967) he was already saying that the whole structure of American life had to be changed, and in the last months of his life he told his own organization that the problem could not be solved until economic and political power was radically redistributed. King's late years were that breaking. The Memphis sanitation strike was that breaking. The breaking is what gets done now, or the arc does not bend now.

So here is the only question in this race that deserves a serious answer. Not whether Brent Taylor is tough. He has demonstrated that he is. The question is whether the voters of the redrawn Ninth District want to send to Washington the man who spent four years proving that his toughness works best on the elected officials of a majority-Black city he does not live in.

The people Taylor wants to represent should make the verdict unmistakable. Memphis has endured nearly four years of his state preemption. It does not need to import it to Washington.
