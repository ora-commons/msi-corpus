The United States spent Tuesday afternoon at the United Nations accusing Havana of looting its own people, and by Wednesday it had thrown a first secretary out of the country for swatting a cellphone. Put those two facts side by side and the week writes itself: an argument Washington could not win inside the hall, settled instead at the door.

Start with what actually happened on the floor. Ambassador Mike Waltz took the podium nearly an hour into the annual debate on the embargo and opened by accusing the late Fidel Castro of sending thousands to "concentration camps." The Cuban delegation pounded its desks; one diplomat made a timeout motion; others demanded the presiding official — Bangladesh's Khalilur Rahman — grant them procedural time to respond. Cuban Foreign Minister Bruno Rodríguez and his team left their seats and walked toward the rostrum. Waltz, tapping his fingers for emphasis, shouted, "This is America, my friend. You cannot silence me. Back up!"

The Wall Street Journal reported that Rodríguez never approached Waltz or spoke to him directly, though both men kept talking over each other in loud voices. They did not need to be face to face; the contempt carried. [As we covered when the two men first traded accusations over the embargo](/articles/2026-10-27-waltz-and-rodr-guez-trade-accusations-over-u-s-embargo-against-cuba-at-u-n/), this has been a shouting match with procedural rules attached. Rodríguez eventually got his time and accused Waltz of "lying grossly." Then came the photos of what Waltz said were expensive watches and a private island belonging to Castro's family and Cuba's elite — a contrast with communist philosophy, Waltz said, that "would make New York socialites blush." For the next half hour the Cuban bench shouted objections and hammered its table.

None of that got anyone expelled. What did was a phone. In a video Waltz posted online Tuesday, first secretary Richard Tur de la Concepcion is seen smacking a recording cellphone and saying it "violated my personal space." Waltz's own post alleged that one of the regime's so-called diplomats tried to intimidate U.N. staff into cutting the microphones while he exposed the regime for the world to see — an accusation of interference serious enough to deserve better evidence than a cellphone clip.

Cuban Ambassador Ernesto Soberon then fired off an open letter to the U.N. secretary-general accusing a Waltz spokesman of harassing Tur de la Concepcion by filming him. Harassment. In a building full of cameras. And the letter said nothing at all about the expulsion, which the State Department had announced that same day. The Cuban Consulate did not respond to questions about the case.

The State Department's line was blunt: "We have made clear to the Cuba Mission that this individual is not welcome in the United States, and as a result this individual has left." No details. Just a door. He was listed as a first secretary.

Here is the part the expulsion is meant to overshadow. At the end of the proceedings, 130 member states voted to call on the United States to end its trade embargo on Cuba. Seventeen voted no. Twenty-five abstained. This is not a close call being spun by adversaries; it is the same annual rebuke the U.S. has collected year after year, and this year [the Assembly formally approved the resolution](/articles/2026-10-28-united-nations-general-assembly-approves-cuba-resolution-on-u-s-embargo/) over American objections, 130 to 17.

Soberon's complaint about personal space is easy to mock, and Waltz's video was posted to make it look easy to mock. But the U.S. position on that episode is harder than its spokesmen admit: a spokesman filming mission staff and the ambassador broadcasting the footage is a choice, and the Cuban diplomat's open letter names it. Washington's answer to the accusation was not a rebuttal. It was a plane ticket.

That is the tell. Historically, the United States has expelled Cuban diplomats over spying — a charge that carries weight whether or not you believe it. This time the charge is that a man struck a phone. A foreign envoy who puts his hands on a camera pointed at him has done something real, and Tur de la Concepcion said out loud what he was doing; that is not nothing. But expelling a first secretary over it, while the General Assembly votes 130 to 17 against your signature policy, is a government reaching for the only lever it can operate alone.

And here is where I have to be straight with you about who actually pays for any of this. Not Waltz, not Rodríguez, not the delegation pounding its desk for the cameras. The embargo Trump tightened until the island's economy ground to a halt reaches the same people every embargo reaches: the woman running a Havana boarding house whose roof has needed patching for a decade because the materials stopped arriving; the mechanic on Calle Ocho in Miami whose family still sends money and medicine north-to-south and watches it get slower and meaner each year; the farmer on the island who cannot get the spare part for the tractor, and the shop here that would sell him the part if the paperwork were not a maze. I run a shop. I know what a stalled supply chain does to a working family — you make do, you wait, you fix it twice instead of once. Multiply that across two countries and thirty years of policy, and what you get is not a regime punished. It is a people wearied, on both ends of the strait.

Secretary of State Marco Rubio and Waltz can denounce Havana as loudly as they like; President Trump can tighten sanctions until the island's economy stops altogether and keep hinting at military intervention. None of it moves 130 governments, and none of it needs to — the embargo does not answer to them. It answers at home, where being seen to shout "This is America, my friend" and then expel the man who swatted the phone plays as strength. That is the actual product of this week: not pressure on Havana, not relief for the mechanic or the farmer or the roofer, but a clip that plays well on the evening shows.

I will grant the administration its one true thing: a diplomat who swats a phone because he does not like being recorded has forfeited some courtesy, and Tur de la Concepcion can watch the rest of this from Havana. But that is a small, cold comfort dressed up as a foreign policy. Eisenhower warned that the machinery of national security would swallow the nation's sense of proportion if citizens let it. This week we watched a 113-vote margin answered with a departure notice, a years-long embargo defended with a shouting match, and the working people who bear the cost of both — in Havana, in Miami, in the towns that would trade with the island if anyone let them — nowhere in the story at all.

The record of this week, written plainly, is that the United States went to New York to win an argument and came home with only the door. That is not nothing to the man who walked through it. It is just not anything to the people the policy was supposed to be about.
