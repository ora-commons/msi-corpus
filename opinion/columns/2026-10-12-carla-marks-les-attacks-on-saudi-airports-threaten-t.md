The Houthis didn't pick a fight with Saudi Arabia last weekend. They finished one Mohammed bin Salman started years ago. [The Journal's account](https://www.wsj.com/articles/attacks-on-saudi-airports-threaten-the-kingdoms-ambitions-044d3e42) reads the strike on Riyadh's main airport — at least twelve killed, more than three hundred wounded, over a thousand flights canceled — as a blow to the crown prince's ambition to turn the kingdom into a global tourism and investment hub. That's the correct frame, and it's the smallest one available. Something larger broke last weekend, and it wasn't a terminal.

One of the last two major air corridors connecting Europe and Asia suddenly looked like what it actually is: a chokepoint controlled by one family. And the man who spent a decade rebranding that airport as the gateway to his post-oil kingdom — the linchpin of a pivot from absolute monarchy to hedge-fund-meets-theme-park — is reportedly shuttling between bunker-equipped palaces. The man who sold the world a cosmopolitan hub is hiding in a hole. I'd rather spend that image on the argument than on the joke.

The costs compound exactly as his own decisions guaranteed. The war in Yemen, launched in 2015 to install a pliable government next door, produced instead a battle-hardened militia with ballistic missiles, drones, and the political will to use them. You cannot bomb a country into submission for eight years and then expect the survivors to respect your airport. The Houthi missiles are not an interruption of Saudi Arabia's rise. They are the receipt.

So that is the disaster ledger. And it is the wrong ledger — for the reason his bankers keep giving him. The same weekend that exposed the vanity validated the pitch. MBS has spent years telling every Western capital that Saudi Arabia is the indispensable Gulf security anchor, and being told in return that the umbrella was a cost center, a subsidy to be trimmed while the real money went to resorts. The attacks made the umbrella a product. Walk the alternatives: the UAE can host, it cannot absorb. Qatar can absorb, it cannot scale. Kuwait has the muscle, not the mandate.

I'll grant all of that. Now notice what the whole argument quietly assumes.

It assumes the corridor is his to price. It isn't.

Look at what he controls and ask whether any of it has to be his. Air traffic control over the route. Overflight permissions and the fees attached. Basing rights for the forces that police it. Procurement of the batteries that defend it. Every one of those is a piece of shared infrastructure — the sky over a trade route half the world's freight crosses — and every one is currently set by palace discretion, in a state that is simultaneously the sole shareholder, the sole regulator, and the sole counterparty.

And here is the part that should embarrass anyone reaching for "there is no alternative." The alternative already exists, in pieces, in places that decided a century ago that common sky should not be a private fiefdom. The Chicago Convention of 1944 recognized exactly the lever MBS is pulling — it affirms that sovereign airspace is complete and exclusive — and then, in the same breath, built the machinery to take the lever back: an international civil-aviation authority, and behind it EUROCONTROL's route-charge system, where the fees for flying over dozens of European states are set by treaty, pooled in one pot, and distributed to the providers that actually ran the planes through by a published formula. No president gets to hold a corridor. No minister gets to price a weekend. The billing is boring, multilateral, and functional — which is precisely why it never makes the newsletter.

There is a second piece. In 1946, Denmark, Norway, and Sweden — three small countries with a great deal of sky between them and no money to fight over it — founded one shared airline, jointly owned, and ran it for decades as public infrastructure. Britain's air-navigation service today has a large minority of its shares sitting in an employee trust, so the people controlling the airspace own a piece of it. Neither is utopia. SAS nearly went under and had to be rebuilt from the wreckage. But those are receipts, not slogans: the people who run the corridor can own the corridor.

Which brings me to the part of this story the Journal's story doesn't contain. MBS's gateway to the future is staffed — ground handlers, baggage crews, catering staff, cabin crew, a large share of the controllers — by migrant workers under a sponsorship system that ties them to their employer and bars independent unions. The most cosmopolitan facade in the Gulf is built on the least free labor market in the region. You cannot run a global hub on workers who cannot leave and cannot organize, then call the hub the future. So the corridor cooperative worth building has one non-negotiable membership rule: no airline, no airport authority, no state provider gets a seat that will not let its people bargain.

Now the part I want to be careful with, because it is where most of this week's writing went wrong.

Twelve people went to an airport and did not come home. Three hundred more were carried out on stretchers. A thousand flights' worth of strangers spent a night on a floor they hadn't planned on. And the first institutional response out of Riyadh — out of every capital watching — was about leverage. That arithmetic is not a market malfunction. It is what happens when the safety of a shared sky is a business model instead of a public good. The contempt belongs there, at whoever does it first, and nowhere near the dead.

This is also where the other prescription in circulation goes wrong. [The Trump administration is weighing whether to join the Saudi strikes](/articles/2026-10-10-trump-weighs-joining-saudi-strikes-after-new-riyadh-airport-attack/), and the case writes itself: the corridor is a Western interest, the Houthis are an Iranian proxy, a demonstration is overdue. But every European basing file that accelerates this month and every new battery ringed around a terminal is the same move that produced this problem. The largest American installation in the region sits at Al Udeid, in Qatar. It has made the United States a target for two decades and the Gulf a dependency for longer. Scaling the footprint is not a solution to the corridor's fragility. It is the corridor's fragility, deferred.

The answer is not less security. It is security that isn't owned. A multilateral civilian authority for Gulf overflight — the international aviation body, the carriers that fly the route, the workers who run it, the publics who depend on it — with route charges set by formula and air defense treated as a common cost rather than a royal asset. Arms-transfer restraint written into that authority, so the parties fueling the Yemen war stop being the parties guaranteeing the corridor. A regional forum where the thing being negotiated is the sky, not the survival of a regime. None of this is quick. All of it is cheaper than the alternative, and every working piece of it already exists somewhere.

Why doesn't it exist here? Because the Gulf's entire political economy is the bundling — security, commerce, airspace, land, and labor rights all under one signature, sold to outside capitals as a single package because a single check is easier than a dozen rules. Unbundling that is not a negotiation with a partner. It is a negotiation with a business model.

And the reason I am not writing this as a column about the Middle East is the same newsletter. Three items below the Saudi reporting sits [Britain's welfare bill](/articles/2026-10-12-uk-welfare-spending-on-track-to-hit-130-billion-as-claims-surge/): seven million people, more than one in ten, claiming at least one benefit; spending on track for £130 billion; a fourfold surge in psychiatric claims. Read the two stories together and they are one story. A monarchy monetizing shared sky and a democracy that let its floor rot until the repair bill arrived as sickness. Britain does not have a welfare problem. It has a thirty-year underinvestment problem wearing a welfare problem's coat. Every rich country that took the floor seriously — Denmark's flexicurity, Sweden's universalism — spends more and produces less sickness, because a layoff that doesn't end in ruin is cheaper than a decade of anxiety converted into a disability claim. And yes, it is harder to copy than to admire: Denmark built its floor over eighty years, on top of unions that actually bargain. Britain can start. It cannot import. It can stop cutting, which is not remotely the same thing as the politically painful cuts its leaders keep promising.

The crown prince does not need a bunker strategy. He does not need a pricing strategy — that is the one piece of advice this week has been unanimous about, and it is the one that keeps the sky in the palace. He needs to be asked the question the whole arrangement is built to avoid.

The corridor is common. Who owns it?

The answer that gets built is either a treaty and a board and a bargaining table — or another king, another battery, another bunker, and another weekend of strangers sleeping on a terminal floor. Those have been the only two items on the menu for a hundred years. Last weekend didn't add a third. It just sent the bill.
