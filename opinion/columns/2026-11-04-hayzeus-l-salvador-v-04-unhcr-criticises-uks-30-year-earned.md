Shabana Mahmood’s government is punishing refugees with waits of up to 30 years.

The government’s “earned settlement” arithmetic turns the route of arrival into the length of a waiting life. Earlier this week, Home Secretary Shabana Mahmood unveiled a package of [changes to settlement rules](/articles/2026-11-02-shabana-mahmood-plans-settlement-rule-changes-for-legal-migrants/) while leaving refugees to face waits of up to 30 years for permission to settle. The policy combines a [10-year standard for permanent settlement](/articles/2026-11-02-uk-government-plans-10-year-standard-for-permanent-settlement/) with route-based penalties that can leave some refugees waiting as long as 30 years. Work or study may shorten some waits. A 10-year penalty applies to people arriving by small boat or concealed in lorries rather than through safe and legal routes. A separate five-year route remains for care workers, who can apply for indefinite leave to remain. These limited concessions sit inside a system that keeps refugees provisionally alive.

That standard binds me too. My own communities have at times used immigrant labor while keeping immigrant families at the margin. My own tradition has known institutions that guarded their respectability while leaving the stranger outside. Governments of different hues in my own country have repeated the same machinery. We who claim the gospel and live in this country have helped make this arithmetic sound reasonable. We have confused a queue with justice and made shelter sound like a reward for good behavior. That complicity is ours, even when the rule is written by others. It does not transfer the cruelty from the government to the stranger. I condemn this rule under any government. Governments may set border rules; they may not punish the least powerful for needing a border crossed.

Home Secretary, call it rigor. I call it cruelty. This is not strictness. It is cruelty with a calendar. This is not border administration. It is a hierarchy of deserving built over people who had no safe road to travel.

The UN refugee agency, UNHCR, welcomed the legal routes but said the extended qualifying period for settlement “raises significant concerns.” Taken together with the government’s short-term stay arrangements and last year’s citizenship changes, the measures are “likely to be detrimental” to refugees’ sense of security, belonging and stability and to their ability to contribute to their new communities. UNHCR also drew the distinction that matters too often blurred: “Refugees are not migrants: they have fled their countries because they cannot rely on their protection nor safely return home.”

Safety is more than a grant of asylum; settlement has an important role. With changes that would block many refugees from ever reaching citizenship, the scheme keeps recognized refugees permanently temporary.

Home Secretary Mahmood, you have made the manner of flight a price of belonging. Your office has significantly reduced legal routes, and the announced routes will be accessible to only a small minority seeking sanctuary. You have retained the 10-year penalty on small-boat and lorry arrivals and left other refugees facing waits as long as 30 years. You have opened shorter paths for care workers and for people who enter work or study. Do not confuse exceptions with repair. They make the cruelty of the general rule visible.

The phrase *earned settlement* turns refuge into a reward for good behavior. Refuge is the recognition that a person cannot safely return. The state’s message is blunt: use the prescribed route, enter work or study, or wait. Work may shorten the clock. A boat or lorry lengthens it.

Picture a woman who escaped torture with her family from a burning village. The legal roads serve too few. She crosses the sea because the fire behind her leaves no time to fill a form. If she crossed by the licensed ferry, her wait is measured by the general settlement rules. If she crossed in a fishing boat, the state adds ten years. If she finds work or study, the clock may shorten. Otherwise, she may wait 30 years for permission to settle. At noon, she is still waiting for permission to settle. The state adds ten years to the wait and calls the result orderly.

Thirty years is not a wait on paper. It is a childhood spent waiting, a working life held in uncertainty, a parent unable to plan a child’s schooling, and a family unable to put down roots. The law calls it a qualifying period. The body calls it life.

Jesus said in Matthew 25:43, “I was a stranger and you did not welcome me,” and made the judgment exact: failing to welcome the least of these is failing to welcome him. The Torah forbids oppression of the resident alien and commands love of the stranger. Leviticus 19:34 says, “The alien who resides with you shall be to you as the citizen among you; you shall love the alien as yourself, for you were aliens in the land of Egypt.” This is not religious ornament. The state is making the manner of flight increase the time a person must live without a home.

The U.S. and Mexican bishops, in *Strangers No Longer* (2003), set out the moral arithmetic: nations may control their borders, while refugees and asylum seekers must receive protection and migrants’ human rights and dignity must be respected. Those duties do not cancel one another. Border authority becomes sin when it is used to turn temporary refuge into perpetual insecurity. The care-worker exception does not establish that balance. The 30-year wait makes one duty devour another.

Óscar Romero, with the laments of a suffering people behind him, cried out on March 23, 1980: “¡cese la persecución!”—stop the repression. When an economic, political and social order preserves privilege for a few while leaving many in inhuman conditions, Romero said, there is social sin, and the Church has a prophetic duty to denounce it. I call this scheme social sin. Its effect is to keep some refugees at the threshold for decades, asking them to prove their belonging before the law allows them to belong.

Does making the journey harder for the desperate make the border safer? Kamena Dorling, director of policy at the Helen Bamber Foundation, calls the 30-year rule “simply inhumane.” I agree. It will not deter people fleeing war, persecution, torture, and violence. It will punish them after danger has driven them to the shore, harm the lives and futures of thousands, and weaken integration and social cohesion.

A humane settlement system would grant asylum promptly, protect vulnerable people, open safe and legal routes broad enough that nobody has to bargain for belonging through labor, preserve family unity, and let families become secure citizens without living under the shadow of removal for 30 years. John XXIII wrote in *Pacem in Terris* (§25): “The fact that he is a citizen of a particular State does not deprive him of membership in the human family.” The common good requires a society in which safety is not rationed by paperwork and belonging is not purchased by a generation’s fear.

Stop the repression. Stop the punishment now. Stop making the route of flight a sentence.

Home Secretary, stop the cruelty. You do not need to defend a rule that makes a stranger earn the home that gives them shelter. You can amend it. You can make protection a place where a person can live, work, belong, and raise children without living under the shadow of removal for 30 years. Replace the thirty-year clock with equal protection, expand legal routes, preserve family unity, and remove labor as the price of belonging.

The door of return is open to you, and it opens inward. You do not have to pretend the policy was inevitable. You have to repair it.

At a station before dawn, a family waits beside bags tied shut. Thirty years of uncertainty stretches ahead of them.

At the Border Security Command compound in Dover, a photograph shows people wrapped in red blankets being escorted toward a van. That is the human reality your arithmetic would govern. A person is not a route. A refugee is not a waiting period. Welcome the stranger.
