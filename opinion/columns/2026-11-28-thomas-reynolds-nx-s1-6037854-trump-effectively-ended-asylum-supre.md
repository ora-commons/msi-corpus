Donald Trump abolished asylum at the border and built a deportation machine to enforce it.

The administration’s strongest case starts with facts that must be conceded. Simon Hankinson of the Heritage Foundation argues that most people arriving do not qualify for asylum, that Mexico offers a safe third country, and that many cross for work rather than protection. Asylum is discretionary; withholding of removal carries a higher burden. Congress delegated broad immigration-enforcement authority to the Executive and has shown substantial deference to Executive judgments about who may enter. *Sale v. Haitian Centers Council*, 509 U.S. 155 (1993), upheld the interdiction and summary return of Haitian migrants intercepted on the high seas without asylum hearings. *Mathews v. Eldridge*, 424 U.S. 319 (1976), permits a process calibrated to the stakes and the legislative judgment. Immigration judges are executive officials, not Article III judges. These are not frivolous propositions. They prove that most claims may fail and that the government has legitimate interests in border enforcement and orderly adjudication. They do not prove that a filed claim can cease to exist because the President says so.

Entry authority and the right to seek asylum are different legal acts. INA § 208(a)(1), 8 U.S.C. § 1158(a)(1), says that an alien “physically present in the United States or who arrives in the United States” may “request asylum.” The statute creates an adjudicable claim; it does not guarantee a favorable result. *INS v. Cardoza-Fonseca*, 480 U.S. 421 (1987), distinguishes asylum’s well-founded-fear standard from withholding’s more-likely-than-not standard. The Fifth Amendment’s Due Process Clause supplies the constitutional baseline for a process that can result in removal. Congress may make the remedy discretionary. The President may not repeal the procedure by proclamation.

INA § 212(f)(1)-(2) authorizes a President to suspend the entry of aliens or classes of aliens during an emergency. According to NPR, the Supreme Court will soon consider whether the president can circumvent Congress and existing federal immigration law and unilaterally suspend access to asylum protections. In legal filings, the administration has defended its January 20 proclamation as a necessary protection against an “invasion” at the southern border and as a response to claimed abuse of the asylum system. Critics answer that the president has no unilateral authority to override the protections Congress afforded people fleeing danger. The government cannot use the power to exclude as the power to erase a statutory claim.

The [proclamation issued on January 20](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/DCPD-202500113/pdf/DCPD-202500113.pdf) calls the situation at the southern border an “invasion.” Two lower courts rejected its theory: *Pangea Legal Services v. Trump*, No. 25-10438 (D. Mass.), and *Centro de Trabajadores Unidos v. Trump*, No. 2:25-cv-02914 (C.D. Cal.). The merits briefs and certiorari-stage filings ask the Supreme Court to decide whether an entry proclamation can suspend an asylum category Congress created. The decree has remained largely in force while the litigation proceeds. Rights are changing on the government’s timetable before the merits are settled. That is the shadow-docket problem Stephen Vladeck identified, even though these cases traveled through ordinary litigation rather than the emergency docket.

The apprehensions fell. The denials rose. Those numbers do not describe the same population. [Border Patrol recorded fewer than 99,000 southwest-border apprehensions in fiscal 2026](https://www.cbp.gov/newsroom/national-media-release/under-president-trump-and-secretary-mullin-dhs-announces-lowest), a 95 percent decline from more than 1.8 million a year under President Biden. That is an apprehension count, not an asylum-filing count, and it says nothing by itself about how many hearings occurred. TRAC at Syracuse University reports a 94 percent national asylum denial rate in June, up from roughly 50 percent in 2024. That is a rate among the decisions TRAC reports, not a rate among all applicants, all pending cases, or all cases in which a hearing took place. Changes in the case mix, withdrawals, incomplete filings, and the number of claims reaching final decision can move the percentage without establishing that every judge was forbidden to hear every claim. No honest audit can use 94 percent to prove a categorical hearing ban. The figures do prove that the system produces mass denials. The 94 percent is its output.

The legal mechanism is specific. The [EOIR policy manual](https://www.justice.gov/eoir/policy-manual-eoir/part-V/memoranda/pm) lists nearly 60 administration memoranda, against 30 across President Biden’s four-year term. That establishes density, not illegality. The charge rests on the written termination rule in 8 C.F.R. § 1240.41(b): when the Department of Homeland Security withdraws an application for asylum or withholding of removal, the immigration judge “shall dismiss the application without prejudice.” That is not a direction to ignore proof. It is a direction to erase the live application. ICE attorneys have urged judges to terminate asylum claims as incomplete or unsubstantiated, and DHS has canceled thousands of pending cases. Terminate the application, and the hearing has no subject. The legal operation is the hearing’s disappearance.

The machine also owns the bench. More than a hundred experienced immigration judges were fired or driven out. Nearly 40 percent of the bench left during the administration’s first year; more than half of the sitting judges are Trump appointees. According to [Geoffrey Heeren’s account in the *Yale Law Journal*](https://yalelawjournal.org/essay/deportation-judges-executive-capture-of-the-immigration-court), about two-thirds of the new judges came from homeland-security or military legal backgrounds. About eleven had clear immigrant-defense experience; only one of those lacked DHS or military experience. Nearly all of the replacements carry the administration’s own name: “deportation judges.” Jennifer Peyton, a former assistant chief immigration judge fired by email in July 2025 after nine years on the bench, told NPR that new hires are taught “the shortcuts” rather than “the process of evaluating a case.” Main Street Independent has documented the concentration of public power over sensitive systems in its [OpenAI-NATO cybersecurity coverage](/articles/2026-11-17-openai-makes-advanced-cybersecurity-models-available-to-nato/). Strip the immigration particulars and the design is portable: a claim against the executive, adjudicated by judges the executive hires and fires, under rules that permit the executive to end the claim before a hearing.

Ask what the machine produces.

Z.G. survived documented torture. He belongs to an Indigenous group caught in an armed conflict. He reached the United States through Mexico in February 2025, turned himself in, was detained, and filed an asylum petition. NPR withholds his name at his counsel’s request because he fears retribution.

His attorney, Madhavi Narayanan, worked the case for nine months and produced documents and letters supporting his claim of torture and the risk of violence and death if he returned to Ethiopia. He received a deportation order to Uganda—a country he had never visited and one that does not recognize the conflict he fled as a war—without an evidentiary hearing. Narayanan recalled the judge saying: “OK, I hear everything that you said, but I read this case and I’m not allowed to consider this.”

The government can answer that immigration judges are executive-branch adjudicators and that the President may direct immigration enforcement. That answer confuses managerial authority with power to dictate outcomes. A judge who will not consider the applicant’s evidence is not adjudicating the claim. He is carrying out the policy. Narayanan’s account does not prove that every immigration judge operates under an identical prohibition. It proves what happened to her client.

The government’s merits papers in the third-country cases will lean on *Kleindienst v. Mandel*, 408 U.S. 753 (1972), and *Trump v. Hawaii*, 585 U.S. 667 (2018). *Mandel* says courts will not look behind a “facially legitimate and bona fide reason” for denying entry. *Hawaii* upheld a presidential proclamation restricting entry under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f). Both decisions support deference at the border. Neither holds that a President may repeal an asylum procedure or transfer a claimant without an opportunity to be heard. *Hawaii* itself permitted statutory and due-process review.

The December argument setting belongs to the consolidated third-country cases, *Trump v. Al-Saad*, No. 24-1274, and *Trump v. Hamdan Al-Alfi*, No. 24-1275. Those cases arise under a different statutory track. INA § 217(a)(2), 8 U.S.C. § 1201(a)(2), permits agreements with foreign governments to provide access to asylum procedures abroad. INA § 235(b), 8 U.S.C. § 1225(b), governs expedited removal. Custody pending removal comes from 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(6). The Government’s briefs argue that those powers permit transfer and that courts must defer. The challengers argue that removing people without their consent to countries carrying human-rights abuse allegations, and without a guarantee that they will not be returned to the danger they fled, violates the Fifth Amendment’s Due Process Clause.

The separate detention litigation asks how long “pending removal” may last. Section 1231(a)(6) ties custody to removal but sets no fixed endpoint. *Zadvydas v. Davis*, 533 U.S. 678 (2001), requires the detention period to remain reasonable under the Due Process Clause. Thousands held for months or years while removal proceedings remain open present a constitutional question the proclamation cannot answer.

An honest application would let DHS police the border, remove clearly ineligible claimants efficiently, and put the burden on applicants. It would not let the President suspend a statutory category, rebuild the bench to reach a preferred result, terminate claims before the hearing, or move a claimant without review. Congress wrote a legal process, not an executive option. If Congress wants a different rule, it must write the new rule itself.

The Court need not decide how many border crossings the country can bear. It must decide whether the President may erase the hearing before a claim reaches the bench. Congress wrote asylum into law. The President cannot convert it into a revocable privilege through a proclamation, a memorandum, and a transfer order.

If the Court upholds these measures, it will not be enforcing a statute. It will be supplying the missing executive code: select the judges, narrow the questions, deny the hearing, move the claimant, preserve the removal. The doctrine this Court built around not looking behind the facially legitimate reason leaves files like these closed. That is judicial authorization of a deportation machine with no hearing behind it.
