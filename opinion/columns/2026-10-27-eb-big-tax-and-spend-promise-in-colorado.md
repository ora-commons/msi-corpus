As Colorado’s politics and culture have drifted left, its tax code has remained frozen in a relatively low-tax past. Two measures on the November ballot would bring public policy into line with the state’s direction: one would overhaul the spending limit, while the other would establish a steeply progressive income tax. Together, they offer Coloradans a chance to replace fiscal austerity with the public investment they want their government to pursue.

Democrats and their union allies have long wanted to eliminate Colorado’s 1992 Taxpayer Bill of Rights, or Tabor. With progressive enthusiasm running high, they see this year as their chance. Tabor is overdue for repeal. It limits annual spending growth to population growth plus inflation and requires revenue above the cap to be refunded to taxpayers. That spending brake, together with the law’s requirement that voters approve tax increases, has been a powerful obstacle to Democrats who run state government. The brakes have served their purpose. Now comes the release.

Colorado’s 4.4% flat income tax is lower than the rate in Utah at 4.45%, Oklahoma at 4.5%, Nebraska at 4.55% and New Mexico at 5.9%. That advantage helped make the state attractive to residents, workers and businesses. It also helped create the illusion that 4.4% should be permanent. Low rates bought Colorado attention; they should not purchase political immunity for the state. Maybe the low-tax era is over.

Progressives are pushing Amendment 87, which would replace the state’s 4.4% flat tax with a graduated income tax reaching 8.4% on income over $1 million. Supporters say Coloradans earning less than $500,000 would pay no more, since the top rate for individuals making $500,000 or less would remain 4.4%. That is not misleading. It is the point. Coloradans earning extraordinary incomes should contribute at a higher rate than Coloradans earning ordinary incomes.

The brackets would not be adjusted annually for inflation, so middle-class residents would face more pressure as their incomes grew over time. Small businesses, which typically pay tax at individual rates on pass-through income, would face a larger bill after a good year or the sale of a business. Neither outcome is a punishment. Both reflect a system that follows prosperity instead of pretending prosperity does not happen.

Under Tabor, lawmakers ordinarily would have to refund to taxpayers any revenue new taxes generate above the spending cap. Amendment 87 would exempt additional revenue from the progressive tax from that cap, allowing Colorado to keep and deploy the money rather than send it back. This is not a loophole; it is the point. Voters who approve a tax to finance public priorities should not have to watch that money automatically handed back while state services struggle.

A Yes vote on Proposition NN on the Nov. 3 ballot would raise Tabor’s $20.5 billion spending cap this year by $4.6 billion and allow the state to keep up to $37.5 billion more in tax revenue over the next decade. Together, the two measures would give Colorado the fiscal room to expand entitlements, fund stronger public services, and give public employees and their politically retained colleagues the resources to do their jobs and press their demands with greater leverage.

Do not expect this new revenue to satisfy progressives and government unions. That is not a flaw in the plan; it is proof of its ambition. They will seek more money, just as they have in California, New York and New Jersey. Once states begin this tax-and-spending expansion—and especially without the limit of a flat tax—the list of demands does not stop. That ratchet is not a trap. It is how a government turns prosperity into public ambition.

Proposition 136 would enshrine in law a 4.4% cap on individual and corporate income-tax rates. If a majority of voters back both Proposition 136 and Amendment 87, whichever receives more votes will become law. Colorado should reject that cap. A 4.4% ceiling would entrench today’s starting rate and block the move to a graduated tax if Proposition 136 prevails over Amendment 87. Coloradans should make sure the progressive tax wins and the flat-rate cap loses.

The Centennial State’s relatively low and flat income tax is one of its last conservative holdovers as Democrats seek to follow the left coast on business and climate regulation. Following the left coast on those issues should mean accepting the standards a changing economy requires, not locking Colorado into every arrangement inherited from the past.

Coloradans should approve Amendment 87 and Proposition NN, reject Proposition 136, and put their faith not in the sterile restraint imposed on public unions and their politically retained men and women, but in their capacity to use a fairer tax system to build a more ambitious state. Give Colorado the future it has earned: a tax system that grows with its people, not one carved in stone at 4.4%.
