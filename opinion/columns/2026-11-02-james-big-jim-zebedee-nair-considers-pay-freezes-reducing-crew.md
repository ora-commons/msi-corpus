Ryanair wants workers to pay for a Middle East war they did not start.

Now, I’m just a simple man, but a war always has a bill, and Ryanair is preparing to hand much of it to the people who work on its planes. The airline says it is [considering temporary pay freezes](https://www.msi.org/articles/2026-11-02-ryanair-weighs-pay-freeze-as-fuel-costs-drive-higher-summer-fares/) in some maturing labour agreements, fewer crew members per flight, slower hiring, and more artificial intelligence in operations and back-office work as it confronts an estimated €2bn rise in jet-fuel costs in the financial year ending March 2028.

The pressure is real. Oil stood at about $106 a barrel on Monday after touching a six-month high on Friday, and airlines’ fuel hedges are expiring. Ryanair chief executive Michael O’Leary expects European short-haul fares to rise materially, with ticket prices across the industry increasing by as much as 20%. Customers may have little choice when they need to take a short-haul flight or visit family. Workers have something else: a multiyear wage agreement covering 2027.

A fuel shock does not create a contractual right to reopen that agreement. It creates a reason to bargain honestly.

At my shop, when a parts supplier raises the price of brake rotors, I can quote the customer a new price or change the repair. I cannot quietly freeze the mechanic’s wage by calling the change procurement. If the job no longer pencils out, the owner changes the business. He does not move the loss onto the person holding the wrench. That does not pencil out as Christian discipleship either.

Clausewitz, in *On War*, Book I, Chapter 1, gives the proper order. The trinity joins government, military, and people: government conducts the war, military absorbs the danger, and people meet the bill. Ryanair now proposes to make its own people the final shock absorber.

In Book I, Chapter 7, Clausewitz describes friction as the small impediments and resistances that make war harder than plans imagine. Fuel is one of those impediments. But the fuel bill is not the war’s political meaning. It is one of the material consequences of that meaning, and institutions can hide a political choice inside a cost line. Once war becomes fuel, fuel becomes pricing, and pricing becomes a reason to freeze a worker’s pay. The chain is clear.

The fuel cost is real. A carrier cannot pretend its hedges cover every barrel it burns. The issue is the order of the remedies, not the existence of the cost.

Ryanair is not without means. It posted €1.61bn in post-tax profit for the quarter ending in September, down 6% from the previous year, while passenger numbers grew 5% to 64.5 million. It is reducing annual aircraft deliveries from about 30 to 15 and plans Boeing 737 Max 10 deliveries in January. The Max 10 has about 20% more seats and burns a fifth less fuel than Ryanair’s current fleet.

It has also hedged 80% of its fuel requirements through the end of March at $67 a barrel and 25% of the following 12 months at $95. That is a substantial buffer, but it is not a full one. Beyond that protection, Ryanair can reprice tickets, reduce capacity, take aircraft deliveries more slowly, and make its existing aircraft work harder. Across the industry, [American Airlines has already slowed capacity growth as jet-fuel costs pressure carriers](https://www.msi.org/articles/2026-10-22-american-airlines-slows-capacity-growth-as-jet-fuel-costs-pressure-carriers/). Capacity is a lever management controls. An existing pay agreement is a promise.

Let AI do what AI can do. Let it make the schedules. Extra seats do not pay a mortgage, however.

O’Leary says AI has already increased Ryanair’s capacity by 4%. He separately argues that machine-designed schedules extract more productivity from the airline’s 650-aircraft fleet. If AI lowers overhead, preserves employment, and helps meet the fuel bill without reducing wages, that is stewardship. Put it beside lower crew numbers and pay freezes, though, and the same 4% figure becomes a productivity badge for demanding more work from fewer people while the airline retains the benefit.

Does added capacity mean more flying, fewer hours, fewer jobs, more intensive work, or simply a larger margin? Productivity is a tool. It is not a distribution system until somebody decides who receives its gains.

The labour side has drawn a clear line. Oliver Richardson, a national officer with the Unite union, said the union had not been advised of Ryanair’s proposals. Unite has a multiyear pay agreement covering 2027, and the agreement is neither attached to nor dependent on company performance. Richardson expects it to be honored.

O’Leary says several pay deals come up for renegotiation from March. Those may be different contracts, but a signed agreement is not a line item in a cost review. A multiyear agreement is a promise for a period. Oil changes every day. Contracts are made precisely because the parties accept that prices will move. Ryanair can seek renegotiation when its labour deals come up for review. It cannot declare that a promise has lost its value because the spot price changed.

A company may preserve its margin. It may not call wage compression a neutral response to war. It may pencil out on a spreadsheet. It does not pencil out as public policy.

The conflict behind the fuel bill is the US-Israel war on Iran. Ryanair did not write the war’s policy or choose its strategic objectives. The airline can manage its exposure, but a private balance sheet cannot absolve the public authorities of the war they chose. War cannot be treated as an infinite external shock for one company’s payroll while treated as a finished event in the strategy debates.

Governments must state what they will ask households and workers to absorb, including the war’s purpose, its limits, and the terms of its end. Ryanair must honour the existing agreements and put any productivity or staffing plan in writing, with workload and safety terms that cannot become a blank check. The public must know which cost is being concealed: higher fuel prices, a war that lasts longer, or a frozen wage. One of them belongs in the open.

Ryanair may call the freeze efficiency. On the worker’s side of the ledger, it is still a war bill written on a frozen paycheck. Michael O’Leary wants the price of a war passed from the oil market to the pay packet. A government that chose the war cannot hide in an airline’s balance sheet, and a worker’s pay should not be the cost that disappears.
