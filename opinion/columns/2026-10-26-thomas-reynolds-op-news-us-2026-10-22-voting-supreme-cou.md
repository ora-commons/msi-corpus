The Supreme Court is helping the Republican National Committee suppress student voters.

A student whose school record supplied proof of identity could no longer use a school ID as backup proof when requesting a ballot.

The state’s strongest case rests on the principle the Court invoked. Election rules should not shift close to a vote. Voters and poll workers need stable instructions before Election Day, and a federal judge should not inject fresh uncertainty into election administration during the final stretch. That is the Purcell principle: federal courts ordinarily should not alter election rules close to an election without a strong justification. *Purcell v. Gonzalez*, 549 U.S. 1 (2007).

New Hampshire enacted its election-identification law in the spring and had months to prepare for the November election. By October, state officials were administering the enacted rule. District Judge Joseph Laplante reconsidered the dispute earlier that month and enjoined guidance from Republican New Hampshire Secretary of State David Scanlan. His reconsideration order held that the guidance went beyond New Hampshire’s election statute and put it on hold. The First Circuit then declined to stay that order. The Court had a serious reason to ask whether a late judicial order had altered the operative guidance without the required showing.

The statute is New Hampshire Revised Statutes Annotated 60:16. Its enumerated list of acceptable identification documents no longer includes student identification. The statute also contains a residual provision allowing “any other evidence that reasonably establishes that it is more likely than not that the person is who they claim to be.” Scanlan’s voter-identification guidance treated a student ID as qualifying “other evidence.” Laplante concluded that the guidance went beyond the statute and stopped officials from relying on it. (Coalition for Open Democracy v. Scanlan, Memorandum and Order on Reconsideration (D.N.H. Oct. 2026); Office of the New Hampshire Secretary of State, Voter Identification Guidance (2026).)

The Coalition for Open Democracy pursued the claim through 42 U.S.C. § 1983, the federal cause of action for state officials’ violations of federal constitutional rights. Its First Amendment theory asked whether New Hampshire’s identification rule imposed a severe burden on voting and, if it did, whether the state justified the burden through legitimate election interests and appropriate tailoring. Its equal-protection theory separately addressed the age-based classification. The published First Circuit decision in the underlying litigation supplies the governing benchmark for the voting-burden question. *Coalition for Open Democracy v. Scanlan*, 75 F.4th 361 (1st Cir. 2023). The Court’s emergency order decided none of those claims.

The procedural sequence matters. The First Circuit denied the state’s request to stay Laplante’s reconsideration order. New Hampshire then filed an emergency application with the Supreme Court. The application asked the Court for relief from the lower-court order concerning Scanlan’s guidance, and the Court granted it in an unsigned order issued on October 22. (Coalition for Open Democracy v. Scanlan, First Circuit Order (Oct. 2026); Coalition for Open Democracy v. Scanlan, Application for Stay (U.S. Oct. 2026).)

The unsigned order did not hold Scanlan’s guidance substantively lawful. It did not invalidate RSA 60:16. It did not amend the list of acceptable documents or announce that student IDs could never count as other evidence. Instead, the order treated election proximity as dispositive and faulted the district court for offering no reason to depart from the timing principle. “Here, the District Court did not point to anything that would justify deviating from that principle,” the Court wrote. (Coalition for Open Democracy v. Scanlan, Emergency Order (U.S. Oct. 22, 2026).)

That reasoning skipped the distinction between changing an election rule and correcting a misleading description of one. Purcell does not make every judicial correction of election administration a forbidden late alteration. If Laplante held that Scanlan’s guidance conflicted with RSA 60:16, the question was whether his injunction removed an official instruction that election workers otherwise would have followed. If he instead changed the operative rule governing identification, the state had a stronger Purcell claim. The unsigned order did not say which was which.

The Court’s own application record put that distinction before it. New Hampshire had enacted the statute months earlier. Scanlan had issued guidance interpreting the statute’s residual provision. Laplante had entered an order affecting the use of that guidance during the final stretch before the election. Those facts supplied the familiar Purcell concern: a court should not permit a late change in election practice without a sufficiently compelling explanation. But they also required the state to identify the reliance interests created by its guidance and the district court to explain why correcting an inaccurate instruction would itself alter the operative rules. The order made proximity answer both questions.

Justice Ketanji Brown Jackson’s dissent identified what the majority omitted. She wrote that she would not disturb “a clearly justifiable federal court order issued to ensure that the public receives accurate information about a State’s electoral processes.” She was not defending judicial election rewriting in the abstract. She was defending the lower court’s effort to prevent officials from receiving inaccurate instructions about how identification was being administered. The majority did not explain why that interest yielded when the Court granted the state’s emergency request. (Coalition for Open Democracy v. Scanlan, Jackson, J., dissenting (Oct. 22, 2026).)

The assertion that the Court removed an “ordinary route” through the statute was wrong. The statute remained intact. The Court did not repeal an identification category or resolve the Coalition’s constitutional claim. The immediate loss of backup proof came from the interim enforcement posture created by the litigation and the Court’s emergency intervention, not from a judicial holding that students had no legal path to identification. What the Court decided was which instruction could operate before ordinary appellate review reached the dispute.

The procedural record identifies who sought the result. The Republican National Committee backed New Hampshire’s emergency request. That support does not establish partisan motive; the state filed the application. It does identify the beneficiary of the order. The RNC wanted the lower-court intervention blocked before the election, and the Court blocked it through an unsigned emergency order without oral argument, full merits briefing, or a signed opinion explaining the distinction between changing election law and correcting misleading election guidance.

An honest application of Purcell would have required the Court to identify the operative election instruction, explain whether Laplante had altered that instruction or corrected an erroneous description of the statute, and then determine whether the state’s showing justified emergency relief. The unsigned order did none of that. It treated the calendar as the answer.

The Court did not invalidate New Hampshire’s law. It did not adjudicate the Coalition’s § 1983 claim. It gave the RNC its emergency election-law victory and left students without the backup proof Scanlan’s guidance had supplied.

Justice Jackson preserved the question the majority left unanswered: when a late order changes what voters and election workers understand the law to require, accuracy cannot disappear merely because the calendar has turned. The shadow docket made the RNC’s preferred timing the final answer.
