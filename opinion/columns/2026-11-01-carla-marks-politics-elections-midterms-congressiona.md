Megan Wingfield had $23 for gas, while Brett Guthrie raised nearly $6 million, and America’s $12 billion midterm campaign is not choosing the next Congress; it is selling the right to remain visible long enough to be chosen. [Main Street Independent’s reporting on the 2026 midterms](https://mainstreetindependent.com/articles/2026-11-01-shoestring-candidates-contend-in-a-12-billion-midterm-election/) puts the imbalance plainly: AdImpact estimates that candidates and their backers will spend nearly $12 billion. Kentucky mechanic Megan Wingfield raised $4,000 in her primary and pawned her power tools. New Jersey challenger Damon Galdo raised just over $16,000. Donald Norcross’s campaign had $2.4 million in mid-October, according to Federal Election Commission filings. The totals show the size of the auction. The candidate-by-candidate gaps show who can keep buying attention inside it.

The optimistic half is true. A candidate without money is not without power. Wingfield won a crowded primary with 41 percent of the vote and received a $5,000 check from the United Auto Workers. Galdo’s debate against a cardboard cutout put his questions into the feed. Local volunteers can shake doors, organize meetings, recruit voters and force an opponent to spend money defending the seat.

Political arguments travel. Campaign money travels farther.

Money buys capacity. Staff. Travel. Advertising. Data. A candidate who cannot afford gas cannot knock doors, and no serious person should hear “shoestring campaign” and pretend money is useless. But money does not guarantee victory. It buys the head start.

Calling these “shoestring candidates” makes a financing system look like a shortage of grit. The budget comes first. Name recognition comes next. Staff, repetition and the freedom to make mistakes come next. By the time voters compare candidates, the campaigns have already compared their balance sheets.

Guthrie can put his opponent’s name before voters repeatedly while Wingfield can buy a little gas. Galdo can afford one billboard. The ballot is supposed to choose among viable candidates. The cash market chooses viability first.

Money does not buy a vote. It buys the list before the vote.

Wingfield’s experience exposes the circular rule: no money, no endorsement; no endorsement, no money. Progressive groups would not even look at a candidate who had not raised $100,000. Those gatekeepers set the threshold. Then failure to clear it becomes evidence that the candidate lacks support.

That is not discovery. It is selection.

And this is not one side’s machine. [Democrats raised more than Republicans in 27 of 35 Senate races](https://mainstreetindependent.com/articles/2026-10-19-democrats-outraise-republicans-in-27-of-35-senate-races/). One side may have bought the larger megaphone in this cycle while the other bought the smaller one. Outside money does the same work at the Senate level, where [the Senate Leadership Fund adds $8 million to defend Ricketts’ Nebraska seat](https://mainstreetindependent.com/articles/2026-10-13-senate-leadership-fund-adds-8m-to-defend-ricketts-nebraska-seat/). Two sides can each be well funded while ordinary voters remain spectators at both. Financial parity between coalitions is not political equality.

The premise deserves demolition. Private command over money should not determine whose arguments reach the public square. I am not against money. I am against concentrated money buying political command.

Guthrie can buy repetition until familiarity becomes recognition. Galdo’s $2,100 billboard was not the moral equivalent of no billboard. Wingfield must turn a one-bedroom apartment, a $5,000 UAW check for lawn signs and a banner, and whatever help friends can provide into campaign machinery. The richer candidate does not have to win the argument because he can buy the repetition. Money buys repetition. Volume becomes perception.

Ruby ElBasha came out of retirement to help Dione Sims run her Texas campaign. Contractors, family members and volunteers fill other gaps. That is admirable. But relying on retirement and friendship because nobody financed the basic work is not a civic model. It is public capacity pushed onto private lives. Volunteerism is fine. A system that makes it compulsory is not.

Some spending is waste. A yard sign in a landfill proves nothing. The problem is not efficiency. It is unequal amplification. One candidate can purchase enough repetition to make an answer sound like the only answer, while another spends the same amount merely trying to enter the conversation.

The burden is simple: if unequal spending is merely political information, show why a dollar from one backer should buy more attention than a dollar from another. If that cannot be shown, money is buying power. The balance sheet is not a neutral scoreboard.

So what do we build instead? Not another public check for the parties. A public option for political voice.

Start with automatic public matching for small donations. When an individual gives $10 or $20 to a ballot-qualified candidate, a public fund can match it under transparent limits, with disclosed per-donor caps and limits on bundled contributions by connected donors. Donors still choose the candidate. The public is not selecting the winner. This makes many modest owners of political voice instead of one grand proprietor.

Then guarantee every ballot-qualified candidate equal public media: free broadcast time and a common digital channel where voters can hear the candidates without purchasing every impression from private platforms. The model should resemble a library, not a franchise. Everyone contributes. Every candidate gets access to the shared room. Voters decide what to do once they enter.

Public campaign grants should follow from the same principle. A candidate who qualifies for the ballot can receive a basic operating grant, with stronger support for primary challengers so they can survive long enough to compete. Private money can still buy advertising. It should not buy the only stage.

Public money also needs connective tissue. Build campaign cooperatives out of unions, co-ops, neighborhood groups and local party organizations. Members pool recurring money. They share field staff. They buy media together. One member gets one vote. The organization belongs to the people funding it rather than to the person whose check happened to be largest.

The prototype is already visible. The UAW sent Wingfield $5,000. Ruby ElBasha came out of retirement to advise a candidate she believed in. Wingfield’s campaign ran from a one-bedroom apartment. Galdo’s crew staged a meet-the-candidate event over trays of macaroni-and-cheese and fried chicken. What is missing is not generosity. It is durable financing.

It will be harder here because campaigns are candidate-centered, and primaries demand cash before durable party organizations can supply the muscle. Donors fill the resulting gap. That is why public matching cannot stand alone. Money without organized voice is just patronage with better stationery.

America’s elections are run state by state and then locally, with rules that change across state lines. National parties are weak. Political muscle is donor-built. No single institution carries political participation into every county, so campaigns lean on whatever civic capacity they can find: a consultant who came out of retirement, a friend who makes websites, a fifteen-year-old aide, a spouse who looked on. Public money must be paired with local organizers, neutral forums and durable civic groups. Unions, co-ops, public libraries and neighborhood organizations can help people meet candidates and compare claims without becoming substitute donors. The law must make sure public money does not become another name for private wealth.

The institutional template already exists in other forms. North Dakota runs a state-owned bank. A state-owned bank does not make the public a party; a public campaign arena need not either. If courts strike public money down, the advocates lose and the plutocracy wins. Either way, the burden falls on whoever wants to keep private wealth in charge of political voice. The claim that the public cannot own a megaphone is a lobbying request in a nicer jacket.

Start with Kentucky: a mechanic, two dogs, a cat, a Jeep and $23. Give her a recurring small-donor account, a union or neighborhood coalition that can pool resources, and public matching for the contributions she receives. Put that money back in the gas tank.

Then the cash buys fuel and media, not the menu. The $23 buys gasoline instead of disqualification. Private money can compete inside the room; it should not own the room. The alternative to a $12 billion auction is not a campaign financed by pawned tools. It is democracy in which the people can hear the arguments without first bankrupting the candidate.

The point of a campaign was never to make the candidate rich. It was to let the people decide.
