The Pentagon is burning through America's missile defenses while denying the country is running short.

Sixty to seventy Patriot interceptors burned to stop about twenty Iranian ballistic missiles. Iran still hit aircraft on the ground at Jordan’s Muwaffaq Salti Air Base. That is the war in one ratio: three or four of America’s most expensive defensive rounds for every incoming missile, followed by damage to the very aircraft those rounds were supposed to protect.

No American troops died this time. That is not the same thing as safety. It is the kind of victory that quietly bankrupts the country that wins it.

The Pentagon’s inspector general put the truth on paper in one sentence: the war “has resulted in strategic inventory shortfalls and revealed industrial base bottlenecks for munitions resupply.” Strip away the government language and it says this: America is firing missiles faster than American factories can replace them.

By mid-July, the United States had expended roughly 1,700 Patriot interceptors, more than 200 THAAD antimissile rounds, and another 150 Navy Standard Missiles. A single recent engagement at Muwaffaq Salti consumed what the United States typically burns in a full week. The number is not an abstraction. Every interceptor is a manufactured object, paid for with public money, shipped to a base, loaded into a launcher, and gone in a few seconds.

The press conference says otherwise. Pentagon spokesman Sean Parnell called reports of depletion “completely false.” President Trump said American stocks are large and production is ramping up. Adm. Brad Cooper, the head of Central Command, told CBS he is “not concerned at all” about interceptor shortages.

These men cannot all be telling the truth. The inspector general has already answered them.

Lockheed Martin produced a record 620 Patriot interceptors in 2025 and has set a goal of roughly 2,000 a year. A goal is not a stockpile. A record annual output of 620 is not an answer to a war that has already burned through roughly 1,700 Patriots, more than 200 THAAD rounds, and 150 Navy missiles. The Pentagon is describing the barrel of a gun and calling it a well.

Lockheed Martin will collect public money on every one of those missiles. The company will call the production increase a national-security necessity. The cabinet will call the purchases protection. Meanwhile, working people are told there is no money for a bridge, a hospital, a school, or a functioning town. The same government that cannot keep every rural emergency room open can find hundreds of millions for interceptors that vanish over the desert before breakfast.

That is not a technical problem. It is a choice about whose losses count.

The troops at Muwaffaq Salti are not chess pieces. They are working-class kids sent into a meat grinder built by men who will never stand beneath the missile. Their families will carry the cost of every deployment, every funeral, every damaged aircraft, and every year of care. The contractors will send an invoice.

The consequences do not stay in Jordan. U.S. inventories of PAC-3 Patriots and ATACMS missiles in Europe are, by some American officials’ own assessment, beyond critical. That reaches from the Middle East to Korea and the Baltic. [South Korea is already confronting the same interceptor drain threatening its own air-defense picture](/articles/2026-08-09-iran-conflict-depletes-u-s-interceptors-raising-south-korea-air-defense-concerns/). Ukraine, which has been counting on Western interceptors to defend its cities from Russian airstrikes, is being told to wait.

Gulf allies are scrambling for interceptors of their own. Israel even shipped one of its closely guarded Iron Dome systems to the United Arab Emirates, with troops to operate it. That is what allied confidence looks like when it has run out of options.

Iran is not standing still. It has been changing missile varieties, trajectories, maneuvers, and warhead designs across successive strikes to see what breaks through. Officials say Iran acquired high-resolution satellite imagery of the Jordanian base from Chinese entities before the attack. The recent strike used warheads that separate into multiple projectiles during descent. One incoming missile becomes several separate problems. The defender must decide how many expensive rounds to spend before the warhead reaches the runway.

Iran has also dug missiles out of underground facilities that American strikes buried early in the war and resumed production from stockpiled parts. Three U.S. service members were killed in an earlier strike at [the same Jordanian base](/articles/2026-07-22-iran-missile-strike-on-jordan-base-kills-three-us-service-members/). This time, Iranian missiles hit aircraft. The next lesson will be written in the lives of whoever is standing near them.

The administration’s answer is to deny the math and keep firing. In late July, a debate over munitions stockpiles helped persuade President Trump to abandon an intensive two-week strike campaign against Iran. That restraint was sold as diplomacy. It was inventory. The same shortage that pulled strikes off the table is now pulling interceptors out of Europe, the Gulf, and Ukraine.

There is no mystery here. Iran is trying to make the American presence in the region too costly to maintain. It is succeeding because every exchange leaves the United States with fewer defensive rounds and Iran with more information about how to defeat the next defense.

The Pentagon’s house position cannot be both well-supplied and strategically short. One of those claims is false. Until the country is told which one, American troops in Jordan and every allied capital depending on American protection are being asked to stand beneath an emptying shelf.

The troops pay for that shelf with their lives. Lockheed Martin gets paid to refill it. And the towns that raised those troops are left asking why there is always money for the next missile, but never enough for the places those young people came from.
