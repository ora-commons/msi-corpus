Trump’s loan caps are handing students to private lenders.

Victoria Chasin was accepted to medical school and expected to borrow $50,000 a year through the federal student-loan program. Then she discovered how much more she would need.

Her father cosigned a $54,000 private loan. Chasin secured a 3 percent interest rate partly by making $25 monthly payments while in school. By private-lending standards, that is a bargain. But she still worries about taking out another private loan during every year of medical school—and about never knowing what rates will cost her.

That is the actual policy working as designed.

The Trump administration’s new federal loan caps took effect July 1, as [Main Street Independent reported amid a widening debate over their effect on tuition](/articles/2026-06-28-new-graduate-loan-limits-take-effect-july-1-as-economists-debate-tuition-impact/). Graduate students can borrow $20,500 or $50,000 annually, depending on their field. Parents can borrow $20,000 a year for their children’s undergraduate education. Federal borrowing is also subject to a $257,500 lifetime limit, including undergraduate borrowing.

The Education Department says the restrictions are intended in part to combat ballooning college costs and keep debt from following graduates into retirement.

They ration federal credit. They do not reduce tuition.

A cap on the federal loan is not a cap on the cost of attending. A student whose education costs $60,000 does not suddenly enroll for $50,000. The shortfall moves to private student lending, where eligibility turns on credit, income and family backing rather than enrollment alone.

The kitchen-table version of this rule is simple: Washington lowers the loan, not the tuition.

Washington capped the loans. It did not cap the debt.

Federal loans still account for more than 90 percent of outstanding student debt. That makes this not ordinary consumer demand responding to ordinary market conditions. It is demand manufactured by government policy. That is the giveaway in its purest form: Washington restricts the public option, and private lenders inherit the demand.

And private lenders are waiting.

South Carolina Student Loan has seen graduate lending increase by between 300 and 400 percent from the previous year. The average amount requested has jumped from $19,000 to $34,000. Sallie Mae, one of the largest private student lenders, projects a 12 to 14 percent increase in private-loan originations this year. ELFI, SouthEast Bank’s student-lending division, has received tens of thousands of prequalification applications and expanded its staff.

Washington shrank the federal channel and seeded the private one.

The public loan system has an advantage private student lending cannot match: access is tied to being a student, not to being creditworthy. Federal loans generally require no credit check, carry fixed rates regardless of credit history and include income-based repayment plans and potential forgiveness for qualifying public-service work. The federal graduate rate is 8.07 percent this year.

That is not cheap. But it is a known number.

The new rules tell students who need more to go looking for a number determined by private lenders.

Chasin’s 3 percent rate is not the private market working. It is a family balance sheet replacing a federal guarantee. Students without a cosigner or a strong income can face private lenders’ other answer: no.

Some families do get a bargain. Marni Sassaman, who works in sales management for a wine company, chose private loans for her son’s education because her strong credit secured a rate below this year’s 9.07 percent Parent PLUS rate.

That is a real benefit. It is also the indictment.

The new system rewards households with credit to trade and forces students without that leverage to compete for private money under terms they do not control. Private student lending does not replace a public guarantee. It divides borrowers by the size of their family balance sheet.

The private market is not a substitute for federal lending. It is a different system, with a different definition of who deserves an opportunity.

That produces a brutal form of privatization: better-connected borrowers receive private credit on favorable terms, while everyone else is left with less federal money, higher uncertainty or no loan at all. The reform intended to make education more affordable has created a new hierarchy based on credit scores.

Anne Helen Petersen’s *Can’t Even* is useful here because she treats burnout as a structural condition rather than a personal failure. A low credit score is not a failure of discipline. A missing cosigner is not a failure of planning. They are the private credit system’s answers to a public policy that withdrew the guarantee.

Even South Carolina Student Loan, which introduced forbearance for medical students in residency, still lowered its credit-score requirement to 600 for students in healthcare fields; students typically need 670 or above to qualify for a loan. Even with that lower threshold, the lender can still say no.

Not everyone can qualify. Not everyone can find a willing cosigner. Not everyone can make $25 monthly payments on $54,000 before training has even begun. Private repayment options may be less flexible, and private rates and terms can be more unpredictable. Access to the private credit filling the funding gap now depends on an underwriting decision made by a bank.

Read Taylor Swift’s “You’re On Your Own, Kid” as the title line this policy writes across the borrower’s family: on your own, kid. The federal cap turns that lyric into underwriting policy. Washington reduces its promise; the family must make the promise.

The financing gap does not politely wait for private lenders to catch up. Education does not wait for a cosigner. Credit may not follow students into the classroom.

Physician-assistant groups say [the new caps are already deterring prospective students](/articles/2026-06-25-new-federal-loan-caps-deter-would-be-physician-assistants-groups-say/).

The consequences are not theoretical. Democratic-led states have [sued over the caps](/articles/2026-05-19-democratic-led-states-sue-over-trump-caps-on-federal-student-loans/), while students are already borrowing private money for the education the federal program no longer fully covers.

If the goal is to reduce student debt, rationing federal credit is the wrong instrument. Lowering the price of education means pressuring colleges to charge less and expanding access to aid. Instead, the administration lowered the amount of public borrowing students can obtain and transferred the burden to families shopping in a less forgiving market.

Chasin’s $54,000 loan at 3 percent may look like a triumph. But a system in which government tells students how much public credit they may receive, then hands the rest to lenders who decide who is worth lending to, has not solved the cost of education. It has made private credit necessary.

That is not reform. That is a carve-out. Washington capped the loans. It did not cap the debt. It reduced federal underwriting, handed students to private lenders and made family credit the price of admission. The risk did not vanish; it shifted from federal taxpayers to households. Students are left holding the bag.
