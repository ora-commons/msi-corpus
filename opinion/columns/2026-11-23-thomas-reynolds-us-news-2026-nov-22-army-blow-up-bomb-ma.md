The Army will poison Middletown with lead-containing particles.

The Army has a serious case. In March, staff at the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant found several dozen barrels of degraded lead azide, an explosive used in munitions production. Crystallization left the material unstable, and officials said falling temperatures would increase the risk of an uncontrolled explosion. The Army assembled experts in munitions chemistry, engineering, and environmental safety. According to EPA documents, officials considered neutralizing and decomposing the drums’ contents with other chemicals. The Army says handling the barrels would increase the probability of an accidental, fatal detonation. Its plan calls for detonating several tons by Tuesday. A court order leaving unstable explosives in place could increase the danger. That is the strongest form of the Army’s case.

Nearly 400 people live in Middletown, and thousands more in surrounding areas could be affected. Residents were told to stay inside, close windows and doors, bring pets and livestock indoors, and clean cars, windowsills, and outdoor surfaces afterward. The Army’s advisory states: “The detonation will result in an air plume with some lead-containing particles which will disperse in the air and deposit on the ground.” The same advisory states, “Lead is toxic to humans and animals.” That guidance assumes contamination. It does not prove that the public will be protected.

The Army’s plan shifts the hazard from its plant to the public. The Army gets its chosen remedy; the public gets a lead-bearing plume, surface exposure, and cleanup. This is not public participation in a safety decision. Residents received shelter and cleanup instructions rather than a decision on whether the plan should proceed.

The EPA had approved an emergency permit earlier this month. On Thursday, the Eighth Circuit Court of Appeals put that permit on hold at the request of the Iowa Environmental Council, Sierra Club, and Earthjustice. In a [Saturday filing to the court](/articles/2026-11-22-army-seeks-emergency-provisions-to-detonate-iowa-chemicals-despite-stay/), government lawyers said the Army planned to invoke emergency provisions and proceed despite the hold. They invoked the Resource Conservation and Recovery Act, the federal hazardous-waste law, including the imminent-and-substantial-endangerment authority of 42 U.S.C. § 7003(c). They said an explosive or munitions emergency-response specialist had determined that the material posed an immediate threat, so the Army could proceed without a permit.

The lawyers wrote, “To protect its personnel and the surrounding communities, the Army needs to take swift action.” They added, “Protection of human health and the environment demand action now, not delay.” That is the strongest legal form of the Army’s position.

The statute changes regulatory procedures; it does not silently vacate a judicial order. An emergency application is an expedited request for immediate court relief. The Army could ask the Eighth Circuit to cancel or narrow the hold on the same expert record. Its lawyers instead told the court that the Army would proceed despite the pause. Unless the court resolves the conflict, the Army cannot treat the hold as optional. An emergency exception is a limited carve-out from ordinary safeguards when delay magnifies danger. It is not a license for the Army to make the governing question disappear by announcing the answer.

The Army’s own sequence makes its premise harder to sustain. The Army says colder weather increases the risk and delay makes the material more dangerous. It also moved the operation from Friday to Monday after an outpouring of public concern and now plans to detonate by Tuesday. If each cold day makes the material more dangerous, the Army must explain why postponement did not cross the threshold it now claims makes the permit hold irrelevant. A safety case that grows stronger as the Army’s own delay lengthens does not explain itself.

The environmental groups’ objection is serious. In their petition, they said officials had not fully considered alternatives and had withheld information the public needed to evaluate them. The Army’s answer is serious too. Officials say handling the drums with other chemicals would increase the probability of an accidental, fatal detonation. Both propositions can be true. A dangerous handling method does not make every less-dangerous method illegal, and public ignorance does not convert the Army’s preferred solution into the only lawful one.

Thien Chau, an attorney at Earthjustice, said the Army “seemed more concerned over the weekend with finding a way to sidestep the judicial process than on meeting the public’s demands for greater transparency, notice to affected communities, and far stronger protection of people, property, and the environment from serious harm.” The court cannot test the Army’s comparative risk judgment without the alternatives analysis and the evidentiary record supporting it. An emergency finding can justify a narrow departure from ordinary procedure, but it does not remove the court’s responsibility to test the statutory condition and the permit requirement.

The lawful emergency path would put the Army’s plume analysis, its review of neutralization and decomposition, the projected deposition area, and its cleanup protocol before the Eighth Circuit. It would ask for narrow relief tied to the expert record, with independent testing for lead after the operation. An appellate pause cannot become a self-executing license for the Army.

The Army can call this emergency action. The sequence shows what the plan would do. The Army’s plant held the material; its plan would turn that storage problem into Middletown’s toxic exposure. The appellate pause is the legal check on a plan that shifts the Army’s danger onto its neighbors.

An emergency can justify swift action. It cannot justify the Army deciding alone whether Middletown residents must inhale lead. Its lawyers treated the Eighth Circuit’s hold as optional.
