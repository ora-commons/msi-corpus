A federal complaint filed on 2 October in Chicago names McDonald's, alleges its pricing platform amounts to algorithmic price-fixing among independent franchisees, and asks for class-action relief. The chain says the tool is optional and that franchisees — not software — set menu prices. Both positions will get tested. The bigger story is what the case unlocks: a once-in-a-generation chance to redraw the contract between a brand, its operators, and the customers who already feel the math at the register.

Consider the timing. According to a fact sheet the company itself published in 2024, the average price of a McDonald's menu item climbed about 40% between 2019 and 2024. That same year, 2019, McDonald's acquired Dynamic Yield, the AI company at the center of the dispute. Five years of price action, under one umbrella, with one set of data flowing back to a single platform. The legal theory is straightforward: when thousands of independent operators consult a shared system that aggregates their nonpublic sales and price data, the antitrust exemption that lets franchises coordinate on branding, training, and supply evaporates on the price side. The remedy is what is genuinely new — and what makes the case a doorway rather than a barricade.

The 2023 viral story of an $18 Big Mac meal at a Connecticut McDonald's already previewed where this road ends. The franchise owner there alleged that the AI pricing tool was the one that suggested the eighteen-dollar tag. The story hit the national press, and the day before the suit landed, the company issued a statement denying it used dynamic pricing and recasting the tool as informational only. A separate investigation found that some operators had been pressured to use the tools and to log their deviations. A spokesperson called that reporting "speculative and uninformed." None of that is reassuring. It is, however, clarifying: the practice is widespread enough to litigate, and the company's defense rests on the word "optional."

This is the first real test of whether the franchise model — the engine of American fast food for seventy years — can survive the algorithmic era without a pricing layer that crosses antitrust lines. It will not be the last. Earlier this year, [California drivers sued gas-station operators over AI-powered price coordination](/articles/2026-06-22-california-drivers-sue-gas-stations-over-ai-powered-price-fixing/), and [state lawmakers began filing bills to constrain personalized pricing as consumer-data tools spread](/articles/2026-06-03-state-lawmakers-move-to-ban-personalized-pricing-as-consumer-data-tools-expand/). The McDonald's case is the largest, most visible front in a multi-front war over who gets to set the number on the menu board.

What opens up, if the plaintiffs win or settle on a structural remedy? A franchise economy where pricing is genuinely local. Michael Thomas, the DeKalb, Illinois, customer described in the complaint as "price conscious," noticed the same thing Chukwama Okeke, the Manhattan smoothie buyer, said out loud in the financial district: the same combo cost fifteen dollars on one block and nine dollars a train ride away in Crown Heights. Beatriz Milander, ordering a snack wrap and iced coffee in the financial district for $11.51, said prices overall had "inched up" in the last year. These are not isolated complaints. They are the demand signal a transparent, owner-led pricing regime would meet. Operators who already price by neighborhood — and there are many — would no longer be the exception. They would be the model.

Lindsay Owens of the Groundwork Collaborative, author of a recent book on the topic, has tracked at least ninety pieces of algorithmic-pricing legislation filed in statehouses this year. Ninety is not a fringe number. It is the legislative class of 2026, and it is heading toward a market that has not yet decided what algorithmic coordination means. The McDonald's suit, whatever its docket number, is the case that will define the doctrine.

A franchisee who reads the complaint and acts first — pulling the AI suggestions off the menu board, publishing a local pricing policy, running the numbers on store-level demand instead of platform-level optimization — does not just avoid liability. They buy the right to be the franchise system of the next decade. The brand gets the headlines. The operator who leads on transparency gets the customer. That is the door the lawsuit just opened, and the people who walk through it first will not be the people in Chicago.
