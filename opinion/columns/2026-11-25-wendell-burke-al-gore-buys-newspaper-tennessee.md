Al Gore’s purchase may keep the Carthage Courier alive; that would not be a rescue. It would be a verdict. The [Associated Press reports](https://apnews.com/article/al-gore-buys-newspaper-tennessee-6d86ec5239dee664d312aac30b38dde5) that the Lake family, which has operated the Carthage weekly for five decades, plans to sell the paper to the 78-year-old former vice president in coming weeks. The terms are undisclosed, his role in operations remains unclear, and he says the purchase will preserve an independent local voice for Carthage and Smith County. [Main Street Independent’s own report](https://mainstreetindependent.com/articles/2026-11-25-al-gore-to-buy-the-carthage-courier-in-his-tennessee-hometown/) records the same unresolved questions. Until the charter, the money and the operating authority are public, this is a sale, not yet community ownership.

Give the purchase its honest due, because the alternative to this buyer was not a purer form of ownership. Weekly papers in counties of 20,000 do not draw bidding wars; they draw silence. The Lake family publicly named one buyer, a man of the county who wanted the paper kept in the county. Gore has earned a hearing rather than a sneer. He spent six months as an Army reporter in Vietnam and then worked as an investigative reporter at *The Tennessean* before entering politics. He is right that a free, independent local press is irreplaceable for the enduring health of a community.

History is no disqualification. Senators and governors have owned papers, and even President Warren Harding carried one in his business portfolio. Tim Franklin, chair of local news at Northwestern University’s Medill School, said Carthage residents may have “some healthy skepticism” about a politician owning their paper, while adding that he would be surprised if the Courier became a party mouthpiece. The warning is fair. Ralph Nader helped launch a local newspaper in Winsted, Connecticut, in 2023; a national media company bought it within months. Gore’s stated intention points in the other direction. But intention is not an institution. The institution is what outlives its founder.

I have never seen Carthage. I recognize it anyway.

I will not pretend the old country paper was a priesthood. In my own county, the *Adams County Press* was edited by Solon Pierce, who was also a lawyer, a state senator, and the loudest railroad advocate in the courthouse. The paper argued the county’s fights because its editor stood inside them. That was not the corruption of local journalism. It was the condition of it. Everyone knew whose paper it was, where he lived, and when he could be cornered at the fair. A paper like that can tell a county what has happened before the national version of the county has decided what it means.

The corruption came later, wearing the face of owners no one in the county had ever met, papers held as tax assets and trimmed by column inches until the lights went out. This autumn, Block Communications [put the Toledo Blade up for sale and warned of a year-end shutdown](/articles/2026-09-08-block-communications-puts-toledo-blade-up-for-sale-warns-of-year-end-shutdown/). My county knows the slower version of that ending. Five mastheads once served this one courthouse, an *Independent* going back to 1858 among them. Two of them fed my parents’ kitchen table when I was a boy. One survivor is left, stitched together out of the rest. The parish, the hall, the newspaper: the inventory does not grow.

Now the part my side does not say out loud.

We were the movement of local control. We wore the word community smooth, defended the little platoons at every convention, and for forty years watched the institutions the word named—the county weekly chief among them—consolidate into fewer and farther hands. We blessed it as scale, called the stripping of a paper a turnaround, and mailed the subscribers a national newsletter written four hundred miles from any county line.

Conservatism claims to be the discipline of conserving.

Conserve what, exactly?

The man we ran against in 2000 is performing the conservative act in his hometown while the movement that owns the rhetoric watches from the op-ed pages. The grief here is not that Al Gore is buying the Courier. It is that he is what is left of the market for memory: a rich man who remembers the place.

Name what the purchase actually is. One man, seventy-eight years old, will hold the record of a county’s life, and the announcement that names him answers none of the questions that follow him home. No members are named. No board of county residents is promised. No annual meeting appears in anything the public has been shown, and when his office and the paper were asked, they answered nothing. Perhaps decent arrangements are still coming; nothing announced lets Smith County hold him to any of them.

Ownership alone does not answer to a county. A conscience is better than a hedge fund’s spreadsheet, but it is not the same thing as an institution. Consciences do not pass down. Wills do not carry intentions. A local name on the masthead is not enough. Local ownership can disappear through a transaction unless somebody local has the authority to say no. And a free press cannot be made safe by the good intentions of its owner. It must be safe by design.

Edmund Burke called society “a partnership between those who are living, those who are dead, and those who are to be born.” A weekly paper belongs to that inheritance. It is a mediating institution between household and state, where the events of a county are not reduced to a market test conducted elsewhere. Five decades of the Lakes, generations of names in the courthouse records and the obituaries, and the children not yet born: this is a common record before it is a commercial asset.

Property in such a paper is legitimate and may be necessary. But property answers to the dignity of the people who depend on it and to the earth given for all. Title alone is not local control. If one famous person’s fortune becomes the paper’s balance sheet and its political voice, local control has merely acquired a new master.

A trust like that cannot rest on one man’s goodwill.

Goodwill is a lease.

When the estate settles, the paper goes wherever estates go. The people of Winsted can explain how that goes.

I will not accept the opposite superstition, that a local paper can be saved only by a state guarantee or a national chain. Concentrated ownership’s cure cannot be more concentrated ownership. A subsidy can preserve the ink while moving editorial judgment to a distant capital or bureau; a national chain can preserve the masthead while moving judgment to a distant owner. Public money for local news can hand a town’s voice to whoever signs the checks in Washington. A paper that lives on an appropriation answers to the paymaster meanwhile.

Subsidiarity asks the better question: what is the smallest institution able to perform the task, and how can that institution remain answerable to the place it serves? That is the principle Pius XI wrote in *Quadragesimo Anno* §79, ninety-five years ago: it is a grave evil for higher bodies to take over work that lesser organizations can do. A county can tell its own story, and when the bureau absorbs the function, the town loses more than money. It loses the habit of speaking for itself. A town’s story is too important to be a line item in anybody’s budget, however friendly.

No. Not the benefactor and not the bureau.

The counter-model for the Courier would be a member-owned community newspaper cooperative on the old Rochdale rule: one member, one vote. The subscribers would be the members, each paying a modest annual share. An elected board of county residents would set the policy. No donor could buy an extra editorial vote. A professional editor would serve under a published charter and a fixed term, protected from any single owner’s demand for favorable coverage and bound to treat every candidate alike. Any sale or merger would require the consent of the members.

The accounts and minutes would be public. A council of readers, elected separately from the board, would keep its own eyes on the editor and the books. A supermajority of the membership would be required before the paper could be sold or remade into something the county never asked it to be. The editor would answer at the annual meeting in the high-school gym. The charter would carry the sentence the Courier’s announcement already chose—“kept locally owned”—written this time into bylaws instead of into a press release.

The money could come from subscriptions, local businesses, foundations, and carefully bounded public grants: seed money or start-up money only, never an operating grant that could be conditioned on coverage. No single funder would control the paper.

We know in Adams County that this form can be more than a slogan. I manage a farm co-op: one member, one vote. The members sit across the counter from me, the board answers to them at the annual meeting, and no one, myself included, can sell the thing out from under them. In my own town of Friendship, the Adams-Columbia Electric Cooperative serves about 31,560 member-owners across twelve counties. Their member families pooled their money and borrowed under the REA in the 1930s to build what the for-profit utilities had called unprofitable. When that work began, perhaps one farm in ten had central-station electricity; by mid-century, most did. The wires end at a farmhouse, but that does not make the utility locally owned. Its members govern it, and professional management carries the daily work.

A newspaper cooperative would need the same discipline. It would be harder than an electric cooperative because an electric cooperative reads a meter, while a paper must sell judgment in a county of 20,000. Smith County’s advertising base is thin, print is dear, and trust cannot be purchased with a famous signature. It would also be harder because the Lake family gave the paper a continuity of fifty years; a new arrangement must earn what a family can sometimes accumulate merely by remaining.

Cooperatives are not sacraments. They can drift into managerial oligarchy unless the members retain the vote, the budget remains public, and the editor cannot be punished for refusing a favored story. In Adams County, where nearly half the houses sit empty half the year and the median age pushes past fifty-five, we know exactly what that arithmetic does to a newsroom. The meetings, elections and annual accounting required by the bylaws are the very habits the last forty years dissolved. That is the bitterest turn in this story: the permanent answer requires the civic fabric whose loss made it necessary.

The sale should be judged by governance, not fame. Can a person who has spent his life in national politics make the paper less dependent on any one personality?

The title can be his. The charter should belong to Carthage.

A paper the Lake family kept alive for fifty years deserves an owner who can defend its future without making the county’s voice depend on the mood of the man who owns it. Leave the town its life—in the bylaws, in the budget and in the newsroom—not merely in the announcement. The *Courier’s* future belongs to Carthage.

Al Gore may have done the generous thing. The county can do the permanent one: one member, one vote, held the way a county holds its courthouse and its fair, in common and on purpose, for readers who have not been born yet.

That is what it means to conserve something. It was never one man’s job to do it for them.

It was the town’s.
