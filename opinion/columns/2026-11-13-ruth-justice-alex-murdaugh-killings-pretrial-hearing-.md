Swear something false to a grand jury and it is a felony. Say it as the state's own agent, building a murder case, and the state's lawyers will call it "a single mistake." That arithmetic goes on display Friday in Colleton County, where Alex Murdaugh's lawyers will ask Judge Debra McCaslin to throw out the original murder indictments — for the killings of his wife, Maggie, and his son, Paul — on the ground that the man who carried the case to the Colleton County grand jury, State Law Enforcement Division agent David Owen, gave the jurors one story while the state's own earlier report kept another.

The motion says Owen told grand jurors that blood spatter was found on the T-shirt Murdaugh wore the night of the killings. An earlier report from state agents said there were no blood stains, and no such evidence was introduced at trial. The motion says Owen also told grand jurors that the ammunition load that killed Paul — buckshot in one chamber, large birdshot in the other — turned up loaded in weapons all over the Murdaugh property. It did not. His lawyers add the fact that has trailed this case from the start: no blood or DNA on Murdaugh or his clothes, though the killings happened at close range with powerful weapons that were never found. The prosecutors' filing answers that they have plenty of other evidence and that "a single mistake is not bad faith." The motion counts two misstatements. The state counts one. The best thing Alex Murdaugh owns is the state's mistakes, and on Friday he is spending two of them.

Owen's own file needs no defense lawyer to read. Last year a judge threw out a different murder case he worked because the dead woman's mother told him about a suicide threat her daughter had made days before she died, and he never passed it along to prosecutors or the defense. Not long before he abruptly retired in 2024, the state had pinned its State Law Enforcement Officer of the Year honor on him. Call it the mistake discount: the same false statement that puts a civilian in an interview room retires the state's own man with a plaque. It is a house standard. HSBC admitted the violations and forfeited $1.256 billion — $1.9 billion in all — for laundering money for Mexican drug cartels, and not one human being was prosecuted. Purdue pleaded guilty twice, in 2007 and in 2020, and not one Sackler was ever charged. Wells Fargo bought its way out of millions of fake accounts for $3 billion, in a deal the Justice Department described as reached "with the bank itself, not with any individuals responsible for the fraud." The discount runs one direction. Up. Again.

The discount was minted long before Owen took his oath. For the better part of a century, the circuit solicitor's office — the elected prosecutor's post for that stretch of Lowcountry — was a family chair: Randolph Murdaugh held it, then his son, then his grandson, and Alex Murdaugh began his career inside it as an assistant prosecutor working under his father before he carried the family name into private practice. Concentrate the power to charge crimes in one family for most of a century, and the office learns where not to look. An in-group the law protects but does not bind; an out-group the law binds but does not protect. I have watched that movie since Nixon. The Lowcountry just cast it with relatives.

Now read what the machine declined to notice for years, all of it confessed by the man himself in open court. The settlement money owed to the sons of Gloria Satterfield, the family housekeeper who died after a fall at the Murdaugh home: pocketed, and admitted. A roadside shooting three months after his wife and son were killed, arranged so the life insurance would pay his surviving son: admitted too. The millions taken from injured clients who trusted the family name: admitted. He stood up in court and called himself a thief, an insurance cheat, a bad lawyer, and a longtime opioid addict. Every one of those crimes had victims with less money, less family, and less courtroom than he had, and not one could make the machine move until the machine itself fell over. The stealing took decades to notice. The cases took months to finish. That gap is the entire biography of the place.

None of it slows the calendar. Murdaugh's first conviction and life sentence were thrown out by the South Carolina Supreme Court over a court clerk's improper influence on the jurors; the retrial has since been [moved to a different county](/articles/2026-08-14-judge-moves-murdaugh-murder-retrial-from-rural-south-carolina-county/) to run clean, and it sits on the [April 2027 docket](/articles/2026-06-29-judge-sets-april-2027-retrial-date-for-alex-murdaugh/). Even if McCaslin grants the motion Friday, prosecutors can seek a fresh set of indictments next month, and the defense says it will not stand in the way. The prosecutors say their other evidence — the video with Murdaugh's voice at the scene, the mounting debts, the thefts closing in — is plenty. April can weigh that. Friday's question is smaller.

Checking the state's work is the work of lawyers, and lawyers are money. Every error in the file becomes a hearing for a man who can afford to find it and a sentence for everyone else. In 2012 the Economic Policy Institute put the money recovered for cheated workers at $933 million — almost three times everything taken in every robbery in America that year — and the worst that comes of taking the rest is paying back what you owe. The people he stole millions from are not in this story at all. They never get a transcript to comb. They got the theft and the hole it left.

No blood on the shirt. No such load in guns all over the property. Both told to the grand jury anyway. One mistake, says the state. Two of them — and every one of them belongs to the Officer of the Year.

Alex Murdaugh pays for the stealing in decades. Owen retired with the plaque. The state calls the first outcome justice. For the second it keeps "a single mistake." The law down there is not broken. It is employed.
