North Carolina election officials removed four college polling places. The question is whether they did so under a valid rule and whether they shifted the burden of voting onto predominantly Black students.

On the first day of early voting on October 15, mostly Black students from North Carolina Agricultural & Technical State University, Bennett College and the University of North Carolina at Greensboro walked off their campuses toward the nearest polling places. The round trips took about two hours.

Dihanna Williams, an NC A&T student, said many students wanted to march because they knew it “would make a statement”: they would show that, despite y’all taking away something that should be a right to us, “we’re still going to go out and vote because that’s also a right for us and that’s something that our ancestors fought so hard for us to accomplish.”

The legal framework matters. North Carolina’s early-voting statute, N.C.G.S. § 163A-8, governs the designation of early-voting sites by county election boards. The State Board of Elections administers early voting through 21 N.C. Admin. Code 20-01-0103 and its adopted Statewide Voting and Election Plan for the 2026 election. Those instruments define the authority behind the removals, the discretion exercised under it and the reasons the State Board was required to provide.

In late 2025, following changes made by the Republican-led General Assembly, the State Board and county boards removed on-campus polling places from UNCG, NC A&T, Western Carolina University and Elon University. The ballots remained available. The sites where students had voted were removed.

The January complaint was filed in the U.S. District Court for the Middle District of North Carolina under the caption *College Democrats of North Carolina v. North Carolina State Board of Elections*. It invokes Equal Protection and Due Process claims under the Fourteenth Amendment, the corresponding guarantees of Article I, Section 26 of the North Carolina Constitution, and a claim under Section 2 of the Voting Rights Act, with 42 U.S.C. § 1983 providing the federal vehicle for the constitutional claims.

Those causes of action supply legal standards the column cannot supply on its own. The Equal Protection claim asks whether race helped determine which polling places disappeared. The Voting Rights Act claim asks whether the removals disproportionately burdened minority voters and whether reasonable nondiscriminatory alternatives existed. The Due Process claim asks whether officials arbitrarily deprived students of meaningful access to early voting.

The Equal Protection inquiry turns on evidence of intent. The pleading alleges that state and county officials “brushed aside urgent warnings that their decisions would disproportionately burden young and Black voters and denigrated students who advocated for their rights.” That is the plaintiff’s allegation, not a judicial finding. Its significance is that it places notice inside the decision the defendants made: the complaint says they knew the projected burden and moved the sites anyway.

The complaint also supplies the practical consequences. At Western Carolina University, where more than 60 percent of students reportedly do not have cars, students without transportation would have had to walk nearly two miles to the new early-voting site. Parts of that route ran beside a four-lane highway without sidewalks or guardrails. The complaint recounts students making that walk in February.

The pleading notes that NC A&T is the country’s largest historically Black university and that Western Carolina’s former campus polling place served more Black voters than any other polling site in its county during the 2024 election. The removals did not affect every voter equally. They placed additional time, transportation costs and physical risk before students, with a disproportionate effect on young and Black voters.

The students did not wait for litigation to make the point for them. Protect Ours, a student-led group co-founded by NC A&T senior Shia Rozier, began running shuttles to the polls. Students offered rides and organized voting-centered cookouts and other events. During NC A&T’s homecoming week, campus leaders held “Votecoming: Party at the Polls.”

The broader Party at the Polls effort of When We All Vote, the Michelle Obama-founded nonpartisan initiative, has been associated with a two-to-four-percentage-point increase in voter turnout. A second march on October 22 brought out hundreds of students. The scale of the marches made the closures visible; the shuttles, rides and gatherings allowed students to turn a day of protest into repeated assistance.

Alexis Cox, a junior honors political science student, said the closures had the opposite effect of producing apathy. Students felt more motivated, marched in the heat, showed other students how to vote and organized shuttle services. The board’s site decisions did not end participation. They changed who had to carry the work of sustaining it.

Rozier has attended state board of elections meetings in Raleigh and protested for campus polling sites since her freshman year. Her explanation of why the fight mattered was plain:

“It’s unpleasant to be told by your representatives that your voice doesn’t matter. We’re pivoting and just deciding, ‘If it didn’t matter as much, you guys wouldn’t be working to take it away.’”

Restore the campus polling places. The board can defend its decisions under N.C.G.S. § 163A-8, 21 N.C. Admin. Code 20-01-0103 and its adopted statewide voting plan, and the plaintiffs can test those decisions under the constitutional standards pleaded in court. What the students have already shown is the answer the board avoided: moving the polling places did not stop them from voting. It only transferred the cost to their time, their transportation and their sense of whether their votes counted.
