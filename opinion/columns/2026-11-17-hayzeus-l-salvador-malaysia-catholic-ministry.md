JD Vance is teaching Christians to love the stranger less.

In Kuala Lumpur, the Archdiocese of Kuala Lumpur feeds strangers the world has agreed not to see. The contrast begins inside my own tradition and my own country. Catholic communities in the United States have welcomed undocumented neighbors even as our politics have made migrant labor disposable. I cannot point only at Malaysia and pretend that my own community, church, and country are innocent. I know the credential habit in my own Church too: the reflex to ask whether a person belongs before asking what a person needs. Nor is the machine in my own country new, or the property of one party. Administrations of both coalitions have fed the deportation machinery for a century, and every one of them has fallen short of the five principles the bishops of the United States and Mexico set down for migration in 2003.

Nor can I make religious difference itself the enemy. I would judge a Catholic ministry that excluded Muslim, Buddhist, Hindu, or nonbelieving families from aid exactly as I judge any government or religious institution that withholds help because of a person’s faith. The standard is the same: assistance without a catechism, justice without a test of belief. We must say it plainly: no child is less human because a parent is undocumented, and no human being is less human because a prayer is spoken in another language.

On January 29, 2025, Vice President JD Vance—a Catholic—stood on television and laid out the order in which a Christian’s love is owed. Family first. Then neighbor. Then community. Then fellow citizens. “And then after that,” he said, “you can focus and prioritize the rest of the world.” The next day he told the country to “just google ‘ordo amoris’” and added that “the idea that there isn’t a hierarchy of obligations violates basic common sense.”

He was not speaking in the abstract. The teaching was deployed to explain why Americans may be prioritized over everyone else in immigration policy. The stranger at the bottom of that order of love is the migrant at the border. Teaching the faithful that the stranger may be loved last is teaching that the stranger may be refused.

The Church he invoked did not leave the teaching unanswered. Then-Cardinal Robert Prevost—the man who would become Pope Leo XIV—replied on X: “JD Vance is wrong: Jesus doesn’t ask us to rank our love for others.” On February 10, 2025, Pope Francis wrote to the bishops of the United States: “Christian love is not a concentric expansion of interests that little by little extend to other persons and groups.” The true *ordo amoris*, the pope wrote, is the one learned at the side of the man bleeding in the ditch—a love that “builds a fraternity open to all, without exception.” A sitting vice president taught a doctrine of ranked love and was corrected by a pope and by the man who would succeed him. Both corrections stand in the record.

His instruction to search the tradition was sound: what the tradition reveals convicts his use of it. Google it. Read it. Augustine’s ordering of love governs *how* we love—what is owed to a thing according to its nature—not whom we are permitted to refuse. Aquinas held that nearness can change the intensity of love, never its content: every neighbor keeps the same fundamental dignity. The doctrine Vance told America to google convicts the use he put it to. Teaching Christians to rank their love so the stranger can be turned away is not a reading of the tradition. It is a betrayal of it.

This is why a story from the other side of the world belongs in this argument. In Kuala Lumpur, the Archdiocesan Office for Human Development, or AOHD, runs ten ministries for women, prisoners, migrants and refugees, and children with special needs. The Rev. Frederick Joseph, who sits on its board, says its priests, nuns, and religious brothers serve Malaysians “regardless of whether those people are Catholic”—Muslims, Buddhists, Hindus, and nonbelievers. The country they serve is 63.5 percent Muslim, 18.7 percent Buddhist, about 9 percent Christian, and 6 percent Hindu. The Church is a small minority there, working in the lineage of the Society of St. Vincent de Paul, which has served people across Malaysia for almost a century. Joseph names the principle underneath: “the preferential option for the poor, not in terms of evangelization.” Read that phrase slowly. The bread comes first, and no one sends the bill.

When her husband’s truck-driving job disappeared during the pandemic, Noorzainny Luthfi was raising three children in one of Kuala Lumpur’s low-income neighborhoods. “We didn’t know what to do,” she said. The family began selling coconut-shell piggy banks. In 2022, after a British friend suggested turning discarded plastic into products people could buy, Noorzainny and her family founded Upcycled. Catholic, Muslim, and Hindu religious leaders came by the little shop with donations for vulnerable neighbors. Then the bishop saw the small factory and asked what they were doing.

Not what they believed. Not whom they prayed to. What are you doing.

The Church now supports Upcycled. A landslide later destroyed the family’s old workshop; the neighborhood gave Noorzainny a sewing machine. She keeps it near the entrance and teaches other women to turn old clothes into clothes for their children. Her neighbors call her *Mak Aini*, an affectionate name for a motherly figure. “Everybody has their own God and the same goes for me,” she said. “If you respect other religions and other people, you can become like brothers, like a family.” *Acompañamiento*—walking with—is what a bishop is doing when he asks a Muslim family what they are building instead of what they believe.

A short distance away, AOHD’s learning center for children with special needs opened in 2001 because three parents could not find a school able to take their children with autism. That unmet need led the founder to approach the Church about opening a center. It serves children from Malaysia’s lowest-income households and offers three age-based programs, from early intervention to vocational training for young adults. Classes are taught in English to provide a common language. Although AOHD supports the center, its coordinator, Gunavathi Thurchinamurthy, says, “We don’t offer spiritual classes because we have people from different faiths. We are open to anybody.” That refusal matters. The children are students, not evidence for anyone’s religious argument. The center makes welcome ordinary.

At a community dining hall near Upcycled, Malaysians from different religious backgrounds gather for meals and celebrate one another’s festivals: Eid al-Fitr, Chinese New Year, and Diwali. Nor Aqidah, a Muslim woman whose family has benefited from AOHD donations, put it plainly: “We can live together as long as we know each other and respect each other’s beliefs.” This is not decorative coexistence. It is mutual dependence.

And none of this launders anyone. [Rohingya refugees in Malaysia still struggle to worship freely amid discrimination](/articles/2026-11-06-rohingya-refugees-in-malaysia-struggle-to-worship-freely-amid-discrimination/), and the Church’s own servants are not spared the world’s neglect—[nuns who gave a lifetime to others now find basic end-of-life care scarce](/articles/2026-06-20-for-nuns-who-gave-a-lifetime-to-others-basic-end-of-life-care-is-scarce/). Grace at the parish table does not excuse the state’s hands; it convicts them. Fidelity does not exempt anyone from the accounting; it only refuses to stop feeding people while the accounting continues.

I know what the other kind of table looks like. In El Paso, where I grew up, classmates disappeared before dawn when the raids came. The school day went on as though the empty desks were ordinary, and no one in charge ever asked where the children had gone. A church—or a country—that checks membership before it opens its hands teaches children exactly that lesson. Kuala Lumpur is teaching the opposite one, to Muslims, at the Church’s own cost.

None of this is improvisation, and none of it is Father Joseph’s invention. AOHD was founded in 1992. The Archdiocese has about 45 churches and serves a region of roughly 12 million people. After the Second Vatican Council of 1962–1965, bishops and priests gathered in Penang in 1976 to ask how its teaching should change their practice. The answer they chose was the marginalized. Behind that answer stand the texts.

“I was a stranger and you welcomed me,” the red letters say—“just as you did it to one of the least of these who are members of my family, you did it to me.” And the other half of the same page: “I was a stranger and you did not welcome me.” The Torah repeats the command more than thirty times: “You shall not wrong or oppress a resident alien, for you were aliens in the land of Egypt.” “The alien who resides with you shall be to you as the citizen among you; you shall love the alien as yourself, for you were aliens in the land of Egypt: I am the Lord your God.” God “executes justice for the orphan and the widow, and loves the strangers.”

Jesus makes welcome an identity claim. Torah makes it justice rooted in remembrance. Francis writes in *Fratelli Tutti* that migrants possess “the same intrinsic dignity as any person,” and that treating them otherwise is “unacceptable” for Christians because it “sets certain political preferences above deep convictions of our faith.” John XXIII, in *Pacem in Terris* in 1963, names the same truth: “The fact that he is a citizen of a particular State does not deprive him of membership in the human family.” The texts agree with the conduct in Kuala Lumpur. The conduct in Washington contradicts them. A church that honors the stranger only when the stranger is useful has abandoned both.

At Lampedusa on July 8, 2013, Pope Francis named the opposite condition: “globalization of indifference.” We had become used to the suffering of others: “It doesn’t affect me; it doesn’t concern me; it’s none of my business!” AOHD is organized resistance to that sentence. It is *acompañamiento* in plain form: staying near the woman without work, the prisoner without an advocate, the migrant without security, and the child without a classroom. It does not solve the entire immigration system by itself. It does something the system cannot dismiss: it shows the alternative in ordinary work.

A just immigration system cannot leave welcome to charity. Governments must protect a migrant’s right to work, learn, worship, and live with family; make legal status a path rather than a trap; and place labor and family unity above employer convenience and political theater. A policy worthy of the texts would provide legal pathways that exist in practice and not only on paper, protection a refugee can actually reach, and dignity for the neighbor who picks the food, builds the houses, and pays the taxes regardless of papers. It would also restore the bishops’ first principle: the right to find opportunity in one’s own homeland, so that leaving is a choice rather than a sentence.

To every public official who mistakes exclusion for order: order does not require cruelty, and you are not the problem; the exclusion is.

Romero said that a gospel that does not unsettle is no gospel at all. The shop in Kuala Lumpur unsettles exactly the right people: every powerful man who claims the same Christ while teaching his people to rank their love. So to the vice president, in the second person, because he is a person: you can still come back. Francis’s correction has room for you too—love open to all, without exception, includes you. That is the hardest part of the teaching and the best part.

At Upcycled, in the corner near the entrance, stands the sewing machine the neighborhood gave the family after the landslide broke their old workshop. The women there turn old clothes into baby clothes. Nothing asks anyone what they believe before it asks what they need. Near the community dining hall, the table is shared without becoming anyone else’s table: not a border drawn around mercy, but a table long enough for everyone.

The door of return is open to every public official, believer, and neighbor who has confused order with exclusion. Repair what your hand can reach. The table is being set.
