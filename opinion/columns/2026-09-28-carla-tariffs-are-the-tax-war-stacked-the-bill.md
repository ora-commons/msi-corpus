Tariffs are a regressive tax, but stacked price shocks are what broke the coalition. In [“The Revolt Against Tariffs”](https://www.nationalreview.com/2026/09/the-revolt-against-tariffs/), National Review’s Rich Lowry argues that Liberation Day began the end of the Republican coalition: voters finally noticed the cost of protectionism, roughly $1,000 a year per household according to the Yale Budget Lab, while three-quarters of Americans saw prices rising and agricultural exports to China collapsed by 66 percent. He is right that Trump promised lower prices and tariffs raise them. That contradiction is real. It is also not the whole bill.

A tariff is a tax paid at the border by the importer. The importer passes it to the wholesaler, the wholesaler to the retailer, and the retailer to you. By the time a sheet of steel, a bottle of olive oil, or a pallet of fertilizer reaches the kitchen, the duty has disappeared into the price tag. The Yale Budget Lab estimates the household cost at about $1,000 a year. For a family spending $300 a week on groceries, $80 a month on gasoline, and $200 on utilities, that is roughly two car payments diverted to whichever industry the administration decided to protect this quarter.

It does not feel like industrial policy at the kitchen table. It feels like inflation.

That is why the policy is regressive. The dollar hit is broadly distributed across households, but the burden is not. A $1,000 bill is not the same thing to a family earning $40,000 as it is to one earning $400,000. The old free-trade theory—concentrated benefits, dispersed costs—was supposed to work because the costs would be small enough that nobody noticed. The surprise is not that voters suddenly learned trade theory. The surprise is that the bill got large enough to land on a $400 grocery receipt and a $5 fill-up.

Lowry’s cost-dispersal logic did not turn on its head. It was always dispersed across imported goods at checkout. What changed in 2026 is that another price shock became visible at exactly the same moment.

The Iran war pushed diesel fuel up by roughly 80 percent and fertilizer by about 15 percent. Those are input costs, not tariff costs. A truck hauling California produce to Chicago burns the same diesel whether the produce was tariffed or not. A Kansas wheat farmer pays the same nitrogen price whether China is buying the wheat or not. Those prices do not depend on the tariff schedule. They depend on a war in the Persian Gulf.

The roughly $1,000 tariff bill landed at the same checkout as the diesel cost baked into every shipped good. The 66 percent drop in China-bound farm exports landed in the same planting season as the fertilizer spike. Three price signals stacked into one shopping trip and one farm ledger. The dispersion mechanism did not reverse. It was overwhelmed by signal density.

Lowry is right that the farm pain is real. China cut purchases. Exports collapsed. The Midwest coalition is paying for it. A soybean farmer is now paying more for fuel and chemical inputs while losing the customer who used to buy a third of the crop. The administration’s answer to grocery prices—drop the beef tariff—collides with its answer to farm incomes—keep protection on everything else. A worker who needs cheaper milk does not get it from a tariff on Canadian potash. A farmer who needs a customer does not get one from a duty on imported fertilizer.

The contradiction is not a bug. It is what happens when a tax on consumers is asked to double as a subsidy for producers.

But the political revolt is not simply against protectionism. It is against the moment a family walks into a grocery store and pays more for everything at once, for reasons that have little to do with one another except that they arrived in the same week. The voter does not separate the tariff from the diesel from the fertilizer. The voter sees the total. The total is being driven by a war the administration did not start over tariffs, layered on top of a policy that was already charging them more.

The midterm is the messenger. The message is the policy—and the stack.

That means the fix is not a sermon about voter ignorance, and it is not a retreat into the lazy claim that industrial capacity should simply be abandoned. Strategic capacity in semiconductors, grid components, and pharmaceutical precursors can be built with public investment, tax credits, and skilled-labor pipelines. It does not have to be financed by a grocery tax. Trade relationships should be rebuilt through negotiation and reciprocal deals, not blank-check duties that punish your own base and invite retaliation from the countries buying your exports.

And while the war shock persists, the administration should suspend the federal diesel tax and send fertilizer relief directly to producers. Make the relief loud and visible, the way the tariff costs are loud and visible. Break the stack. Stop asking one blunt instrument to serve as industrial policy, farm subsidy, consumer tax, and foreign-policy threat all at once.

Tariffs raise prices by a measurable, recoverable amount. Wars raise prices by an unmeasurable, unrecoverable amount until the war ends. The administration cannot end every external shock. It can stop adding a regressive tax to the checkout line, stop cutting farmers off from their customers, and build strategic capacity without making working families finance it.

The kitchen-table story is written every week. Build the country’s productive strength with public investment and skilled labor. Protect producers from a war-driven input spike. Reopen the markets that buy American crops. And if protection remains, make it narrow enough that it protects something besides the people who wrote the tariff schedule.
