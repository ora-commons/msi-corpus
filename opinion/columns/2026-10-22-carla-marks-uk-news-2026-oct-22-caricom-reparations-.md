A royal apology can open the door; Caribbean ownership of the money must build what walks through it. [The Guardian reports](https://www.theguardian.com/uk-news/2026/oct/22/caricom-reparations-commission-king-charles) that Hilary Beckles, chair of the CARICOM Reparations Commission, appealed to King Charles at a press conference as a fellow human—a “human brother”—and as a parent and grandparent. Beckles argued that the royal family benefited from slavery and that the wealth Charles is passing to his children and grandchildren came from chattel slavery. He wants the Commonwealth summit next month in Antigua and Barbuda to become a “moral institution committed to human justice.” There is something radical in that demand. There is also an easy way to turn it into another summit photo.

An apology matters. Without one, the British state preserves a convenient ambiguity: that a private family should somehow shoulder a public crime. Beckles is right to press. But do not confuse the threshold with the settlement. A royal apology acknowledges the wrong in the name of the state; reparations establish what the state and the institutional beneficiaries owe.

The king’s ability to convene is not in doubt. In September he [hosted AI executives in Scotland to build consensus](/articles/2026-09-17/king-charles-hosts-ai-executives-in-scotland-to-build-consensus/). Consensus, it turns out, is available by appointment. Technology executives are not the hard part. The hard part is money, historical records, public authority, and the consent of communities that did not benefit from the old arrangement. An AI summit can end in consensus language. A reparations settlement cannot.

That is why asking Charles to use his “influence” is both too much and too little. Too much for one man. Too little for an institutional debt. A personal appeal can open a diplomatic room another ministry memo cannot. Charles has a platform before Commonwealth leaders, so his convening power is worth using. I am not against a royal speech. I am against using the speech as policy.

The royal family’s inherited wealth is not Britain’s reparations account. Beckles says the family benefited from slavery. The settlement thesis rests on a broad historical claim that must itself be tested: slavery’s wealth passed through plantations, shipping, insurance, banking, commerce, and state power, and was converted into land, securities, and institutions that continued earning after slavery itself was abolished. A credible claims process must trace those links through evidence, claim by claim and asset by asset, rather than treating an institutional inheritance so vast and complicated that it can never be examined.

One household cannot carry that liability, and it should not have to carry it alone. The descendants of any beneficiary did not choose the crime. Nor should the bill fall wholly on today’s workers. If reparations are paid entirely from general taxation, people who inherited nothing from slavery finance a settlement while identifiable owners and institutions retain the assets that grew from it. The British public can contribute through a transparent scheme. That is different from using the PAYE statement as an antique receipt.

The claim must go after the wealth.

Jamaica has taken one part of the institutional work seriously. Its government lodged a landmark petition seeking legal advice on its reparations claim, with a Privy Council hearing scheduled for March 2027. That is a process. A petition can clarify claims and test arguments. It cannot, by itself, change ownership.

That is why the current summit matters. Beckles calls it “the Chogm of reparations” and “the first round of reparations negotiations.” Fine. Negotiations need a settlement mechanism, not merely another paragraph.

The current machinery produces photo ops. There is no dedicated official reparations event at the summit. One paragraph in the draft Commonwealth communique commits governments to dialogue. Another, directing foreign ministers to advance “structured, inclusive, and good faith dialogue” on historical harms from the transatlantic slave trade, has not won unanimous agreement. The machinery can arrive before the appetite to use it.

Meanwhile, on 3 November, campaigners plan a Global Reparations Caravan combining advocacy, demonstrations, and culture. That arrangement is not a settlement. It is a plan to make the absence of policy visible from the sidewalk.

At Antigua, the summit must become the first negotiating round, not merely the place where officials pose beside protesters. Put reparations on the official agenda. Secure agreement on the disputed paragraph requiring Commonwealth foreign ministers to advance dialogue on historical harms. Publish a timetable for pooled claims, British records disclosure, and the creation of a Caribbean reparations authority. Those are the immediate demands. The longer-term architecture should grow out of them.

There are growing calls for Charles to apologize formally for slavery. The machinery still has no payment formula, no schedule, and no institution empowered to release money. Those omissions matter. A formula says how much and for what. A schedule says when. An empowered body says who can be made to answer.

Norway offers a better model than royal conscience. At the level of principle, its petroleum system places resource income in public ownership, invests the resulting capital, and returns investment income to public purposes. The analogy concerns that chain of control, not a ready-made reparations template. The point is not that every Caribbean state needs a Norwegian fund. The point is that extracted wealth can be owned collectively, managed competently, and made to answer to the people it is meant to repair.

So build three things.

First, secure a formal apology and legal acknowledgment. This must establish the moral foundation without ambiguity: the wrong was committed, the British state bears responsibility, and a settlement follows.

Second, turn the CARICOM Reparations Commission from a summit fixture into a permanent regional settlement institution. Build it as a federation. A regional secretariat should handle valuation, pooled claims, and cross-border settlement. National and community panels should determine how distinct claims relate to particular assets and harms. Community panels must appoint their own grant makers and control immediate disbursements under published criteria. Independent appeals must be available. Caribbean officials must have no discretion to redirect money away from the communities that suffered.

At the regional layer, an intergovernmental pooled fund should be governed by member-government appointees plus reserved community-nominated seats, with published accounts, independent audits, and transparent allocation rules. The authority must have deadlines and the power to receive money, land, debt instruments, and ownership stakes. Every offer, asset, and distribution decision should be public. CARICOM’s commission should move beyond issuing first-round negotiating claims to administering those claims.

Third, create Caribbean-owned reparations trusts and public development funds. They can hold land, housing, infrastructure, equity stakes, and other productive assets locally. The fund should have two parts: immediate community claims and permanent shared capital.

The first part should support land restoration, education, cultural preservation, and grants controlled by the communities that lived the harm. The second should finance community land trusts, worker cooperatives, and local credit institutions, so the money circulates in the Caribbean instead of disappearing into another dependency. Public contributions can provide immediate relief. Debt relief can enlarge the room governments have to operate. But long-term ownership is what prevents a payment from becoming another round of dependency administered from London.

Reparations should turn extraction into shared capital.

Other former colonial powers should join a common framework rather than forcing every Caribbean government to renegotiate the same principles one claim at a time. The claims cross legal systems, contested records, and governments that may prefer ambiguity to liability. That difficulty does not erase the need for a mechanism. It tells us why the mechanism must be stronger than a communiqué.

No London foundation with a tasteful mission statement should turn reparations into consultancy revenue. If London controls the money, the old dependency simply gets nicer stationery. No cheque to a royal household can substitute for disclosure and negotiation. Britain must open its records and negotiate pooled claims. Caribbean governments must build a regional authority capable of receiving, governing, and auditing money. The disagreement over the communique shows that the Caribbean machinery must be built too.

Charles should apologize. Britain should state responsibility, disclose its records, and negotiate. CARICOM should run the settlement. The money should end in institutions Caribbean people own and govern.

Charles can open the door. The Caribbean must own the room. The Commonwealth becomes a moral institution when money follows justice. Until then, the apology is a threshold, not a settlement.

A moral institution is not a mood. It is a balance sheet.

Build the settlement.
