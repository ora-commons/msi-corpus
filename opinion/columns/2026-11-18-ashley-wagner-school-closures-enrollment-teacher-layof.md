Seattle and San Francisco school districts squandered federal relief and delayed staffing decisions.

Enrollment collapsed. Staffing did not. Thomas Dee and Jaymes Pyne’s Stanford analysis of enrollment and staffing data found that public-school enrollment has fallen by more than 1.4 million students since the pandemic and that one in every four U.S. public schools has lost at least 15 percent of its student population since 2019. Yet the number of schools in operation remained virtually unchanged while teacher headcount grew 1 percent nationwide. “School districts have been playing Hamlet on this for a while now,” Dee said, adding, “We’ve never had more teachers per student in our nation’s history.” In many districts, the smaller system kept the larger payroll.

Expanding school choice complicates the arithmetic. EdChoice’s review of state voucher records found that more than 80 percent of Arizona voucher applicants had never attended public school and that two-thirds of Iowa’s first-year recipients already attended private school. None of that is fake, and none of it tells a district how to finance the smaller system it now operates.

More than $190 billion in federal pandemic relief, as tallied in the [U.S. Department of Education’s relief-funding records](https://www.ed.gov/grants-and-programs/school-improvement/elementary-and-secondary-school-emergency-relief-fund), let superintendents maintain staffing while enrollment shrank. That is what emergency money was supposed to do. It bought time. It also allowed recurring payroll commitments to outlast the temporary dollars paying for them. When the relief was exhausted, many districts confronted structural deficits. The aid delayed the reckoning; it did not repeal it.

The kitchen-table version of this number begins with our combined net income after taxes: $8,800 a month. Childcare for Eva and Ben costs $2,400, leaving $6,400 before the 7 percent mortgage, utilities, groceries and school taxes. Our margin is not representative. David’s grandmother’s estate helped us buy the Fishtown rowhouse; our college-educated parents navigated our financial-aid paperwork; and two professional incomes bought us time that many households do not have. I flag that because we are the privileged version of this arithmetic, and the math still fails.

My household’s margin came from an inheritance. A district’s temporary margin came from federal relief. Recurring payroll outlived the cash. What a district has instead of an estate is a finite tax base, a workforce and a grant with an expiration date. For parents who work fewer hours, the daycare bill does not shrink with the paycheck. The line item that does not show up in the press release is the cost that lands after the grant disappears.

Seattle school-board member Liza Rankin put employee compensation and benefits at roughly 80 percent of a district budget: “Closing schools doesn’t save much money to begin with. Payroll is where the big money is.” School-closure studies in Arkansas and California find only modest, temporary savings while documenting academic disruption; research from Chicago finds that teacher layoffs can be even more disruptive to learning. The menu in front of a school board is therefore close schools and hurt children, lay off teachers and hurt them worse, or drain reserves into recurring costs that never disappear.

None of that is painless. Losing a neighborhood school can mean a longer ride, lost access, disruption and no comparable alternative. Parents who defend their school are not enemies of public education. But a local veto is not a national financing plan.

Nor can the claim that payroll is mostly salaries excuse a federal default. The Individuals with Disabilities Education Act authorized the federal government to cover 40 percent of special-education costs. The [Congressional Research Service’s 2022 report on federal IDEA funding](https://crsreports.congress.gov/product/details?prodcode=R42726) found that the federal share was 12.7 percent in fiscal year 2021-22, leaving a $23.92 billion nationwide shortfall; it remained under 13 percent in 2024-25. More than 7 million children with disabilities are served by the program. Title I’s basic and concentration formulas have likewise been frozen at fiscal year 2001 levels since No Child Left Behind. Those gaps land in local budgets as larger classes, thinner counseling and arts programs, and less room for the teachers and aides students with disabilities need. Federal abandonment becomes a local payroll emergency.

Seattle has been looking at this for nearly four decades. State auditors warned that low-enrollment campuses had to close to avert a deepening budget crisis. Thirty-four years later, in 2024, the superintendent proposed closing up to a fifth of the district’s schools; community resistance scrapped the plan. Enrollment is now roughly 8 percent below 2019, at fewer than 49,000 students, and officials are again resisting a proposal to shutter nearly a dozen campuses. Seattle has spent nearly four decades converting a budget decision into a sequence of temporary answers.

San Francisco shows what waiting costs. Enrollment has fallen 10 percent since 2019. A late-2024 proposal to shutter more than a dozen schools was shelved after community opposition and the superintendent’s resignation. District budget documents show that early retirements and central-office cuts are expected to erase the structural deficit next school year, yet the district still projects a $26 million deficit the following year. Its rainy-day fund stood at nearly $139 million last year and is expected to fall to $52 million over the next two years. Jason Willis, a school-finance expert at WestEd who trains K-12 budget officers, said using one-time reserves for ongoing salaries “doesn’t come close to resolving the structural issues that San Francisco has.” Reserves buy time. They do not create a plan.

No single official has to own the whole problem. A board can protect a familiar school from a closure fight. A superintendent can keep teachers on payroll while federal aid lasts. A central office can cut itself before anyone powerful has to choose. The diffuse cost falls on the children, families and educators who remain. At least 21 state takeovers have occurred since 2023, according to Chalkbeat’s tracker, and under those takeovers local boards and superintendents often have much less freedom to decide what to cut or whom to lay off. A takeover is not a cure. It is the failed local plan arriving with fewer choices.

MSI has already documented both [the highest U.S. school-closure count since the pandemic](/articles/2026-11-09-u-s-school-closures-reach-highest-count-since-the-pandemic/) and [the staff and program cuts forced by falling K-12 enrollment](/articles/2026-10-28-falling-u-s-k-12-enrollment-prompts-public-school-staff-and-program-cuts/). Those are not contradictory trends. They are what happens when one district waits and another finally cuts.

Taylor Swift’s “You’re On Your Own, Kid” names the infrastructure this budget story exposes. Its turn from isolation to lateral solidarity matters: the title is not a command to make mothers tougher; it is a record of what happens when care becomes a household’s private burden. When temporary money postpones a staffing plan and a state bureaucracy takes over the decision, the public system makes families prove they can absorb the loss alone. The friendship bracelet is not a substitute for a functioning school board. It is the patch we make after the public institution has failed.

None of this means every school should remain open forever. It means closure and layoff without a child-level plan is not caution; it is passing the bill to children and families. Before a board votes, it should publish the transportation, staffing, special-education and learning consequences for every affected child. The 2025 IDEA Full Funding Act, with more than 30 Senate and 60 House co-sponsors, would pay the promised 40 percent and has not been advanced. A real pro-family federal budget would replace the relief cliff with automatic operating aid adjusted for enrollment, disability costs and local tax capacity; fully fund IDEA, protect Title I, and fund universal pre-K and paid family leave.

At eleven at night, cold tea beside the laptop and a spreadsheet with two columns: what the district says it can afford and what families must absorb. The second column is where accountability goes to hide.

I close the laptop when Ben wakes. Children’s futures cannot be spent down like a rainy-day fund.
