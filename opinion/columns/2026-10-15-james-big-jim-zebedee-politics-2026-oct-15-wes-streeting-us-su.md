Trump cut Ukraine's battlefield internet to squeeze Zelenskyy and chase cheap diesel before the midterms. That is what happened Monday morning when the switches flipped at one o'clock and the drones went deaf.

Ukrainian soldiers reported the terminals on their drones would not work above a hundred miles an hour. For a man flying one of those things, that is the same as your headset going dead on the runway. Attacks on Russian-occupied ground stopped in the middle of a run. And the warnings about what Russia was getting ready to drop on their cities, the early-warning feed the United States had been handing them, went quiet too, or so Ukrainian officials reported, unless Kyiv quit hitting Russian oil refineries. You do not need a security clearance to understand what that is. That is a man with his boot on somebody else's throat, turning the pressure up until the customer says yes. We covered exactly what that dependency looks like from the inside earlier this week: [Starlink throttling and the missed missile warnings](/articles/2026-10-15-starlink-throttling-and-missed-missile-warnings-expose-ukraine-s-us-dependency/).

Zelenskyy said he felt personally betrayed by Donald Trump. He was not being dramatic. He was describing what it feels like to find out that the wire you have been running your war on belongs to somebody else, and that the somebody else wants something. Elon Musk owns the satellites. He built that network with American contracts and American launch business, and when the switch got thrown he pointed back at Washington rather than take the credit, [as our own pages reported the same day](/articles/2026-10-15-musk-appears-to-suggest-us-government-behind-ukraine-starlink-speed-limits/). So the richest man in the world owns the wires in the middle of another country's war, and the government of the United States tells him how tight to pull them. Nobody voted on that. Nobody in Congress wrote it into a law. It is a settings menu, and a president can reach it at one in the morning.

From where I sat in the gunner's seat in Iraq, the wire mattered. We had satellites, we had air cover, we had a whole country behind everything we carried, and the one thing nobody tolerated was us going deaf. The Ukrainians have been handed about half of that apparatus on a string, and this month somebody yanked it. The people who pay for that are not the men in Washington or the man in the settings menu. They are the drone operator whose screen went gray over occupied ground, the crew that could not see the jet-powered aircraft coming at their city, and the families in the basements under those cities who spent Monday night listening for a siren nobody could sound. A free people have a right to determine their own destiny. They also have a right to know when the country that keeps telling them so has decided to make them beg for it.

Which is why the British defence secretary was in Washington this week. Wes Streeting flew over, gave a speech at a Washington think tank, and told the room that backing Ukraine is not charity and not a favor to Europe. It is an "enormous opportunity," in his words, tied to the "defence-industrial relationship" the two countries are about to build together. Then he asked for a meeting with Pete Hegseth at the Pentagon and got one the same day.

A man does not cross an ocean to tell his neighbor he has an opportunity. He crosses because he cannot hold what he is holding without help, and he has run out of ways to pretend otherwise. Streeting said it himself, more or less, when he talked about the "challenges of recent days" and admitted that Britain and the Ukrainians would feel the absence of American support. That is a defense secretary telling a foreign audience that his own country cannot guarantee the safety of its allies, or of its own people, without American permission. "Together," as he put it, "has rarely been a more important time." What he meant was "under," and everybody in the room heard it.

Behind him stand real people with real paychecks. There are shipyard workers in Barrow-in-Furness cutting sections for American attack submarines, and workers in Groton and Newport News doing the same work on our side. There are towns in Ohio and Pennsylvania and upstate New York that lost their plant fifteen years ago and have been waiting, through one election after another, for somebody to bring a reason to come back. A signed defence-industrial package would put shifts in some of those places. That is not nothing, and I will not pretend it is. A shift is a mortgage payment. A shift is a kid who does not have to leave.

But somebody pays for all of it, and it is the same somebody it always is. It is the family whose health premiums went up while the plant went south. It is the veteran I know who waited eight months on a claim with his knee swelling up in a truck cab. It is the taxpayer who will be told, on a Friday afternoon in August, that the number is bigger than expected and that the oversight hearing has been postponed. Eisenhower, on the night of January 17, 1961, told this country to guard against the acquisition of unwarranted influence, whether sought or unsought, by the military-industrial complex. He said it as a five-star general who had run the war in Europe, and he was not warning us about the enemy. He was warning us about the habit. Streeting's pitch is that the habit pays. That is the most honest version of this argument anyone has put on the table in years, and it is still the thing Eisenhower told us to guard against.

The British pledge to stand by Ukraine, repeated by Prime Minister Andy Burnham in London the same week, is worth what a pledge is worth when the country issuing it cannot keep an airbase open against an Iranian threat. The Americans pulled their B-1 bombers out of Fairford in Gloucestershire last week because Tehran rattled the cage. That is the whole arrangement in one photograph: Britain hosts, America decides, and when the decision changes, the bombers leave and the empty tarmac stays. Saudi Arabia is eating Houthi drones and missiles with American and British help, and the two countries are the only ones with the ships and the eyes to do anything about it. Streeting came with requests, not offers. He dressed them up as a partnership because that is the only coat they fit in anymore.

The constitutional posture here is plain. If an American president uses a private satellite network to blind an ally's army in order to force that ally's leader to stop a military operation before an election, then Congress has a duty to say so in public and to take that lever out of one man's hands. War powers were never meant to live in a settings menu.

So the pitch is on the table. Not charity. A contract. The switch is in Washington, and it is still switched off. Every senator up for a vote next year gets to stand in a union hall or a church basement in his own state and explain to the people in it why their country can throttle a war it has been telling them we are winning. The drone operators know what Monday cost. The families in the basements know. The question is whether anybody with a microphone plans to ask the president why he did it, and make him answer where the workers can hear him.
