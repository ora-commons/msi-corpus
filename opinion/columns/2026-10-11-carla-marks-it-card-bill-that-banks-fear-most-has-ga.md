Visa and Mastercard have been taking a cut of nearly every credit-card transaction in this country for decades, and the only thing that has finally threatened them is a bill they spent ten years branding a danger to the free market. That is the top of [this morning's Risk Report from the *Wall Street Journal*](https://www.wsj.com/risk-compliance-journal/the-morning-risk-report-the-credit-card-bill-that-banks-fear-most-has-gained-trump-as-an-ally-ae8807db): President Trump, JD Vance and several administration lieutenants have endorsed legislation giving merchants the option to route a card transaction over a network other than the one printed on the customer's card. The same briefing runs Vance's antifraud task force suspending Microsoft, Adobe and several recruiters from the H-1B green-card process, a $350 million American Express fine from the Comptroller of the Currency, the WTO's forecast of an AI-driven trade surge that most of the world is not invited to, and the digital-sovereignty squeeze on multinationals. Six items on a newsroom desk. They are one story: who owns the machinery, and who has been quietly paying to keep it running.

Concede the true half, because it is true. The routing bill does threaten Visa and Mastercard — that is its entire function, and it deserves backing for exactly that reason. And the H-1B program does have real fraud in it. Recruiters who pad applications are not a fabrication, and neither is the documented pattern of a work visa being used to hold a skilled worker's residency hostage to a single employer. The trouble is not the stated goals. It is that a bill to open a market and a task force list aimed at labor supply are being run in the same breath, as if they were the same project.

Start with the toll booth, because the language around it is the tell. The networks' lobbying arm has spent a decade arguing that letting a merchant choose a routing network is a threat to the free market. Read that twice. A free market in road tolls means: merchant here, customer there, two roads between them, the merchant picks. The version the lobby means is the one where the road charges you for wanting an alternative — which is not a market, it is a gate. Now that the gate has bipartisan backing, the gatekeepers have quietly moved to the argument that the gate is load-bearing.

The enforcement is worse. American Express is looking at $350 million for bank-secrecy and anti-money-laundering failures, and the OCC's own words are the receipt: "inadequate resources, including staff without sufficient expertise, systemic internal control gaps, weak independent testing." Read that as a description of a machine and it is damning. The company under-staffed its own compliance function for years, and the government's answer is a check. A fine is the state's favorite remedy because it is revenue and it changes nothing about the machine — the compliance office that was too thin stays too thin, the executives who approved the budget keep approving budgets, and the shareholders pay the entry fee. Booked as a cost of business, that is exactly what it becomes.

The aviation item in the same briefing is the one that is not about ownership, and it belongs here anyway because it shows what the bill looks like when nobody can be invoiced. At least 800 people have died worldwide since 2005 in crashes that U.S. officials believe or confirmed were deliberately caused by pilots — Malaysia 370 in 2014, China Eastern in 2022, Air India in 2025. As commercial flying has gotten safer, that number has come to stand alone on the graph. There is no market answer to a human being in the cockpit. The answer is screening, training and removal, and the industry has done all three at the pace of a quarterly call. Some things do not respond to a price. That is not an argument against markets; it is an argument against the reflex that a market exists for everything.

So what do you build instead? Three things, none of them exotic.

On payments, stop negotiating the toll and build a rail. The Federal Reserve already operates an instant-payment rail — FedNow — that answers to no shareholder, and that is the start of a public option in the plumbing. The cooperative version is not hypothetical either. There are roughly 900 rural electric cooperatives in this country serving about 42 million people across 56 percent of the land — member-owned utilities that exist because investor-owned utilities concluded the countryside was not worth serving. A merchant-owned routing network is the same idea in a different aisle: the people who pay the toll own the road. If Visa and Mastercard can still win on service, they should win on service.

On enforcement, structural remedies exist and they are boring. License conditions. Mandated remediation on a public schedule that somebody outside the company checks. Officers who sign off on the compliance budget answering for it when the budget turns out to have been the problem. The OCC already knows what was broken — it wrote it down. The question is whether the answer is a number or a machine, and a number is what they reached for.

On the trade surge, the WTO's forecast is that AI investment is driving goods-trade expansion at a pace not seen since the pre-2008 boom, that a small number of economies are doing the driving, and that the rest are watching from outside the glass. That bifurcation is the place where both American political teams go quiet. The honest answer is old and unglamorous: when trade moves that fast, a country owes its displaced workers more than a retraining pamphlet. Denmark spends around 2 percent of GDP on active labor-market policy — when a firm fails, the state retrains and re-places the workers fast enough that failure is a setback instead of a household bankruptcy. Firms fail; workers do not fall. Here is why that is harder to copy than it sounds: America has no sectoral bargaining, no organized employers sitting across a table from organized labor, and roughly half the state capacity the Danes spent a century assembling. The version that could pass is not Denmark. It is sectoral wage boards in the industries where the AI surge is actually landing — already legal in several states — plus the money following the worker rather than the firm.

The bill is not the threat. The toll booth is. And the answer to a toll booth is not a better toll schedule. It is somebody else owning the road.
