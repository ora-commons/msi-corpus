Capital owns the new machines, so capital decides how much of the productivity reaches the people who operate them. That is the fault line the Wall Street Journal’s Editorial Board avoids in [The U.S. Investment Boom Arrives](https://www.wsj.com/opinion/economy-gdp-investment-boom-tariffs-inflation-donald-trump-071da0e2?mod=rss_opinion). The Board reports that third-quarter gross domestic product grew 3.4%, led by business investment and consumer spending, and credits artificial intelligence, deregulation, and the 2025 tax reforms with a productivity surge. It asks why voters remain sour and blames the price-level hangover from the Biden years, tariffs, and gas prices from the Iran war. The investment boom is real. The claim that it will deliver shared prosperity is unfinished. The Board wants to settle the disagreement with a scoreboard. It offers no wage receipt and no ownership receipt.

The Board’s strongest point deserves a straight answer. Business investment contributed 1.78 percentage points to third-quarter GDP. Labor productivity has grown at an average annual rate of 2.25% since January 2025, compared with 1.3% from 2010 to 2019. Those numbers are a receipt. Investment is not a parlor trick. I am not anti-investment. I am anti-extraction.

But productivity is not a pay raise. It measures output per worker-hour. GDP is not household income. Follow the machine from purchase to paycheck.

A company buys servers, turbines, software, and factory equipment. The equipment may produce more, sell more, or make the same output with fewer hours. Who gets the extra value? If shareholders and senior managers do, productivity has done its job. If workers get it through higher pay, shorter hours, training, profit sharing, or ownership, productivity has also done its job. The first result is not an accounting error. It is a choice about ownership and bargaining.

The Board says investment will “ultimately lift wages.” The catechism of the comfortable is short: productivity lifts wages. It leaves out who owns the machine.

At one company, an owner can automate, set the terms, and keep the productivity gain unless workers have a counterweight. The forecast has a missing switch.

That switch is bargaining power. In 2025, about 11% of American wage and salary workers were represented by unions. Private-sector union membership was 5.9%. Among full-time wage and salary workers, union members had median weekly earnings of $1,404; nonunion workers had $1,174. The difference was $230 a week. Without a counterweight, one employer sets the terms and the employee has nowhere else to go. More productivity does not automatically become more pay. Labor power is not a sociological detail. It is the wage-setting mechanism.

Now add the accelerator the Board praises. The 2025 tax bill restored 100% business expensing, allowing companies to deduct qualifying equipment immediately rather than depreciating it over time. It also restored immediate deductions for eligible research. That gives owners cash sooner. If the firm would have bought the machine anyway, the tax break does not produce a new machine. It transfers value to the owner sooner. If it prompts an extra purchase, the public still has to ask what claim it gets on the additional output.

The article says investment in intellectual property—patents and software—has risen at an average annual rate of 9.7% in Trump’s second term, compared with 4% during the last two Biden years. That is the policy the Board wants credit for. Show us where the growth went. Profit on the investment is not a raise.

Deregulation changes the rules around capital. It does not make shareholders answer to workers. AI may transform work. It may also concentrate control. Some of these forces will spread prosperity. But prosperity is not a spillover. It travels through wages, hours, ownership, and bargaining power. “Ultimately lift wages” is not a mechanism. It is a promise wearing an economic costume.

The article’s own numbers make the ownership question sharper. Information-processing equipment accounted for roughly half the increase in business investment. These are the computers and servers powering AI systems. New machines can make firms more productive while reducing the number of workers they need. If output rises and the company owns the machine, the firm can take the gain. If workers own part of the company or bargain across the industry, they can claim some too. Markets reward firms that deploy capital efficiently. They do not pay a dividend to the worker who happens to operate the machine.

AI infrastructure is not a cloud raining productivity. It is chips, gas turbines, copper, land, transmission lines, substations, cooling, permits, and construction. Some costs stay with the firms buying and using the systems. Others can be shifted to households through utility rates, tax incentives, or public infrastructure spending. If public money helps make the buildout possible, the public deserves a share of the return. A company may use the added productivity to finance another round of machines. The public gets the substations. Unless it owns a share, it is simply lending its balance sheet to somebody else’s dividend.

The Board’s answer to public unease is inflation, and here the article supplies its own evidence. The Federal Reserve’s preferred gauge is the Personal Consumption Expenditures price index. It measures what households pay. Prices rose at a 2.1% annual pace in the third quarter. That is a slower rate of price increase, not a lower price level. Americans are not irrational because GDP rose. They know what their paychecks buy.

Tariffs pushed U.S. steel prices up 52% over the past year. American steel prices ended about two-thirds above European prices. Manufacturing investment fell 36% in Trump’s second term and another 4% in the third quarter. Housing subtracted 0.12 percentage point from third-quarter growth. The Board attributes most of that drag to higher interest rates. Immigration raids have disrupted beef plants. They have also made labor harder to find for construction firms. These are not moods. They are bills sent to appliance makers, machinery makers, power-equipment makers, builders, workers, and households. If a policy raises steel prices by 52% while it is sold as pro-growth, the label has to explain who pays.

One more piece of accounting needs a quick correction. The Board calls the 3.32-point import subtraction deceptive because imported chips and gas turbines help fuel investment. No. Imports are subtracted because they were produced abroad. The subtraction is accounting, not a verdict. Those inputs can help build American capacity, but the national accounts do not pretend foreign production is American production. The problem is not that they subtract imports. The problem is expecting a production number to certify broad prosperity.

So what do we build? Not a command economy. Not a freeze on innovation. We build the counterweight that turns productivity into shared gain.

Start with a floor for industries where low-wage competition is fierce. Let sectoral wage boards set minimum pay and working conditions across an industry. That keeps the lowest-paid workers from losing ground while larger firms install new machinery.

A guaranteed floor matters. It can keep a bad layoff from becoming a ruined life. But it does not give anyone a claim on the machine that makes the employer richer. We need institutions that turn output into bargaining power and ownership.

Start with sectoral bargaining. Build an industry floor. Where sectoral agreements cover most workers, nonunion employers face the same standards or must match them to compete for work. Wages and conditions get set across an industry rather than one employer at a time. States can create wage boards for home care, fast food, and agriculture, starting where the work is hardest and bargaining power is thinnest.

Denmark and Sweden give unions and employer organizations a role in bargaining across entire industries. That is the kind of system measured by collective-bargaining coverage. America’s 5.9% private-sector union membership rate and roughly 11% union-representation rate measure something else: how many workers are members or represented by unions. The measures are not interchangeable. In Denmark and Sweden, many workers bargain through an industry-wide union. In America, most workers lack that channel.

Then extend bargaining upward. Industry bargaining, coordinated training, and shared ownership can give workers a claim in sectors filled with expensive new machines.

Now make ownership mean something. A stock certificate is not a seat at the table. A worker may own shares and receive a dividend while having no vote on major decisions and no power over wages. Put the rights together. Give workers a clear vote on major company decisions, a share of profits, and representation in the room where investment and pay are set. Profit sharing delivers cash. Board representation delivers power. Stock without those rights is a tip jar, not control.

Worker-owned cooperatives go further. Mondragon-style cooperatives let workers help govern the enterprise and share the value they create. They need patient capital, shared reserves, and a plan for workers when a plant closes. A stake without control is weak. Control without shared capital is also weak.

The workers who run the business hold the voting rights and share the results. Productivity gains can show up as higher wages, shorter hours, and more shared capital. Worker ownership leaves markets intact. It changes who has the right to the profit.

If public support helps build the next capital wave, the public should own a slice of the return. A Norwegian-style sovereign wealth fund offers the basic model: government can own productive assets for the public and keep part of the return in public hands. A public equity stake or revenue share tied to support for data centers, power infrastructure, or research would turn “the economy” from a spectator into an owner.

The obstacle is not Scandinavian fate. It is American rulemaking: low union membership, fragmented employer groups, right-to-work laws, and fifty separate state governments. A state can let a nonunion shop stay nonunion, weakening a union’s ability to hold an entire industry to one deal. Corporate rules put shareholders first. Fifty sets of state rules make national action harder.

Fine. Institutions are what harder places build. The countries with these systems took decades to develop strong unions, organized employers, and capable public administration. Do not transplant Denmark on Tuesday. America already has worker cooperatives and public institutions we can expand. We are not starting from too little public capacity. We are starting from too much ownership in too few hands.

Start with wage floors and industry bargaining. Strengthen unions. Tie part of the investment tax break to worker ownership and profit sharing. Build the institutions before importing the outcome.

Markets can allocate the gains. Institutions decide who owns them. Build bargaining and shared ownership before the next productivity surge turns the gap into a chasm.

The boom is real, and capital will keep investing. The question is whether productivity becomes a shared dividend or another entry in the capital account. Build sectoral bargaining, give workers real ownership and control, and keep public returns in public hands. Then the investment story stops being a victory lap for owners and starts being what an investment boom is supposed to be: more power for the people who made it possible.
