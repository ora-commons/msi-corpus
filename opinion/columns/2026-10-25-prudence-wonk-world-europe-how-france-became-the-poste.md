French politicians are hiding the cost of debt from the voters who must pay it.

France’s debt crisis is a political honesty crisis wearing a financial costume. It is not waiting for another warning from the bond market; it is waiting for politicians to tell the truth. France’s problem is not that investors misunderstand its debt. They understand it perfectly. What they are pricing is whether French politicians can ever impose the adjustment required to keep the country’s promises affordable.

This is not yet a French version of the eurozone crisis. It is something more instructive: a mature democracy discovering that debt can postpone the argument over who gets to pay for the state until the argument becomes impossible to postpone.

France has become the developed world’s clearest casualty of soaring global yields. After years of cheap money, investors looking for weak links found a country carrying immense debt, taxes already near their practical limit and a political system incapable of imposing either spending cuts or higher taxes.

The warning has already begun. [France’s 10-year bond yield rose above 5% amid budget gridlock](/articles/2026-10-22-france-s-10-year-bond-yield-rises-above-5-amid-budget-gridlock/) last week, the first time it crossed that threshold since 2002, when France had far less debt and much stronger growth. On Thursday, the yield reached a 24-year high.

A sharp fall in U.S. Treasury yields did not pull France’s borrowing costs down. That divergence exposed the country-specific vulnerability. France was not merely riding a global rate move; it was failing to escape one.

First, the fiscal wall. Tax and other government revenue already equals 52% of GDP, more than in any developed country except oil-rich Norway and tiny Finland. There is little room to raise taxes without hurting economic activity and shrinking the revenue France expects to collect. The tax-and-spend fantasy cannot survive contact with the arithmetic.

Second, the political wall. Voters will not support either the spending cuts or tax increases needed to stabilize the debt. That leaves the government where it started: borrowing more, at higher interest rates, to finance promises it cannot afford. The market does not regard that as a temporary inconvenience. It regards it as regime risk: France has chosen a democratic political arrangement that makes fiscal repair exceptionally difficult.

Third, the arithmetic. The International Monetary Fund expects France’s net debt to pass 111% of GDP this year. The budget deficit is running at about 5% of GDP, while nominal growth is forecast to reach only 3% at best. Because taxes broadly rise with the economy, France still needs to borrow roughly 2% of GDP every year.

Two percent does not sound like an existential crisis. Applied to an already enormous debt pile, however, it becomes a ratchet. Cheap debt from the zero-interest-rate era is continually maturing and being replaced at much higher cost.

Were France paying 5% on everything it owed, it would add roughly 4% to 5% of GDP to the deficit every year. That is not a stabilizing deficit. It is a feedback loop in which higher yields increase borrowing costs, which pushes yields higher still. Refinancing costs enlarge the deficit; the deficit enlarges the debt; the debt makes each new borrowing more expensive.

None of this means France cannot pay. It means France cannot continue financing the state the way it has.

The financial cure is almost embarrassingly straightforward: cut spending, though not necessarily overnight, and show where balance comes from. Even a plan that did not reduce the deficit immediately would reassure markets if it credibly pointed toward balance within a decade. Even after meaningful cuts, French government spending would remain above Germany’s, where it accounts for a little more than half the economy. Lower interest costs would then help repair the deficit.

The political cure is much harder. Sovereign defaults almost always happen not because countries cannot pay, but because politicians refuse to make voters pay—through lower spending, higher taxes or both. France’s extreme politics, recurrent riots and parliamentary deadlock make that danger more concrete.

The country has borrowed in a currency it cannot control. Paris cannot simply order the Banque de France to buy French debt. If the crisis becomes severe, it will have to ask the European Central Bank for help. Euro membership limits one escape route and creates another: France cannot devalue its way out. The ECB would probably buy French debt well before markets reached panic, and it could cap much of the sovereign tail risk. It cannot supply France with the political capital required to cut the state.

The ECB can restrain a panic and buy France time. It cannot repair the arithmetic or replace political courage with an emergency program. Nor will central-bank support solve the underlying demand problem: markets will keep charging France for the privilege of financing habits that neither side of French politics is willing to abandon.

That makes France’s predicament uncomfortably similar to the emerging markets. It is also a reversal of France’s old role. During the eurozone crisis of 2012, France was one of the grown-ups, joining Germany to force Greece through a painful default and other weak countries through austerity. Italy’s 10-year spread over Germany reached 5.5 percentage points before its prime minister backed down and quit. France’s spread over Germany is now about 1.5 percentage points, the highest since 2012.

France is not being priced for a disorderly default. It is being priced for the possibility that its politics will postpone adjustment while the cost of adjustment rises.

The warning has already reached the currency. [French deficit concerns pushed the euro to its weakest level since May 2025](/articles/2026-10-05-french-deficit-concerns-push-euro-to-weakest-level-since-may-2025/). A stronger currency would not solve France’s fiscal problem, but a weaker one is the market’s preliminary verdict on the government’s room to maneuver.

There is time, but the next election may consume it. On current polling, Marine Le Pen of the National Rally is the likely next president, although her parliamentary support and therefore her power are far from assured. She has previously advocated lowering the pension age from 62 to 60, making France’s already expensive state pension still costlier. Her party helped force President Emmanuel Macron to reverse his signature increase of the pension age to 64.

A French president does not automatically control a parliament divided among three antagonistic blocs. Austerity cannot be delivered by presidential fiat: every retrenchment must win support across those blocs, making coalition support—not executive intent—the binding constraint.

Investors are not required to choose among those blocs. They only have to observe that none of them appears ready to tell voters who must surrender something. The next president will not automatically control the legislature, which means even a winner with the clearest fiscal mandate could be unable to deliver it. That is why markets charge a premium: not because they expect the government to disappear, but because they doubt it can make painful decisions.

The deeper disease is democratic. Roughly half the electorate apparently believes the government can finance itself by adding debt or taxing the other half of the electorate.

For much of the post-war era, that fantasy was sustainable. One side promised higher taxes and more public spending; the other promised lower taxes and smaller government. The gold standard constrained them, but only for a time. When those constraints disappeared in the early 1970s and borrowing costs collapsed in the 1980s, both sides could pursue their promises. Debt filled the gap.

Four decades later, the debt in many developed countries has become too large to buy off the voters. France has arrived first at the point where fiscal room is exhausted, though not yet at default. A 5% yield remains far below the level that would have emerging-market finance ministers adding the IMF on speed-dial. That is not an all-clear; it is the lag before market pain becomes political recognition.

For decades, debt could buy off voters cheaply. Now every election adds another layer of obligations while making the necessary cuts less tolerable. France is simply where this contradiction has become too large and too visible to hide.

The eventual resolution will not come because the bond market stages a dramatic run on Paris. It will come because a French government finally wins or loses an election over the size of the state.

At 5%, the yield is the market charging interest on a promise France has not yet broken. It has not inflicted enough pain to force recognition; that lag is precisely how the deadline drifts while borrowing costs compound. The bond market is not demanding a blood oath or an instant default. It is asking for arithmetic and a president willing to say it aloud.

Until then, the 5% yield is not the crisis. It is the countdown. France’s creditors do not need drama. They need arithmetic administered by politicians willing to lose an election over it.
