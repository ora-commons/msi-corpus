Ohio lawmakers smuggled an industry-backed clean-air repeal into the state budget. A judge did what Ohio’s budget-makers would not: apply the Constitution to what they had slipped into a spending bill. Franklin County Common Pleas Judge Julie Lynch stopped the raid.

In her order granting the injunction, Lynch wrote: “The Air Pollution Amendment has nothing to do with appropriations or state operations. Therefore, it violates the Constitution’s one-subject rule.” The precise holding was that the amendment lacked a constitutional connection to the budget. The precise remedy was an order barring state officials from enforcing the rider.

That was not a technical quibble. It did not hold that industrial emissions are harmless. It did not declare Ohio’s air-nuisance rule beyond future amendment. It enforced the boundary between spending legislation and a substantive rewrite of environmental law. The court made no finding about concealed motive. It did not need one. The text supplied the constitutional defect.

Ohio Constitution Article II, Section 3 provides: “No bill shall be passed containing more than one subject, except that the general subject may be expressed in the title, and all matters properly connected with such subject shall be embraced in one bill.” The final clause matters. A budget bill may carry measures connected to its subject. This amendment, according to Lynch, had none.

The consequence was not abstract. Donna Ballinger has used the rule against a neighboring 2,800-acre steel plant owned by Cleveland Cliffs in the Butler and Warren county area. A Dayton-area case involving a nuclear-waste disposal company produced a $1.3 million settlement. Ohio itself and private citizens have invoked the rule.

Calling that weapon a “nuisance rule” can make it sound quaint. It is not.

The primary text, Ohio Administrative Code Chapter 351-14, reaches the emission or placement into the atmosphere of substances that are injurious to or menace public health, safety or property, or that interfere with the use and enjoyment of property. It gives residents a legal route to challenge emissions from neighboring industrial operations. Ohio’s EPA-approved Clean Air Act plan confirms the rule as part of the state’s plan for implementing federal pollution law. These are legal tools separating a community from the consequences of living beside a polluter. (Ohio Admin. Code ch. 351-14; 40 C.F.R. pt. 52, subpt. AI.)

Then came the Air Pollution Amendment in Ohio’s General Appropriation Act. It directed the Ohio EPA to remove the nuisance rule from the state plan. Companion budget language barred the agency from considering measurements from privately owned air-monitoring devices when deciding whether to issue a civil penalty. The measure was not merely a statement of legislative preference. It commanded an agency to abandon an enforcement rule and another command disabled evidence the agency might use to enforce it. (H.B. 96, 136th Gen. Assem., Ohio 2025.)

Air is not less real when the sensor belongs to a neighbor.

The strongest defense of the monitoring restriction deserves a hearing. A privately operated sensor can be poorly sited, drift out of calibration, lack quality-control documentation or fail to establish which source produced a measured reading. A regulator has legitimate reasons to require validated instruments, consistent methods and proof adequate for a penalty. Excluding arbitrary data protects the integrity of enforcement and the rights of companies against penalties resting on defective measurements.

The strongest defense of the constitutional position is likewise serious. Budget legislation necessarily contains directives. The Constitution permits matters “properly connected” with the bill’s subject. Legislators can set agency priorities, direct staff to revise regulations and address administrative burdens without rewriting the law in the name of appropriations. Removing an obsolete state-plan rule might reduce litigation or conserve agency resources.

Neither defense answers the rider actually enacted. The monitoring restriction supplied no reliability standard, no distinction between raw and validated readings, and no exception for devices that pass objective tests. A rule requiring calibration and validation would address the concern. A categorical ban would prevent the agency from making that judgment at all. On the constitutional question, the court found no connection between this rider and either appropriations or state operations. The court did not condemn private monitoring in every case. It enjoined this blanket exclusion.

The political history comes from an Associated Press report carried by Signal Ohio. In 2019, SunCoke, the coke producer serving the steel industry, hired a law firm to petition the Environmental Protection Agency to remove Ohio’s nuisance rule from its Clean Air Act plan. SunCoke argued that the rule’s inclusion had been a technical “error.” The EPA under President Donald Trump granted the request. Environmental advocates sued, and the Biden administration reinstated the rule in February 2025.

The state then took up the same project. With support from SunCoke, Cleveland Cliffs and the state Chamber of Commerce, lawmakers inserted the amendment into the budget. Senate Finance Chairman Jerry Cirino, a Kirtland Republican, declined to identify which senator proposed it.

Industry support for the repeal was explicit. The court made no finding about who directed the legislative vote or why the measure was placed in the budget. Its holding was narrower and stronger. Whatever the sponsors’ political purpose, the amendment had no constitutional connection to the spending bill it rode into law.

The AP/Signal Ohio account also placed the rider within a broader Republican effort to limit community air data. [Main Street Independent’s review of the same industry-backed strategy in three states](https://www.mainstreetindependent.com/articles/2026-09-10-three-states-limit-community-air-data-under-industry-backed-laws/) found the same sequence: weaken enforcement, restrict proof, then market the retreat as regulatory reform. It is not reform. It is the erosion of enforcement by budget amendment.

An honest defense of changing the air-nuisance rule would proceed openly. Legislators could introduce a freestanding measure, hold hearings, establish standards for any monitoring data they wished to exclude, and use the proper process for revising Ohio’s federal-law plan. The public would then see the proposed policy, the evidence and the interests behind it. A constitutional ruling does not decide whether pollution controls should change. It decides how Ohio must seek such a change.

The Ohio Attorney General’s office is reviewing the ruling and has not said whether it will appeal. The injunction should stand. Any renewed repeal effort must be defended in daylight rather than smuggled through another procedural disguise.

The air-nuisance rule remains in force. The repeal attempt failed. The Constitution worked. The legislature’s scheme did not.

Clean air should not have to be smuggled into a budget. The budget should not be used to smuggle clean air out of Ohio. The air outside Ohio’s steel plants and other industrial sites does not pause because politicians ran out of room in a budget bill.
