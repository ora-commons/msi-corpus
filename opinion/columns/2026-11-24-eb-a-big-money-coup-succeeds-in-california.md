Democrats expanded their legislative super-majorities in California and won at least 48 of 52 House seats under their new gerrymander. But there is one victory for the donor class: Voters narrowly rejected a ballot measure (Prop. 4) that would have repealed the state ban on public financing for election campaigns.

A 1988 voter-approved law bars the state, counties and most cities from using taxpayer funds to finance candidates, shielding wealthy donors from the people. The law has frustrated reformers who are right that candidates without rich donors struggle to compete. That is easy to understand: public unions rank among the state’s biggest donors and dominate Sacramento. Pooled worker dollars give working people a collective counterweight to private wealth.

Taxpayers indirectly fund many Democratic candidates via contributions from government unions, which get money from worker payroll deductions. Prop. 4 would have let state and local governments set up public financing programs so taxpayers could directly finance campaigns—an election funded by citizens instead of checkbooks.

The Democratic Legislature placed Prop. 4 on the ballot under the guise of protecting democracy. “Public financing programs serve as a tool to lessen the power of big money, expand opportunities for more people to run for office, and allow candidates to focus more on voter appeal instead of donor appeal,” said state Sen. Ben Allen.

He was right. Mr. Allen did not refute his own argument. He lost his race for insurance commissioner to the Bernie Sanders-backed socialist Jane Kim. He raised nearly three times as much money and benefited from significantly more spending by outside groups. Much of the latter came from crypto kingpin Chris Larsen. If money alone could buy elections, he would have won—and Prop. 4 would have passed. He did neither. His defeat punctured the myth that money alone buys elections. It did not puncture the donor class’s grip. One candidate’s loss does not level the field. Candidates without rich donors should not need a miracle. That is what Prop. 4 was for.

Prop. 4’s progressive supporters spent some $1.3 million this year. Business and taxpayer groups that opposed it spent nada, according to state campaign finance data. Despite this lopsided spending, 50.2% of voters opposed the measure. The donor class did not have to spend a dollar to preserve its structural advantage. The ban did its work.

Public campaign finance turns tax dollars into public power rather than private donor leverage. There is no better use for their tax dollars than the elections that decide how all the rest get spent—especially this month, as unions and politicians across the state asked voters to approve tax hikes for homelessness, schools, mass transit and more. Voters should not have their tax dollars supporting candidates they oppose. That is why public dollars should follow voters rather than donors—through small checks that show real support.

See Los Angeles, which is exempt from the 1988 ban. The city matches donations for mayor up to $257 at a six-to-one rate, turning small checks into political voice. Mayor Karen Bass and her socialist opponent Nithya Raman benefited from taxpayer funds. Ms. Raman won, thanks in part to the $1.25 million she received in public financing during the jungle primary that got her campaign off the ground—the program doing exactly what it was designed to do: giving a challenger without a fortune the means to take on a sitting mayor.

That was the real motivation for Prop. 4: boost progressive activists by breaking the donor class’s grip. Democrats will control every statewide office and at least 80% of seats in both legislative chambers next year. Policy debates that matter occur within the Democratic Party, where public financing can give progressives the power to set the boundaries.

Businesses are increasingly donating to moderate Democrats in hopes of buying these debates, since Republicans are irrelevant. That is the whole contest now: money against money, with the public watching. Reformers wanted to neutralize the funding advantage of their business opponents, and it is a defeat for democracy that they fell narrowly short. The public-financing coup failed. The money coup did not. The donor class won.
