FCC Chairman Brendan Carr tried to silence ABC for mocking Donald Trump. U.S. District Judge Loren AliKhan said as much on Wednesday. She granted ABC a preliminary injunction—a temporary order requiring the FCC to stand down while the case proceeds—halting the agency’s early review of eight ABC television station licenses. The accelerated scrutiny can put a broadcaster at risk of losing its licenses. AliKhan wrote that ABC was likely to succeed on its First Amendment claim: the Commission was considering revoking the licenses as retaliation for programming critical of President Trump.

The Commission’s strongest case is that it may review broadcast licenses and ask whether Disney’s diversity, equity and inclusion initiatives violate its prohibition on unlawful discrimination. ABC cannot convert that enforcement authority into blanket immunity from oversight. AliKhan accepted that boundary. She denied ABC’s request for a broader injunction barring FCC threats over editorial decisions. Her order stopped the early license review, but not the separate investigations into Disney’s employment practices and “The View.” The latter concerns equal-time rules, which require stations carrying political candidates on non-news programs during elections to provide similar airtime to their opponents. A communications lawyer defending the FCC would recognize that account as its strongest case.

The sequence mattered. The dates carry the finding. The Commission opened the early review on April 28, the day after President Trump criticized remarks by Jimmy Kimmel, whose late-night program frequently makes fun of him. Trump had already suggested that ABC should lose its broadcast licenses because it did not carry his July election-integrity address. ABC alleged retaliation in its [August lawsuit](/articles/2026-08-18-disney-s-abc-sues-fcc-over-license-challenges-the-view-probe/). In her order, AliKhan found ABC likely to succeed on the First Amendment claim. She wrote that Carr “has taken President Trump’s rhetoric as his marching orders.” The FCC’s DEI-based explanation was not “credible on the record before the court.” She called the case “not a close case.” The announced discrimination inquiry was the cover. The trigger was President Trump’s criticism of Jimmy Kimmel.

The law has barred government retaliation for protected speech since long before this chairman. *Hartman v. Moore*, 547 U.S. 250 (2006), protects the right to criticize government officials and their conduct. The authority is real. The announced motive, on this record, is not enforcement. When the official explanation collapses on the record, the sequence carries the retaliation claim. Here, the Commission opened licensing scrutiny one day after the President’s criticism, after the President had suggested ABC should lose its licenses. AliKhan found the stated DEI ground not credible and the First Amendment claim likely to succeed. That is not a dispute over whether agencies can investigate. It is a dispute over whether the FCC can convert a license into presidential leverage.

The license is the choke point. *Red Lion Broadcasting Co. v. FCC*, 395 U.S. 367 (1969), upheld the FCC’s authority to regulate broadcasters in the public interest. A company can answer an adverse judgment with money. A broadcaster facing a hostile early review can risk the licenses it needs to broadcast. The Commission did not need to revoke anything to exert pressure through eight licenses. Leverage did the work.

This is a machine, not an episode. The FCC was weaponizing eight licenses to punish disfavored speech. The lever is not ABC-specific. Any early license review that follows a President’s grievance can become an instrument of presidential pressure, whether or not a formal revocation follows. On this record, a regulator that opened a license review the day after the President’s latest grievance was not doing enforcement. It was doing punishment, wearing enforcement’s paperwork.

The machine did not stop with the injunction. The separate employment investigation and the equal-time inquiry into “The View,” a program that often criticizes Trump, remain open. Whether the program’s format triggers those obligations is genuinely contested. The order left that question alone.

Her line between enforcement and retaliation was exact. “No one doubts that the Commission may enforce the law in the manner it sees fit. But in doing so, Defendants may not retaliate against or punish Plaintiffs and other broadcast license holders for disfavored speech.” The FCC can enforce anti-discrimination law without making President Trump’s approval a condition of ABC’s licenses. A lawful application would require the Commission to prove that the early review rests on the discrimination inquiry it announced rather than on the President’s criticism of Kimmel or ABC. The separate probes can proceed on their own records. The license review cannot carry the President’s objection to ABC’s programming. The injunction did not require ABC to prove that Disney’s employment practices or any exchange on “The View” violates the First Amendment. It targeted the Commission’s use of licensing power to punish disfavored speech.

One threat has crumbled. The files remain open, and the chairman who opened them is still chairman. Carr may enforce the law. He may not make eight broadcast licenses the price of mocking the President.
