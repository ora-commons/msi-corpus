Both parties are stealing from the next generation to buy this election.

Here are the numbers. Publicly held federal debt reached 100 percent of gross domestic product in the fiscal year that ended September 30 — the first time in eighty years, more than triple its level in 2001, with the postwar record of 106 percent expected to fall within a few years on current projections. The annual deficit [climbed 12 percent in fiscal 2026 to just shy of $2 trillion](/articles/2026-10-08-u-s-budget-deficit-climbs-12-to-nearly-2-trillion-in-fiscal-2026/), or 6.2 percent of GDP — a seventh consecutive year above 5 percent, the longest such streak in at least a century outside war, recession, and emergency. Interest on the debt has more than doubled in four years and now costs more than the entire defense budget. None of this is hidden.

The arithmetic underneath has been stable for two generations, in one direction only. Congressional Budget Office data show federal spending above 23 percent of GDP, up from about 18 percent in the 1960s, while revenue sits near 17 percent — a structural gap of six points, covered every year by borrowing. Neither party proposes closing it from its own side of the ledger. The residue is debt service — now larger than defense, the one federal spending category that buys nothing visible, protects no one visibly, and therefore campaigns for nothing and against nothing.

The campaigns have decided to say none of this. AdImpact, the firm that tracks political advertising, counted 30 ads mentioning the debt or the deficit this cycle, down from 140 in 2018; the topic ranks 52nd among subjects in this year's elections. Two closing arguments went out this week, one from each party, and the debt appears in neither — while both agendas add to it. One is drafted around the Iran war, the other around transgender athletes. Both parties dropped the same subject in the same cycle. The candidates are not unaware. Steny Hoyer, retiring after nearly 46 years in the House, put the finding plainly: "Nobody asks me about the debt. I don't think they ask any other members about the debt." Mark Sanford staked a Senate primary campaign this summer on the debt reaching a tipping point, and reported afterward that the sale was hard to make. The omission is the finding.

I produced baselines for thirty-five years. A baseline is arithmetic — the law as written, projected forward, expirations included — not a warning and not an opinion. The arithmetic did not change between the cycle that produced 140 debt ads and the one that produced 30. What changed is not the data. What changed is the audience.

What the silence buys is itemized. President Trump has pledged a $5,000 check to every adult if his party wins on Tuesday, at an estimated cost of $1.2 trillion — sixty percent of a single year's deficit, committed in a sentence with no financing attached. The opposing platform is priced the same way: reversing the Medicaid cuts, restoring the health-law subsidies, repealing tariffs that currently raise revenue. Each is a cost. None is paid for. The financing, in every case, is the debt both parties have stopped mentioning — and a pledge with no identified payer is charged to the only party excluded from Tuesday's election: the people who will service the interest on it, at whatever rate the bond market sets, without having been old enough to object.

The convergence was built, not inherited. A budget deal that raised taxes helped cost George H.W. Bush the 1992 election, and his party adopted a no-net-tax-increases posture that has held since. In 2005 a Republican president proposed touching Social Security and supplied the opposition with its attack ads; in 2012 his party's nominee ran on restraining Medicare and lost with it; by 2016 the party's standard-bearer had adopted the other party's position outright — no cuts to Social Security or Medicare, ever — and the two largest programs in the budget have barely been discussed in a presidential campaign since. The other coalition made the mirror conversion on the revenue side, settling on a pledge that no one making under $400,000 a year would pay a penny more — and, fearful of repeating the slow recovery of the early 2010s, priced its 2021 relief law at $1.9 trillion without full offsets; the inflation spike that followed was billed to everyone. In 2025 the governing coalition cut spending and cut taxes by more. The choice left on the table narrowed to a party that will not raise taxes on 100 percent of Americans and a party that will not raise taxes on 98 percent of them. Joseph Thorndike, the tax historian, dates the loss of deficit shame to the 1970s and 1980s: "Once you loosen the norm a little bit," he said, "it's only a matter of time until it frays completely."

The result on the ballot is both parties offering voters more spending and lower taxes at once. Paul Ryan, who ran on Medicare restraint in 2012 and later served as House speaker, called the current President "the apex populist" and dated his party's fiscal consensus to its death at Trump's rise. Jessica Riedl, a former Republican budget aide now at the Brookings Institution, described the settlement the ratchet produced: "The feeling is very clearly that all fiscal sacrifice should be borne by someone else — the rich, foreigners, immigrants, the Defense Department." This is not drift. It is a stable equilibrium, constructed over forty years: each party learned that the cheapest vote is the vote bought with someone else's money, and each perfected the method.

The intellectual license for all of this arrived with an expiration date, and the date has passed. In 2019, Olivier Blanchard, then chief economist at the International Monetary Fund, told the American Economic Association that rich countries could carry far more debt than convention assumed so long as the government's borrowing cost ran below the economy's growth rate — when growth outruns interest, the debt shrinks relative to the economy without anyone paying it down. Rates stayed near zero and central banks bought the bonds themselves, the crisis never came, and politicians heard the license and not the condition. Then rates rose, and growth did not keep pace. Blanchard has since said so on the record: "The very low rates — the negative r-g for a long time — probably created a false sense of security. I have to plead guilty here." Note what the confession covers. The finding was conditional — cheap debt, while rates stayed low. The politics kept the conclusion and discarded the condition. That is intellectual laundering in its standard form: a technical finding, qualified by its own author, converted into a slogan — deficits have no consequences — and deployed for a decade. Jürgen Stark, formerly chief economist at the European Central Bank, described the residue: "Once the bad lessons are learned, they're impossible to unlearn."

The condition got detached again, on schedule. [Long-term Treasury yields touched 24-year highs this month](/articles/2026-10-11-treasury-yield-hits-quarter-century-high-as-trump-bets-on-ai-growth/), and the administration's answer is to bet that artificial intelligence grows the economy fast enough to carry the debt — that an unplanned productivity surge will service borrowing no one plans to slow. That is not a forecast. It is the 1981 argument restated. A conditional finding, cited for a decade without its condition, is not economics. It is a permission slip.

The promise that nobody has to pay is forty-five years old, and it has never survived contact with a score. David Stockman, who assembled the 1981 tax cut, spent his retirement confessing it — a book's worth of concession that the books had been cooked and the savings invented. Bruce Bartlett, who helped draft them, later called the claim that the cuts paid for themselves what it was: false. In 2017 the Joint Committee on Taxation scored that era's last big tax law at $1.46 trillion over ten years on a conventional basis (JCX-69-17) — conventional meaning behavioral responses are counted and the size of the economy held fixed. The dynamic supplement credited the same law with roughly $385 billion of macroeconomic feedback, about a quarter of the cost; the outside models favored by the law's advocates assumed a growth effect substantially larger, and the growth they promised has not arrived at anything like that scale. In the years since, the outturn has tracked the official number. Each round was sold as self-financing. Each score said otherwise. The score is the score.

The 2017 law's individual provisions were scheduled to expire in 2025 — an expiration written in so the bill could fit the Senate's reconciliation rules, which forbid deficit increases beyond the budget window. The extension was then argued on a baseline that assumes scheduled expirations never happen. The statute, Section 257 of the Congressional Budget Act of 1974, requires the opposite assumption: current law, expirations included. A baseline is not a forecast. It is a record of what the law on the books produces. One of these is a budget convention. The other is a press release. If the policy is permanent, the cost is permanent. If the cost is not permanent, the policy is not permanent. Pick one.

Symmetry, as always, before anyone reaches for a partisan explanation. The inflation bill of 2021 and the tax cuts of 2025 are the same operation run from opposite sides of the aisle: spend without taxing your own coalition, and let the bond market hold the difference. The difference is no longer costless. The International Monetary Fund projects advanced-economy deficits averaging 5.2 percent of GDP this year, double the level of a decade ago, with public debt near 110 percent — the highest sustained level since the Napoleonic wars. France has burned through four prime ministers in three years; [its ten-year borrowing cost is the highest since 2002](/articles/2026-10-06-france-s-10-year-borrowing-cost-climbs-to-highest-level-since-2002/); and the politics answer the squeeze by promising more — Marine Le Pen pledges a retirement age of 60, and Jean-Luc Mélenchon proposes taking the debt held by the central bank and, in his phrase, throwing it "in the fire." Germany has loosened its debt rules to borrow for defense; Japan is cutting taxes into rising yields; Britain carries its heaviest sustained debt since the early 1960s. France shows the sequence: investors lost hope of a political fix and sold the bonds before the arithmetic moved.

The United States holds genuine defenses — the world's reserve currency, the safest asset in a storm — but the repricing is already underway, and it arrived without either campaign mentioning why. The reason the hawks' old warnings never landed is on the record: the non-revolt was manufactured, and the yield record is what the withdrawal of that buyer looks like. A reserve currency buys time. Time is not a plan.

When carry costs crowd everything else — and interest now exceeds defense — the squeeze does not fall on the tax cuts. It falls on food inspection, on national parks, and eventually on the programs for the elderly; the projections put Social Security at the point where it cannot pay full scheduled benefits in 2032, within the term of the Senate class elected this week. That is the bill, and it is denominated in the programs working families use now and will use later.

There are three responses to a six-point structural gap: raise revenue, cut spending, or carry the debt and pay the interest. There is no fourth response. Both parties have spent two decades ruling out the first for their donors and the second for their voters' programs, which converts every future adjustment into cuts to whatever is left. The honest version of either platform would score its pledges the way legislation is scored — a ten-year cost on a current-law convention, meaning the law as scheduled to operate, expirations included, printed beside the advertisement. And it begins on the revenue side — at the top of the distribution, in the tax expenditures that concentrate there, spending delivered through the tax code — not because austerity is a virtue, but because the tax side is where the borrowed money went. A party that cannot put a spending number and a revenue number in the same sentence is not proposing a fiscal plan. It is proposing a press release.

Nothing here was concealed. Both platforms were published in the same season the debt crossed 100 percent of GDP. The check is a promise. The 2032 insolvency date is arithmetic. The promise is on the ballot. The arithmetic is on the calendar regardless, priced in interest and billed to people who are children now. There is no other way to read the record.

The fix arrives, if it arrives, only after the bond market makes the sale for them — the demonstration France already put on. The silence about the second number is not neglect. It is the plan.
