Pentagon, you had the count. You kept it.

274 buildings damaged across 24 bases in six months of war, and the count is a floor under the true number.

The roofs fell at the places you put your people. Under them were bunks made up tight, photographs taped over desks, the ordinary rooms of soldiers' lives.

Your mouth stayed shut. The lenses were pulled back. Tehran's broadcasters did the announcing. Strangers did the counting — a newspaper, a European camera, ten meters to a pixel.

You declined to say which. You declined to say how many.

The households at the gates are waiting on you.

The war with Iran began on February 28 and ran to its six-month mark on August 28. On October 31 the Wall Street Journal published what it calls the most comprehensive public accounting of that damage to date: 274 damaged structures at 24 installations with a U.S. military presence across the Middle East. The Pentagon has declined to give specific assessments of damage at installations housing U.S. forces. The major U.S. commercial satellite companies stopped releasing high-resolution imagery of military installations in the region early in the war. The Journal built the count from the European Space Agency's Copernicus Sentinel-2 imagery — ten meters to a pixel, returning roughly every five days — running a statistical method called anomalous change detection against a prewar baseline drawn from the quiet of a year earlier. More than 30,000 flagged locations were reviewed by hand. Signs of damage had to persist for at least two weeks. Every finding was corroborated by NASA or European fire and flare data, by verified ground imagery, or by higher-resolution satellite pictures. Five independent analysts at three institutions helped develop and test the method, and one of them reviewed every finding and confirmed it. The Journal says its tally almost certainly undercounts the damage.

The list of installations came from a 2024 Congressional Research Service report and from 5,400 posts the Journal archived from four Iranian state-affiliated outlets. Tehran's announced strikes coincide with damage to more than 250 of the identified structures. For 26 buildings, the corroboration is the enemy's own press release — a statement from Iranian state-affiliated media announcing a strike on that installation. Among the claims that named a kind of building, the Journal counted the ones that named troop housing. The analysis shows where and roughly when, not what caused it. Many of the installations are shared with, or owned by, allied nations, and not every building under those roofs is American or used only by American forces. The Journal sorted the damage into phases around the ceasefire and measured what the strikes took — the average damaged structure ran 12,000 square feet before the ceasefire and under 5,000 after it. Sam Lair of the Foreign Policy Research Institute, who reviewed and confirmed the findings, said: "The restrictions on satellite imagery in the region have made it pretty hard to get a real sense of the scale and extent of the damage done by Iran."
