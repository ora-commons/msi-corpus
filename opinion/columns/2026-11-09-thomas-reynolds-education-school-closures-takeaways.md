Vallejo school officials sacrifice children’s education to balance a $36 million deficit.

Superintendent Rubén Aurelio has the strongest version of the case: Vallejo built its school system for nearly 18,000 pupils in 2006 and now enrolls about 9,300. The closure of a naval base in the 1990s deepened the loss. A district built for twice as many students cannot treat its buildings, transportation network, and staffing obligations as if they automatically shrank by half. Concentrating students and resources at the surviving campuses may improve the schools that remain. “Right-sizing” is a defensible description of that arithmetic. The problem begins when arithmetic is mistaken for a closure plan.

The national record strengthens Aurelio’s case. An [Associated Press analysis of federal education data](https://apnews.com/article/education-school-closures-takeaways-128c1d86bcd2463f1e5f2d657d85c861) collected by Stanford economist Thomas Dee and sociologist Jaymes Pyne found that public-school enrollment fell by more than 1.4 million students, roughly 3 percent, between fall 2019 and 2024. The number of schools declined only to about its pre-pandemic level. Districts are not closing faster than enrollment losses. They are lagging them.

Lower birth rates drove most of that decline. Separate [reporting in this record](https://www.vallejo.k12.ca.us/) documents how intensified federal immigration enforcement has accelerated enrollment losses at public schools, while private-school vouchers and homeschooling have added to the contraction. None of those changes restores a child to a classroom or a dollar to the district. They also do not make every closure lawful, wise, or financially sound. Vallejo’s deficit is real. Its answer still has to be costed.

California supplies the rule and the boundary. There is no federal constitutional right to a public-school education in this record. [California Constitution article IX, section 2](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=CONS&sectionNum=SEC.%202.&article=IX) does something different and more modest: it directs the Legislature to establish and maintain a system of common schools. It does not promise a child a particular campus, teacher, or bus route. It makes the system a public duty rather than a favor.

The operative policy instrument is school closure and consolidation: close a campus and move its pupils, transportation, staff, and costs elsewhere. Under [California Education Code sections 35160 and 35160.1](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=EDC&sectionNum=35160.1.), that power exists only within the Legislature’s conditions, including the required closure process and findings. Those are enforceable state-law constraints. A school board may close a school. It may not replace a cost finding with a slogan.

The board’s [closure resolutions and findings](https://www.vallejo.k12.ca.us/page/board-meetings), its [adopted budget and staffing plan](https://www.vallejo.k12.ca.us/page/budget), and its oversight-exit documents are the primary record. The resolutions establish five campus closures since 2025, already a quarter of the district’s schools. The budget and staffing plan identify the $36 million deficit and place up to one-third of all staff positions at risk. The district’s enrollment and transportation data show the scale of the operation: nearly 18,000 pupils in 2006, about 9,300 now.

The same record must be read with the severance and security costs that follow closure. Those entries matter because shutting a building lowers one expense while moving others. The primary documents establish the deficit and the closures. On the record cited here, they do not establish net savings after the transportation, severance, and security obligations are counted. Exit from state oversight changes the district’s administrative status. It cannot make children smaller or transfer the district’s educational duties back to the state.

Associated Press reporting on the broader closure research reaches the same mechanism from the national record. Busing displaced students, severance for laid-off staff, and security for empty facilities often consume the projected savings. Consolidation is not savings. It is the relocation of an obligation from a building to a bus route, a payroll, or a receiving classroom.

Vallejo can keep demonstrating its seriousness by cutting the places where the remaining children learn. That is not seriousness. It is the conversion of every avoided building expense into a claim that the closure paid for itself.

The cost arrived at Glen Cove Elementary. Penny’s previous elementary school closed. She moved to Glen Cove, where her new class contained 32 children. Weeks into the academic year, the first-year teacher resigned effective immediately, leaving the district to rely on a long-term substitute. The closure arithmetic treats closure, transfer, staffing, and family response as separable. A student’s school year is not. The district lost the employee needed to make its own cost-cutting plan work. The closure is not the savings. It is the receipt.

Studies from the University of Chicago Consortium on School Research find that displaced students often experience long-term drops in reading and mathematics. The effects are greater when they land at low-performing schools. “It takes a while to recover, if they recover at all,” Marisa de la Torre said in the reporting. Closures also make it harder to attract teachers who already see unstable employment inside the district.

Closure also feeds back into the budget. The Associated Press analysis reports that school closures can prompt families to choose charter schools, private schools, homeschooling, or neighboring districts. In Vallejo, some parents chose homeschooling for the fall or planned to sell their homes and move. Because schools receive funding according to enrollment, departing families can take revenue with them. The district then faces a smaller revenue base and another closure that appears more necessary. This is not a one-time accounting correction. It is a self-executing closure regime.

[Big Local News at Stanford University](https://biglocalnews.org/) counted 1,156 school closures during the last academic year, the highest number since the pandemic. The closures are spreading faster than the public argument that enrollment loss has already done the necessary work. [Bellwether and WestEd](https://bellwether.org/) estimate that declining enrollment could cost schools $11.5 billion in state revenue annually by 2030–31, potentially prompting layoffs, program cuts, and still more closures. Lydia Rainey, a research principal at the Center on Reinventing Public Education, says states are not about to open their coffers for schools.

Those facts explain the pressure. They do not supply an accounting rule that makes every closure beneficial. A deficit justifies looking for savings. It does not justify counting gross avoided costs while leaving transferred obligations uncounted.

An honest closure decision must compare the full bill before the doors close, require demonstrated net savings after transportation, severance, and security, protect instructional continuity, and show that displaced students will land in schools equipped to serve them. Those are my policy conclusions, not findings contained in Vallejo’s resolutions. Permanent closure should be the last costed step, not the first reflex.

Vallejo calls it right-sizing. Penny’s new class had 32 children before its teacher resigned in October. The closure is not the savings. It is the receipt.

Vallejo school officials sacrifice children’s education to balance a $36 million deficit. Unless the district counts the full bill before the doors close, it is choosing children as the line item.
