The filing is not a scandal. It is a census of what has already been lost. The Guardian reported on September 22nd that Donald Trump made more than a thousand stock trades in July — over four hundred purchases and seven hundred sales, valued somewhere between $79 million and $270 million — and that on July 20th he sold Amazon and Microsoft in transactions each worth up to $25 million while buying shares in Intuit and Salesforce. The disclosure law requires a date and a value band. It does not require an exact figure, a reason, or a word about whether the man who can move markets with a tariff announcement had any interest in the outcome. That is the law as it stands. The White House says the portfolio is independently managed by third-party firms using automated model portfolios, and that neither the president nor his family "has any ability to direct, influence, or provide input" on the trades. That explanation is supposed to settle the matter. It is, instead, the measure of how far the standard has fallen from what the office once required.

The honest case for what Trump describes is not trivial. A president cannot be expected to freeze his personal finances on the day he takes the oath. Automated model portfolios invested through diversified index funds are what tens of millions of Americans use, and requiring a sitting president to liquidate everything and hold only Treasury bonds would impose a financial penalty on public service that would, over time, ensure only the independently wealthy could afford the office. That is not a small thing. The country should not design a system in which the cost of serving is ruin, because the effect would be to make the presidency a preserve of those who never needed the salary — which it increasingly is, and which is itself a wound the republic sustains every time it narrows the field of who can serve.

But a passive index fund is not a blind trust. A blind trust, managed by an independent trustee with no communication to the beneficiary, is the instrument designed to sever the connection between a public official's private gain and his public action. What Trump has described is a model portfolio managed by firms whose independence he asserts but the public cannot verify, invested in sectors his administration shapes every day — trade, technology, defense, energy. On July 20th, the same day he sold Amazon and Microsoft and bought Intuit and Salesforce, his administration was making decisions that moved every one of those companies. The passive-indexing defense says the trades were automated. The question a self-governing people must ask is not whether they were automated but whether a structure that permits the president to profit from the same sectors he governs is compatible with the trust the office demands. A man who can move markets and whose portfolio moves with them is not in a conflict that disclosure resolves. He is in a conflict that disclosure merely names — and then sends the country home.

What has been lost is not a rule against insider trading. It is the older understanding that public office is a vocation, not an opportunity — that when a man swears to serve the common good, he submits his financial life to the office rather than allowing the office to serve as the engine of his private accumulation. Catholic social teaching calls this the social mortgage on property: the principle that even legitimate ownership carries duties that override private interest when the common good is at stake. If that is true of a family farm, and it is, how much more true is it of the most powerful office on earth? The president does not own the economy. He stewards a nation whose public life depends on the credibility of the people who govern it. When his disclosure shows a thousand trades in a month and his spokesperson says a computer made them, the country is asked to believe not that the president is honest but that his office is irrelevant — that the power to move markets and the profit from market movement have been separated by an algorithm he selected, managed by firms he chose, disclosed on a form he filed. The alibi is the architecture. The architecture is the alibi.

The House of Representatives passed the Stop Insider Trading Act this summer, a bill that would prohibit members of Congress and their families from trading individual stocks while in office. It was a meaningful step. It does not cover the president. It does not cover the vice president. The bill's text was drafted to exclude the executive branch entirely. The people who drafted the exemption will tell you the presidency presents complications a congressman's portfolio does not. Perhaps. But the effect is that the man who made more trades in the first six months of his second term than all of Congress combined is the one person the reform does not reach. This is not hypocrisy as a personal failing. This is what it looks like when a covenant is allowed to unravel from the center outward — when the people with the standing to bind the most powerful are the ones who have exempted him.

I used to work a desk where the contracts were abstract paper claims on corn and cattle grown by people I knew. The distance between the trade and the field was the whole point of the instrument — it made the risk liquid and the profit anonymous. That is exactly the distance a president's portfolio creates between the oath and the accumulation, between the public act and the private gain. The difference is that the farmer had no choice about the distance I imposed. A country still does.

What the people already know — and have known for some time — is that the separation between public duty and private profit has been abandoned. They do not need a survey to confirm what the filing shows. They need leaders who will submit their financial lives to the offices they hold with the same fidelity they already expect of one another. In Friendship, Wisconsin, the co-op board members sit across the table from their neighbors and their financial interests are visible to anyone who cares to look. The parish council submits its books to the diocese. The volunteer fire department holds its meeting in the same room where the township votes on the tax levy. These are small things, and they are not sufficient to govern a nation. But they are the places where the habit of submission — the habit of putting the office before the portfolio — is still practiced, still enforced by the community that depends on it, and still understood as the cost of serving. A president's financial life should be no less transparent than a co-op manager's. A blind trust is not a punishment. It is the price of the oath. And a thousand trades in a single month is not evidence of innocence. It is evidence that the price has been waived.
