The Trump administration is weaponizing public history to indoctrinate schoolchildren.

A commuter on the Orange Line had a document open on the laptop of the man seated beside him. In the last week of October, the commuter photographed “Smithsonian Institution Renewal Plan,” sent the pictures to [ProPublica](https://www.propublica.org/article/dc-metro-smithsonian-american-hi), and watched as reporters identified the man in the gray tartan suit as Sam Bock, a 27-year-old associate policy adviser with the White House’s Domestic Policy Council.

Bock’s office produced the administration’s July attack on the Smithsonian. The White House and Bock did not answer questions about the plan. The Smithsonian declined to comment.

A draft is not an enacted order. It is also not harmless brainstorming. It is a written blueprint for taking control of a national museum, its curators, its grant policy, its public exhibits and its free K–12 curriculum.

The 25-item list calls for firing National Museum of American History director Anthea Hartig and a dozen other top administrators and curators. It supplies titles for “The Riches of Our Western Civilization Inheritance” and “Great Families of American History,” plus a “Colossus Statue” of George Washington at the entrance.

The rest of the plan would end the museum’s reliance on a diversity style guide, halt the traveling exhibition “The Bias Inside Us,” review grants to “woke third parties” such as the American Alliance of Museums, and replace the immigration module “Becoming US” with a founders-focused curriculum titled something like “Architects of Liberty: The Heroic Journey of America’s Founders” OR “Profiles in Liberty: Washington and America’s Heroic Men of Courage.”

The draft puts it exactly as a national-history curriculum for children, with options and all. The administration’s policy apparatus could not choose between two titles. It could choose which children would receive which version of their country.

A curator argues for an exhibition. The draft dictates the curator’s employment, the exhibit’s title and the lesson assigned to children.

Someone also penciled in “announce exciting cool exhibit on D-Day” beside “no X rated exhibitions policy.” The latter had a likely identifiable target: the chrome-and-rubber harness in a gay history exhibit. A whole community’s history, reduced to its most shocking prop, then made the object of an executive-branch prohibition.

On July 4, the White House released its 162-page critique of the museum. Back-to-back congressional hearings followed, along with an executive order requiring warning signs outside the Smithsonian announcing the institution’s supposed “ideological capture.” The signs have not gone up. The pressure helped prompt Secretary Lonnie Bunch’s departure. In September, Bunch [defended the institution against this campaign and received a standing ovation for it](/articles/2026-09-26-bunch-defends-smithsonian-against-trump-attacks-gets-standing-ovation/); by October, he was gone.

The administration’s own report supplies the contradiction. It says the museum should tell “the whole story of a nation,” including “the good and the bad,” without whitewashing slavery or Jim Crow. The draft answers that promise with the whole approved story.

Unauthorized passages are cut. Authorized heroes get permanent exhibits. Disagreement becomes a bias. The draft is not curation. It is lying to children: replacing a contested record with an official answer before the child can enter the archive.

That is an ownership claim.

Until President Trump tried to remove National Portrait Gallery director Kim Sajet last year, no president had tried to remove a Smithsonian executive. Sajet left after the institution resisted. Now the draft calls for firing the director of the American History museum and a dozen more senior employees and curators.

The Smithsonian’s 17-member board is led by the chief justice of the Supreme Court and includes the vice president, three members of Congress from each party and nine private citizens. Its private-citizen seats are expiring without replacements. Its secretary’s chair is empty. The institution encompasses 20 museums beyond the American History museum, receives more than $1 billion a year from the federal government, employs several hundred people at that museum, houses several million artifacts and draws close to 2 million visitors annually.

The draft would turn all of it into an instrument of presidential control.

My parents raised three children in Lansdale and took us to St. Stanislaus, the Polish national parish formed by immigrant workers. They did not assume that public schools, parish institutions and libraries belonged to one family. They treated them as common goods. The draft turns “no family owns it” into “the executive branch decides.”

At my kitchen table, the numbers are plainer. Eva is four. Ben is one. Childcare for both costs $2,400 a month, and our combined take-home pay is $8,800. That is $2,400 ÷ $8,800 = 27.3 percent before the 7 percent mortgage, utilities, groceries and everything else.

I am writing from this household, not from a national sample. Two incomes and help from David’s grandmother’s estate gave us the down payment. That budget has no slack for the draft’s unpaid bill; it gets paid in parent and teacher time.

Rachel Cusk wrote that the demand on a mother is to “merely to be there”—not leisure, but a condition that leaves nowhere else to be. Put an ideological curriculum beside that demand and the unpaid work becomes legible. The institution supplies the approved answer; the parent supplies context, questions and emotional labor when a child notices what was left out. The museum cannot dictate the child’s reaction. It can arrange for every adult around that child to absorb the contradiction.

Swift put the mechanism on record twice. On *folklore*, “the last great american dynasty” examines how an inherited house shapes who gets treated as eccentric and who gets treated as illegitimate. “Great Families of American History” would commission the dynasty wing from the federal government. The draft pairs the dismissal of staff with a predetermined exhibit slate because the story follows ownership. The narrator buys the house and says, “I had a marvellous time ruining everything.” The Renewal Plan is the same insight with the self-awareness deleted. That is an interpretive reading, not a claim that Swift wrote a blueprint for the Smithsonian.

The catalog’s other diagnostic is “You’re On Your Own, Kid”: government hands the family an approved story while leaving the family to absorb every contradiction the story creates. The song’s rescue is lateral—friendship, not public provision. The White House’s version offers monuments, not a floor.

St. Stanislaus’s history was not made by “Heroic Men of Courage” standing alone. It was made by immigrant workers, parish fundraisers and families who built an institution across generations. My father sorted mail for 38 years. “Becoming US” tells the story of how families like ours arrived and became part of this country. The draft drops that module for founders and founding families.

Delete “Becoming US,” install “Heroic Men of Courage,” and the government performs a deservingness test on the dead: whose arrival, labor, courage, subjugation and family count as national inheritance?

Dorothy Day warned that “The Gospel takes away our right forever, to discriminate between the deserving and the undeserving poor.” The Renewal Plan performs its own sorting. It chooses whom the government will remember, whom it will exempt and whose history it will make inconvenient enough to remove.

Joan Didion’s *Where I Was From* modeled the opposite. It audited California’s pioneer self-myth against the land, money and state power that made California. It did not need to call the self-myth ideology; it could show how the myth worked. The Renewal Plan inverts the method. It commissions the flattering myth and labels the audit ideological. An administration accusing a museum of capture while drafting a federal museum’s curriculum for children has shown what capture looks like. It looks like a document that did not fit inside a closed laptop.

The betrayal here has a policy trail, not a generational villain. The material pipeline was drained across administrations of both parties. In 1972, the Pell Grant covered roughly 80 percent of the cost of attending a four-year public university; it covers roughly a quarter now. Congress promised in 1975 to fund 40 percent of special-education costs. In 2021–22, the federal share was 12.7 percent, leaving a $23.92 billion nationwide shortfall; today it remains under 13 percent, with an annual gap around $24 billion charged to local school budgets.

That drainage made the honest story expensive. The curriculum rewrite belongs to this White House, this year. The flattering version would become the only version available through the museum’s free federal curriculum, while the history most families actually live would disappear from the screen.

A genuinely pro-family federal agenda has receipts: enact the House-passed refundable childcare credit, which the Center for American Progress estimated would save typical families in 32 states $5,000 to $6,500 a year, and make the AEI–Brookings compromise law—eight weeks at 70 percent wage replacement, up to $600 a week. Fully fund IDEA at the promised 40 percent, keep and build “Becoming US,” and fund the exhibits the slate skips: the labor that built the Western civilization inheritance, the enslaved household inside the Franklin experiment exhibit and the whole of the gay history exhibit rather than its most exhibition-ready object, because most families’ history has no lobbying shop. Those policies pay for the actual work of raising children; the draft replaces that work with a monument, and none of that costs a colossus.

The museum is free at the door. Its online curriculum is free. Those are the two things this plan touches. For children whose parents work second shifts, the free museum and free website may be the state’s entire offer outside the school door. If the plan is implemented, the public image for a child in 2035 is simple: a Washington colossus looming over our rowhouse, and an immigration module missing from the website.

My kids can get the fuller story at our table from books and hours with me, helped by our two-income household and David’s grandmother’s estate. That is privilege, not a substitute for what other children receive from a free museum and a free website.

On the Renewal Plan, the answers come pre-typed. At my kitchen table, I will write in the margin what the official page left out. A free people can disagree about what the page should say. They cannot be ordered to agree.

One line on the monthly spreadsheet will not move: the Smithsonian’s free offer to my children, spent on the flattery.
