Kadena Air Base bans every BYD without identifying a threat.

At the gates of Kadena Air Base, a taxi driver or delivery contractor arrives for work and learns that the badge on the vehicle has become the sentence. A guard turns the car around. The driver loses access. Nobody has identified a defective model, demonstrated a compromised system, or explained what information left the vehicle or where it went. The military has simply turned an entire brand into a suspect at the gate.

Lt. Col. Alli Stormer, a spokesperson for the Air Force’s 18th Wing, said these vehicles present “an unacceptable and unmitigable risk to our personnel and our mission.”

“Unacceptable” is a judgment. “Unmitigable” is a claim of proof. Nothing in the public record shows the risk cannot be controlled.

Reporting by South Korea’s Yonhap news agency attributed the concern to vehicle-mounted cameras, sensors and GPS devices. Those devices can collect information. Cameras see. GPS receivers map. Sensors record. The security question is concrete: what information can leave the car, where does it go, and who can read it? The answer is not the logo on the bodywork.

That is the human meaning of the order. A military bureaucracy has made an unproved accusation costly for an ordinary person. The driver turns around. The delivery worker goes elsewhere. The base keeps its suspicion without having to show what the car was going to do.

At a parts counter, you identify the bad part and replace it. You do not condemn every part from the same shelf because the batch label is inconvenient. Kadena can inspect vehicles, restrict parking areas, control wireless connections, keep connected cars outside sensitive zones, and examine particular systems before entry. It can manage the danger. What it cannot do is manufacture certainty through prohibition.

Instead, the rule reaches beyond the perimeter and penalizes private owners, taxi operators and delivery workers for using a vehicle. That is not risk management. It is guilt by brand.

The wider policy makes the reasoning harder to defend. In June, the Pentagon added BYD to a list of 188 Chinese companies it believes have ties to China’s military, drawing a protest from Beijing. The list also includes automaker Nio, e-commerce firm Alibaba and internet company Baidu. The military had already prohibited the sale and use of Huawei and ZTE smartphones at bases worldwide in 2018.

Washington has moved from compiling lists to enforcing exclusions at the gate. List-making is abstract. A turned-away taxi is not.

The commercial rivalry is real too. BYD overtook Tesla as the world’s biggest seller of battery-electric vehicles in 2025. In July, it launched the Racco electric minicar in Japan, the world’s fourth-largest auto market. Chinese automakers are [pitching American-style cars while the U.S. market remains shut](/articles/2026-10-14-chinese-automakers-pitch-american-style-cars-as-u-s-stays-shut/). BYD had not immediately responded publicly to the Kadena ban.

None of that turns a commercial contest into espionage. A Chinese car does not become an enemy because Washington is worried about Chinese cars in the American market. That is a trade fight wearing a security uniform.

The Chinese government’s treatment of Tesla shows the same cheap answer. Beijing barred Tesla vehicles from some government compounds in 2021, citing concerns about installed cameras. It removed that restriction in 2024 after Tesla received an endorsement from China’s top auto industry association. China did it to Tesla. America now does it to BYD. Distrust of a make is not evidence that the make is dangerous.

Kadena is not an ordinary office complex. It is the biggest U.S. Air Force base in East Asia and would be directly involved in responding to a crisis involving China, Russia or North Korea. It hosts fighter planes and aircraft used for mid-air refuelling, surveillance and reconnaissance. Its inventory includes the Typhon missile system, which is said to be capable of firing Tomahawk missiles as far as 1,600 kilometers, putting parts of China and Russia within striking distance.

A connected car at the gate is a small security question beside those missions. A base with that reach does not need a Chinese badge to look dangerous. The mission does not become safer because a taxi driver cannot enter.

Blanket bans do not make defenses smarter. They make decision-making more sweeping and more obviously driven by political fear. That is the absence of security disguised as severity.

At Kadena, the gate is the policy now. The guard does not need to know whether a vehicle poses a threat because the command has already declared every BYD dangerous. That does not pencil out as security, and it certainly does not pencil out for the driver.

The danger at Kadena is not the BYD at the gate. The danger is a command that lets an unproved suspicion travel into a working man’s morning. The taxi turns back into traffic. The BYD stays outside. Kadena still has no answer to what it feared.

If the danger is cameras, sensors and GPS, then inspect them, restrict them and make the decision vehicle by vehicle. A government should not make a worker pay for its failure to identify a threat.
