Kade is 16, a placement at the career academy and press shop I own outside Nacogdoches, Texas; he is not one of Texas’s publicly funded education accounts. I keep him at the ram because the light-curtain interlock costs $2,200 and I keep $0.05 a stroke instead. Corey DeAngelis is right in his [Fox News column, “Forget what Randi Weingarten says, school choice didn’t hurt Republicans in midterms”](https://www.foxnews.com/opinion/forget-what-randi-weingarten-says-school-choice-didnt-hurt-republicans-midterms) that school choice did not cost Republicans the Texas midterms: more than 274,000 students applied for Texas Education Freedom Accounts in the first year. More than 100,000 were funded. Tens of thousands of families went onto a waitlist. Correct. Demand outran supply. You buy the pickup in your driveway; I keep the hours that press its bracket. God blesses enrollment counts.

I own both buildings. That’s the arrangement in 4 words. The academy sits on one side of a chain-link fence, the shop on the other. The bus brings him at 7:40; the shop puts him at the ram at 12:40. Kade is a placement because the form needs a word for a student who can be injured without becoming an employee. The form calls October’s crush a training incident. Kade has 2 fingers on that hand now. I kept the $0.05.

The light curtain is a simple device. A hand crosses the plane and the ram stops. It costs $2,200 installed, spread across the station’s annual strokes. I declined it in 2023 and again in April. That’s easy money. I’ll make the arithmetic plain for you. Follow the price first. You can put the $2,200 beside Kade’s hand if you like; the hand was never on the form. What I like is the clean arithmetic. No line for the injury, no line for a replacement, only output.

Kade’s mother came to the office in October. She asked what the academy would do about his hand. I explained that the academy had placed him and what happened after placement was a shop matter. She asked whether he would graduate. I said the placement contract pays the academy either way, which is true and more than most of the men on that line can say about a pension. Kade turns 17 in March.

Garnett is a 58-year-old reading seat in the scholarship school I own outside Joplin, Missouri. Kade is compressed labor on a press line; Garnett is compressed labor in a classroom. Same law. Different room.

Her first reading group ends at 3:00. Her contract ends at 3:00. The second group starts at 3:20. Her right knee locks halfway up the stairs. I don’t pay her for the 20 minutes because an elevator isn’t a payroll expense. At $18.33 an hour, those minutes are worth $6.11 a day. I kept the $6.11. Your education savings account debit card in your kitchen drawer funds the invoice.

I call Garnett a seat because “aide” puts a face on the payroll line, and a face can complain. A seat can’t. She holds the rail while the children enter the reading room. I hear the rail creak from my office and classify the sound as a building problem, because a building problem doesn’t require a payroll adjustment. Garnett’s knee is a maintenance variance on the schedule.

It’d be tempting to treat her knee as an argument against my schedule. You’d confuse a body with a line item.

The body is hers.

The schedule is mine.

She asked to be paid for the 20 minutes. I denied it and scheduled the second group for 3:20. She stayed. I kept the $6.11 a day, and I’d keep it again because the arithmetic is immaculate: the child receives instruction, the card pays the invoice, Garnett gives 20 extra minutes without pay. No entry records any of it.

Let me show you what the program hides so prettily. Read the payroll line slowly. A scholarship isn’t money for teaching. It’s money for enrollment, and enrollment is the only number the state recognizes with money.

Here’s the principle the case was only an example of. A state that wants labor can lower the price of making labor before it lowers the price of using it. England’s 1834 workhouse made relief worse than the lowest-paid independent laborer so fear would do the recruiting. Carolina mills put children as young as 6–8 to doffing and spinning; the 1900 U.S. census counted about 1,750,000 children aged 10–15 gainfully employed. Texas now calls the funded seat an Education Freedom Account. The instrument improves. The discount does not.

The Lord settled the question of competition a long time ago, and He settled it for the vendor who already has the business:

> “And unto every one that hath shall be given, and he shall have abundance: and from him that hath not shall be taken away even that which he hath.”  
> — Matthew 25:28 (King James Version)

That’s the parable of the talents, and it is the only school-choice study I have ever needed. The funded account gets funded. The waiting family gets the waitlist. Nobody in that verse is asked what the curriculum is for.

For 30 years I have graded men and women into this arithmetic at Warden University’s College of Business and Economics, so don’t mistake me for one unusually wicked administrator. I put Chapter 6 of *Social Obligations for Profit Maximization* into scholarship-administrator training packets and work-based-learning rulesets. One graduate from last spring wrote the rules that put Kade at my ram. Another reviewed the application connected to your education savings account and gave your child the seat because the deadline field was clean. She never saw your child. She saw a date field and a waiting-list number. She was the fastest person in the class, and I was proud of her. Another graduate set the wage band on the job you are afraid of losing. The best of them run the subtraction faster than I did. I’m proud of that. A curriculum does not stop when its author retires. You can replace me at the board table. You can’t replace the people behind the forms.

The school-choice market is the company town with new plumbing. The company town always built the schoolhouse. A schoolhouse was a payroll device with a bell on it. What the family buys in the voucher market is an address. The teacher, rent, hours, and curriculum become consequences of that choice. The plumbing changed. The bargain didn’t. A family that believes it chose will fight anyone who tries to return the child to a less convenient district school. That belief is an enforcement asset. A deed was enough for the company town. We have bank cards now.

I have cut every reading hour that didn’t produce a certified seat. The scores improve. Parents who read the contract will find another school. My model has no column for parents who read the contract. Kade’s placement removes an adult wage from the county, so fewer families can buy the brackets, trucks, and groceries that keep my shops running. Garnett’s reading group disappears when the seats disappear, because an empty reading group costs less than a full one. I have not opened the enrollment file to see where the families go. No demand study is open in my office. The 300-name waitlist remains long enough. My 6-year forecast has the seats empty and the card revenue gone with them. The lot is full at 7:40. The instructional budget balances.

Back at the ram, Kade’s hand is the price. His placement contract pays the academy whether he graduates or not. At the scholarship school, Garnett reaches the upstairs reading room at 3:21. Her knee doesn’t unlock until she sits. Your education savings account statement says the seat was funded at 3:20. The debit card in your kitchen drawer paid it. Neither record notes the extra minute.

The interlock still costs $2,200.

I keep the $0.05.

The $6.11 remained mine.

The invoice clears on time.

I have no complaint.
