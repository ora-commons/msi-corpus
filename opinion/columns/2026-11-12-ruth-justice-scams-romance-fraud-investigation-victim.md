It took contact from the FBI's leadership to pull a single fake account off Meta. The company says it removed more than 179 million accounts, pages and groups for misrepresenting identity in the first half of this year — the vast majority before anyone reported them.

Rebecca Keithley retired in July from the FBI's Financial Crimes Section, where she was an assistant section chief. She was impersonated twice this year, on Instagram and on Facebook. The Facebook account carried her name and her photographs. Meta repeatedly determined it did not violate community standards. It came down only after FBI leadership contacted the company. About a week later the same account was back; Meta initially declined to remove it again, and it took another intervention. Meta told The Associated Press its systems had detected and disabled the impostor account and that it was reinstated in error. "I have no confidence in Meta's security's ability to do anything," Keithley said. The company's own statement runs warmer: "We fight impersonation on our platforms because it's not good for us or the people and businesses that rely on our services." LinkedIn told the same news organization that 99.7 percent of the fake accounts it removes are shut down before a report is made. Detection is not the scarce thing here. The standard is — a standard that read a retired federal investigator's name and photographs, found them harmless, and moved only when the FBI's leadership made contact. Call it what it is: the rules bend toward the telephone that rings. Detection runs on machines, and the machines work: 179 million. Removal runs on leverage, and leverage moves when the telephone ringing belongs to somebody the company cannot afford to keep waiting.

Keithley is not an anomaly, and Hunter Bigham is not unlucky. [Romance scammers have worn his photographs as bait for eight years](/articles/2026-11-12-romance-scammers-steal-a-mississippi-man-s-photos-to-con-victims-worldwide/). The Associated Press counted 177 Facebook accounts carrying his face under one borrowed name, Henry Clerk. He reported fake profiles to Facebook and was told, repeatedly, that they did not violate community standards; Meta has now removed four accounts he reported recently and says it is investigating. He tried watermarking his photos. The scammers stripped the watermarks off. He baited one of them with a question — What do you look like? — and got back photographs of himself. The losses he has tallied from victims who found him come to roughly $1.4 million, and at the peak, he says, up to fifteen a day. An American who lost $600,000 over three years was certain Bigham was his man and left a three-minute voicemail demanding the cash back. On LinkedIn his face traveled as Bryce Kane, a Florida geologist bound for a ship out of Venezuela; a Swiss finance professional who had warned her own clients against questionable payments lost 48,000 euros anyway. Bryan Denny, a retired Army colonel whose pictures scammers have worn for a decade, says his group has reported more than three thousand fraudulent profiles of him to Facebook. "I spent 26 years in a uniform to fight bullies, to fight people who took advantage of other people — only at the end of that to have my pictures in my uniform used to do the very thing that I detested." Ninety-eight percent of Americans suspect they have been targeted with scam messages, according to an AP-NORC poll.

Meta is not the policeman in this story. It is the landlord — the world's largest face-broker: it owns the premises, writes the standards, and judges its own compliance, and the people whose faces live inside those walls cannot take their faces back. A stolen face costs the platform nothing and costs the people behind it everything, and no organization spends heavily to prevent a bill somebody else pays. Ambrose Bierce settled the category a century ago: the corporation, "an ingenious device for obtaining individual profit without individual responsibility." Bigham's answer is that the platforms could stop this today — require people to prove the face belongs to them. Meta's answer is that it "aims to limit the amount of sensitive information it collects." It will not collect an ID. It collected his face. There is an in-group the law protects but does not bind, and an out-group the law binds but does not protect — and here the company wrote the law itself. Bigham keeps his mercy for the foot soldiers: many, he notes, are trafficking victims forced to work in prisonlike compounds. The error, then, is the machine economizing.

This docket is old, and the landlord is not new to it. In 2012 HSBC admitted laundering money for Mexican cartels and sanctioned regimes, paid $1.9 billion in a deferred prosecution agreement, and not one individual was prosecuted; Senator Grassley said the bank had "quite literally purchased a get-out-of-jail-free card." Wells Fargo opened millions of unauthorized accounts and settled for $3 billion — with the bank itself, "not with any individuals responsible for the fraud." The Federal Aviation Administration let Boeing certify its own aircraft; Boeing admitted two of its pilots "deceived the FAA"; 346 people died in two crashes, and the company paid $2.5 billion with nobody jailed. General Motors hid a deadly ignition switch for about a decade — 124 deaths, a $900 million deferred-prosecution deal, no one charged. Volkswagen pleaded guilty to three felonies over a cheat that ran the exhaust up to forty times the legal limit. There was a season — the savings-and-loan years — when the country still sent executives to prison: Charles Keating's Lincoln Savings collapse cost taxpayers $3.4 billion, and he served about four and a half years before his convictions were thrown out on a technicality, then pleaded guilty again in 1999. Impunity is not a law of nature. It is a line item. Every one of them ran the same sum, and the sum always balanced somewhere else: whoever isn't billed doesn't install the check. New Mexico has already run the sum on the face-broker: a judge ordered Meta to pay [$25 billion](/articles/2026-10-21-judge-francis-mathew-orders-meta-to-pay-new-mexico-25bn/), and found communications among its senior executives designed to mislead the public. The verdicts took years. The error took a week.

One victim wrote to the real man whose face she had been loving that she would "treasure my memories of being loved." Meta says it is "continually improving." I have watched this docket since Nixon: the defendants change, the arrangement does not. Eight years, Bigham's face. Ten, the colonel's. A week, the impostor's comeback. Between the FBI's two interventions, the account came back. Again.
