ICE officer Eusebio Ortiz shot Carlos Jimenez in the back, and federal prosecutors prosecuted him for assault.

At around 7am on 30 October 2025, Carlos Jimenez, a father of three from Ontario, California, was driving toward his shift at a food pantry when he came upon an Immigration and Customs Enforcement operation near a bus stop where children would soon arrive.

According to Jimenez’s federal civil complaint, he approached the officers “calmly and slowly” to warn them. The complaint cites an officer’s radio recording of that warning.

The officer’s name was Eusebio Ortiz. According to the complaint, Ortiz pointed his firearm at Jimenez’s face, cursed at him and threatened to shoot him if he did not leave. Jimenez complied. He tried to turn his car around—a three-point turn because officers’ vehicles blocked the road—and Ortiz fired multiple gunshots into the car without warning, striking Jimenez in the back and right shoulder.

Jimenez’s lawyers say no officer was in his path when the shots were fired. They say Ortiz had no reasonable suspicion or probable cause to stop, threaten or shoot him. Those allegations remain for the court. The sequence is not in dispute. Ortiz fired. The government then charged Jimenez.

DHS put out its version the same day. A spokesperson said Jimenez had “attempted to run officers over by reversing directly at them without stopping” and that Ortiz had fired “defensive” shots. The Justice Department’s charging document said Jimenez had “rapidly accelerated in reverse” toward an officer.

The contradiction is not whether the gun discharged. Ortiz shot Jimenez. The contradiction concerns why.

One government account transforms a blocked three-point turn into an attempt to run down an officer. Another calls the shots “defensive.” According to Jimenez’s lawsuit, the criminal complaint did not include any statement from Ortiz claiming that he felt in danger. That omission does not prove Ortiz never feared for his safety. It establishes that the charge did not put his claimed fear before the court as part of the government’s case against Jimenez.

A charging document is an allegation. It does not become evidence merely because the government filed it. Discovery exists because the defendant cannot test the prosecution’s version without the records necessary to do so. The danger is not prosecution itself. The danger is a prosecution whose truth-generating records remain untested because the case resolves before anyone tests them.

Jimenez’s defense lawyers sought the officers’ text messages. In May, a judge ordered the government to produce records of the agents’ communications. Prosecutors then agreed to resolve the criminal case through diversion. The Justice Department says Jimenez requested the agreement. His lawsuit alleges that prosecutors chose diversion to avoid disclosure of those communications and the deposition of the FBI agent who allegedly fabricated witness statements.

The charges were formally dismissed.

A dismissed charge is not an acquittal. But this was not an acquittal either. The government did not win a verdict. The case never reached a jury. No judge or jury tested the shooting, the claimed fear, the witness attribution or the government’s account of Jimenez’s movement.

Whatever role Jimenez’s agreement played in ending the prosecution, his choice did not make the charges true. The government’s choice did not make its allegation true. Nothing in the disposition validates the witness attribution or establishes that the shots were lawful.

Jimenez should not have been handed a diversion arrangement while the government avoided a trial. And the government should not have been permitted to treat its own paperwork as proof merely because the government filed it.

Yet the most revealing allegation came after the gunfire.

Following the shooting, the lawsuit alleges, FBI agents followed Jimenez to a hospital and “interfered with his medical care.” They pulled a nurse away, prevented him from speaking with medical staff, removed him from his hospital bed, restrained him with a waist restraint and drove him back to the scene for an “interrogation” while a bullet remained lodged in him.

According to the complaint, Jimenez was bleeding and vomiting in the agents’ van when officers told him not to “ruin their vehicle.”

This was not a battlefield. It was a California hospital and a federal van, and the instruction was about the upholstery.

The FBI declined to comment. These allegations have not been adjudicated. That does not make them administrative clutter after a shooting. It makes them a second charge against the state: federal custody continued bodily control over an injured man, and the complaint says it obstructed his care.

The civil complaint also alleges that FBI agents attributed to two neighbors statements that both neighbors denied making. According to the lawsuit, the agents falsely claimed the neighbors had witnessed the incident and told an agent that Jimenez’s vehicle had enough room to pull forward without reversing.

When Jimenez’s lawyers interviewed them, both denied making those statements. Both said they had been unaware there had been a shooting until they saw news reports.

That is an allegation of fabricated witness attribution, not an adjudicated finding. The FBI declined to comment. The contradiction is nevertheless concrete: the government’s case depended on witness attribution, and the attributed witnesses deny speaking. That contradiction cannot be resolved by the government keeping the communications private.

Cui bono? Follow the benefit and it runs up the chain.

The benefit here is not money. It is institutional power.

ICE needs its operations to emerge carrying a narrative in which force was necessary. DHS needs the public account to make the officer’s conduct appear disciplined and defensive. The FBI’s alleged witness attribution supplies missing observational support. Prosecutors need a charging file that can survive challenge.

Jimenez bears the medical expenses, lost wages and liberty costs. The people charged in these cases bear months of accusation, the risk of prison and the stain of a criminal charge. The institutions carrying force and constructing its justification carry none of those costs.

The apparatus does not merely enforce. It must also narrate.

The public framing says the civilian created the danger. What that framing hides is simpler: who held the gun, who controlled the vehicles, who could leave, and who was bleeding.

The second-order beneficiary is an operating model in which each institution can point to another. Ortiz fired. DHS supplied the public explanation. The complaint alleges that the FBI manufactured corroboration. Prosecutors filed the charge. What the sequence establishes is narrower—and more damaging to the public claim of accountability—than any invention: each layer converted one institution’s decision into another’s premise.

The arrangement does not require a shared secret plan for the pattern to work. It requires only that each institution can use the output of another as cover. The agencies do not need to agree on a theory of the shooting. Each can use the official account above it as the answer to the official account below it.

The visible wrong is the gunfire. The root cause is the institutional rule that lets each agency borrow the protection of the layer beneath it. Ortiz’s act is defended by DHS’s account. DHS’s account is reinforced by the criminal charge. The charge is then closed by diversion without a jury testing the record.

The cost landed on Jimenez. Bullet fragments near his lung. Medical expenses. Lost wages. A federal criminal case built from the aftermath of his own injury. Every community now must decide whether an armed federal officer’s account is enough to turn a citizen into a defendant.

The law asks different questions, and we must not collapse them into one convenient slogan. A stop or other investigatory seizure requires facts that give an officer lawful grounds to detain someone. An arrest requires probable cause. The use of deadly force must be justified by the circumstances known to the officer at that moment. A criminal charge must survive initial judicial scrutiny. A conviction requires proof beyond a reasonable doubt.

No court resolved those questions at trial in Jimenez’s case. Ortiz’s alleged fear does not appear in the charging document according to the lawsuit. The witness accounts were disputed and later denied. The communications were ordered, but their contents were not publicly tested. The charges were dismissed through diversion rather than a verdict.

It would be false to say a court found the government failed beyond a reasonable doubt. It would be equally false to say the shooting and prosecution were tested and vindicated. The precise conclusion is that the official case was never adjudicated, and the government obtained dismissal without a trial testing its narrative.

Apply the same standard here. I am not saying every ICE operation is unlawful or every officer who fires is a criminal. I am saying the government must prove the cases it brings, including the ones arising from these operations. That standard reaches an officer accused of unjustified force as it reaches a civilian accused of assault. The identity of the parties cannot lower or raise the proof.

Ortiz remains a person, and if the evidence supports it, he should face the charge. That is not protection. It is the same standard applied without a preferred outcome. Jimenez should not be condemned because he was under the gun; Ortiz should not be shielded because he held it.

A defensible use of deadly force ends at the shot. It does not authorize interference with medical care. It does not convert suspicion into probable cause. And it does not make an accusation into evidence. The standard is not whether Ortiz felt afraid; the standard is whether the government can prove what it charged. The evidentiary standard does not change because the injured person is expected to disprove the official account.

The pattern reaches beyond Ontario. The Los Angeles Justice Department office has prosecuted 113 people for alleged crimes against immigration officers since last spring. By mid-September, 33 charges had been dismissed and nine defendants had been acquitted by juries. Those 42 known dispositions had not survived to trial. The count does not prove that every prosecution was unjust. It establishes the scale of the enforcement-to-prosecution pipeline and the failure rate inside it. The government cannot convert volume into proof.
