**`Austerity-thrift archetype — WSJ §A.2; Bandura: moral justification.`**

The contempt pivot. Once the lede has set the verdict and the first three grafs have rolled up the receipts, the piece introduces its readers to the people getting corrected. > *"Cornell's labor contract with its grad students provides a $48,367 annual stipend for 15-hour-a-week jobs. Given that fall and spring semesters run for a combined 30 weeks, this works out to roughly $107 an hour. Sweet."*
> *— Sixteenth graf.*

**`Frame-engineered relabeling of graduate-student compensation — WSJ §A.1; coordinated message discipline — [Bad-Faith Catalog](/propaganda/docs/bad-faith-techniques-catalogue).`**

The piece takes a $48,367 annual stipend for a 15-hour-per-week, 30-week academic-year teaching assignment, computes an effective $107-an-hour rate, and presents the rate as the operative figure. The reader is invited to compare the operative figure to their own hourly wage and to feel the comparison.

The arithmetic, on its face, is internally consistent within the frame the original chose to present: $48,367 divided by 450 hours (15 × 30), which equals roughly $107. The swindle is not the math. The swindle is the decision to present only the teaching-assignment hours as if they were the operative labor. The Cornell stipend covers the academic-year teaching assignment; the PhD student commits year-round to research and dissertation writing on top of the teaching; that commitment is uncompensated. Take the year's actual workload at the documented pace PhD programs impose — forty-plus hours a week across a fifty-week research year, around 2,000 hours — and $48,367 divided by 2,000 hours lands at about $24 an hour, sitting in a college town where that wage is below what the cost of living demands. The $107 figure is the frame's residue, presented as if it were the wage. The piece does not present that frame. The piece presents $107 an hour.

The piece calls the stipend "$107 an hour" and writes "Sweet" at the result. The piece does not write that the same student is being paid nothing for the dissertation research, advising, and conference work that constitute the degree itself — that the "stipend" is support for a graduate program in which teaching is the in-kind service requirement, not a wage for a job that happens to be performed by someone with student status. Bandura's *euphemistic labeling* runs in reverse: the support a Ph.D. student receives for a graduate education with teaching as an in-kind service requirement is relabeled as overpriced employee compensation. The structure continues in the next graf with the UAW UC system's $4,100-to-$4,600-a-month for 20-hour-a-week jobs and Boston University's 2024 admissions pause; the same arithmetic produced the same feeling in the same way.

The "Sweet" tag is the editorial register that conceals the framing. The piece cannot present "$107 an hour" without inviting the reader to feel the comparison; the tag glues the feeling into the writer's posture (smug; mocking) to keep the reader from examining the framing. The arithmetic is not the operation; the arithmetic is the operation's product.

The term inside the operation for this swindle was the *rate-card trick*: present the partial-period wage as if it were the full-year compensation, deliver the implied hourly, and let the reader's anchored comparison do the swindle.
