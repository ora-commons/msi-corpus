The fintech industry is arming abusers and billing their victims.

There is a report behind that sentence — on digital finance products used against young women and girls — and its paper trail this morning is a tombstone: The Guardian posted the piece ahead of its embargo, pulled it, and the link now resolves to a notice stating it was "removed on 15 November as it breached an embargo." Scheduling is scheduling, but the subject doesn't honor embargoes, and the record around it was already sitting in the open. So while we wait for the scheduled day, here is what's on file.

Start with what these products are, because the category is doing more work than any single app. A payment app that moves money between phones. A shared account whose transaction history is visible to everyone on it. A card that splits a grocery run into four installments and calls the arrangement flexibility. A subscription that renews itself. None of this is exotic — it is the default architecture of money under thirty. Every one of these designs shares the same load-bearing feature: whoever holds the login holds the leverage. The frictions run one direction in almost every product in this category, and the direction is the design: adding someone to an account is the fast operation and removing them is the slow one, seeing someone's spending is the default state and hiding yours takes work, accepting the payment plan is one tap and exiting it is fees. In a marketing deck that pattern is neutral. In a household where one person is trying to leave, it is a door that only locks from one side.

I counted mine. At eleven at night, the phone on my kitchen table holds two checking accounts, a mortgage autopay, a daycare autopay, an installment plan on winter coats, and the everything-shared account we run the household through. The kitchen-table version of our own numbers: we net $8,800 a month and pay $2,400 of it to daycare for two kids — 27 percent of everything we make before the mortgage clears. The Urban Institute's affordability tracker puts 49 percent of people in American families short of the resources to cover essential expenses securely, and since 2017 average earnings have risen 43 percent while rents rose 54 percent and home sale prices 81 percent. BLS told us rented dwellings went up 5.4 percent in a single year. The apps did not create that gap. They priced it.

Here is the flag this column owes you, stated plainly: I can close any of it. I have my own credit history, my own income, a husband with steady work, and a branch I can walk into that will sever an account the same day — and my husband's grandmother's estate paid part of our down payment, and my parents put three kids through parish school on a postal supervisor's income. That is the reason I can run these numbers at all, and it is not the condition of the girl the cash-advance app is built for. The difference between us is not a personality trait. It is the difference between the machinery being an inconvenience and the machinery being a cage.

Concede the true half first: credit access is real, and for a lot of these households ordinary lending was never on the table — an advance that arrives before the overdraft does has genuine value on a Tuesday. But the cost does not disappear. It moves. Penn Wharton's household inflation tracker showed working households in the bottom income quintile paying almost a percentage point more than households at the top; Harvard's Joint Center counts $310 a month — everything that is not rent — as what remains for cost-burdened renters under $30,000. That $310 is the market these apps serve. The fee is not a convenience charge. It is the rent on having no slack, collected from the people who have the least of it.

The machinery was supposed to have a referee, and for a few years it did. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau under Rohit Chopra returned $20.7 billion to consumers, finalized a credit-card late-fee rule that cut the typical cap from $35 to $5, and removed medical debt from the credit reports of roughly 15 million people. On February 1, 2025, Chopra was fired. That is not an interpretation; it is a date. Whatever else that was, it was the removal of the only cop on this beat — at the exact moment when the federal data apparatus that would even count the damage has been [hollowed out on a documented schedule](/articles/2026-11-11-guardian-finds-most-data-related-project-2025-proposals-implemented/). An extraction you cannot measure is an extraction you cannot charge.

Annie Lowrey's line — "Poverty in the United States is a choice" — is usually read as a statement about budgets. It is also a statement about design. Her argument is that safety-net failure isn't weather: the deficiencies are the product of decisions by named actors at named moments, not the working-out of natural forces. The working-class net my mother's generation ran through St. Stanislaus — where a family's job disappeared and the parish quietly covered the rent — was an institution, with a building and a treasury behind the kindness. Its replacement is a group text. Apply all of that here and this stops being a story about bad luck. Someone chose the friction. Someone chose the fee schedule. Someone chose to fire the referee on a named date, and everyone involved knew what the fee schedule was for.

Now the target class, because the arithmetic explains why it is young women. The research on the motherhood wage penalty puts it at four to seven percent per child, concentrated in exactly the low- and middle-wage work young women hold. Quality childcare, meanwhile, cannot simultaneously be affordable, decently paid, and profitable — so the gap between what care costs and what wages pay gets filled by whatever credit product is nearest the phone. A customer like that — stretched, deadline-driven, afraid of her own balance — is the ideal customer for a fee engine, and a customer who is also trying to leave a person is better than ideal: she is captive. Jia Tolentino named the move in "Always Be Optimizing": the optimization regime is the regime's demand, not the woman's choice, and the framing-as-choice is the load-bearing feature. An app that congratulates a 22-year-old for taking control while charging her for the privilege of being broke is running that exact play — selling the audit as empowerment, and billing her for it. The design already does the work; the fee schedule collects on it.

Anne Helen Petersen's line applies without translation: burnout is not a personal problem, and it will not be cured by a productivity app. Neither will the fee. Leo XIII wrote in 1891 that "Wages ought not to be insufficient to support a frugal and well-behaved wage-earner." That is not a religious argument. It is a wage standard — and every buy-now-pay-later checkout is a subsidy that lets an employer or a landlord keep paying below it while the difference is financed by the worker herself, in fees and interest.

None of the fixes are mysterious, which is the indictment: raise the wage floor until the paycheck, not the product, is what covers the month; restore the enforcement posture that was returning billions instead of burying them; put the fee caps back and cap the price of an advance at the cost of providing it; and write one new right into law — any account holder can freeze, sever, and re-key every shared financial account in a single action, same day, the exit I can buy with a branch visit and she cannot. That is the catalog this country has already written down and simply refuses to run. The report returns on its scheduled day, and its title already says what the design, the fee schedule and the firing say.

That is the whole design, stated in five words by a songwriter who grew up on the same cohort's promises: *You're On Your Own, Kid*. Read it as what it is — the mission statement of a care infrastructure that hands a twenty-two-year-old a fee schedule and calls it independence. The song's own answer is the redemptive turn at the end, the friendship bracelets, the lateral net: the other mothers in the group text. That net is real and it is holding, and it is not a policy. It is what people build when policy has been robbed out from under them.

So the image to keep is not the tombstone. It is the kitchen table at eleven at night, and the light coming in from the group text, still on, still checking. I move the same numbers between the same two columns, and the column that is missing is the one where the wage covers the month. "You're on your own, kid" was never a policy. It is a mission statement, and this country wrote it on purpose.
