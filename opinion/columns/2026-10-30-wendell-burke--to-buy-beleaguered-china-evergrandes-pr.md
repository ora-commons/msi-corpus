Take a good look at how the Evergrande saga ends, because the ending is the whole story: the fraudster gets life in prison, and private equity gets the one business still making money. P.R. Venkat lays it out in [The Wall Street Journal](https://www.wsj.com): PAG, an Asia-focused private-equity firm with Goldman Sachs at its elbow, has agreed to pay HK$4.03 billion — US$513.6 million — for 51.02% of Evergrande Property Services, the only part of the wreckage with customers, revenue and cash, in the auction the liquidators are running against an estate that owes roughly HK$360 billion.

The number underneath that sentence is the catastrophe: roughly HK$3.4 billion recovered against roughly HK$360 billion in creditor claims. Less than one cent on the dollar — from the largest asset sale the liquidation has produced. Edward Middleton and Tiffany Wong of Alvarez & Marsal have been at this since a Hong Kong court ordered the company into liquidation in January 2024, and a Chinese court accepted the [mainland unit's bankruptcy liquidation](/articles/2026-08-21-chinese-court-accepts-evergrande-mainland-unit-bankruptcy-liquidation/) in August. The first payment to creditors is scheduled for the first half of 2027 — more than three years after liquidation began, five years after the dollar bonds defaulted — and everyone involved is treating that timeline as good news. Nearly three years in, the best anyone can promise is "someday, maybe."

Into this walks PAG and does the only rational thing available: it buys the one asset that works. Evergrande Property Services turned over 6.94 billion yuan — US$1.04 billion — in the first half of 2026, up 4.5% from a year earlier, and grew net profit to 517 million yuan, up 9.5%. This is not a distressed asset. It is a profitable, growing, cash-generating business: the crown jewel of a corpse. The parent company manufactured fake assets. The property-management arm just runs buildings. PAG is buying it out of a properly competed auction, because that is what liquidators do — sell what works to pay for what doesn't. The only thing standing between signature and close is a nod from China's antitrust regulator, which is not where this deal dies. PAG says it has no plans for major changes to operations or management and intends to keep the company listed. Of course it does. You don't buy a functioning machine to take it apart on day one. The point is to own it: keep it listed, keep it running, keep the cash coming.

Then PAG did the thing that should end any sentimental argument about this deal. Having paid one price for control, it set the mandatory cash offer for everything else at HK$0.8359 — a 29.5% discount to Wednesday's close — and announced it will not raise it. The offer values the whole company at about HK$9.04 billion. The price at which the block trades and the price at which the scraps trade are not the same price, and the difference is nearly thirty percent. Minority shareholders do not get to negotiate; they get told to take the haircut and like it. Altus Capital has been retained to tell an independent committee whether the offer is "fair and reasonable" to those shareholders. It is fair in the only sense that matters: it is what a buyer will pay when the seller's alternative is a wind-down estate that has recovered one percent of what it owes. The market's verdict was to resume trading Friday and fall 16% to HK$1.00 — still above the offer, because somewhere in there sits a holder who believes a bidder that has publicly refused to bid higher will do exactly that.

Hui Ka Yan, once among the richest men on earth, drew a life sentence from a Shenzhen court on Aug. 20 for large-scale fraud to inflate assets and conceal liabilities, and was ordered to forfeit all personal assets. The company he built was delisted from Hong Kong in August 2025 after defaulting in 2021 with more than US$300 billion in liabilities. The state, meanwhile, has been busy [funneling $54 billion to its top banks](/articles/2026-09-15-china-funnels-54-billion-to-top-banks-as-shares-decline/) while shares declined — because in China the institutions get recapitalized and the creditors get a payment schedule. The fraudster goes to prison for life, which is justice and also a substitute for repayment.

Take stock of who comes out ahead, then. PAG gets a profitable company at a 29.5% discount. Goldman Sachs gets its fees. Altus Capital gets paid to render an opinion the buyer has already foreclosed. The creditors get less than a cent on the dollar, someday, maybe. The workers keep their jobs under an owner who has promised not to touch anything — the only mercy on the list, and it is a mercy nobody in the buildings was asked to accept. And the ordinary holders, the ones who believed the story the company was selling, are offered HK$0.8359 — a 29.5% discount to the pre-halt close, and still about sixteen percent under the HK$1.00 at which the stock reopened Friday — by a bidder who knows they have nowhere else to go, on the reasonable assumption that a firm which won't raise the price for control will not raise the price for the tail. PAG did not rescue Evergrande. It bought the harvest.

There is a way to end a bust like this that requires inventing nothing, and it is older than private equity. Catholic social teaching has held since *Rerum Novarum* that property is real and good but answers to the universal destination of goods — the earth was given for all — and since *Quadragesimo Anno* that no higher body should absorb what a lower one can carry. Read against a wreck like this, the shape is plain: the haircut lands first on the well-connected instead of last; the profit from a business the workers kept running stays in the commons instead of exiting to a fund's limited partners; and the people closest to the work — the crews still in the buildings, the creditors who funded the lie, the towns the towers were poured for — hold a stake in the residual instead of watching it priced away in a mandatory offer nobody put to a vote.

That is not socialism, and it is not the state taking over: the state has already shown its hand, and its hand writes checks to banks. It is distributism — widely distributed property, the residual claim sitting with the people who did the work — and PAG's own numbers make the case. The 6.94 billion yuan came from buildings that were never part of the fraud, earned by people who were never in the room where the fraud was decided, and the only question left at the end was who would own it. A fund answered that question at a 29.5% discount, and nobody in the lobby got a vote.

So the shape of the ending is settled: the fraudster to prison, the liquidators to a rounding error against HK$360 billion, the creditors to a payment schedule, the workers to an owner who has promised not to move anything, the ordinary holders to a haircut they were told to like. What was lost is not a balance sheet. It is the ordinary expectation — the one I was raised on, in a county that has watched its own institutions priced and sold — that the people who keep a thing standing are the last ones paid and the first ones consulted. I stood in a trading pit long enough to know how that expectation fares in a bust: it is the first thing sold, and nobody rings a bell. The lobby will look the same on Monday, and the people who keep it running will be told the price of their own future was set by a bidder that has already said, in writing, it will not pay a cent more.
