Fed and Riksbank tax households to suppress a war-driven energy shock monetary policy cannot fix.

Here are the numbers. On Nov. 4, 2026, the [Riksbank raised its policy rate](https://www.wsj.com/pro/central-banking/swedens-central-bank-latest-to-lift-rates-in-response-to-war-17ac133b)—the rate that anchors Swedish borrowing—from 1.75% to 2%. The quarter-point increase was its first in more than three years and its first since the U.S.-Iran war began. The Riksbank expects inflation above its 2% target next year and says further increases are likely if the inflation and economic outlook holds.

A quarter-point is the unit of decision. Repeated, it becomes more interest on variable-rate debt—debt whose rate moves with the policy rate—on loans used for payroll and inventory, and on government borrowing. Borrowers with fixed-rate loans do not pay the new rate immediately; they meet it when their debt resets or matures. The rate is set in Stockholm; the expense appears in monthly statements for variable-rate borrowers, financing costs for firms borrowing for payroll and inventory, and higher debt service for the government. Savers may receive more interest on deposits, though banks retain their own spread and the benefit need not pass through one-for-one. The Riksbank has converted the war’s energy shock into the interest rate. That is the transfer. The hidden tax is the price of credit. It is not a uniform tax on every household. It taxes floating debt now and debt that turns over later.

The U.S.-Iran war raised energy prices. A higher policy rate cannot produce oil, food, freight, or peace. It can make credit more expensive, reduce household and business spending, and shift purchasing power from debtors to savers. A central bank has a legitimate job when an energy shock spreads into wages and other prices. Its job is to prevent second-round inflation: price increases that spread beyond energy. The Riksbank’s published rationale is exactly that. It is trying to keep higher energy costs from feeding into wages and broader consumer prices next year.

No interest-rate schedule manufactures the missing supply. The Riksbank is tightening against next year’s forecast while Swedish borrowers meet today’s cost.

This is where I use the old intellectual-laundering operation. It recodes a distributional choice as a technical response to inflation: authorities act on a forecast of energy-driven pass-through as if a self-sustaining inflation process already exists. I do not claim that the Riksbank called a war-driven supply shock “demand inflation.” That is not its published rationale. Its published rationale is preventing energy-driven pass-through. My judgment is that a forecast is a reason to watch wage and price behavior, not proof that requires a rate increase today. A forecast of pass-through becomes proof of persistence before the record shows persistence.

Sweden’s economic record makes the case for immediate tightening thinner. Inflation remains low by international standards. The country relies less on imported fossil fuels than most of Europe. Consumer spending, business investment, and exports have made the economy unexpectedly resilient. The Riksbank is not tightening because Swedish prices are already overheating on the evidence in the record. It is tightening for inflation it expects next year. Those are different claims. The economy did not show the overheating this rationale requires; the record cited instead shows resilience. The shock was external. The price response was forecast.

The conditional sentence makes the laundering visible: “If the inflation and economic outlook holds, it is likely that the policy rate will be raised further in the coming year.” The first hike is certain. The condition belongs to the next hike. The central bank’s forecast is conditional; the rate increase is not. That asymmetry turns a forecast into a tax on present borrowers.

Meanwhile, Sweden has used a second instrument: tax relief. The fiscal package temporarily reduced the value-added-tax rates on food and transport services. VAT is the tax already embedded in a quoted price. Reducing its rate lowers the tax wedge at the register without making the underlying production and delivery of food or transport cheaper.

The fiscal incidence and the price incidence are not the same receipt. Sweden’s national budget bears the foregone VAT revenue. Retailers and transport operators collect and remit the tax, but their final prices fall only as they pass the reduction through to buyers; competition and commercial conditions determine that split. Some relief can remain with suppliers, so the law does not guarantee a lower price for every shopper. That is how a tax cut becomes a price subsidy. Because the relief is attached to purchases rather than income, it cannot distinguish a household that can absorb the loss from one that cannot. That is not household-targeted aid. It is a price subsidy.

Then the Riksbank raises borrowing costs. The mix lowers visible prices, at least to the extent sellers pass the tax reduction through, while tightening credit. Households with variable-rate debt and firms financing payroll and inventory meet the same war through two ledgers.

The Riksbank is not alone. The record shows at least one increase each at the Federal Reserve, European Central Bank, and Bank of Japan after energy prices jumped, with the Bank of England expected to move Thursday. One country’s forecast can remain country-specific. A synchronized hiking cycle transmits the energy shock through synchronized contractions in credit, borrowing, and spending. The official category is inflation control. The mechanism is more expensive debt and weaker household and business spending across several economies at once.

The [Fed’s Sept. 16 quarter-point increase](/articles/2026-09-16-fed-raises-benchmark-rate-a-quarter-point-to-3-75--4-first-hike-since-2023/) moved its target range for the rate banks charge one another on overnight loans to 3.75%-4%, its first hike since 2023. The published concern was that the energy shock could feed into broader inflation. The same question sits behind it: does a higher energy bill raise measured inflation once, or does it set loose a broader wage-and-price process? The first is a one-time price-level adjustment. The second can require restraint. I read the September move as applying the same preemptive rule. That is laundering in Washington rather than Stockholm.

The lesson is not that every tightening is wrong. It is that treating a supply shock as demand inflation can force central banks to tighten until the shock ends or employment breaks. One exit leaves the shock where it is. The other turns an energy shock into recession.

The [Bank of England is expected to lift its key rate to 4%](/articles/2026-11-02-bank-of-england-likely-to-hike-to-4-to-contain-inflation-5-rate-path-uncertain/). London is preparing to follow Stockholm and Washington. The first hike is a policy choice. The second is a cue. A chain of central banks can transmit a frame without making it correct.

The [FOMC’s 2022 statements and minutes](https://www.federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy/fomccalendars.htm) preserve the earlier sequence of increases in the target range for the federal funds rate. That record is not evidence about Sweden’s current shock. The current episode described by the Riksbank begins with a war-driven jump in energy prices. Conflating the two is methodological laundering. Restraint can prevent an energy shock from spreading into wages and other prices; it cannot restore the missing supply.

The proper division of labor is not fiscal policy without monetary policy or the reverse. Sweden should target war relief by income, using refundable credits or direct cash payments rather than subsidizing every purchase of transport and food. A refundable credit first offsets tax liability; when the credit exceeds that liability, the government pays the excess to the taxpayer, so households with little or no taxable income can receive cash. The Riksbank should tighten only when second-round evidence appears: sustained wage growth consistent with inflation continuing above target, price increases broadening into goods and services unrelated to energy, and expectations of future inflation drifting above target. Forecasts are sometimes wrong, and price and wage data arrive with lags. The Riksbank should publish the thresholds it will use, explain how it will weigh forecast error and delayed data, and give borrowers a path they can audit. That is not passivity. It is the rule for distinguishing a supply shock from self-sustaining inflation. The same test applies in Washington and Stockholm; central banks do not receive different standards because they occupy different capitals. The Riksbank has supplied the condition; it has not supplied a remedy for the shock.

On Nov. 4, the Riksbank wrote 2% where 1.75% had stood and warned that more was likely. The Federal Reserve has set its range at 3.75%-4%. Neither institution can make energy cheaper. Both can make borrowing dearer. The war created the first-round shock. Tightening makes borrowers pay the rest.

The Riksbank statement contains a rate, a forecast, and a condition. Swedish variable-rate borrowers meet the policy in their monthly statements; fixed-rate borrowers meet it at reset. Monetary policy cannot make energy appear. It can only make credit dearer. The Riksbank chose the second while the first remains unsolved. This is a deliberate choice.
