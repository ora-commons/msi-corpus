Victoria binds books by hand in a fourth-grade classroom without screens. Her father, German Gil, has spent much of his life looking at screens; he has worked at Netflix, Facebook, and GitHub. For Victoria, he bought his way out: San Francisco Waldorf School’s Pacific Heights campus, where children learn by hand and, as Gil put it, his “kid could be a kid.”

The exit has a price. Waldorf charges $40,000 a year for kindergarten and nearly $60,000 for high school. The campus sits two miles from Anthropic’s headquarters. The school offers financial aid, but its price list remains a declaration of intent: Silicon Valley families can purchase an education free of the products Silicon Valley sells, while the public system remains charged with serving everyone else.

That is not merely personal hypocrisy. It is the model.

The people best equipped to understand these technologies are paying a fortune to keep them away from their children. And they are right to do so. Screens are not pedagogy. Software is not a teacher. A dashboard is not a relationship. The problem is not the parents’ diagnosis; it is who can afford to act on it.

A screen-free childhood is becoming another luxury good, while children in poorer communities are left with the platforms, monitoring, and automated instruction that wealthy parents can reject. The market is working exactly as designed.

Silicon Valley parents possess something other parent movements often lack: intimate knowledge of the products being pushed into schools. When San Mateo Union High School District formed a volunteer task force to examine artificial intelligence in education, at least two members worked at major technology companies, including one in Google’s AI division. Their advantage is not vague suspicion. They know product roadmaps, data pipelines, incentives, and the machinery behind the pitch.

They are not Luddites confronting equipment they barely understand. They are insiders asking what the machinery is built to do, whom it serves, and who is expected to carry its costs.

Parents such as Palo Alto college counselor Candice Wei, who leads a local chapter of Schools Beyond Screens, have told her that they toured public campuses, saw children on screens, and decided to go private. More fortunate still are technology workers with the social capital and technical knowledge to pressure district boards. Their first move is often not to reform the public system but to leave it.

Their insider knowledge is reaching Washington, where [bipartisan senators are seeking answers from Google about classroom AI risks](/articles/2026-10-21-bipartisan-senators-seek-answers-from-google-on-classroom-ai-risks/).

And they are winning ground. San Mateo has stopped sending Chromebooks home to children from kindergarten through second grade and imposed a blanket cellphone ban for high schoolers. Mountain View is moving away from i-Ready, the adaptive reading and mathematics program criticized for repetitive lessons and accused of sharing student data without consent; its developer disputes those allegations. Palo Alto has prohibited smart glasses.

The revolt has spread beyond California. New York City council members have [called for a student YouTube ban](/articles/2026-09-14-nyc-council-members-call-for-ban-on-student-youtube-use-in-schools/), while major districts in New York and Los Angeles have embraced their own technology rollbacks.

What began as an emergency response to remote learning has hardened into permanent infrastructure. In 2025, 99% of teachers surveyed by the *New York Times* said their students received school-issued devices, and 81% said their schools gave children screens by kindergarten. The pandemic merely supplied the excuse. Once districts bought devices, licenses, testing programs, teacher training, and parental expectations, the system began demanding that children keep using them.

This is not a neutral convenience. It is a business model.

The people building educational technology understand better than anyone how difficult it is to extract attention from children. A classroom filled with screens extends that business beyond the school day. Homework extends platform use beyond the school day. Assessment turns learning into continuous data collection, while multimillion-dollar contracts place a vendor’s claims at the center of a child’s curriculum. Teachers become the human face attached to a software dashboard.

Yet the most consequential argument for the rollback is not technological. It is educational. Research cited by educators points to stronger retention when children learn with pen and paper. Critics also argue that constant screen use can weaken the student-teacher relationship and remove the productive struggle through which children learn to reason rather than merely respond. As Yvonne Kao, senior director of learning sciences and technology at WestEd, puts it, people are more likely to work for a teacher they love than for a computer incapable of loving them back.

Parents such as Candice Wei are therefore demanding that every classroom tool justify itself against the human alternative. They are asking schools to do what schools should have done before signing multimillion-dollar contracts: define the learning outcomes, examine the student data, demand evidence, and establish what happens when the promised gains fail to arrive. They are refusing to let a vendor’s sales pitch substitute for an educational result.

That is a modest demand. It should be the minimum.

The revolt will fail if it remains a private revolt.

Private schools can change direction faster than public systems and have more money to spend on teachers rather than apps. Public schools do not have a Waldorf waiting with a scholarship. Staff salaries are the largest expense at any school, which means that when budgets tighten, technology becomes an easier saving than another classroom. Less-resourced districts do not get the luxury of replacing automated programs with adults. They get the reverse: adults stretched thinner while the software spreads.

The Trump administration’s backing for school-choice vouchers and education savings accounts can harden the divide. When families use public money to leave for private schools, that funding leaves with them. A family can purchase screen-free schooling with public dollars while the district left behind faces fewer teachers, larger classes, and longer commutes for families that remain. The rollback becomes a two-tier system: affluent children surrounded by adults, poorer children handed the devices their wealthy neighbors rejected.

This rebellion has a class address.

Jon Valant, director of the Brown Center on Education Policy, warns that children in the lowest-income areas may spend even more time on screens, even when their parents do not want them to. That is not a side effect of an otherwise sound reform. It is the class logic of the reform.

Communities with the most technological expertise use that knowledge to humanize education. Communities with the least bargaining power are handed the platforms because software is cheaper than teachers and easier to purchase than political power. The result is a brutal inversion. The techlash, unless made public, will make that inequality permanent.

Nor can “screen-free” be mistaken for a complete philosophy of education. The linked reporting warns that [school screen bans risk isolating millions of students with disabilities](/articles/2026-06-04-school-screen-bans-risk-isolating-millions-of-students-with-disabilities/), and human-centered education has to provide accessible alternatives rather than order every child into the same low-tech room. Reducing distraction and supporting disability are not the same objective. The tech parents should know better; they helped build the systems in the first place.

The fight is over who controls technology, what it replaces, and whether it can prove its results. These parents should put their insider knowledge to work inside public institutions: contracts tied to measurable learning outcomes, enforceable privacy protections, meaningful human review, and consequences when software fails. Above all, they should defend the teachers whom districts treat as the first thing technology can replace.

Restrict screen use by age, especially in the early grades. Require districts to publish the evidence and privacy terms attached to educational-technology contracts. Prohibit vendors from selling student data or using it for advertising without explicit consent. Redirect savings toward teachers, counselors, and the human work machines cannot perform. Ensure that disability-related accommodations remain genuinely accessible. Make human connection a standard of public education rather than a benefit attached to a $40,000 private-school invoice.

Silicon Valley built the future in which every classroom becomes a market for its products. It cannot then complain when parents ask whether that future deserves to be compulsory. If technology cannot survive in a school allowed to teach children without it, then it was never educational technology. It was a business model wearing the costume of pedagogy.

German Gil’s daughter has a paper-and-pencil childhood because her family could purchase one. A child cannot buy back their own attention. The Silicon Valley parents who built these systems know what they do. They should use that knowledge to make public education more human, not make human education a private subscription.

Otherwise their rollback will do what it has already done: succeed in the wealthiest classrooms while the poorest children are handed the screens everyone else rejected.

Children are not users. Classrooms are not markets. And human teaching is not a luxury for those whose parents happen to work for the future.
