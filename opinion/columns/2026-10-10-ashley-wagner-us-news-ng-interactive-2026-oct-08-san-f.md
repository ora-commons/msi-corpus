San Francisco is evicting the working class and calling it progress. The city's rent board reports eviction notices up 44%. The average one-bedroom now runs $4,400 a month — more than $53,000 a year — and the mayor has declared a rent emergency. The deal that was supposed to make this country work was: do everything right and you earn a place. Get the degree, hold the job, save what you can. San Francisco is breaking that deal in public and calling the wreckage an economic boom.

Start with what the rent actually means for the people who keep the city alive. A home health aide in San Francisco earns about $52,000 a year. A one-bedroom costs $53,000. She would have to spend 103 percent of her income on rent to stay. A childcare worker — the person who watches your toddler so you can go to work — earns around $41,000. The rent eats 129 percent of that. A public-school teacher at $75,000, which is good money for the job, still hands over 71 percent of her paycheck for a place to sleep. Housing advocates have a number for this: anything above 30 percent and you are "cost-burdened." These workers are not burdened. They are underwater before the first grocery trip.

The Joint Center for Housing Studies puts a dollar figure on what drowning looks like. Renter households under $30,000 that spend more than half their income on housing — and there are more than 12 million of them nationally — are left with about $310 a month for everything that isn't rent or utilities. Groceries. Gas. A kid's shoes. Meanwhile, a childcare worker in San Francisco earns $1,000 too much a year to qualify for food stamps. Too rich for assistance. Too poor for the city where she works. That's the trap: the income cutoff and the rent ceiling close in from both sides until there is nothing between them.

The math my parents did in the 1990s doesn't exist anymore. My father made about $42,000 as a postal supervisor in Lansdale, Pennsylvania. A modest house there cost three or four times that. Today a home health aide in San Francisco earns $52,000 and a one-bedroom costs more than she makes. That's not a housing shortage. That's a decision — by landlords, by the tech capital pouring into the city, by every policy choice that treats a place to live as an instrument for returns rather than a place for a family to stand.

*The Last Black Man in San Francisco*, the 2019 film, is about a man trying to reclaim his grandfather's house in the Fillmore. He watches it slip away to someone with more money. What the film understands — and what the Guardian's reporting on the Powell Street building confirms — is that displacement isn't just an economic transaction. It's the erasure of a community. When the teacher who organized the school play gets priced out, what leaves with her isn't just a tenant. It's the person who made the neighborhood worth living in.

San Francisco's landlords are willing to combine a seven-unit Powell Street building — a mile and change from Anthropic's headquarters — into four luxury condos. They are invoking the Ellis Act to withdraw buildings from the rental market entirely. Maria Zamudio's Housing Rights Committee logs a 23 percent increase in landlord-dispute calls. The city's own Labor Council reports that only 7 percent of union members — nurses, teachers, firefighters — could afford to live in the city even before the most recent spike. None of this is an accident. It is what the market does when you let it treat a house the way it treats a stock. Anne Helen Petersen wrote that burnout "isn't a personal problem," and neither is this. Jia Tolentino wrote about the pressure to treat yourself as a market asset to be optimized; San Francisco is treating its residents that way too, optimizing them right out of the city.

Here is the choice a San Francisco parent faces right now: stay in the city and hand over nearly $3,000 a month for childcare on top of the rent, and watch the bank account empty every month. Or move to Tracy — sixty miles east, a two-hour commute — where the rent is survivable, but the free childcare from the grandparents who live three blocks away disappears, and the school district you spent a year fighting to get into is gone. That isn't a market finding its level. That is a family being told that the price of keeping your kids near their grandparents is the same as the price of keeping your job. The only family-wage argument that ever worked in this country was the one that said a person's pay should be enough to support a household, not just a worker. That is the argument the boom is demolishing.

Swift wrote a song about this — "The Lucky One," from *Red*. You spend your whole twenties doing what you were told to do, and then you look up and realize the people who got the life you were promised weren't playing by the rules you followed. The lucky ones weren't luckier. They were standing closer to the money. In San Francisco, the money is AI capital, and it is buying the city block by block. A seven-unit building near Anthropic's headquarters becomes four luxury condos. A teacher becomes a resident of Tracy. A childcare worker becomes a statistic in a Labor Council report. Swift's narrator looks at the bargain and finally sees it for what it is. So should the rest of us.

The math is the math. And even with every advantage — my husband's grandmother's estate put a chunk of our down payment on our Philadelphia rowhouse; we clear $8,800 a month after taxes — the numbers still barely work for us. I am not writing this from San Francisco. I am writing it from a kitchen table where I have run the same math these families are running, and I know what it feels like when the promise comes apart in your hands. The promise was: do everything right and you get to stay. We did everything right. The deal was gone before we got there.
