In West Virginia this year, a Republican finance chairman tried to put a $5,000 cap on a school voucher program that had ballooned from $9 million in its first year to a projected $300 million. The Republican Party's billionaire backers — the same people who had spent millions creating the program — decided that counting the receipts was a firing offense. They spent more than $145,000 to install a 23-year-old gas station cashier over him in the primary. The challenger won with 61 percent of the vote.

This is what "school choice" looks like in 2026. Not parents choosing schools. Not competition. Not innovation. It is an uncapped entitlement with no income test, no sunset, no fiscal guardrail — defended by billionaires, executed by party organizations, and ruthlessly enforced against anyone, including members of their own party, who tries to slow it down.

Vernon Criss, chairman of the West Virginia House Finance Committee, had done the math. He voted to create the Hope Scholarship in 2021 — the nation's first universal school voucher program, no income test, no exceptions — back when it cost the state $9 million. Five years later it serves 26,000 students. The state is on track to have spent roughly $150 million on it for the year just concluded, and pre-session estimates for the coming year ranged from $230 million to $300 million. Combined with the Morrisey-era tax cuts, the math stopped working. Criss said the state and its lawmakers needed to "face the music." He proposed capping individual awards at a little more than $5,000 a year. He was not trying to kill the program. He was trying to make it survive.

Within five days, parents had crowded the Capitol hallways with their children and their homework. Lobbyists and organizers peddled the line that Criss was trying to "kill the program." They weren't there for the children. They were there because three billionaire-funded operations — the Koch family's Americans for Prosperity, the Club for Growth's School Freedom Fund, and a Yass-funded Sugar Maple PAC — had decided in February that Criss had failed what one of them called "a litmus test."

What followed was a textbook execution. Sugar Maple, seeded with $450,000 from Pennsylvania billionaire Jeffrey Yass — a man who has spent more than $350 million on political campaigns since 2015 — dropped $86,000 to attack Criss and $15,000 to back his opponent, Charles Hartzog, a 23-year-old gas station cashier who had never held public office. Across the statehouse landscape, the School Freedom Fund poured nearly $1 million into West Virginia races and Americans for Prosperity spent around $700,000. The combined three-PAC outlay against Criss came to more than $145,000, a sum that swamps anything the candidate could have raised on his own.

The ads they ran were not about school choice. They were about Criss's vote against a workplace citizenship check. They called him a liberal. They mailed fliers depicting him driving a garbage truck — "Vernon Criss' liberal trash" — over his vote against an employer citizenship verification law and his vote against West Virginia's abortion ban. The substance of his actual sin, the one nobody wanted to discuss in attack ads, was that he had tried to slow down a program that was eating West Virginia's public schools alive.

The schools are dying. Since 2019, seventy public schools have closed and enrollment has fallen by more than 30,000 students. By the end of this school year, up to twenty more could close. Here is the scale of the trade: the Hope Scholarship now serves 26,000 students — more than the state's largest public school district enrolls. The entitlement grows without limit while the buildings closing are in the public system. In that same legislative session — the one in which lawmakers walked past families in winter jackets crowding the finance committee's hallway, and in which Criss's $5,000 cap died in committee — the legislature passed emergency legislation to keep a single school district open. No crowds in the galleries. No PAC money. No headlines. The broader bill to fix the state's public schools had died entirely. Criss's actual sin was trying to prevent that outcome. His reward was a 22-point primary loss and a long-shot write-in campaign for the general.

This is not a partisan story. It is a story about the capture of a state political party by out-of-state money, and the machinery behind it has been built for years. Americans for Prosperity has operated in West Virginia since the mid-2010s. It helped write the Hope Scholarship. It recruited the Cardinal Institute, a homegrown conservative think tank, and the Institute for Justice, a libertarian litigation outfit bankrolled by Yass, to defend the program in court. It helped put Patrick Morrisey in the governor's mansion with $10.8 million from a Yass-funded super PAC. Morrisey then amplified the unseating effort by signaling which legislators were "not aligned" with his worldview. Then, when one of its own elected officials — a Republican, a committee chairman, a man who had voted to create the program five years earlier — proposed a single fiscal guardrail, the same network spent a quarter of a million dollars to destroy him.

Read that again. The donor class responded to a budgeting request by destroying the career of the man who had built the program they liked. This is not corruption in any classical sense — no bribery, no quid pro quo required. It is something more efficient: a pure primary challenge designed as a deterrent. The Criss operation cost the donors a rounding error. The Morrisey operation cost them ten million dollars, almost all of which came back to them in policy outcomes. The math works for them every time.

The Club for Growth's president, David McIntosh, was jubilant afterward. "These elections are a warning to Republican leaders like Vernon Criss — if you oppose school freedom, expect to be held accountable," he wrote. Americans for Prosperity's West Virginia state director, Jason Huffman, called the cap a "litmus test." A litmus test, enforced by funded primary challengers, is not an idea in competition with other ideas. It is a command. The PACs targeted ten incumbent Republicans. They ousted four. Their biggest prize was the man who tried to count the receipts.

Meanwhile, the money does not have to win elections honestly. It only has to scare the next Criss into silence. It does not have to persuade the public. It only has to punish the official who tries to do basic arithmetic on a $300 million line item.

Criss is running a long-shot write-in campaign. He is almost certainly going to lose. A 22-point loss in a primary you were unopposed in two years ago is not easily reversed, and the donor class didn't need to defeat him in November — they needed to make the example. They have made it. The gas station cashier who beat him will go to the Capitol with no mandate except the one the PACs purchased. Vernon Criss thought he was defending a program he had helped create. He learned, in real time, that creating it was the easy part. Keeping it within the state's means was the unforgivable offense.

For everyone else still serving in a legislature that takes Koch money or Yass money, the message is the one McIntosh already delivered: vote our way, or be made into the next cautionary tale. There is no third option. There is only the next primary. The voucher machine works exactly as designed. The only thing it does not tolerate is arithmetic.
