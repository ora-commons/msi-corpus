OpenAI, Meta, and Anthropic are shopping for places where the water doesn't get a vote. Sam Altman negotiated for your water behind nondisclosure agreements. That is not a rival's accusation; it is the plain sense of his own company's Thursday announcement — two days after candidates promising to freeze data-centre development won statehouses in Texas, Georgia, Ohio, and Michigan, as [voters backed limits and complicated the AI expansion](/articles/2026-11-05-voters-back-data-center-limits-complicating-ai-expansion/) — that OpenAI would no longer require public officials to sign nondisclosure agreements before discussing data-centre deals, and would publish the energy, water, and noise figures for its projects. The election was Tuesday. The conversion arrived Thursday. A company does not promise to stop requiring a signature unless it was requiring one, and a company that discloses its water numbers the week after the public acquires the power to stop it has told you exactly what those numbers were worth to it before, and at what price they were being kept.

Altman spent Wednesday fielding the mood among his own staff. During an employee question-and-answer session he acknowledged, according to people familiar with the matter, that the public is concerned about water use, and said that whatever the reason, he understood why people might not want to live near a data centre; at one point he mused that the company should perhaps look at building in the desert. Senior OpenAI leaders were, in the same days, discussing whether to reset expectations for next year's data-centre capacity, as the Journal had previously reported, and employees assigned to data-centre development were exchanging intelligence on the newly elected Democrats in key states and comparing notes on how best to engage them, in messages the Journal has reviewed. Across the industry, companies that have committed hundreds of billions of dollars to the AI race and cultivated relationships with friendly officials at every level of government woke up facing blanket moratoriums, tighter environmental regulations, and longer review timelines.

It is worth being precise here about what a data centre is, because the industry has been remarkably successful at getting the public to think of it as a vaporous thing called "AI infrastructure" rather than as a building. A data centre is not a metaphor. It is a machine for converting clean water and firm electricity into arithmetic and heat, with water carrying the heat away in evaporative cooling towers — where the water leaves as vapour and does not come back to the river — and a "closed loop" that only moves the bill to the grid. It is a machine of a size that now requires its own power plants: Meta has already signed a reported twenty-year agreement to buy the entire output of Constellation's 1.1-gigawatt Clinton nuclear station in Illinois, and Microsoft's reported twenty-year arrangement to restart Three Mile Island Unit 1 is of comparable size. Altman's concession treats the public's water concern as a sentiment. It is not a sentiment. It is a flow rate, and flow rates can be published. The desert, it hardly needs saying, is where the water is scarcest. The man who had just conceded that water was the concern proposed, in the same breath, to move closer to the shortage.

It is true that data centres bring money to towns, in the narrow sense in which any very large industrial customer brings money to a town: a few years of construction work, a permanent operating staff famously small relative to the site's footprint, and tax revenue that arrives pre-discounted, because the abatement is part of the product. That is a real want, and no column should pretend towns that take the deal are fools. The trouble is that nobody outside the room was permitted to audit the ledger while it was being written, because the negotiations ran under the very nondisclosure the company has now promised to stop requiring, and the people whose aquifer was on the table were not in the room. These are decisions about a town's water, its grid, and its skyline of cooling towers, made in the venue where a town has the least standing: the private negotiation. Tuesday was the first time in years that the venue changed. The moratoriums that follow will be signed by governors, not plebiscites — but the governors are the vote. OpenAI's pledge to publish its water figures is, read plainly, an admission dated this week that the figures existed all along and were not public. Nobody issues a press release about a lock they never had.

The executives' response has been quick and, within its own terms, coherent. Across the industry, the people in charge of government relations were opening lines to incoming governors' transition teams, working out how campaign-trail rhetoric translates into policy. Dina Powell McCormick — president of Meta, overseeing its AI-infrastructure operations — told the Journal's tech conference on Wednesday night that the company had already found an incoming governor ready to parley, and quoted him: "As soon as we get past the inauguration, let's meet, because we don't want to lose this investment in our town. We don't want these jobs to go elsewhere." Set that beside the campaign that won him the office: data centres were the issue he ran on, and the promise was a freeze. The voters were promised a moratorium; the transition team is being offered a meeting, a site plan, and a tax schedule. Meta, Powell McCormick said, assumes that building in the United States is "going to be harder," that doing it from scratch will be "very challenging," and the company is increasingly weighing sites outside the country. Her other avenue was to scale up the data centres that already exist, whose surrounding communities, having banked the jobs and the tax revenue, "don't require as much persuasion." That is this page's own argument delivered in the industry's quotation marks: a data centre goes where the persuasion still works.

Brad Gerstner — an investor with capital on every side of this table — put the industry's exposure on the "All-In" podcast at as much as 35 percent of the American data-centre construction planned for next year. Treat that as one man reading his own portfolio, which is precisely what makes it worth reading.

Anthropic saw the weather coming and said so in writing. To be fair, the filing that names the risk is doing what filings do: warning the people about to buy shares. Its S-1 — the registration statement a company files with securities regulators before selling shares to the public — was filed last week, before the election, and warns that state and local moratoriums "could delay or prevent the construction or expansion of facilities on which we depend for compute." The same document counts roughly $895.4-billion of AI computing and infrastructure commitments through 2045. Read the filing's two items together and you have the industry's position in a sentence: a company that must build at a scale it has already promised its investors is now being told, in places, that it may not build where its customers and its water are. In a prospectus, a risk factor names a threat to the business; read plainly, the threat this filing names is a vote. The filing also reports that Anthropic is pursuing projects in several non-U.S. jurisdictions as part of a plan to "reduce concentration risks associated with operating a significant portion of our compute infrastructure in the United States." Concentration risk, in the ordinary usage of such a document, means dependence on one supplier, one region, one chokepoint; here the hazard named is that the compute sits within reach of the American public. Translated out of prospectus: when the locals acquire a vote, move the machine to where they have not got one yet. Those are the power appetites towns are being asked to host, on grids those towns share — and the repositioning had been underway for weeks, because [the backlash was already forcing both parties to reposition](/articles/2026-10-14-data-center-backlash-forces-bipartisan-repositioning-ahead-of-us-midterms/) before a single vote was counted.

Kate Crawford's *Atlas of AI* is built on the insistence that these systems are not disembodied intelligence but mines, smelters, grid electricity, water, and human labour arranged in a very long industrial tail — that AI is made from natural resources, fuel, and human labour, and that the supply chain is the thing you should actually look at. The offshore move is that observation in practice. The arithmetic is portable; the aquifer is not; so the aquifer gets swapped for one sitting under a friendlier process. This is not a bug that surfaced on Wednesday. It is the extraction, repriced — the same buildout, resumed at the price consent used to cost.

Harold Innis built a national school of political economy on the observation that a staple economy is one that ships the raw material out and books the value-added somewhere else — cod, fur, pulp, hydro; fur, fish, timber, wheat, steel — while the colony keeps the tailings, the decisions are made from outside, and the value leaves on the trains. The data centre is the staple economy in its final form, and the staple is compute. It consumes a power station's worth of electricity and a community's worth of cooling water, and it exports the product over fibre to customers everywhere else. It exports arithmetic. It keeps the noise, the draw on the aquifer, and the twenty-year power contract. Canadian readers should not watch this from the balcony. Provinces and municipalities have spent two years courting the same companies with the same tax schedules, the same cheap hydro, and, where reporting has surfaced it, the same quiet terms, on the theory that this time the jobs will stay. The theory has a track record.

My father spent thirty years as a millwright in a mill town that was there because the mill was there, and the mill was there because the river was. The mill was bought in 1995 by Gerdau, a Brazilian steel group, and the argument for every concession that followed was the same sentence the incoming governor used this week: we don't want to lose this investment; we don't want these jobs to go elsewhere. The sentence outlived the jobs. My father kept his place and several of my uncles did not. A third of the line was laid off, and the men who kept their places worked sixteen more years beside the absence of the men who did not. I offer that not as sentiment but as pattern-recognition. The people who live where the extraction happens are consulted last and repriced first. I have been watching this playbook for a long time. The sentence works. The industry has had thirty years to learn exactly how well it works. The mills never got a vote either.

Powell McCormick's proposed remedy for the public's doubts is communication — the industry must "double and triple down on telling the story" — and OpenAI's president, Greg Brockman, concurred that the companies need to do a better job of "communicating to local communities about why data centers are something that can be beneficial to them." It is fair to grant that a freeze is a blunt instrument, and that some of the projects now at risk would be genuinely useful; the trouble is that the industry spent a decade ensuring no finer instrument existed, because a negotiation conducted under nondisclosure leaves the public exactly one lever, and this week it pulled it.

Cory Doctorow's account of platform decay — enshittification, he calls it, and the coinage has held up better than most — belongs here precisely because it is a theory about constraints rather than virtue: executives did not become worse people between 2019 and 2026; the frictions that once stopped them — competition, regulation, self-help, labour — were dismantled one clause at a time, and Tuesday was one of those frictions being reinstalled by the bluntest instrument a democracy possesses. Nothing about the machine changed this week. What changed is that the machine has to ask. Doctorow has also spent a decade documenting how technologies and practices get normalized first on people with the least power to refuse, and climb from there, and a decade of data-centre siting followed that curve faithfully — into counties where the zoning board meets part-time, the tax base is thin, and the daily paper left years ago. It is true that data centres have to be built somewhere, and somewhere is always somebody's county. The trouble is that "somewhere" was chosen, for years, by the party least interested in being asked — and on Tuesday the practice finally climbed into states with the political capacity to answer. Texas, Georgia, Ohio, and Michigan are four of the biggest data-centre states in the country, which is why freeze candidates could campaign on the issue at all.

It is tempting to file the whole business under local squabbling, but by one recent accounting [AI-related companies now make up roughly half of the S&P 500](/articles/2026-11-05-ai-related-companies-now-make-up-roughly-half-of-the-s-p-500-npr-editor-says/), which makes the buildout the load-bearing wall of the American market. John Kenneth Galbraith famously called the bezzle the interval in which the embezzler has his gain and the victim does not yet feel poorer, and observed that the bezzle swells in every boom. Several hundred billion dollars of the current boom is committed against water tables, substations, and town councils that were never shown the plan. Elections are a discovery mechanism. Tuesday began the discovery.

The remedies are unglamorous and close to hand, and not one of them forbids a data centre from being built. Legislatures can ban siting nondisclosure agreements by statute — a press release can be quietly retired; a statute cannot — and require water, energy, and noise models to be filed publicly before approval rather than published after the election that follows it. Utility commissions can treat a request to connect a gigawatt-scale load as the industrial demand it is, priced at full cost of service; every tax abatement can carry a clawback that triggers if the promised jobs do not arrive; and where a community votes for a freeze, the freeze should be treated as the answer rather than as an opening bid. If an industry finds domestic votes inconvenient enough to move the plant to a weaker jurisdiction, then the terms on which it uses the American grid — the interconnection queue, the ratepayer arithmetic, the twenty-year power contracts — deserve a public second look, with the towns at the table. The point is not to stop the industry. The point is to make it negotiate in the room where the aquifer lives.

Altman mused that OpenAI should perhaps build in the desert. The desert is where the water already isn't. That is not a siting strategy; it is the objection, restated as a plan.

The meetings are being requested, and the companies have told us, in their own words, that the story is coming. The numbers, meanwhile, were always in a drawer somewhere — that was the arrangement. OpenAI put them on the table on Thursday, two days after the voters found the drawer. Forty-eight hours turns out to be the going price of transparency in this industry. The week's genuine discovery is that the voters can set it. The story was never the problem. The problem is a flow rate — and as of Tuesday night, the flow rate can lose an election.
