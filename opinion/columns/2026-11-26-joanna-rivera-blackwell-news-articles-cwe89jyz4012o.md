Doug Wilson teaches women to surrender their votes to their husbands.

He said as much this month from Moscow, Idaho, in the polite grammar the movement prefers: patriarchy is “in accordance with the way God made the world,” a society led by men is “a very great blessing,” and the household votes as one—by its head, and the head is usually a man.

The practice is not merely theoretical. Seven percent of the votes in his church’s polity are cast by women. Only in “odd” circumstances—divorce, bereavement, a quadriplegic husband—does a woman presume to hold the office of household head. Pressed about the arrangement, Wilson granted what the arithmetic already proved: it gives men “outsized influence.” That is an admission, not a neutral description. The rule puts the man’s judgment in the place where the family’s civic voice belongs.

Wilson would go further. He wants the Nineteenth Amendment repealed, women excluded from leadership in the church and roles in civic affairs, and other faiths barred from public office. He has described the country he wants as a theocracy in which consensual same-sex sex would be criminalized and abortion outlawed. He calls repeal a low priority, then adds the sentence that matters: “But we do think it.”

Current law is not what Wilson wants. The Nineteenth Amendment now forbids denying the vote on account of sex. Until that amendment is changed, women vote and hold office in the United States; a husband has no legal authority to cast his wife’s ballot. Under Article V, Congress can propose an amendment after a two-thirds vote in both chambers; when legislatures of two-thirds of the states apply, Congress must call a convention to propose it; and either state legislatures or state conventions in three-fourths of the states must ratify it. Wilson’s repeal is an ultimate goal, not the present law of the land. For his program, it is not a side issue or a down payment. It is the constitutional gate through which the larger order would pass.

I know this way of reading from the inside. I was raised under it, served the apparatus for thirty years, and taught its texts the old way. Wednesday night, the women’s-study workbook would open to Ephesians 5:22. The teacher’s pen would skip verse 21—“Submit to one another out of reverence for Christ”—because that verse would ruin the lesson. I kept the room safe the way the rules defined safety: by teaching women not to ask too many hard questions of the men who held authority.

I was wrong. I was not defending a difficult verse. I was teaching women to encounter that verse through rules that turned it into permission for their exclusion. I helped build the culture in which Wilson now speaks. I did not leave the Bible to leave that culture. I left it by reading the Bible. I am still in church.

Let me show you what is on the page.

Galatians 3:28 says, “There is neither Jew nor Gentile, neither slave nor free, nor is there male and female, for you are all one in Christ Jesus.” Paul is not murmuring a pious sentiment in a detached paragraph. In Galatians 3:26–28, baptism into Christ and being clothed with Christ surround the verse; the old distinctions are named and retired in the same breath. The woman Wilson would disenfranchise did not enter the church through some lesser doorway. She came in through that baptism.

The legalist reading keeps Galatians 3:28 in one compartment and women’s civic standing in another, as though Paul could establish equal standing in Christ and then authorize unequal standing under law. The chasm is plain: the text says all are one in Christ, while Wilson’s rule says the woman’s vote belongs to the man who heads her household. That does not settle every question about ministry, authority, or home. It does show what Wilson’s rule must do: keep one verse from following a woman out of the church and into the square.

First Timothy 2:11–15 contains difficult language, and I will not turn a hard passage into an easy one. It tells women to “learn in quietness and full submission,” prohibits a woman from “teach[ing] or assum[ing] authority over a man,” and speaks of childbearing. Within its first-century congregational setting, its plain reading is prohibition, not permission for women to exercise teaching authority over men. The verb translated “assume authority,” *authentein*, appears nowhere else in the New Testament. Verse 15 grounds a woman’s future in childbearing—a line that Wilson’s opponents do not preach as universal and that his allies do not treat as an obvious principle of human worth.

But whatever First Timothy 2 requires inside a first-century congregation, it does not mention ballot boxes, household voting, the Nineteenth Amendment, religious tests for office, or a theocratic constitution. Wilson’s proposed rule is specific. In the home, the man votes for the family. In the state, the Nineteenth Amendment is repealed. In public office, non-Christians are excluded. The people placed at risk are women, religious minorities, and anyone who understands American citizenship as a public equality rather than a privilege awarded by theological preference.

That is the legalist chasm. One difficult command concerning teaching and conduct in an early church is made to authorize a civil hierarchy the passage never describes. No ballot box appears anywhere in the chapter. Wilson’s rule must make First Timothy 2 say more than it says because the system needs the text to say more than it does.

The rest of Scripture will not cooperate with that silence.

Judges 4:4–5 names Deborah “a prophet” who was “judging Israel,” and says the Israelites went up to her to have their disputes decided. When Barak would not march without her, he said, “If you go with me, I will go; but if you don’t go with me, I won’t go” (Judges 4:8). The ESV says Deborah was “judging Israel”; the NIV says she was “leading Israel.” Either way, Israel came to her for judgment. For a theology that says God made the world with men on top, Deborah is not an exception tucked into the margins. She is a prophet and chief magistrate in the middle of the Hebrew Bible. The modern apparatus that must call her an exception lets a footnote do the work the verse refuses to do.

When the Book of the Law was rediscovered in the temple, King Josiah sent his officials to Huldah the prophet, and her verdict became the kingdom’s authorized reading of Scripture (2 Kings 22:14–20). The canon’s own precedent for who may authenticate the text is a woman.

Paul greets Phoebe as a deacon, using *diakonos*, the same word translated “deacon” when it describes a man (Romans 16:1–2), and calls Junia “outstanding among the apostles” (Romans 16:7). Junia was rendered “Junias” by medieval scribes and later translators; no man of that name is otherwise attested in antiquity. Priscilla and Aquila took Apollos aside and explained the way of God more adequately to him (Acts 18:24–26). Peter declares that God will pour out his Spirit upon daughters as well as sons (Acts 2:17–18), and Luke reports that Philip’s four daughters prophesied (Acts 21:9). Ephesians 5:21 places mutual submission before the household instructions that follow.

Matthew 23 gives Jesus’s own warning about religious leaders who shut the kingdom in people’s faces (Matthew 23:13), neglect justice, mercy, and faithfulness while preserving their status (Matthew 23:23), and make religious practice into a performance of godliness. Jesus is not opposing precision. He is exposing what becomes of meticulous rulekeeping when it becomes a way to keep others outside while preserving power for the rulekeepers. Wilson’s rule asks for the opposite: use religion to shut women out of self-government and call that fidelity.

None of those passages erases First Timothy 2. A difficult passage does not authorize us to stop reading the rest of Scripture. But one text is made to silence the rest of Scripture, and that is the legalism.

The modern system is younger than the Bible it claims to derive. Beth Allison Barr’s *The Making of Biblical Womanhood* traces the contemporary apparatus to the 1987 Danvers Statement, issued by the Council on Biblical Manhood and Womanhood, and the 1991 publication of Piper and Grudem’s *Recovering Biblical Manhood and Womanhood*. The Southern Baptist Convention’s 2000 revision of the Baptist Faith and Message, chaired by Adrian Rogers, then restricted the office of pastor to men. That revision even deleted from the 1963 confession the clause naming Jesus Christ “the criterion by which the Bible is to be interpreted.” The rule is younger than the fax machine, even if the verse is nineteen centuries old.

Wilson did not excavate male headship from the original Greek or Hebrew. He inherited a twentieth-century construction and sharpened it. Jemar Tisby’s *The Color of Compromise* supplies the needed historical warning through the American church’s pattern of complicity, silence, and resistance. Institutional loyalty does not settle what Scripture requires. The moral question is not whether Wilson can find words in a Bible. It is whether his use of them can withstand Christ, the prophets, the whole witness of the Church, and the people his rule excludes.

He has preached a sermon titled “The Lost Virtue of Sexism.” A man who renames sexism a virtue and expects the canon not to answer him is not reading the Bible with more care. He is rubber-stamping a preference across Deborah, Huldah, Phoebe, Junia, Priscilla, Mary, and the daughters who prophesy, while trusting that no one checks the index.

He built a denomination. He did not build a consensus. Wilson’s Communion of Reformed Evangelical Churches has roughly 150 churches in the United States, compared with about 350,000 Christian congregations nationwide. His view is a fringe within Evangelical Christianity, and the overwhelming majority of American Christians support women’s right to vote. Even a canon of the Washington National Cathedral has said of Wilson’s proposal, “In my mind, there is no biblical precedent or standing for what he proposes.” He does not get to speak for the tradition he drafted it from.

Dale Partridge and Joel Webbon are not interchangeable with Wilson, and the record does not show that either supports Wilson’s household-voting polity or shares his precise constitutional destination. It does show that each seeks to curtail women’s voting. Partridge, who has about 900,000 followers across social media, has promised, “We will repeal the 19th Amendment within ten years,” and has said most women cannot responsibly vote because too many of them are liberal. Webbon has said women’s desires are wicked and advocated taking away their vote. Wilson calls the two men disruptors and rage-baiters who “overshot by a long way.” He allows that repeal could take a century or more. Whether their rhetoric is tactically reckless is not the only question. Their stated demand to remove women’s votes should not be softened because Wilson scolds their delivery.

We have heard “they will never touch it” before. I spent thirty years inside the apparatus that said no one would undo *Roe*—said from pulpits I sat under and workshops I helped run—and then watched forty-nine years of precedent fall in one June. Julie Ingersoll, who has studied this movement for decades, draws the same warning: they chip, and chip, until the wall comes down.

Household voting is not a new argument either. It is the argument the anti-suffragists made a century ago—the home is already represented by its head—reissued in modern fonts. What is new is the address list: a secretary of defense in the pews, a church in England freshly added to Wilson’s network, more than thirty congregations abroad, and influencers in Brazil pressing the same repeal with the same religious language. The brand travels.

Now set Pete Hegseth beside Wilson. The man who oversees the country’s war plans calls Wilson his mentor. Hegseth’s Pentagon describes him as a proud member of a church affiliated with Wilson’s Communion of Reformed Evangelical Churches and says he “very much appreciates many of Mr. Wilson’s writings and teachings.” He worships at the network’s Washington, D.C., church. In November, Hegseth joined Wilson on stage at a Pentagon prayer service and said, “Thank you for your leadership, for your mentorship, for the things you’ve started, the truth you’ve told, your willingness to be bold.” Wilson calls the relationship warm and “largely a pastoral one, not a political one.”

That distinction does not erase the facts, but the facts do not prove that Wilson wrote Pentagon policy. Hegseth chose to make a pastoral relationship public in a national-defense setting and elevated Wilson as the mentor who told him “the truth” and emboldened him. A prayer service at the Pentagon does not, by itself, establish an Establishment Clause violation, and nothing cited here shows an official Defense Department endorsement of Wilson’s theology, a departmental policy based on it, or an allocation of resources to advance it. The supported accusation is narrower and still serious: Hegseth has turned devotional allegiance toward Wilson into a public national-security credential. He should stop presenting his mentor’s “truth” and “boldness” to Pentagon personnel as gifts he carries into the office of defense.

Reporting has also documented leaders within Wilson’s network publicly backing [Hegseth’s plan to livestream an execution](/articles/2026-10-15-crec-leaders-back-hegseths-plan-to-livestream-hasan-execution/). Pastoral counsel with a camera mounted on it is still not proof that the Defense Department has adopted Wilson’s platform. It does show men within that network intervening in national-security debates under the authority of their religious formation.

The immediate political fight over whether [Hegseth vows to remain in office as Democrats and some Republicans seek his removal](/articles/2026-11-07-hegseth-vows-to-stay-as-democrats-and-some-republics-seek-his-removal/) may determine his political future. It cannot substitute for the narrower question of what a secretary of defense does when he presents one minister’s theological authority to the people who oversee the armed forces.

The wider movement has a political destination as well. At a recent Dallas conference, [Christian nationalists vowed to push MAGA further right](/articles/2026-11-22-christian-nationalists-vow-to-push-maga-further-right-at-dallas-conference/). Wilson supplies that movement with something the conference cannot supply by itself: a biblical grammar for male supremacy and religious exclusion.

Andrew Whitehead and Samuel Perry, who developed the standard survey measures of Christian nationalism, found that Christian-nationalist identification predicted support for Donald Trump in 2016 more strongly than evangelical identity, denomination, church-attendance frequency, or theological conservatism. They named the engine in a plain clause: Christian nationalism “is all about power.”

Kristin Kobes Du Mez’s *Jesus and John Wayne* traces the seventy-five-year trade by which white Evangelicalism exchanged the Jesus of the Gospels for John Wayne. Wilson’s patriarchal rule is the domestic wing of that trade. Hegseth is its most prominent public customer now holding the Pentagon: he has called the minister his mentor, joined him on the Pentagon stage, and praised the truth and boldness he says Wilson gave him.

The proposed exclusion of non-Christians from public office cannot be enacted as constitutional law. Article VI provides that “no religious Test shall ever be required as a Qualification to any Office or public Trust under the United States.” The clause answered a colonial history of religious tests. It does not forbid every religiously grounded argument; it forbids government to decide who may hold office according to the Faith. Wilson’s proposed exclusion of other faiths would be invalid under Article VI.

If Wilson wants outsiders to envy the country he describes, Matthew 25:35–36 offers another measure: the hungry are fed, the stranger welcomed, the naked clothed, and the sick visited. Luke 1:52–53 names the reversal still waiting inside his own Scripture: God has brought down rulers from their thrones, lifted up the humble, filled the hungry with good things, and sent the rich away empty. The Bible nowhere instructs the church to win an envy contest.

The cost falls where the rules fall. Wilson’s household-voting rule gives male church leaders religious authorization for authority they claim in the home, the church, and the state. His repeal campaign gives Hegseth a mentor he can honor before the Defense Department. His example gives Partridge and Webbon a religious brand for removing women’s votes. Women bear the cost. Religious minorities bear the cost if exclusion becomes constitutional practice. Democratic pluralism bears the cost whenever citizenship is treated as a theological privilege.

The women in Wilson’s pews are not the enemy. The rule that tells them their vote belongs to their husband is the enemy. They are not gullible or weak. They were taught by people who had authority, and the teaching was made to look like Scripture.

Repentance here cannot mean feeling better about a sentence in a sermon. It requires Wilson to withdraw his push against women’s suffrage rather than relabel it a lower priority. It requires Partridge and Webbon to withdraw their advocacy for removing women’s votes, whatever differences they have with one another. It requires churches to restore women to teaching and leadership as a matter of justice, not as a courtesy. It requires Hegseth to stop treating a devotional relationship with Wilson as a national-security credential. The political fight over whether Wilson remains a low-priority repeal advocate cannot substitute for those acts.

I would name this legalism whichever coalition used it. If a progressive operation made a liberation text into a coalition loyalty test—conscripting a prophet to bless a platform rather than disturb one—I would name that the same capture of Scripture by power. The offense is not the side of the fight. The offense is making power look holy.

The discipline cuts toward my own tribe, too. The post-Evangelical world I write from carries its standing temptation: contempt for the women who remain in patriarchal pews, as though staying were the sin and leaving the virtue. They are not Wilson’s bylaws. They are readers of the same Bible I am, working through the same texts I worked through, with less time so far to read in this direction—as I once had not. The indictment lands on Wilson’s rule. The pew is where I write from.

Luke opens his Gospel with a song, and he gives it to a girl. “He has brought down rulers from their thrones but has lifted up the humble. He has filled the hungry with good things but has sent the rich away empty” (Luke 1:52–53). Before the church had a single bylaw, its first recorded theologian was a young woman singing the reversal of thrones. Men who want women’s ballots returned to their husbands sing her song every December without hearing what she said.

I am not going to tell you I have audited the mind of God. I can read. The page says daughters prophesy. The page says a woman judged Israel and the nation came to her. The page says the new covenant’s first word came from Mary. Wilson says patriarchy is the way God made the world; Mary’s song is older than his sermon, shorter than his bibliography, and still in the Bible.

So leave the Bible open on the kitchen table. Read Judges 4 aloud. Ask your class who judged Israel, sit through the silence, and read the verse again. Your Bible is not his. Your grandmother’s ballot was counted. The text has not changed; the operation has. The operation has a name: repeal the Nineteenth Amendment, transfer the ballot to the male head of the household, and tell women that their chains are a sacred arrangement. They said “we do think it” about *Roe* for thirty years before the wall came down. Believe them about this.

The women in those pews do not need to be driven from the tradition. The tradition needs them in the room where its doctrine is made. Do not let a difficult verse make the church smaller by making the women in it silent.
