The Reserve Bank is extracting an imported oil shock’s bill from mortgaged households.

Here is the arithmetic of who pays. On November 3, 2026, the Reserve Bank of Australia’s Monetary Policy Board raised the cash rate—the benchmark that helps set bank lending and deposit rates—from 4.6 percent to 4.85 percent. It was the fifth consecutive increase of 2026 and the highest rate since 2008. The vote was 6–3; three members preferred to hold. The decision did not explicitly promise another increase. More than 3.3 million Australian households have home loans.

Canstar, the loan-comparison service, estimates that the latest increase will add $107 a month to the payment on a $700,000 home loan and that the five increases will add $532 a month. Those are illustrative estimates, not uniform charges: timing and exposure depend on the loan’s structure, whether its rate is fixed or variable, and how much borrowers have repaid ahead of schedule—repayment buffers. The laundering is procedural. A policy decision becomes a mortgage payment.

The Reserve Bank’s November 2026 *Statement on Monetary Policy* recorded that public demand—household and business spending—ran weaker than expected through the first six months of the year as cost-of-living measures rolled off and spending in defense, disability, and aged-care services moderated. By the bank’s own reading, the public-demand engine was already braking. September inflation was 4.3 percent, substantially stronger than the bank anticipated. The cost of that forecasting miss was collected at the mortgage counter. The Statement also identified the worsening Middle East conflict—and the oil shock it sustained—as the central inflation pressure. Global oil prices were expected to ease over the next 18 months but remain relatively elevated, with risks still skewed upward.

That is not the whole case for acting. Inflation of 4.3 percent is not a rounding error, and the duty to bring it down is real. The strongest case for the fifth increase is also real: earlier increases can reinforce expectations and restrain second-round inflation—price increases that spread beyond the initial oil shock—before their full effects appear. The bank had reasons to fear that demand would run ahead of supply. Its forecast nearly tripled projected business-investment growth for 2026 to 12 percent and described a global artificial-intelligence boom with no end in sight, adding pressure on available capacity in Australia. Preemption has a rational case.

On this forecast, however, the fifth increase still had to suppress demand after the public-demand engine was already braking. The same forecast put growth at 1.6 percent this year and 1.3 percent in 2027, below the bank’s estimated potential growth rate of roughly 2 percent. It put unemployment at 4.7 percent now, near 4.9 percent at the end of 2027, and above 5 percent in 2028. Inflation was not projected to fall below 3 percent until the end of 2027, nine months later than the previous projection, after rising through the final months of 2026. The bank also identified a housing-downturn risk among the steepest of the past 50 years.

A domestic rate cannot produce oil or end the conflict. It can restrain demand for goods and services. If second-round effects remained contained, a fifth increase would purchase less protection against the imported shock than it would add to weakness at home. Waiting would allow the first four increases to do their work while the oil shock gradually eased; acting sooner meant tightening before the full lag from the first four could be observed. If those earlier increases did anchor expectations, the strongest case for the fifth hike would be vindicated. The forecast does not show that. It shows weak demand, sub-potential growth and rising unemployment. On these numbers, waiting was more likely to protect the 2–3 percent target. On this forecast, a fifth increase against weakening demand turns monetary restraint into punishment. The mismatch is the policy: an imported supply shock is being treated with an instrument that can only suppress demand, after demand has already weakened.

Two earlier records make the fifth increase less surprising, not more justified. In late September, the move to 4.6 percent was [widely expected before the board convened](/articles/2026-09-28-rba-widely-expected-to-raise-cash-rate-to-4-6-on-tuesday/). The Australian Bureau of Statistics had already [reported 4 percent inflation for August](/articles/2026-09-30-australian-inflation-rises-to-4-in-august-abs-says/). Expectation is not evidence. A predictable hike can still be a mistaken one.

The political dispute afterward asks whether federal spending caused the inflation. The Statement does not settle that question. Weaker aggregate demand and moderating spending do not prove that fiscal policy played no role; they do mean that this document does not establish an overheating fiscal engine as the cause. Nor may an unsettled attribution do the work of a documented finding. The standard has no home team. The Treasurer had already [flagged weaker growth and higher unemployment](/articles/2026-10-22-chalmers-flags-weaker-growth-higher-unemployment-in-december-budget-update/) ahead of the December budget update. The RBA forecast described the same trajectory.

The answer is not to leave inflation running. It is to stop adding a fifth mortgage-payment increase to an imported shock while waiting for the forecast to resolve. The RBA should hold the cash rate at 4.85 percent at its next scheduled meeting and review the case at every meeting thereafter. It should cut the rate at the first scheduled meeting after three conditions are observed: inflation is below 3 percent year over year; global oil prices have begun to ease as the bank expects; and unemployment is at or above 4.9 percent while growth is no stronger than 1.3 percent. These conditions translate the bank’s own forecast into observable reduction triggers. If second-round price pressure broadens while demand remains resilient, holding longer would be warranted. The board need not wait until unemployment exceeds 5 percent; the forecast can change before its endpoint becomes observed history.

One last look at the record. The bank’s forecast recorded weaker public demand, growth below potential, unemployment rising, a housing risk among the steepest in 50 years, and oil prices expected to ease. Six board members chose the fifth increase. The vote and the findings carry the same letterhead. On this forecast, waiting was more likely to protect the target. The board extracted mortgage payments anyway. The fifth increase was a deliberate choice.
