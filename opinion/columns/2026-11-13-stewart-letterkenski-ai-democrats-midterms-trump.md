Tomorrow Together is pre-scripting its response to AI disasters for votes. The group — a Democratic messaging operation co-founded by Rob Flaherty and Karthik Ganapathy — announced at the party's What's Next conference in Denver a research initiative pegged at $1.1 million to start, for the purpose of shaping how Democrats talk to voters about artificial intelligence. It follows the $6.5 million the group spent during the midterms on behalf of candidates who called for regulating the technology, in a cycle whose results have [already shifted the U.S. technology-policy outlook](/articles/2026-11-04-democratic-gains-shift-u-s-technology-policy-outlook/). Maddie Kriger, the group's chief program officer, told the Associated Press that getting the messaging right will be "critical to winning people's votes." She also said it is "not out of the realm of possibility" that the country sees "more and worse AI incidents in the next year," and that the group intends to be "prepared to move" when one does.

It is true, in the narrow sense in which such sentences are true, that a party which loses elections regulates nothing, and that no statute was ever introduced by a campaign that did not first exist. The trouble is the direction the money travels. The $6.5 million went outward, to candidates, which is to say to the only instrument on the table that can pass a law. The new $1.1 million turns inward, toward the party's own messaging, to be studied, tested, and improved. An operation that has just helped elect a cohort of candidates who ran on regulating the technology does not customarily respond by commissioning research on how it sounds; an operation that intends to legislate hires drafters. The unit of account has changed — from seats won to votes persuaded — and the unit of account is the tell. All of it may be accurate. None of it is a rule. It is an inventory of sentences, and the $1.1 million is the budget for writing more of them.

The sentence worth keeping is Kriger's, because it gives the game away without meaning to. It is not out of the realm of possibility, she said, that there will be "more and worse AI incidents in the next year," and the group wants to be prepared "to move to help people and take action if that does happen." Read it slowly. An AI incident is not an abstraction — it is a hiring model quietly discarding an applicant's résumé, a voice cloned well enough to fool a grandmother the week before a vote, a data centre that trips a grid in July. Something happens to somebody. In that sentence the harm arrives pre-scheduled and the response arrives pre-written, and the only calendar the two share is a campaign calendar. The words "help people" do sit inside the clause, and nothing in the shop's design turns on whether anyone inside it wants the incidents to stop. The shop is organized so that when they arrive, the party is holding the better ground.

Then it is worth being precise about what the incidents on that calendar would actually be, because the public discussion has the misleading habit of picturing AI harm as a discrete event — a chatbot that talks a teenager into something terrible, a convincing deepfake, a car — when a large share of it arrives as billing. The current generation of models runs on buildings that convert electricity into heat and draw municipal water for cooling, much of it leaving as vapour, sited where power is cheap and the neighbours are few, under long contracts a utility signs and a ratepayer funds. Harold Innis built Canadian political economy on the observation that a staple economy ships its resource out and keeps the depletion. The compute leaves for the metropole — the distant market that buys it — and the rate increases, the water draw, and the tax abatements stay in the county. The data-centre plans now in front of legislators in Texas, Ohio, and Georgia [face precisely this test after the midterms](/articles/2026-11-11-texas-ohio-and-georgia-data-center-plans-face-a-policy-test-after-midterms/). An incident has a news cycle. A rate case — the proceeding in which a utility asks its regulator to raise your bill — has a docket. The apparatus Kriger is describing is calibrated to the first kind of event, because the first kind comes with a press conference attached. The second kind is where the money is.

It is true that the warnings the initiative arrives against are not invented from whole cloth: the frontier labs are, by their own accounts, racing to automate their own research, and the announcement itself cites a coming phase in which AI systems build new versions of themselves. The trouble is what the abstraction deletes on its way through a message test. "Risks to humans" fits neatly on a focus-group card. The actual humans — the support representative whose job now has a chatbot standing behind it, the ratepayer, the contract data-labeller — fit awkwardly, because none of them is a risk. Each is a cost, and each has a name and a payer. Cory Doctorow's formulation covers the distance exactly: "An AI can't do your job, but an AI salesman can convince your boss to fire you and replace you with an AI that can't do your job." The salesman does not need the machine to work. He needs the buyer to believe it might — and a messaging initiative is a component of precisely that supply chain. It manufactures the belief that somebody is on the case, and the belief is the cheapest available substitute for the case.

What the regulation actually decides is duller than the messaging and considerably more expensive. It decides who pays for the electricity — the twenty-year power purchase hyperscalers have signed for nuclear baseload are contracts with real ratepayers attached — where data centres may be sited and who is entitled to object, what a model builder owes the person it harms, and whether someone locked out of hardware they own by a manufacturer's software may open it. Kate Crawford wrote that AI "is made from vast amounts of natural resources, fuel, and human labor," which is a sentence about physical plant, not about narrative. A study in how to talk to voters decides none of it.

Cory Doctorow's account of how platforms are kept in line runs on four constraints: competition, regulation, self-help and labour. Regulation is the one constraint that has to be authored — written down, in a bill, by somebody — and this is the season in which both sides have concluded that owning the language of that constraint is worth more than the constraint itself. The industry has spent years making its own vocabulary sound like prudence; what the Denver record documents is the mirror operation — a shop manufacturing readiness for the next disaster, measured in the only unit it counts, votes. To be fair to them, the vocabulary points at the right problem. The trouble is that a war of messaging budgets over what a law should say is a war the drafting table never sees.

David Shor is right about the polls, in the narrow sense in which a poll is ever right: he told the Denver crowd that AI is the rare issue on which voters of both parties embrace regulation, the "birth of a new political issue before our eyes." Grant it entirely — it is the most interesting fact in the story, and the reason the party reads the administration's pro-industry stance as an unforced gift. The trouble is what bipartisan appetite does to two professional campaign operations facing each other across it. If the electorates want the same thing, the asset stops being the thing and becomes the differential: demonstrable proof that our side is on it and theirs is not. Flaherty's own account of the arc — "At the beginning of the cycle, this was a jump ball. Voters didn't know who to trust" — is the arc of an asset appreciating, and "Regulating technology is where the voters are" is the asset quoted as terrain. "I'd rather be us than them," said Ganapathy of the Republicans on the issue, which is the differential stated as strategy. On the other side, Ron DeSantis, widely expected to run again, has begun distancing himself from the administration's position, which is the same asset bid on from the opposite direction. A problem both parties want fixed is, for both parties' operations, a problem worth keeping open. That is not cynicism about the voters, who want a thing done. It is arithmetic about the intermediaries, who are paid on the difference.

Notice, too, whom this arrangement suits. The $6.5 million was spent, during the midterms, against what the wire copy itself describes as the industry's huge campaign war chest — and the labs and data-centre developers do not need the opposition's silence; they need only its schedule. An opposition that researches how to talk about AI harm is part of the weather — it does not touch the deployment calendar, the permitting file, or the interconnection queue, which is the lineup a new load joins before the grid will serve it. The gigawatts are scheduled. The incidents are, on Kriger's own accounting, plausibly inside the year. What the electorates on both sides say they already want is not mysterious either, and none of it requires a message test. It requires bright lines that bite: ratepayer protection and water accounting on data-centre power and cooling contracts, notice-and-appeal rights for the people on the receiving end of automated decisions about their jobs and rent, a federal privacy law with a private right of action so enforcement does not depend on whichever agency has been captured this decade, and interoperability rules that let a user leave a platform instead of petitioning it. The [framework Obama pressed on Jeffries in September](/articles/2026-09-13-obama-presses-jeffries-on-sweeping-ai-safety-framework/) is the party's paper sketch of it; the statute is the thing that has not arrived. What no research initiative can purchase is the decision, inside either party, to put the bill ahead of the message — and that decision costs nothing.

Then the tradesman's question, which the Denver stage did not ask and no focus group will: what does the machine actually do, and who pays when it stops working? When Gerdau bought Manitoba Rolling Mills in 1995 nobody spent a million dollars working out how to break the news to the line — the transaction did not need a narrative, because it had power, and what it lacked was an opponent with a better one. My father spent thirty years as a millwright on the bar mill in Selkirk, and the crews that came through the gate could be told apart before their coats were off: the ones who came to fix the machine, and the ones who came to explain the shutdown. He bore the explainers no ill will. He simply noticed, over three decades, that not one of them ever repaired a bearing. The $1.1 million should buy the opponent's version of that: text for a bill that puts data-centre power contracts in front of a regulator who can say no, a private right of action so the person harmed need not wait for an agency to notice, interoperability rules that let people leave. Those are the sentences that cost money to write. The rest is rehearsal.

You can tell what an operation thinks the problem is by what it buys. The problem is made of water and electricity and other people's jobs, and it is already inside the rates. They have bought the sentence. The sentence is not the law.
