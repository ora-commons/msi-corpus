Noah Sanford and Dean Kirby are entrenching their political power by redrawing Mississippi’s legislative map.

The maps are nearly finished. The people who will live and vote under them have not seen them.

Republican Rep. Noah Sanford of Collins and Republican Sen. Dean Kirby of Pearl have been meeting lawmakers individually to show them the proposed districts while they wait for Gov. Tate Reeves to call a special session. Sanford and Kirby say they are close to finishing the redraw. Kirby told Mississippi Today, “We’re actually on hold waiting for the governor to call a special session,” while his office added that it does not know whether Reeves will call one or what he will put on the agenda. Reeves recently told WLBT that he still has “every intention” of a special session for redistricting.

The calendar supplies the urgency. Candidates begin filing paperwork for legislative seats on January 4, one day before the regular session convenes on January 5. Lawmakers say a special session is necessary because candidates otherwise might file for districts the Legislature is still redrawing. Sanford and Kirby do not see the same urgency for congressional maps; those can wait until the regular session.

Procedure is not housekeeping. Procedure is the vehicle.

Give Sanford and Kirby their true claims first, because they are true. Candidates should not spend money and organize campaigns in districts that may disappear before the primary season. Compact districts, roughly equal population, fewer split voting precincts, and keeping small counties together are legitimate mapmaking considerations. Sanford has made the last two priorities central to his proposed House map. Kirby says the Senate map will be compact, roughly equal in population, and guided by voting results in the last three statewide general elections rather than race.

A genuine filing problem gives them a responsibility. It does not give them a blank check.

The deadline does not choose the map. Sanford and Kirby do.

And the public process has placed the politics ahead of public examination. The joint redistricting committee held hearings across Mississippi throughout the summer and fall. Sanford and Kirby, meanwhile, have been walking lawmakers through draft districts one by one. That is consultation among insiders. The people who cannot read the maps until the Legislature adopts them are waiting to learn what the lines will do to their votes.

The public has been told why the session is necessary before it has been given the maps that make the session necessary. If the product is merely technical, publishing it before the vote costs nothing. If publication is withheld because the maps reveal who benefits, then the urgency belongs to the politicians, not the voters.

Start with the special session and work backward. It exists because the map is unsettled. The map is unsettled because the Supreme Court’s Callais decision removed protection for majority-minority districts and, in Republican leaders’ own assessment, opened the road to partisan redrawing. The same scramble appeared [in Mississippi and other states](/articles/2026-04-30-states-move-to-respond-after-supreme-court-ruling-limits-race-in-redistricting/).

The legal sequence is the indictment. A federal three-judge panel ordered the Legislature to redraw its legislative districts in 2024. Lawmakers complied in 2025. Then the Supreme Court overturned that order. After the change in the law, lawmakers formed the joint committee, conducted the public hearings, and returned to the map with Sanford and Kirby leading the work. What had been compelled was now optional. The optional task became a political prize.

The compliance maps are now the objects of the erase-and-redraw. In 2025, Mississippi drew because a court required it. Now the Legislature draws on its own authority, guided by a method its leaders selected after the referee left the room.

Hear the schedule and hear the refusal in the same breath.

Most of the Mississippians who spoke at the hearings told lawmakers they did not want the districts redrawn. Some asked for maps that would be “race neutral.” Hundreds had already [gathered in Mississippi against the Republican redistricting push](/articles/2026-10-02-hundreds-rally-in-mississippi-against-republican-redistricting-push/). Some Democratic politicians have speculated that the hearings helped produce a surge in Black voter turnout in the recent U.S. Senate election, in which Democrat Scott Colom ousted Republican Sen. Cindy Hyde-Smith.

That speculation belongs to the politicians making it, and the case does not need it. The result stands on the record without their theory of causation. A hearing is not a referendum, and no columnist should pretend otherwise. It is evidence. The evidence is not a blank check for Sanford and Kirby.

Now watch the criterion, because the word is doing the work. Kirby says the committee is examining how districts voted in the last three statewide general elections rather than race. That distinction does not accomplish what its defenders want it to accomplish.

“Race neutral” can mean two different things. It can mean that no racial variable was entered into the drafting instructions. It can mean that the finished map produces no racial effect. The first does not establish the second. A map can be race-neutral in its recipe and partisan in its effect.

Past voting can carry the geography of race and politics without anyone writing race into the instructions. Nearly two in five Mississippians are Black. The 2024 court order placed the relationship between Mississippi’s racial geography and its electoral map at issue. In a state where racial order and ballot order have long tracked one another, an election-history key can become race with the serial numbers filed off.

The maps have not been published. No responsible analysis can manufacture a precise seat count or pretend to know what every proposed district does. But the absence of the maps is not a defense of the mapmakers. It is the defect. The public cannot judge whether a map protects representation, dilutes votes, advantages one party, or does all three when it cannot see the lines or the data behind them.

Sanford says his redrawn House map will not look radically different from the current one. That is not an acquittal. Legislative elections can turn on modest shifts. “Not radically different” is not a finding that the map is neutral; it is a description that only becomes meaningful when the map is available for inspection. Compactness, equal population, fewer split precincts, and whole counties can coexist with partisan advantage.

Cui bono? Start with authorship. Sanford and Kirby lead the mapmaking. The immediate beneficiaries are Sanford, Kirby, the Republican incumbents whose seats the lines protect, and the Republican candidates who will organize behind those lines. The durable beneficiary is the Republican Party’s control of the next map. The cost falls on Black voters and communities whose majority-minority representation was protected before Callais, and on every candidate forced to learn the political terrain after the mapmakers have finished shaping it.

Follow the benefit upward through office. A line allocates votes. Votes allocate seats. Seats allocate control of the next map. The party that draws the map protects not only the election it wins but the power to redraw again. That is not an abstraction. That is the mechanism.

A statewide race can be moved because every vote counts equally on election night. A district cannot be moved because the district was drawn first. Lines keyed to past elections can convert a surge-capable electorate into a locked one between surges.

Colom’s victory does not prove that the hearings caused a turnout surge. It does not need to. Its practical lesson is that a statewide electorate can break through a statewide contest. The maps now being finished in Jackson can prevent the same electorate from breaking through the next legislative contest. Functionally, that is insurance against the next one.

Whether the collision between the filing deadline and the session is design or indifference, it lands where the slack is thinnest: on first-time candidates, on underfunded campaigns, and on the voters over whom the lines were drawn. Reeves controls whether the session happens before those candidates file. Sanford and Kirby control what they file into.

The *Star Wars* prequels understood that republics can be hollowed out through legalistic means: manufactured crisis, emergency authority, and procedural assent that transfers power while leaving the formal shell intact. Mississippi’s mechanism is less theatrical. A filing calendar creates urgency. A special session supplies the venue. A leadership-authored map supplies the result. Each step has respectable vocabulary. Together they move power without requiring the public to choose it openly.

Padmé named the mechanism in *Revenge of the Sith*: “So this is how liberty dies—with thunderous applause.” Mississippi’s applause is procedural silence. No one has to defend the map before the affected public sees it.

The machinery is the tell.

Karis Nemik’s manifesto in *Andor* gives the operating cost of concentrated power: “Tyranny requires constant effort.” Mississippi’s mapmaking machinery requires sessions choreographed around filing windows, legislators tutored map by map, and hearings endured and then overridden. A majority secure in its own numbers does not need that much machinery. The effort is not accidental. It is the tell.

The late King’s structural critique applies with force here. In his August 16, 1967, SCLC presidential address in Atlanta, “Where Do We Go From Here,” Martin Luther King Jr. argued that racism and economic exploitation were not isolated prejudices but parts of a larger structure of American life that had to be changed. A legislative map is one place where that structure becomes geometry. The lines determine whose votes count as a bloc, whose votes are scattered, which communities acquire recognizable political power, and which remain fragmented enough to be managed from above.

King was also right about the order-loving moderate in his April 16, 1963, “Letter from Birmingham Jail.” The obstacle he named was not only the man in the hood, but the reassuring moderate who valued calm over justice and believed he had the authority to set another person’s timetable. King was right, and he was incomplete if we stop at individual character. The structure matters because Sanford and Kirby are not merely exercising a preference. They are deciding the geography through which everyone else will exercise theirs.

Their case has the cadence King distrusted: compact, equal, whole counties, not radically different. Fluent order, and not one sentence that answers the question the order is built to avoid. A peace that leaves the sort standing is not peace. It is the sort, renewed.

Mississippi has been at this drafting table before, and the state was honest about it the first time. The delegates who wrote Mississippi’s 1890 constitution declared the elimination of the Black vote to be their purpose, then built the machinery—the poll tax, the literacy test, the understanding clause—to accomplish it. The Twenty-Fourth Amendment barred the federal poll tax in 1964. The Voting Rights Act of 1965 suspended state poll taxes and literacy tests.

The methods succeeded one another. The function did not change.

Medgar Evers was shot in his own driveway in Jackson on June 12, 1963. James Chaney, Andrew Goodman, and Michael Schwerner were murdered and buried in an earthen dam in Neshoba County in June 1964 for registering voters. Fannie Lou Hamer told the Democratic National Convention in Winona in August 1964 what had been done to her for the crime of trying to vote. Mississippi wrote this book chapter by chapter, and every outlawed chapter was followed by a rewritten one.

No one in Kirby’s committee room is the 1890 convention. The election-history key, however, can be read as that convention’s newest edition. The test is the test the convention failed: does the machinery of the state track the census, or does the census get laundered through the ballot box before it is allowed to reach the map?

The standard cannot be that no map may favor a party. Every map has effects, and pretending otherwise produces useless analysis. The standard is that no party may use a judicial opening to manufacture its own advantage while the public sees only the schedule. That rule binds the other party with equal force. It binds Sanford and Kirby now. That is not balance. That is equal accountability.

Reeves should not call the special session until the proposed House and Senate maps are public. The committee should publish the maps, precinct assignments, population totals, compactness measurements, split-precinct counts, alternatives it considered, and an analysis of the maps’ racial and partisan effects. Then the committee should hold another public hearing before the vote. Sanford and Kirby should state their criteria in writing and defend their districts line by line. Every legislator should cast a public vote and answer for it.

Until those things happen, “race-neutral” is an assertion rather than a verifiable fact. The phrase is doing inoculation work: a label attached to maps before the public has seen them so the maps can be waved through as settled. It is not a method anyone can check until the maps, precinct assignments, population data, and voting analysis are public.

Publish the map. Explain the map. Let the affected public see its consequences before the Legislature makes them binding. If the map survives the light, let it stand. If it cannot, the process failed before the ink dried.

The Beloved Community does not require every Mississippi district to look identical on a map. It requires that no party convert public power into its private inheritance and call the result procedure. The people who came to the hearings asked lawmakers not to redraw the districts. The people who rallied made the demand harder to dismiss. Sanford and Kirby can explain why their maps should still prevail. They cannot ask Mississippi to accept the result before it has seen what they have done.

The moral arc bends only when specific people, in this specific moment, refuse to let power draw itself in the dark. Reeves can hold the session until the people can judge it. Sanford and Kirby can publish the lines they want to govern. The Legislature can conduct the vote in public and answer for every district it enacts.

Read the map the day it drops. File to run on the fourth of January. Organize the next election that moves a statewide tally. Vote in numbers the lines were drawn to make impossible. The arc is not a weather pattern. It is a joint, and joints turn when they are worked. By every means the law and the ballot place within reach, work the joint.

A map is government drawn with lines. Mississippi deserves to see the hand behind every line before power inherits it.
