Nikki Floris and Bradley Benavides will face a federal judge in Washington on Tuesday in a Hunter Biden prosecution announced days before the midterms and brought without a grand-jury indictment; the combination makes the case a test of political power.

A complaint is lawful. A complaint is not an indictment. Both remain true. Prosecutors may charge by complaint, and Tuesday’s preliminary hearing asks whether probable cause supports the charges. The missing indictment neither proves the evidence weak nor bars the case from proceeding. It means the government chose to ask a federal judge to start the prosecution without first obtaining a grand-jury indictment. The Justice Department is thought to have repeatedly struggled to persuade District of Columbia grand juries to return indictments in high-profile Washington cases. That makes the choice unusual and worth explaining. It does not make the evidence inadequate.

[The complaint now at issue](https://mainstreetindependent.com/articles/2026-10-23-complaint-alleges-task-force-leaders-misstated-roles-in-hunter-biden-inquiry/) alleges that Floris and Benavides, former leaders of the FBI’s Foreign Influence Task Force, falsely denied contemporaneous knowledge of Hunter Biden’s abandoned laptop and participation in internal FBI discussions about it when they spoke to lawmakers in 2023. Their lawyers deny wrongdoing and call the prosecution baseless and politically motivated.

False statements to Congress can be criminal. But prosecutors must identify what each defendant said, why it was false, and that each knew it was false when he said it. Evidence that Floris and Benavides once briefed skeptical senators does not by itself prove misconduct years later.

The laptop is not on trial. Neither former official is accused of stealing it, planting Russian material, manipulating Hunter Biden’s later gun and tax cases, or undoing President Biden’s late-2024 pardon of his son. The court is not being asked whether the computer was part of a Russian influence operation, whether Hunter Biden engaged in foreign corruption, or whether the 2020 election was stolen. It is being asked whether probable cause exists for two alleged false denials.

In 2020, [earlier reporting traced the laptop’s discovery and the disinformation concerns surrounding it](https://mainstreetindependent.com/articles/2026-10-23-hunter-biden-laptop-discovery-drew-concern-about-possible-russian-disinformation/). The New York Post reported that Hunter Biden had left the laptop at a Delaware repair shop in 2019 and that the Post had obtained its hard drive from Rudy Giuliani, a close ally of President Donald Trump. The Post reported emails appearing to show some of Hunter Biden’s foreign business dealings. The discovery raised fears among Democrats and former intelligence officials that the computer might be part of a Russian influence operation. Then-national intelligence director John Ratcliffe later said there were no signs it had anything to do with Russian actors. The Russian-involvement theory was left unsupported. What remained was less an unbroken criminal investigation than a prolonged political struggle over who could use the laptop and whom it could damage.

In August 2020, Floris and Benavides briefed Republican Sens. Chuck Grassley and Ron Johnson as the senators pursued their own investigation into Hunter Biden’s work in Ukraine. The briefing leaked, and the senators accused the FBI of discrediting their inquiry and falsely linking it to Russian disinformation. The FBI was trapped in the middle, defending the integrity of its information while politicians tried to turn the laptop into a campaign weapon.

Congressional Republicans had long criticized the task force, saying it tried to discredit their investigations into the Biden family. Then-Attorney General Pam Bondi disbanded it in February 2025. The charges came afterward, announced Friday in the final stretch before the midterms. That sequence does not prove selective prosecution. It puts selective prosecution at the front of the docket: after years of political attacks on the task force and its disbanding, why these two defendants, these two statements, this charge, and why no indictment?

Wilhoit’s law has two speeds: the law protects an in-group without binding it and binds an out-group without protecting it. Charles Keating was the rare exception. After Lincoln Savings cost taxpayers $3.4 billion, he served about four and a half years before his convictions were overturned on a technicality in 1996; he pleaded guilty again in 1999. Asked whether the roughly $1.3 million he gave five senators bought influence, Keating said: “I want to say in the most forceful way I can: I hope so.”

The common exhibit was HSBC, which admitted laundering money for Mexican drug cartels and sanctioned regimes, forfeited $1.256 billion and paid $1.9 billion under a deferred prosecution, and saw no individual prosecuted. Sen. Grassley said HSBC “has quite literally purchased a get-out-of-jail-free card.” These are not rival systems. They are the same law at two speeds.

Probable cause is the court’s question. The election calendar is the Department’s question. A grievance is not a case. A lawful complaint can be. The timing is politics attached to the charging document. Tuesday, the government must show its receipts and explain why it did not seek a grand-jury indictment.
