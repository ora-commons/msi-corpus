If the investigative report is right, Navatek routed $150,000 through a shell company to the Super PAC backing Susan Collins in exchange for assurances of $32 million in federal contracts, and Donald Trump’s purge of the FBI helped bury the inquiry.

Let that land.

The alleged arrangement was not a campaign contribution in the ordinary sense. It was a price tag beside a contract promise. Navatek CEO Martin Kao, now in federal prison after pleading guilty to fraud, reportedly told other executives that Collins would deliver the business. The FBI was moving to investigate the matter in late 2024, according to the report. After Trump returned to power, agents involved in the investigation were pushed out and the Justice Department office overseeing investigations of sitting politicians was dismantled.

That is not a paperwork dispute. It is an alleged pay-to-play scheme followed by an alleged cover-up.

Collins’s deputy chief of staff, Annie Clark, called the reporting “categorically false.” Her campaign manager, Steve Abbott, claimed the Biden Justice Department and FBI had already cleared Collins. No such clearance was announced by the Biden Justice Department. The second defense also carries an awkward little weight: if the FBI was never investigating the arrangement, what exactly was cleared.

Kao is a convicted fraudster. Fine. That does not make his testimony worthless. Federal prosecutors use cooperating witnesses because people inside a scheme know where the money went, who was promised what, and which door opened after the payment. A witness who pleaded guilty is not automatically a liar. He is a witness whose account must be tested against the shell company, the money trail, the contract trail, and the investigators who were reportedly sent home.

And $150,000 is not a rounding error when the promised return is $32 million. It is the arithmetic of corruption: a small payment beside a large favor, with enough room for every participant to call the purchase something else.

Collins has held the Senate seat since 1997. She chairs the Senate Appropriations Committee, the gavel that directs federal money toward favored places and favored projects. That power is precisely why a defense contractor would allegedly pay to keep the gavel in friendly hands. The question is not whether Collins personally stuffed $150,000 into her pocket. The question is whether access to federal contracts was offered for a price and whether the institution capable of finding out was dismantled before it could finish.

That is the corruption problem.

Maine Democrats understand the charge. Troy Jackson, the former state Senate president and Democratic nominee, called the allegation “corruption of the highest order.” State party executive director Devon Murphy-Anderson said Collins “sold us out yet again” and was “bailed out by the president of the United States.” The first phrase is campaign language. The second points at the load-bearing allegation: that Trump intervened to protect a Republican senator from a federal inquiry.

The race has been assembling around that question for months. Jackson was [endorsed by Kamala Harris](/articles/2026-07-27-harris-endorses-jackson-in-maine-senate-race-boosting-democratic-bid/) and [backed by Reproductive Freedom for All](/articles/2026-08-26-reproductive-freedom-for-all-backs-jackson-in-maine-senate-race/). Graham Platner’s [suspension amid an assault allegation](/articles/2026-07-09-platner-suspends-maine-senate-campaign-after-assault-allegation/) cleared the way for the [July 25 convention](/articles/2026-07-14-maine-democrats-to-pick-maine-senate-nominee-at-july-25-convention-after-platner-s-exi/). Harris’s endorsement consolidated the opposition. The infrastructure is in place. Now the voters have a number.

Lance Dutson, who ran three Collins campaigns, said the report was “definitely not helpful.” He also said the “headwind from Trump is very strong” and that voters would decide whether the national argument mattered more than Collins’s “direct relationship and positive impact over the state.” That is a strategist’s way of saying the old coalition may no longer be enough.

In 2020, Collins defeated Sara Gideon by eight points, even as Joe Biden carried Maine. She has survived by presenting herself as the durable exception: Republican enough for Washington, independent enough for Maine, bipartisan enough for the aisle. But a senator who chairs Appropriations cannot now ask voters to treat a reported $150,000 payment beside a reported $32 million promise as ordinary politics.

The issue is not whether Collins has done good things. The issue is what power was used for, who paid to reach it, and who shut down the inquiry.

Maine voters can decide whether a Senate seat is public office or a contract menu. The math is on the table. The gavel is theirs.
