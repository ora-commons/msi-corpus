Scott Kirby made $32 million in 2025, called wealth inequality a “danger to society,” and said he would pay the taxes he proposed. The chief executive of United Airlines said it in a *New York Times* interview about growing public distrust of executives and whether he felt responsible for answering it. His answer contained no corporate boilerplate. He called for a national wealth tax, arguing that it would not work state by state because people would move. He said inheritance taxes should come close to 100 percent at some level. Then came the sentence that turned the argument into a political grenade: “I would pay them.”

Fox Business and the *New York Post* led with his compensation. Glenn Beck, a former Fox News host, called for a United boycott. Louisiana governor Jeff Landry called the proposal “audacious,” noted that Kirby had not built United, and told him to distribute his salary to employees at the bottom of the ladder.

The backlash is not proof that the $32 million was reasonable. It is proof that inequality finally found a mouth. The response did not begin with the claim. It began with the man. Every answer moves from the wealth tax to Kirby’s paycheck. The policy disappears. The messenger remains. The reaction looks like a defense of principle. Read the pattern, and it is a defense of the perimeter. The sequence is the tell: compensation is an outrage, taxation is audacity, and the passenger gets the referendum. Compensation outrage. Tax tantrum.

Now take Landry’s strongest point seriously. The $32 million deserves scrutiny, and one person did not build United alone. The pilots, attendants, mechanics, ground crews, and everyone else who makes the airline run create the value on which that compensation rests. Collective labor can be turned into a very private pool of executive pay. Landry’s demand is not a plan for running an airline. It identifies a real grievance. It is still only a demand for a gesture. The grievance is real. The gesture is not an institution.

Salary and inheritance are not the same money. Dividing one year’s pay may help once. An inheritance tax reaches accumulated wealth that can keep shaping work, politics, and opportunity long after a chief executive retires. Kirby is not asking Landry to split his paycheck among the mechanics and flight attendants. He is asking the country to tax concentrated wealth and inherited capital. Dodge accomplished. Pay debate addressed. Structure avoided.

Fine. He should write a check. He should give up his salary. He should put his money where his mouth is. Better yet, he should distribute it to workers. But the catechism wants the remedy converted into private performance. A personal check leaves the tax rules intact. A personal check makes the billionaire feel useful for an afternoon. A wealth tax says the concentration is too entrenched to be corrected through charity, remorse, or one executive’s unusually expansive sense of shame. That is why personal gestures are preferred. They permit discomfort without changing the arrangement.

The wealthy can oppose a wealth tax as long as they do not have to hear a wealthy person demand one. Unity works through discipline. Kirby broke it by making the argument in his own voice. The response did not need to refute him. It could make his compensation the story until the substance disappeared. That is not an accident. It is the mechanism. A defense of principle becomes a defense of the perimeter when the principle is allowed to vanish as soon as its author has a pulse.

The backlash can turn him into a hypocrite over his $32 million salary. That is the trick. It keeps attention on whether one man gives enough and away from whether too many people have too much. So keep the headlines. Call for a boycott. Demand the check. Turn the argument into a morality test conducted entirely by the wealthy. The question is not whether Kirby should donate his salary. The question is whether accumulated wealth can pass to heirs without limit while the people who built the assets keep bargaining one company at a time.

This is not an abstraction floating above the country. Just two weeks before California voters were set to decide Proposition 40, the so-called billionaire’s tax—a proposed 5 percent tax on the state’s billionaires—wealthy people were already spending millions to defeat it. California has turned the class argument into a ballot. Kirby did not create the resistance. He named it. The resistance is the point.

At the UN, [global corporate-tax talks carry a $500bn revenue potential](/articles/2026-08-02-un-opens-global-corporate-tax-talks-with-500-billion-revenue-potential/). The United Nations has opened those talks as a window into what coordinated rules can do, not as a forecast of what a U.S. wealth tax could raise. The donor-class instinct is to recast coordination as an assault on national sovereignty. The scale is larger. The instinct is identical: money moving across a border becomes a threat to the nation, while money accumulating without limit remains merely creative entrepreneurship.

The catechism needs the word “socialism” to land before the sentence is finished. A wealth tax is socialism. A public option is socialism. A credit union is socialism. The label is designed to stop thought before the mechanism can be examined. California can put the question to its voters. The UN can put it before governments. Scott Kirby put it in a direct interview and attached his own wallet to the answer. He did not merely say somebody else should pay. He said he would pay them.

A wealth tax can be hard to administer. It can encourage relocation. It can interfere with saving. Concede all of it. Those are real design problems. They do not answer whether accumulated wealth should pass to heirs without limit. Whoever wants unlimited inheritance must show the operating budget where a dollar of investment disappears because a child inherits less. Show it. Otherwise retract the blanket claim that taxing inheritance punishes success.

Kirby was also candid that flying is expensive. He defended a $70 charge tied to aisle- or window-seat access for non-frequent flyers and said customers who pay more should get more. Labor Department data show airline fares rising about 20 percent from September 2019 to September 2026, while overall consumer prices rose 31 percent. Fine. Flying is expensive. But a product hierarchy is not an economic constitution. Markets can price a seat. They cannot certify that executive pay is natural, that workers should bargain one company at a time, or that inheritance should be unlimited. I’m not against the market. I’m against using one fair market decision to sanctify every rule around it.

So build two different machines.

First, bargaining power: sectoral negotiations across the airline industry, stronger worker representation, and serious employee ownership. A union negotiates the price of labor. An employee stock ownership plan gives workers a stake. A worker cooperative gives them a vote. Those are not the same thing, but each makes ownership more than a word. U.S. bargaining coverage—the share of workers covered by a union contract—sits in the low double digits; Nordic systems cover the dominant share of workers, with figures commonly cited around 80 percent and up. We cannot copy the faucet without the pipes. Airlines are capital-intensive and heavily regulated. A worker-owned carrier still needs patient, long-term finance, strong safety rules, and clear liability. Hard is not impossible. It is why we build.

Second, common capital: a broad national wealth and inheritance tax, rules that make it harder to avoid taxes by moving across states, and proceeds placed in accounts that belong to children, plus public care, housing, and banking. That is the desired genre: boring rules that reduce the reward for moving every dollar to the friendliest tax address. America can do pieces of this at home. Alaska’s Permanent Fund turns oil revenue into a public dividend distributed to state residents. North Dakota has run a state-owned bank since 1919, long the country’s only institution of its kind, and continues to operate it. Federalism and concentrated political money make the national route harder. They do not mean nobody knows where to start.

My grandfather sat on dairy and electric co-op boards in Minnesota. He would not have called Kirby’s proposal socialism. He would have asked who owned the company, who set the wage, and who inherited the gain. My Swedish relatives would point to universal services. Different arrangements. Same test: can ordinary people keep a claim on what they help build?

The sentence remains: wealth inequality is a danger to society; the United States should have a national wealth tax; inheritance taxes should approach 100 percent at some level; and Scott Kirby says he would pay them. The catechism needs the rich to speak with one voice. Kirby just made him speak alone.

Anyway.

Build bargaining. Build worker ownership. Build broad taxes and child capital. Make the money answer to people before they have to answer to a boycott.
