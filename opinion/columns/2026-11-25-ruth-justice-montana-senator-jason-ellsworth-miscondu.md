Jason Ellsworth tried to steer $170,100 in Montana taxpayers’ money to a longtime friend’s weeks-old company without a public bid, and the fine attached to trying came to five hundred dollars.

The former Montana Senate president entered a no-contest plea to one misdemeanor count of official misconduct before Lewis and Clark County District Judge Christopher Abbott. Abbott gave him six months of probation, a $500 fine and permanent forfeiture of his Senate seat. A no-contest plea admits nothing and disputes nothing. It accepts the conviction. Defense attorney Joan Mell says the sentence will not take effect until the case is fully adjudicated and has promised a prompt appeal to the Montana Supreme Court. Montana Department of Justice prosecutor Dan Guzynski told the court the appeal came as a surprise because it had not been discussed in the plea negotiations. “The idea of this plea agreement was to have finality,” he said. Mell said the appeal would raise “every one of the issues from the inception.” The Justice Department did not immediately say why it took the plea rather than the retrial. The prosecutor wanted finality. The defense wants another court.

A [jury convicted Ellsworth in July](/articles/2026-07-22-montana-jury-convicts-former-senate-president-of-official-misconduct-for-no-bid/), but [Abbott threw out that verdict](/articles/2026-07-23-judge-throws-out-montana-senator-s-misconduct-conviction/) after finding the jury had been inadvertently tainted. The plea replaced the retrial set for next month.

The contract tells the rest. In late 2024, Ellsworth attempted to contract with longtime friend Bryce Eggleston’s company, formed only weeks earlier, for $170,100 in state funds over which he had discretion. Eggleston had no relevant expertise. The job was tracking the progress and implementation of 27 bills from the special judicial committee on judicial reform that Ellsworth had formed in 2024. The money was left over from that committee. Emails between Ellsworth and state staff showed that he sent two nearly identical contracts totaling $170,100. Each was worth less than $100,000, the threshold at which the work had to be posted publicly for bids. Legislative staff rejected the split.

Official misconduct is the charge’s proper name. Here, it is the law’s polite name for trying to walk public money past the public’s bids. A man who cuts a check in half has already told you where he knows the line is. The scheme died at the staff door. The charge did not.

Public officials with discretion over contracts can turn friendship into a qualification. The bid threshold supplies the hiding place; the discretionary purse supplies the freedom. Give one legislator a purse and a threshold to cross, and the scissors come with the office. This is not one man’s peculiar appetite. It is the machinery that makes one friend possible at public expense. A bid means a stranger gets to see the price and the work. The splitter meant to keep a stranger out.

Here, the threshold was an invitation to the man who knew it. Staff rejected the split before anything could be signed, a jury convicted him, and in 2025 the Montana Senate banned him for life from its floor but stopped short of expelling him. The law bound him at every door. The open question is the price list.

Here is the price list. In 2012, HSBC admitted laundering money for Mexican drug cartels and sanctioned regimes and paid $1.9 billion in total under a deferred prosecution agreement. No individual was prosecuted. Purdue Pharma pleaded guilty twice over OxyContin while the Sacklers, who extracted the billions, were never criminally charged; the 2025 settlement set aside roughly $850 million for individual victims, payable over 15 years. Wells Fargo opened millions of unauthorized accounts its customers never requested and settled for $3 billion; the Justice Department said it made the agreement “with the bank itself, not with any individuals responsible for the fraud.” A shoplifter gets a record. A bank that launders cartel money gets an invoice.

On the other side of the ledger, the Economic Policy Institute found $933 million recovered for cheated workers in 2012, almost three times what every robber in America stole that year. Almost none of those bosses saw a courtroom. After AIG accepted roughly $182 billion in committed federal support, its Financial Products unit paid $165 million in retention bonuses in 2009; 73 employees received at least $1 million each. The 2008 record shows no major Wall Street CEO went to prison for the conduct that caused the financial crisis. The law has two drawers. Corporations cannot be jailed, so the corporate form writes the check. Individuals can be jailed, so the individual gets a chance to negotiate a plea. The powerful arrange the smaller sentence.

I have watched this movie since Nixon. There was a time the law bound this class—the savings-and-loan era, which I watched put executives in prison. Charles Keating gave $1.3 million to five United States senators. Asked whether the money bought influence, he said: “I want to say in the most forceful way I can: I hope so.” He served about four and a half years before his convictions were overturned on a technicality in 1996, then pleaded guilty again in 1999. Frank Wilhoit put the standing rule in a sentence: “there must be in-groups whom the law protects but does not bind, alongside out-groups whom the law binds but does not protect.” Ellsworth got the bound half of that sentence—at the power discount.

Ellsworth called continuing to “burn through Montana taxpayers’ money over a misdemeanor” “unconscionable” and said stepping off the path was “the righteous thing to do.” He had tried to move $170,100 of that money to his friend’s company without a bid. The righteous path was the no bid. The unconscionable part was the state charging him for it. “It has been an honor to represent Senate District 43,” he wrote. The honor is retained. The floor is not.

As for the “permanent” forfeiture: his term ends in January regardless, and the door it closes is a future run he has already tested—this year he lost the Republican primary by nearly 77 points, or 2,468 votes. The court fined the check-splitter $500. His own party’s primary voters fined him 2,468 votes. He is appealing the conviction carrying the $500. Again.

No bid. One friend. Five hundred dollars. Sentence entered.
