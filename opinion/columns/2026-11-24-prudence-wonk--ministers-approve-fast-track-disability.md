Stephen Timms is engineering disability-benefit extraction through a two-tier compassion review.

Here are the numbers. On November 24, the Department for Work and Pensions committed to a fast-track route for adults in England and Wales with severe, well-evidenced conditions and for people nearing the end of life, and to fewer reviews for lifelong, progressive, or unlikely-to-improve conditions. Those are the announced reforms. Personal Independence Payment (PIP), the cash benefit at issue, has rates as high as £194.60 a week.

The Office for Budget Responsibility (OBR), the independent forecaster, expects PIP spending to rise from about £16 billion in 2019-20 to just over £41 billion by the end of the decade if policy does not change. That is the pressure. A forecast is not a blueprint. The government turned it into a severity threshold. The fastest route to lower spending is a lower payment for disabled people who miss the new threshold.

The Timms review’s final report, written with disability-rights advocates Sharon Brennan and Clenton Farquharson, recommends the fast track, fewer reviews, and an “impact assessment”—a test of how a condition affects daily life—to set PIP for everyone else. It recommends more face-to-face assessments but identifies no specific area where the overall bill can be cut. That is the favorable half of the package. The route to lower cash payments for those outside the fast track sits inside the same review.

The government response is due next month, with a white paper—the government’s statement of planned policy—in the spring. MSI’s earlier account of the [PIP review to recommend overhaul, retaining benefit for all ages](/articles/2026-11-22-pip-review-to-recommend-overhaul-retaining-benefit-for-all-ages/) put the processing question and the cash question in the same frame: the overhaul retains the benefit for all ages; the cut is being built underneath it. Processing becomes faster for the most severe claimants. Payment becomes less certain for everyone else.

The cut’s history is short and checkable. Before the review began, the government dropped a plan to cut PIP by £5 billion after objections from backbench MPs. Mr. Timms defended those cuts as a minister, warning that without spending reductions the welfare bill would become unsustainable. The methodology did not change. The packaging did.

“Unsustainable” is not a finding. It is a laundering device that turns a political preference for lower spending into a test of disabled people’s daily lives. Mr. Timms now insists cash payments remain “at the heart of the system”; the assessment he is building exists to decide which disabled people get less of it. His own interim finding was that “while Pip is valued, the system is no longer fit for purpose.” Easing the burden on the sickest claimants is sound on its merits. It is also the cover.

The fast track draws a line. Severe, well-evidenced conditions and cases nearing the end of life get the quicker route; others undergo the new daily-life assessment and more face-to-face contact. The assessment is not a clerical adjustment. It is the mechanism for deciding which disabled people receive less. Shortening reviews is repair. Routing that repair into the machinery that sets the cash payment is extraction. The government has tied the two together.

Mr. Milburn’s separate review of youth joblessness, the one that [called the numbers an economic and moral crisis](/articles/2026-11-17-milburn-review-to-call-neet-numbers-an-economic-and-moral-crisis/), recommended ending PIP for under-25s and spending the savings on schemes to move them into work. If both reviews are adopted, the destination is a welfare system split by severity and birthday: people 25 and older may claim PIP while younger disabled people cannot. That is the plan as reported, not enacted law. The reviews call the difference savings. The arithmetic calls it income removed from disabled people.

[The Guardian reported](https://www.theguardian.com/uk-news/2026/nov/24/ministers-approve-fast-track-disability-payments-in-england-and-wales) that ministers are working on a PIP system combining extra work and training support with cash cuts for every age outside the fast track. The fast track for the most severe and the payment mechanism for everyone else are not separate reforms. They are the front and back of the same transfer.

The documents are British; the move is not. The record has the shape of a scoring exercise run backward: the government tried the £5 billion cut, the review returned a kind report with a blank savings chapter, and the later white paper is expected to supply the arithmetic. In the scoring work I did at the Congressional Budget Office, the dishonest version looked familiar: decide the cut before commissioning the review, then return a kind report with a blank chapter and let the later document fill it. The convention was chosen after the answer. That is the entire operation.

The consultation with disabled people and the report’s co-authorship were real. Campaigners and disability charities broadly welcomed the review. They secured the improvements at the top of the system. The cut is written at the bottom, and consultation does not travel there. The same temperature applies to a cut regardless of which government signs it. The consultation makes the laundering more worth naming, not less.

What the arithmetic lands on is a weekly payment that exists because the extra costs of disability are real. Reducing the payment does not reduce the cost. It moves the cost onto households with the least capacity to carry it. Alexa Knight of the Mental Health Foundation put the warning on record: “No one receiving Pip should be pushed into deeper poverty as a result of these changes, even in the short term. The consequences for individuals and their families can be devastating if support is removed before suitable alternatives are in place.”

The fast-track route should stand, and so should fewer reviews for conditions that cannot improve. Any government that needs money should name the program, disclose each cash reduction before a claimant reaches it, and argue the merits in the open. It may not commission kindness to carry the arithmetic.

The final report names not one pound to cut. Mr. Timms defended the £5 billion proposal, led the review that declined to name one, and left the arithmetic to the white paper. A fast track for severe claimants and people nearing the end of life, a reduction machinery being built for everyone else, and Stephen Timms at the junction. The extraction has a timetable.

There is no other way to read the record.
