Chile’s Congress approved a bill to catch people it cannot deport.

More than 75,000 pending removal orders are not abstractions. Behind each stands a human being with a life beyond the state’s file. About half involve Venezuelans. An estimated 330,000 people live in Chile without legal immigration status. By October 10, authorities had carried out 1,874 deportations in 2026. A state that cannot finish its own orders wants more power to find the people bound by them.

My own tradition is in this accounting. The U.S. and Mexican bishops’ 2003 pastoral letter, *Strangers No Longer*, recognizes a sovereign state’s right to control its borders. It also insists that refugees and asylum seekers receive protection and that undocumented migrants retain their human rights and dignity. We cannot claim the whole inheritance and leave its harder half on the floor.

The measure won final approval in both chambers on Wednesday. Once Congress delivers its final text, President José Antonio Kast will have 10 days to sign it. The bill was introduced in 2024 under former President Gabriel Boric, but under Kast it has become a centerpiece of the administration’s enforcement project.

The Investigative Police may seek written judicial authorization to enter a property and detain people subject to removal orders. Judges must rule on those requests within 24 hours. Authorities may expand biometric records, access information held by public and private institutions to facilitate deportation notices, and raise the minimum reentry ban from three years to five. Information concerning children under 18, educational institutions, and healthcare institutions is excluded.

Those exclusions matter. The rest does what its authors intend. The state will reach more deeply into private records, homes, and identities. It will locate, notify, and detain more people. Written judicial authorization gives the police a route into property. The 24-hour clock structures how quickly a judge must answer. Expanded data access gives officials more places to look.

Those powers could help locate and detain people. They cannot manufacture the capacity to remove them. Congress has approved a faster front end for a back end that remains overwhelmed.

The bill proposes to solve that shortage with speed: more judicial requests, more judges, more cases, more biometric records, more information searched. More pressure to rule quickly. The 24-hour deadline can make judicial oversight appear swift, but a clock is not a correction for an enforcement system built to move faster than it can responsibly manage.

Jaime Abedrapo, an international law professor who says the new powers could increase the number of deportations, put the limit plainly: the law is “far from reaching 300,000 in four years.” Authorities are issuing removal orders far beyond the state’s ability to enforce them. Additional resources are not an optional refinement; they are the whole operation.

That is cruelty dressed as competence. The bill intensifies coercion against people the state has already failed to remove, while offering no plan for completing their removal.

At the border, the administration has a measurable result. Police data published by CIPER showed irregular entries fell 93.5 percent between January and August. Kast has also begun work on the [“Border Shield” barrier to curb illegal immigration](/articles/2026-03-15-kast-begins-work-on-border-shield-barrier-to-curb-illegal-immigration/).

Stopping entries at the border is one task. Returning more than 75,000 people already subject to removal orders is another. The first depends on controlling physical crossings. The second depends on courts, trained personnel, financial resources, and foreign governments willing to accept those Chile sends back. Treating them as the same problem will produce the same familiar result: paperwork without completion.

International cooperation is the other decisive constraint. Political science professor Hernán Campos said the legislation’s success depends heavily on countries willing to receive deported migrants. The recent restoration of consular relations with Venezuela, part of [Chile and Venezuela’s move toward renewed diplomatic ties](/articles/2026-09-24-chile-venezuela-move-toward-restoring-diplomatic-ties/), could facilitate some procedures. Restored relations can remove a diplomatic obstacle. They cannot create an unlimited deportation machine. A receiving country must still accept the people Chile wants to send away.

Campos also said the legislation complements border controls and could become an electoral legacy if irregular migration declines by the end of the administration. A legacy cannot rest on an enforcement bill whose human consequences remain unresolved.

Abedrapo said the bill received support from nearly all political sectors except the Communist Party. That breadth matters. This is not an extremist measure smuggled through by a narrow faction. It is a broad expression of a governing philosophy: irregular migration treated as an enforcement target rather than as a legal and humanitarian problem. I would write the same judgment about any government that turned removals into a show of strength. The breadth of support changes the politics; it does not change the human fact.

The deeper contradiction is that greater visibility makes cooperation less likely.

Francisca Vargas, director of the Migrants and Refugees Legal Clinic at Diego Portales University, warns that the reform moves toward criminalizing migrants and eroding due-process protections. Her warning about the bill’s data barriers is more consequential: “The more barriers are imposed, the more people are pushed into hiding.” That is not an unfortunate side effect. It is the predictable result of a policy built around expanding state visibility into private life.

More barriers mean fewer people willing to provide personal information. Fewer migrants willing to cooperate with authorities. A government that makes undocumented residents afraid to answer the phone has not made itself more effective. It has simply made more of its population unavailable to the institutions responsible for deportation.

A government cannot manufacture cooperation by making cooperation dangerous.

The bill’s treatment of children makes Vargas’s warning concrete. Excluding information concerning children under 18 does not say what happens to children when their parents are deported. The statute accelerates the state’s reach toward the parent and leaves the child without an answer. As Vargas notes, irregular immigration status often marks a condition of vulnerability involving people who crossed the border without authorization and without committing a crime. A legal status is not a moral status. The state may determine whether a person may enter and remain; it cannot turn that determination into a claim upon the person’s worth.

Jesus told his disciples, “I was a stranger and you did not welcome me.” The Torah commands, “You shall love the alien as yourself,” because Israel once knew what it meant to be the alien. Those words are not decoration. They set a moral limit on public power. Irregular status is a legal condition, not a lesser kind of human being. A government may set immigration rules. It cannot consecrate the transformation of human beings into quarry.

Francis writes in *Fratelli Tutti* (§39) that migrants “possess the same intrinsic dignity as any person.” A humane immigration law can secure a border, hear asylum claims, protect refugees, respect the rights and dignity of undocumented people, and give removal orders the resources and due process they require. That is not a contradiction. It is the moral arithmetic of *Strangers No Longer*, which recognizes both the sovereign right to control borders and the obligation to protect human dignity.

On March 23, 1980, Óscar Romero told soldiers facing an order to kill that no soldier is obliged to obey an order against the law of God. I do not tell Kast to disobey the law. I tell him not to sign an immoral bill. The door remains open while the bill is still unsigned.

Kast can sign it. He cannot sign away the arithmetic. Ten days is not long. It is the distance between a political victory and governing responsibility. More power to locate people is not the same thing as more power to return them. Congress has passed the easy part. The border is already tighter. The next frontier is the rule of law—and Kast’s administration is charging straight at it.
