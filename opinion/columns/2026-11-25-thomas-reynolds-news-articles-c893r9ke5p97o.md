Donald Trump is preparing to impose import taxes priced by his personal trade deals. 

[BBC News reported](https://www.bbc.com/search?q=%22depending%20on%20the%20trade%20deal%20it%20had%20previously%20struck%20with%20Trump%22) that the program covers 16 jurisdictions—China, the European Union, Mexico, and Japan among them—and that each country’s rate would be set, in the report’s words, “depending on the trade deal it had previously struck with Trump.” A tariff is an import tax paid by American importers at the border. Critics told BBC the duties raise consumer prices and damage the global economy.

The administration’s lawyers have a case, and it deserves its strongest form. Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974 gives the United States Trade Representative authority to investigate foreign trade practices and answer proven violations with tariffs or other measures. The statute requires USTR to investigate foreign “acts, policies, and practices” that violate international obligations or are “unjustifiable and burdensome” and impede or distort American commerce. Before retaliation, USTR must consult the foreign government, provide public notice, hear public testimony, accept written submissions, publish its findings, and allow further public comment. The President may then impose duties, restrict imports, or withdraw trade concessions; if a foreign government refuses to end the offending practice, the statute also permits retaliation—additional penalties against that government. See [19 U.S.C. §§ 2111–2117](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?path=/prelim@title19/chapter21&edition=prelim).

On this account, Jamieson Greer’s contention—that foreign overproduction is displacing American manufacturing and deterring investment—can supply the type of foreign practice Section 301 was designed to investigate. An attorney defending the administration can argue that the Court invalidated one program, not the President’s entire tariff power. The old duties were global. The new duties would be country-specific. Different rates can reflect different facts, different trade obligations, and different corrective measures. This theory is recognizable on the working bar.

Recognizable is not the same as lawful. The steel man must meet the Court’s actual holding. In the consolidated cases [*Trump v. V.O.S. Selections, Inc.*, No. 25-334, and *Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump*](https://www.supremecourt.gov/search.aspx?filename=/docket/docketfiles/html/public/25-334.html), the Supreme Court held that the International Emergency Economic Powers Act did not authorize the sweeping April 2025 tariffs the administration had imposed. It did not hold that the President may impose no tariffs. It did not invalidate duties authorized by Section 301 or any other statute. The holding was narrower than the political claim that the Court had ended the executive’s tariff power, and narrower than the opposite claim that the Court approved whatever tariff program replaced it.

The replacement starts at the beginning. It has an investigation; it does not yet have a legal foundation for sixteen country-specific rates. [USTR’s official Section 301 docket](https://ustr.gov/issue-areas/enforcement/section-301-investigations) contains the initiation materials. The record cited here contains no final USTR findings identifying a specific unlawful act, policy, or practice for each jurisdiction and explaining why that jurisdiction’s proposed rate fits the violation. Until those findings exist, there is no rate for a court to adjudicate. Section 301 cannot inherit legal validity from the IEEPA program the Court rejected.

If the reported rate key is right, the administration’s defense has a serious problem. Each rate would depend on the trade deal its government struck with Trump. Foreign overproduction does not pause because a government signs a different bargain with Washington. A factory does not move because the President grants a concession. If the Section 301 findings establish the wrong, those findings must explain the rate. If the prior bargain establishes the rate, the investigation is measuring presidential leverage instead. Those are different legal stories. The administration cannot run them at once.

If the bargain supplies the rate, the deal is the law. That is not trade policy operating through a statute. It is one man’s price list wearing a statute as a coat.

The timing makes the leverage visible. The planned announcement comes before the interim agreement between Washington and Beijing expires on January 10. Beijing has extended its suspension of planned gallium, germanium, and antimony export bans until January 27. Washington’s stated grievances—agricultural purchases, rare earths, and critical minerals—match the live bargaining issues. Section 301 supplies the trade-remedy vocabulary. The January deadlines supply the pressure.

An honest application of Section 301 would require USTR to publish, for each jurisdiction, the specific foreign act, policy, or practice found unlawful, the evidence supporting that finding, and the reason the proposed duty fits the violation. Trade agreements may supply context. They cannot replace the violation. If the deal supplies the rate, the statute supplies vocabulary rather than authority.

The enforcement problem remains. [The Brennan Center’s 2025 shadow-docket accounting](https://www.brennancenter.org/search?search_api_fulltext=shadow%20docket%202025) found that the second Trump administration filed 34 emergency applications through the end of 2025 and prevailed in 80 percent of them, while the Bush and Obama administrations filed eight combined across sixteen years. “The more you look at the body of work,” [Stephen Vladeck has written](https://us.macmillan.com/books/9780374721399/theshadowdocket/), “the more it looks like the best explanation for when the court is intervening and when it’s not, is partisan politics and not neutral substantive legal principles.” Those figures do not decide the Section 301 question. They identify the enforcement asymmetry the new program must survive. The Court’s IEEPA ruling binds the program it decided. For the next route, it is a toll, not a wall, until the docket makes someone enforce it.

The Supreme Court did not tell Donald Trump no. It told his April 2025 IEEPA program no. The tariff project moved to new paperwork. Sixteen jurisdictions, sixteen rates, each keyed to the deal its government struck with him. The Court struck one tariff program. The deals pricing the next one are still his—and nothing yet in the record says the law travels with them.
