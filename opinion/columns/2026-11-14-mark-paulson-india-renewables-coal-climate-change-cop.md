India is throwing away solar power to keep coal plants running.

The line that feeds this shop comes down off Adams-Columbia Electric Cooperative, headquartered here in Friendship — the largest rural electric co-op in Wisconsin. One ice storm a few winters back it went down past the section road, and I sat in a cold shop running the little Honda EU2200i to keep the lights on and the compressor alive while the co-op crew worked the poles in the dark until the hum came back up the line. The generator was a candle. The wire was the system. Nothing is electricity until a wire carries it — not the panel, not the turbine, not the fuel pile. In 1936 fewer than eleven percent of American farms had electricity, because the investor-owned utilities had run the arithmetic and decided rural lines were not worth the copper. Congress answered with long, cheap loans routed to member-owned cooperatives instead, and by 1950 about eighty percent of American farms had power. Somebody built the wire first. That is the whole of what is happening in India right now.

India announced its goal in late September: nine hundred gigawatts of nonfossil power by 2035 — solar, wind, hydro and nuclear — nearly twice the five-hundred-gigawatt target it already carried for 2030. Clean capacity stands at just over three hundred gigawatts, third in the world behind China and the United States, and Berkeley's researchers say India has already overtaken the United States in annual renewable growth. The announcement lands ahead of COP31 in Turkey, where speeding the move off fossil fuels is the headline business. The target counts installed capacity rather than electricity actually produced, and that distinction is where the trouble starts.

Because the panels are not the problem. Clean power was fifty-five percent of India's installed capacity by September, and it generated about a third of the country's electricity in the three months before that. Over the past sixteen months India curtailed enough solar generation to power about ten million homes because the grid could not take it — [nearly eleven terawatt-hours over fifteen months](/articles/2026-09-02-india-curtailed-nearly-11-twh-of-solar-power-over-15-months/), generated and discarded. Not because the panels broke. Not because the sun quit. Neshwin Rodrigues, an energy analyst at Ember, laid the choice out the way any operator would: "You either have more transmission to get the power out... Or if you cannot get the power out, you store it and then transfer it during some other hours." India is doing some of both, slowly, and the sun keeps coming up to no purpose. This is the same bind [outdated power grids create for renewable expansion](/articles/2026-11-09-outdated-power-grids-threaten-renewable-energy-expansion/) wherever the wires lag the plants.

The government has noticed. It approved nearly twenty billion dollars for transmission and battery storage under the Green Energy Corridor plan — thousands of kilometers of new lines, meant to help states take on up to a hundred and thirty-five gigawatts of renewable energy by 2033, plus more storage. Berkeley's researchers estimate India needs as much as two hundred and fifty gigawatts of storage over the next decade to integrate the power it is already building. Nikit Abhyankar of the India Energy and Climate Center said it straight: "If the grid doesn't catch up, then there is no way India can meet this target." Twenty billion dollars against a two-hundred-and-fifty-gigawatt gap is bailing with one hand while the other signs for more coal.

Coal is the other half of the waste. It generated about seventy-one percent of India's electricity in 2025, by Ember's count. Ghanshyam Prasad, chair of the Central Electricity Authority, said earlier this month that India could need as much as a hundred and fifty gigawatts of additional coal-fired capacity by 2035. The same Berkeley researchers estimate that beyond roughly thirty-five gigawatts already under construction, additional coal plants would not be economically necessary under the new goals; Abhyankar's warning is that anything past that becomes a stranded asset, paid for over decades by people who will not get a cheaper kilowatt out of it. And the coal already standing makes the solar waste worse: many plants cannot cut output quickly when renewable electricity arrives, so the grid sheds the solar instead. Solar is what gets cut. Coal is what keeps running. Wendell Berry's line on nuclear power — that it will be "well used by the same mentality that has egregiously devalued and misapplied man- and womanpower" — is the same line on coal. The technology is never the question; the mentality running it is. A grid built around plants that have to run is a grid that throws away the power needing no fuel.

When someone tells you renewables cannot run a modern grid, India is the counter-receipt. India generates the power. The grid cannot carry it. The failure is not the sun and not the turbine; it is the wire and the battery, and both of those are things countries build on purpose. We ran this experiment on ourselves in February 2021, when Texas went dark in a freeze and the story on cable was wind turbines. The federal autopsy by FERC and NERC, finished that December, found about three-quarters of the unplanned outages traced to frozen equipment and fuel-supply failures — the gas system, mostly. The gas plants could not get fuel, and that part did not lead the broadcast. Delivery is boring, expensive and public, which is exactly why it keeps getting skipped.

Ajay Shah, an economist at Mumbai's XKDR Forum, describes what is emerging as an Indian "electrostate," where electricity takes the place of coal and petroleum in the economy, and his complaint is about the middle: government acting as "a man in the middle" between a private generator and a private buyer, which he calls a choke point. He says India needs prices that rise and fall with supply and demand and easier foreign money for power projects, because domestic financing alone will not carry the build-out. Under all of it sits a supply-chain catch: India makes its batteries on imported cells, mainly from China, so part of the money the transition moves leaves the country again.

The gap between the pledge and the wire is not India's alone. Brazil projects renewables at nearly ninety percent of its electricity within the next decade even as it expands oil and gas production. In the United States, where President Donald Trump supports fossil fuels, electricity demand is expected to grow forty percent with natural gas a major source. Different flags, the same habit: announce the clean future, keep the fossil, and let the wire catch up if it ever does. This year's climate talks in Turkey, where [world leaders rallied behind a goal to electrify 35 percent of energy use by 2035](/articles/2026-11-12-turkey-rallies-world-leaders-behind-goal-to-electrify-35-of-energy-use-by-2035/), were built on exactly this kind of promise, and a promise is only as good as the wire under it.

What it looks like when somebody means it is not complicated. Build the wire and the storage ahead of the target instead of behind it, let prices carry the signal to where the power is needed, and commit to no coal past the thirty-five gigawatts already under construction. A hundred and fifty would be somebody's loss for forty years. Berry has the test for this, in "Solving for Pattern": a real solution solves more than one problem and creates no new ones. A grid that carries clean power does exactly that — cheaper electricity, less coal smoke, less imported fuel, more of the value staying in the country that built the panels. A new coal plant solves nothing and carries a thirty-year debt.

I picture a village where the solar farm sits at the fence line and the coal plant sits up the valley, and at noon the panels are making electricity nobody can carry while the smoke drifts the other way. Then I think about that ice storm, and the co-op crew on the poles, and the hum coming back up the line. The difference between a country that has clean power and a country that can use it is a wire. Wires do not fall out of the sky. Somebody decides to build them, somebody pays, and the kids down the road inherit either the wire or the smoke. That decision is on the table in India, and it is on the table here. I know which inheritance I am working for.
