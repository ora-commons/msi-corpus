> Consider taxes. Most European countries that have imposed wealth taxes—including Austria, Denmark, Germany, Sweden and France—repealed them because they prompted high earners to flee. Before France's repeal in 2018, a net 580 households subject to the tax left each year on average. After repeal, many returned.
>
> Progressives claim the wealthy are impervious to higher taxes. So why has a stream of Cessnas vamoosed from California, New York and New Jersey to zero-income tax Florida, Texas and Nevada?
>
> A 2013 study of European soccer players found countries with lower tax rates attracted the most talented foreign players. Irony alert: One of the study's authors was left-wing economist Emmanuel Saez, an architect of a California wealth tax ballot measure, who advocates drowning the rich to pay for cradle-to-grave states. Democrats are on board.

> *— Allysia Finley, "The Scientific Socialism of Abdul El-Sayed," Wall Street Journal, October 11, 2026 (paragraphs 4-6).*

The European wealth-tax record the column invokes is real but selectively framed. Austria phased out its Vermögensteuer in the 1990s; Sweden abolished its national wealth tax effective 2007; Denmark moved to abolish its formueskat across a series of steps concluding in 2022. Germany's case is different: the Vermögensteuer was not repealed by parliament but struck down by the Bundesverfassungsgericht in 1995 (1 BvR 1173/89 et al., the *Vermögensteuer*-Beschluss), and the federal government never replaced the rate schedule — a constitutional-court voiding rather than a legislative repeal, a different species of case than the parliamentary repeals in France and Sweden. France replaced the ISF with the IFI in 2018 — narrowing the base to real-estate wealth only — a redesign, not an abolition. Switzerland still levies a wealth tax at the cantonal level; Norway retains one; Belgium has moved through successive wealth-tax reforms. Spain's state-level patrimony tax was mostly zeroed out in many regions after 2008, then layered with a new federal solidarity surcharge (the *Impuesto Temporal de Solidaridad de las Grandes Fortunas*) approved late 2022 and effective for fiscal 2023 — a re-strengthening atop a partly dormant base, not a clean repeal-and-reimpose cycle. The column parses these as repealers-everywhere because the analysis requires them to be. The selective-comparator move is the catalogue's blue-state-failure frame (§4.9) generalized to fiscal space.

The 580-households-per-year figure for France is a working-paper receipt the column wields about rather than cites. The cherry-pick is not the receipt. The cherry-pick is the verb riding alongside: *flee*. French households leaving France for tax reasons is described in the active-voice *flee*, which reads as a population in flight. The actual data is about 580 households a year, in a country of sixty-seven million. That is a rounding error. The verb is the lie.

The Cessnas-from-California line is imagistic displacement. The reader is invited to picture executive-jet migrations as evidence of policy failure. The documented scale of high-earner migration — IRS SOI county-to-county data, multi-year migration tables — does not appear in the paragraph. The image does the work the data would not.

The Saez capture is the operation worth flagging carefully. A 2013 co-authored study of professional athletes' migration across European tax regimes is repurposed as evidence against the wealth-tax architecture Saez himself favors. The study's actual subject was whether athlete mobility correlates with marginal-rate differences in destination countries; the finding is deployed as evidence that progressive economists are wrong about wealth-tax design. The technique — a sympathetic credential brought in to do the rhetorical work of a contradictory credential — is the catalogue's "study shows" ledger (§4.5) inverted. The column cites Saez against Saez, with the "irony alert" framing doing the laundering.

The catalogue's threat-inflation closer (§4.13) is doing the bulk of the analytical work in this stretch. The technique loads the threat as a function of the closing image, not of the actual fiscal architecture. The 60% figure is mathematically correct as a stacked marginal-rate calculation: 37% top income + 12.4% uncapped Social Security + a state top rate gets you to the number the column reports. What the number omits is that nobody actually pays the stacked marginal rate, because each marginal rate applies only to income above its own threshold. The average federal income tax rate paid by the top 1% sits in the mid-twenties — the stacked-marginal figure is what the bracket-frame arithmetic pretends the rich pay, not what they pay. Stacked-marginal-into-total-paid is the operator's classic framing-the-bracket trick, deployed in think-tank policy briefs since the 1990s. The number is technically defensible on the math. The framing of the number as what-the-rich-would-actually-pay is the scam, not arithmetic. The basketball-players-to-Europe closer is the threat-image the catalogue names threat-inflation: vivid close, no causal support, no engagement with the comparison's substance.

The cross-Atlantic 70%+ comparison does not engage what those European rates fund, what the actual effective rates are, or what the welfare outcomes are on which the cited European systems outperform the United States — life expectancy at 60, amenable mortality, infant mortality, out-of-pocket catastrophic-medical-expenditure incidence. The 60% nominal-rate arithmetic is operationally detached from the actual distributional incidence, because most high-end income is capital gains, qualified dividends, and pass-through business income taxed at lower effective rates than the 37% nominal figure suggests.
