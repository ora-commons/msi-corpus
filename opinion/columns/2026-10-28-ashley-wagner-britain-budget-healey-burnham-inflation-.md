John Healey’s government is forcing working families to sacrifice for broken promises.

That is not generosity. It is a description of the damage.

“Some breathing space” is John Healey’s phrase for what his first budget is supposed to deliver on Wednesday: relief for households and businesses squeezed by an Iran war that has disrupted oil and gas through constraints in the Strait of Hormuz. It means stopping the squeeze long enough for people to adjust, not giving them room to flourish. The country’s economy has grown strongly this year, more resilient than expected, but even a green GDP line cannot make an imported energy shock disappear. The arithmetic explains the squeeze. It does not absolve the government that assigned it. Healey has no room for fantasy. The figures leave him with something closer to a straitjacket, but the government chooses who gets tied into it.

The [preparations for Healey’s first budget]( /articles/2026-10-27-john-healey-prepares-his-first-budget-amid-war-driven-economic-pressure/) have already been overshadowed by war-driven economic pressure. Inflation has reached a near one-year high of 3.5% as fuel prices climb, and it is expected to rise further. The government wants more public spending, better defense and relief from the cost of living while Healey, who became chancellor in July when Andy Burnham became prime minister, tries to reconcile the government’s ambitions with the war and its debt bill. Government can choose priorities; it cannot choose priorities and then blame the household that pays for them.

At eleven at night, my kitchen table belongs to a spreadsheet. The first line is what comes in after taxes. The second is what goes out for childcare for both kids. The remainder has to cover the mortgage, fuel, groceries, health care and the care we will have to arrange for my parents as they age. My kitchen is in Fishtown; Healey’s statement is in Westminster. The currency differs; the order of operations does not. For a millennial family with a mortgage, a childcare bill and aging parents, the month is the unit of public policy—not a story about personal spending.

At 3.5% annual inflation, a recurring £100 basket cost last year is £103.50 today. Applied across fuel, childcare and groceries, the increase sounds small until it repeats across every monthly bill and across the year. On the government’s side of the ledger, British public debt has passed £3 trillion—$4 trillion—for the first time, and debt service already takes 8% of government spending: eight pence of every pound. The Bank of England is expected to raise interest rates soon, making the government’s borrowing costs still more expensive. Next month, the household sheet still begins with a paycheck and still sends money to childcare before fuel, groceries, the mortgage and care enter the remaining column. The government does not get to call a household short after it has assigned the money elsewhere.

I should be exact about where my family sits. We have two incomes, help from my husband’s grandmother’s estate with the Fishtown down payment, and college-educated parents who helped us navigate financial aid. Those are buffers, not proof of affordability. If this budget works only for households with help like ours, it has failed the people with the least margin.

Every budget must disappoint somebody. The statement has already been expected to deliver [smaller tax and spending changes](/articles/2026-10-27-john-healey-prepares-uk-budget-expected-to-have-smaller-tax-and-spending-changes/). A smaller package is responsible government only when the government says plainly what it is dropping. Burnham has promised to reduce and even eradicate homelessness and improve care for the elderly; the government wants to restore public services. Those commitments are worthy. They are not paid for by adjectives. There is not enough money for all of those ambitions; government can choose its priorities, but it cannot choose arithmetic. It can choose who pays. Every new spending commitment needs durable revenue or an explicit reduction elsewhere. A promise without a payer named in the same document is an IOU mailed to working families. Borrowing to sustain the headline promise is not fiscal honesty.

Healey has two plausible revenue sources in his sights: a higher surcharge on bank profits and higher taxes on the gambling sector. The distinction matters. Banks have been documented winners of higher interest rates; the surcharge would reach money the rate shock helped produce. The gambling sector is a revenue category, not proof that it profited from this war-driven energy shock in the same way. Tax gambling if the numbers justify it, but call it a tax on a sector rather than laundering it as a windfall levy. If the government puts both in one basket and calls the whole thing “taxing the winners,” it is lying about where the money comes from.

A surcharge on temporarily elevated bank profits can raise cash while rates are high, but that yield recedes if rates normalize; it cannot by itself sustain a permanent spending commitment or compensate households in the round. Taxing those bank profits is sensible. Treating that as a substitute for a cost-of-living strategy would be an abdication, and fiscal prudence would become respectable cover for social austerity. Healey must put the scale in pounds and the destination on the page: direct energy-cost relief, public-service protection or debt reduction. If new revenue merely pays more interest while households receive no offset, the government has not shared the breathing space it promised.

Defense exposes the contradiction most sharply. Healey quit as defense secretary earlier this year in protest over inadequate funding. Now, as chancellor, he must find money for the defense commitments he once lacked, while Burnham has pledged to meet NATO spending targets. The choices are tax rises, spending cuts or some combination of the two; growth and financial goodwill cannot fund them. Every increase in defense spending must arrive with its offset named in the same document—a specific tax rise or a specific civilian service cut. An unfunded defense promise is austerity in advance.

Matthew Amis, an investment director at Aberdeen Investments, has the tactical case right: lower government borrowing costs can later free resources for schools, hospitals and the military. A dull budget is a tactic; the weak fiscal story is the constraint. The dull budget earns its name by taxing documented winners, protecting public services and naming every deferred promise. Used instead to conceal an austerity transfer from fuel pumps and childcare invoices to protected spending, it is laundering. Markets punish weak fiscal stories quickly. Just ask France; France’s recent fiscal protests are a warning about confidence, not an invitation to reproduce disorder.

Taylor Swift’s “Anti-Hero” is not a policy song. Its chorus turns depression into the private conviction that the problem is the self: “it’s me, it’s me, it’s me.” Read beside this budget, that is the psychological handoff the arithmetic makes: first the family absorbs the shock, then the mother concludes she should have made a better spreadsheet. Swift did not write about budgets. The song shows the internalization; the budget shows the mechanism.

Anne Helen Petersen wrote in the introduction to *Can’t Even*: “Burnout isn’t a personal problem, and it will not be cured by a productivity app or a face mask.” A government budget failure is not a personal failure either. No color-coded calendar can negotiate a fuel price, and no productivity app can pay childcare. The exhaustion of families doing this arithmetic is not a virtue, and it is not a policy platform.

The Catholic social teaching is sharper than “breathing space.” *Rerum Novarum* says, “Wages ought not to be insufficient to support a frugal and well-behaved wage-earner.” In Lansdale, my parents raised three children on my father’s USPS salary through my elementary-school years, sent us through parish school and paid off their house. My husband and I earn more in real terms than they did combined, and we still cannot afford for two children what they afforded for three. That is not nostalgia. The rules changed after they built their lives, and the bill was mailed to us. The family wage must support a family, and private property must serve the common good.

The affirmative policy catalog is not empty. Refundable child-care credits, paid parental leave, universal pre-K and direct energy relief are tools, not sentiment. The question is whether Healey has the nerve to put money behind them.

Healey should tax the documented winners. He should tax gambling as gambling, not disguise it as a war windfall. Put the proceeds into energy relief, childcare and care, public services or debt reduction; name every defense offset; tell the public what the government can do, what it must defer and what it cannot afford. If he instead repeats “breathing space” while borrowing more to finance the sensation of progress, the same squeeze remains and the reckoning is postponed. Britain cannot afford empty promises; it can afford an honest accounting.

At my kitchen table, the next month still brings income, then childcare, then fuel, groceries, the mortgage and care. If the bill is paid by families struggling with fuel bills while the banks’ interest-rate windfall remains untouched, the budget may be dull and the markets may be calm. That will not make the country feel better. Every pound must find a home. Breathing space has to be shared to count as breathing space. And the government promising it had better demonstrate that it knows who deserves it. That is not dull government. It is the only government the country can afford.
