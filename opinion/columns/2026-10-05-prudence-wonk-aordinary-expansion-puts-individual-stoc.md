Treasury opened a channel for wealthy donors to place stock in children who cannot refuse.

Here are the numbers. Last week's two-rule change at Treasury — [documented in our read of the auto-enrollment flip](/2026-10-05-treasury-auto-enrolls-60-million-in-trump-accounts-permits-stock-donations) — moved Trump Accounts from roughly 7.2 million opt-in sign-ups to more than 60 million universal accounts, and it added a second authorization at the same moment: individual stock donations to targeted groups of children, locked for five years, sorted by age, geography, or both. Every newborn through 2028 carries a $1,000 federal seed. Sixty million accounts in a single week, on top of the 7.2 million who had signed up under the old system. That is a generational reset, and almost nobody is talking about it at the scale it deserves.

The first rule is the one to defend on its own receipts, and it deserves the defense. For years the dream of progressive wealth-building advocates was a universal, auto-enrolled child savings account — every kid, not just the kids of patient or wealthy parents, holding a stake in the capital markets before they write a college essay. Treasury delivered it. The opt-in era was capped at roughly 7.2 million accounts, despite Super Bowl ads and a multi-year outreach push that exhausted every voluntary channel. Flipping the switch to auto-enrollment crossed the line overnight. The new accounts did not come from a new generation of parents deciding to be good citizens; they came from the program refusing to leave anyone out. As the Aspen Institute's Ray Boshara put it, the auto-enrollment flip is "really a game-changer" — the difference between an aspirational program and an actual structural shift in who owns equity in America. Universal coverage is the entire point of universal coverage, and it took one rule change to make it real. The hard part of the policy fight — universal access, automatic enrollment, indexed compounding over a working lifetime — was won in a single week.

The second rule is a different instrument, and the receipts on it describe something else.

Consider what is now legal. A Treasury-approved charity controlled by a wealthy donor can place individual stocks directly into the accounts of at least 5,000 children, sorted by age, geography, or both. The shares cannot be sold for five years. Children cannot refuse them. Parents cannot refuse them. The donor gets no tax deduction for the gift to the Trump Account itself — and keeps the deduction on contributions to the donor's own foundation, which is where the concentrated stock sits before it moves. The child gets a real, equity-based claim on a specific American company that compounds for half a decade before it can be touched, and then converts into an individual retirement account at 18. What the donor retains through the transaction is selection. Which company. Which cohort. Which map.

That selection is already in motion. Gwynne Shotwell, president of SpaceX, pledged in July more than two million shares to children aged 11 to 17 in lower-income areas — a pledge that predates Treasury's September rules and maps cleanly onto what is now legal. Note who that reaches: children aged 11 to 17 this year are the roughly 2009–2015 birth cohort, several years older than anything the federal seed or the newborn pledges touch. The Dell family foundation has placed $250 into the accounts of 1.2 million children under the cash-donation pathway and has pledged enough to cover 25 million kids born between 2016 and 2024 in zip codes with median family income under $150,000 — a different cohort entirely, reached by a different instrument. Treasury's own framing holds that other multibillion-dollar gifts are "poised" to follow. What the next twelve months deliver in Trump Account stock contributions may end up being the largest single philanthropic transfer of equity in American history — locked in for at least five years, riding in the accounts of children whose parents never asked for it and could not have afforded to provide it. The Dells' twenty-five-million figure is going to look quaint within a year.

The mechanism is new because the old giving channels did not let a donor hand appreciated shares directly to a kid without selling first. Wealthy donors were sitting on concentrated equity positions with no authorized route into a child's account. Now they have one — and executives with concentrated equity positions who have been waiting for an authorized mechanism that does not run afoul of the congressional index-fund-only language have a clear path. The actors who understand it first will be the ones positioned to use it. That same foundation, and others built around concentrated philanthropic capital, can now consider stock gifts to comparable low-income cohorts.

Treasury wrote the design intent into the rule. Donors are attracted, the regulation states, by the hope that a stock contribution with a five-year holding period may lead recipients and their families to feel that they have a stake in the fate of the corporation to a greater extent than if the child's holdings of the corporation were only through an index fund. Not the criticism. Not the legal hair-splitting. The rule tells you what it is for. Read that sentence again. It is a statement about what a child should feel — not about what a child should own, not about what a child should be paid, not about what a child should be able to refuse. The desired outcome is affective loyalty to a specific company, held for five years, in an account the child did not open. That is the desired outcome, written into the regulation.

There is an honest case for the pathway, and it should be stated in full before the objections arrive. A child who grows up with two shares of a company they know, contributed by a donor they may never meet, is a child who learns what equity actually means — they learn it by holding it. The index-fund orthodoxy is right for adults with diversified balance sheets; it is a poor tool for reaching a ten-year-old who needs a story to attach the concept of ownership to. The index fund does not give the child a story. The stock does. That is the strongest thing the defenders have, and it is not nothing.

It is also not the point. The objections are on the record, and both critiques are technically true and completely miss the scale of what just happened — because what just happened does not turn on either of them.

The Cato Institute's Adam Michel argues that Treasury's stock-donation rule violates congressional intent, because the statute authorizes Trump Accounts to invest only in low-cost index funds. Treasury answers that the prohibition governs how account funds are deployed, not gifts of equity received in kind from approved donors. That distinction is textually defensible: no federal dollar, parent contribution, employer contribution, or payroll deduction buys a share. But a defensible reading is not the reading Congress wrote. The index-fund limit was the one substantive investment constraint Congress placed on how children's accounts would hold equity, and Treasury has now held that constraint inapplicable to the one inflow Congress never contemplated — on the strength of a distinction no one wrote down. The question of which reading governs is live, and the agency does not get to settle it by choosing the construction that authorizes the policy it has already announced.

The Washington Center for Equitable Growth's Lily Roberts raised the sharper operational concern: that executives could offload stock through the mechanism if they expected the value to drop. The five-year hold answers the classic philanthropy problem — the donor cannot dump a laggard and walk away, because the lock ensures the child is not the bag-holder of a 48-hour liquidity event. It does not answer the selection problem. A donor who knows a position is heading down can still choose which children receive it. Treasury's rule reserves the right to approve or reject donor requests using "objective criteria" including cost, feasibility, and conflicts with other federal laws. That is the right framework. Build it out and enforce it — sharper donor vetting and conflict-of-interest review, not abandonment of a mechanism that has already attracted commitments at the scale of Shotwell's. Donor-fatigue skeptics who want to use the stock channel to dismiss the whole program have it backwards: the universal core does not depend on the pipeline, and defending the core by defending the pipeline is exactly how the core gets taken hostage.

What the universal core does over the next eighteen years is the part that does not depend on any of this. Every child turning 18 between now and the early 2030s will have had at least the option of a multi-year, compounding equity position by the time they reach working age. The 2025–2028 newborn cohort carries the $1,000 federal seed. The Dells' cash pledges reach the 2016–2024 birth cohort in low-income zip codes. Shotwell's stock pledge reaches an older group again — the roughly 2009–2015 cohort, in lower-income areas. Two donor cohorts, two instruments, one auto-enrolled population. The cohort that benefits most is the one whose parents would never have opted in under the old rules — the working-class families who never saw the Super Bowl ad, never signed up, and now have accounts by default. The opt-in era was capped at the patience of well-meaning parents. The auto-enrollment era has no such cap.

The move for working-class families is unambiguous: if a child has been auto-enrolled into a Trump Account, claim it, fund it up to the $5,000 annual parent-contribution cap if the household can, and treat it as the longest-horizon equity vehicle available. The move for community foundations, donor-advised funds, and regional philanthropies is to study the stock-donation pathway now, before the largest donors lock in their commitments and the Treasury donor pipeline becomes harder to enter. The move for employers is the payroll-deduction mechanism — the most tax-efficient wealth-building benefit available to a workforce that has been shut out of equity compensation for decades. None of that requires the stock channel. All of it is stronger without it.

Sixty million accounts, a new channel for giving equity directly to kids, and a five-year lock that turns every donated share into a long-term instrument. What gets built on top of the universal core is the interesting part, and it should be built on the accounts, not on the pipeline. Treasury wrote into its own rule the sentence that says what the pipeline is for: a stake in the fate of the corporation, held five years, in an account the child did not open. There is no other way to read it.
