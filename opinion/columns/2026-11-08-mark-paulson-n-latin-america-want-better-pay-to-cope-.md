UK supermarket buyers rob banana farmers and workers to hold prices down.

A farmer brought a John Deere tractor into my Adams County shop after a wet week. The hydraulic line had failed, the battery had gone flat, and he needed the machine back before harvest. He asked whether I could wait for the crop money. The shop rate was mine to set; the crop was his. I said yes. That meant I waited to be paid, not him, but his field and my shop were on the same clock. That arrangement was small, but it was not quaint. It is the contract underneath the contract: one side carries the delay while the other keeps control of the price.

The grain elevator down my road posts a price before the fall harvest is finished. It does not ask what the rain did to the beans. Up here in Adams County, the farmer can choose what to plant but cannot choose the rain. The elevator posts the price; the field sets the cost. The banana trade runs the same arithmetic, only the plants are taller and the storms are followed by a receipt.

The weather is already in the ledger. Fairtrade reports high winds in parts of Colombia, heavy rain in Peru and Ecuador, and drought expected in the Dominican Republic. Colombia is one of the biggest suppliers to the UK market. During the previous El Niño cycle, productivity among Fairtrade farmers and workers fell 13% in the Dominican Republic, 22% in Ecuador, and 6% in Peru compared with the year before.

The 1983 event, one of the strongest ever recorded, affected 15% of Ecuador’s planted banana area and reduced exports by 36%. Dr Carley-Jane Stanton, the Fairtrade Foundation’s climate and environment lead, expects this El Niño to produce more severe effects than the 2023–24 cycle. The [UN says this year’s El Niño is the strongest in 70 years and set to persist into 2027](/articles/2026-09-03-un-says-this-year-s-el-ni-o-is-strongest-in-70-years-set-to-persist-into-2027/), while [strong El Niño has already disrupted global commodity and shipping markets](/articles/2026-08-29-strong-el-ni-o-disrupts-global-commodity-and-shipping-markets/).

Volatility is not foreign to me. For twelve years I have kept a weather notebook. The syrup season has moved earlier in those pages, and in some years the mosquitoes reach my shop before the old May line. That is not a model of Latin America. It is evidence of the ordinary change in the season around me. The work calendar no longer follows the old calendar, while the buyer can still write the old price into a contract. The grower absorbs the variance.

El Niño is not the climate bill. It is the weather that brings the bill due all at once.

Now the losses are showing in money. Exports from Colombia’s Caribbean region fell 32% to $122 million between July and September, according to the Association of Banana Growers of Magdalena and La Guajira, as reported by Fruitnet. In September alone, shipments were down 42% year on year. Earnings came close to halving, from $70 million to $40 million.

The association said climate-related productivity losses and falling export earnings, made worse by currency problems, were undermining the industry’s economic sustainability and threatening the continuity of banana production. That is not a weather inconvenience in a distant country. It is wages, payrolls, and family income moving in the wrong direction.

Now look at what happened to the price while the crop failed. Bananas are becoming harder and more expensive to produce. Supermarket prices have remained nearly unchanged for almost 40 years despite rising costs for fertiliser, fuel, and labour. The Ethical Trading Initiative found that prices paid to suppliers in 2026 did not cover basic production costs for 66.7% of the suppliers it studied.

The buyer gets a price it can hold. The grower gets a bill that cannot wait.

That is not a producer asking for luxury. That is a buyer paying less than the work costs.

Fairtrade is calling for UK legislation against low or late payments and contracts that push risk down the supply chain. Farmers cannot protect a crop they have already lost, but they can strengthen drainage, diversify varieties, improve pest control, and prepare soil for the next planting if the contract leaves them money to do it. When wind flattens the plants or rain brings disease, the grower must replant, protect the next crop, and pay workers while the contract stays where it is.

The fixed price sits at the shelf; the variable cost lands on the farm. Wind, drought, flooding, fertiliser, diesel, and late payment become the producer’s problem, while the chain keeps the price on the shelf. That contract does not share the weather. It assigns it.

Ever Antonio Flores Muños, a worker at the Agrosiete banana producer in Colombia, told Fairtrade: “Over the past 10 years the climate has changed a lot. It’s been getting much hotter … it’s really intense.” He described losses from flooding and strong winds and said wages would decrease. That is what the contract does. It makes the weather bill a wage problem.

This is the Worker Self-Exploitation contradiction named in *We Too*, Chapter 16: working-class dignity becomes an order to finance the worker’s own defeat. The buyer sets the terms and calls them market conditions; the producer is expected to wait, absorb the loss, and then let wages fall. Self-exploitation gets called resilience because the alternative is exit.

Wisconsin’s own dairy record carries the same arithmetic. The 2022 Census of Agriculture counted Wisconsin’s dairy sector at 15,366 fewer farms than in 1997, while the average herd grew from 55.6 cows to 203.4. A University of Tennessee study, reported in 2022, found average milk-production costs of $25.80 per hundredweight against average milk-sales income of $18.57 over 2005–2020. The gap was $7.23 for every hundredweight of milk. At the co-op or the grain elevator, a farmer can bring in the crop and bargain over the price. He does not set it alone.

Wendell Berry’s “The Insects” in *The Unsettling of America* set out the extractive mind. Land, labour, and animals become inputs, while the costs attached to them get pushed down the ledger. Climate damage enters the farm’s books. Purchasing power keeps the buyer from moving the price. That is extraction with a weather forecast attached.

Eric Schlosser’s *Fast Food Nation* put concentrated purchasing power at the centre of the food business. A few buyers can set terms for many producers while individual sellers carry the risk. The countries are not the same, and a banana is not a hamburger. The power is the same where a buyer with many outlets can set terms for producers with one harvest: it can make change somebody else’s problem while its own price stays familiar.

The buyer has one advantage: the shelf price is visible to consumers, and the farm’s losses are not. A chain can spread its buying across growers and countries; a grower cannot spread one failed harvest across another crop. That asymmetry turns an El Niño into lower wages and a lower farmgate price.

Some supermarkets have moved. Sainsbury’s, the Co-op, and Waitrose have taken voluntary steps by sourcing only Fairtrade bananas, which guarantee a minimum price. That is a real step. It is still a voluntary patch. A voluntary floor is not a substitute for a fair contract.

Eleanor Harrison, the Fairtrade Foundation’s chief executive, put the terms plainly: “Fair prices, climate resilience and decent work go hand in hand.” Caoimhe Buckley, chief corporate affairs officer at Fyffes, says the supermarket price needs to recognise the real and sustainable cost of production, including labour, environmental management, human-rights due diligence, and the investment needed to adapt to climate change. That is the supplier asking the buyer to change the shelf price, not merely bless the farmer after the storm. When extreme weather can cut exports by nearly a third, a label cannot supply the resilience producers need.

Research published for the Inter-American Development Bank in 2020 estimated that land suitable for banana cultivation across Latin America and the Caribbean could shrink by an average of 61% by 2050. The current El Niño sharpens the bill, but the longer pressure is the land itself. Suitable land is the industry’s factory, its soil, and its future crop. Producers are not merely producing food. They are being asked to keep the harvest alive while the land that can carry it shrinks. A price system that underpays growers cannot also pretend it is preparing them for a smaller and more difficult landscape.

Public purchasing and supermarket contracts should require payment above full production cost, prompt settlement, a living-wage floor, and a transparent formula for extraordinary climate losses. That would not abolish weather; it would put money into resilience before the next storm and stop the buyer from treating the producer’s ruin as a market price. The shelf price should tell the truth about labour, fuel, environmental management, and storms; that is not charity, but the price of expecting the crop next year.

Back in Adams County, the repaired John Deere has to go back into the field, and I want Mike and Quinn to inherit a country where the people who feed it can keep a share of what they make. A fair contract should leave the farmer able to work after the storm, not merely alive to answer for the next shortage. A storm can take a banana field in one night. It cannot make an underpaying contract fair.
