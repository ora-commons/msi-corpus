On November 10 the Department of Agriculture released a proposed rule for public comment. Comments close December 10. If the rule is approved, it takes effect October 1, 2027. It would eliminate broad-based categorical eligibility, [the automatic SNAP eligibility states currently tie to TANF](/articles/2026-11-13-usda-proposal-would-end-automatic-snap-eligibility-tied-to-tanf/), and it would end the states' power to align SNAP income and asset limits with local economic conditions. The Ohio Workforce Coalition says the rule would impose significant benefits cliff penalties on working families with low income. The Food Research & Action Center, whose Gina Plata-Nino and Dory Thrasher say broad-based categorical eligibility "streamlines the administration of" SNAP, says eliminating it would raise administrative costs and processing delays, shift bigger cost burdens to the states, intensify food insecurity, and undermine work incentives.

The Beyond the Cliff Coalition, citing USDA's own estimates, projects the rule would cut SNAP benefits by about $14 billion in the first full year, reduce benefits by an average of about $340 a month for affected households, and make 3.7 million households now receiving SNAP ineligible, with a disproportionate impact on working families. Nearly 18 percent of SNAP households with children would lose eligibility. [This publication has already reported the department's own analysis at 6.3 million people](/articles/2026-11-16-usda-proposal-could-remove-6-3-million-people-from-snap/). The Children's Defense Fund finds the proposal would risk the food security of more than six million children in America. The coalition projects Ohio would lose 10 to 15 percent of its SNAP caseload, and the decline could run larger. The rule would reach SNAP's employment and training programs too, so a person who loses eligibility midstream would have to disenroll from the program built to move people off assistance.

The One Big Beautiful Bill Act, championed and signed by Donald Trump in 2025, carried the largest cuts to SNAP in the program's history. Experts estimated millions would be left without food assistance, including hundreds of thousands of Ohioans. More than one million children in the United States have already lost access to SNAP, and the cuts are projected to spill over into the free breakfasts and lunches children have during the school year. Those cuts come alongside Medicaid cuts and new work requirements in the same legislation, and [Ohio is already preparing for those work rules to phase in](/articles/2026-11-17-ohio-prepares-for-medicaid-work-rules-as-federal-law-phases-in/). The department has also cancelled the Current Population Survey Food Security Supplement, the annual survey that counted hunger nationally.
