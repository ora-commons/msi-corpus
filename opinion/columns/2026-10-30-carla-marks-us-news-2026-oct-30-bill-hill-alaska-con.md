A gallon of gasoline now costs more than $25 in Ambler and Shungnak, two villages on the Kobuk River above the Arctic Circle. Alaska is not expensive because it is far away. It is expensive because of choices, and the choices have receipts: a truck in Naknek that went from a $118 fill-up to more than $200 once this spring's fuel barge docked, heating fuel up from $4.50 a gallon to $8.30, gas climbing from $6.70 toward $9 when the barge landed. As [The Guardian reported this week](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2026/oct/30/bill-hill-alaska-congressional-race), that gallon has become the whole argument in Alaska's lone House race, where Bill Hill — Dena'ina Athabascan, commercial fisherman since he was eight, union construction worker, twenty-five years as a union teacher, principal, and school district superintendent, with roughly $119,000 in reported assets to his name — is running on a one-item platform, what life costs and who's been choosing the price, against Representative Nick Begich, worth at least $7.4 million. The Cook Political Report just moved the race from "likely Republican" to "leans Republican," which in this district counts as a tremor worth watching. The fuel is the argument. The rest is commentary.

Give the incumbent his strongest hour first, because the courtesy costs nothing and the record costs him more. Begich won this seat in 2024 by 2.4 points in a state Trump carried by thirteen, in a district that favors Republicans by six on Cook's own baseline, so the lean is real and incumbency is not a scandal. Hill is a first-time candidate trailing by single digits. He hasn't said whether he'd caucus with Democrats, and the fact that national House Democrats put his race in their "red to blue" program in September writes the opposition's next ad for free — independent in name only. It is also true, as his office has said, that a man's bank statement doesn't mechanically write his votes; a spokesperson previously told the Anchorage Press that Begich's personal finances "have no impact on his legislative decisionmaking process." Being wealthy is not a crime, and the case against him was never his net worth. The record writes the votes.

On the record: a signature on the National Right to Work Act, a 0% lifetime score from the AFL-CIO, a "transformative victory" praised over a bill that shrinks SNAP and Medicaid — the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, with the cuts timed to land in 2027 — and continued support for a war in Iran that Hill and a lot of his neighbors credit with pushing fuel prices up. Transformative, yes. That transformation is dated 2027. Anyway. Walk me through it slowly — which part of that record is the bank statement, and which part is the $8.30 heating fuel?

Now the pump. The instinct, in the coverage and in the campaign ads, is to blame the distance, the barge schedule, the ice. Anything but the arrangement. Distance explains why bush gas is dear. It cannot explain why heating fuel nearly doubled, because the distance never moved and the price did. The barge is old. The doubling is new. And the squeeze is landing on households that were already underwater: a report out this month from the United Way of Anchorage and Alaska 211 finds that more than one Alaskan household in three cannot cover housing, food, childcare, and the essentials.

Fuel doesn't get expensive because a village is far away. It gets expensive because a supply chain gets squeezed, and the squeeze gets passed downhill until it stops at whoever can't pass it further. Village fuel is priced off world crude plus barge freight — a long chain on which every premium added at the top compounds on the way down and arrives, fully grown, at the bottom, which is where the $25 gallon gets paid. This year the squeeze had sponsors: a war in Iran pushing crude, tariffs taxing everything that arrives on the barge, expiring Affordable Care Act tax credits, federal jobs pulled out of the state, and the renewable projects that would have cut diesel bills, canceled by the same administration. Hill backs both the oil industry and the renewables one, and counts himself among the voters those prices are squeezing — which is the point of him.

Here's the part that should embarrass everyone involved. Alaska is not a poor state with unlucky geography; it is a rich state with poor villages, and the richness is publicly owned. The state holds its oil in common and mails every Alaskan a dividend check from it every year — the Alaska Permanent Fund, public ownership of a natural resource, running for decades in the reddest state in the union without anyone marching on Juneau. Norway ran the same idea to continental scale and now owns about one and a half percent of every listed company on earth out of a fund measured in the trillions of dollars; my cousins in Sweden treat the whole arrangement the way we treat the interstate, which is to say nobody there gives a speech about it. And yet none of that shared richness is pointed at the squeeze. The state owns a barrel of oil under every one of those households. Follow the cost down and it doesn't land on the fund. It lands on the family with the least room to absorb it.

Now the part the horse-race coverage will miss. Alaska has the third-highest union density in America — 18.1% of workers as of 2025, behind only Hawaii and New York — and that is the quiet reason a fisherman with $119,000 in assets can put a man worth at least $7.4 million on his heels. The assets gap is the least interesting fact in this race; Hill still offers his own summary — "a multimillionaire working for billionaires" — but he doesn't need it, and neither do I. The record says it better than any adjective. A union is a thousand people negotiating one price together — a market mechanism, not a manifesto — and Alaska has run that mechanism long enough that Hill's résumé reads like the membership roll: fishing boats since he was eight, construction with Laborers' Local 341, twenty-five years in the schools as a union teacher, principal, and superintendent. Begich's contribution to all that organized bargaining power was to sign on to the National Right to Work Act — the device that obligates a union to represent workers who pay nothing toward the representation, starving it slowly and on purpose. That isn't an economic theory. It's a decision about who's allowed to bargain.

Begich's office did not respond to requests for comment this week, but the defense is already on file, and it misses the point politely: nobody said the money was voting. The voting record is public — a zero, lifetime, with labor, in the third-most-unionized state in the union. That takes commitment. More than twenty labor organizations, the Alaska AFL-CIO among them, have spent their endorsements on Hill anyway, and organizations like that do not line up behind a party man, which is why the "independent in name only" ad dies on contact. "Unions built the middle class of this country," Hill says, "and they're the ones who can do it again."

Here's the part worth building, whatever happens Tuesday. Hill could afford this run because institutions built him a floor: union construction wages, a quarter-century in union education work, a life steady enough to jump from. That is not celebrity politics. That is a candidate pipeline — the unions that built the middle class fielding one of their own. Pipelines are infrastructure, and a working district that can staff Congress from its own economy has solved a problem the whole country is circling.

His path runs through a four-way ranked-choice count that includes a fourth candidate — the Democrat Eric Hafner, currently serving time in a federal prison in New York, with no visible connection to the state. A sentence nobody should have to write about a ballot, but there it is, and it is precisely the kind of detail the press will spend November on; the spoiler storyline is already [running hot in the Senate race next door](/articles/2026-10-30-spoiler-allegations-roil-alaska-s-tight-senate-race/). Here is the objection at full strength: every Hafner ballot that never ranks Hill survives the first round and counts for nobody in the last one, and the margin it bleeds is a margin Begich collects without changing a single mind. Now watch it die on Hill's own terms. A spoiler only spoils when people vote for him as a refuge from a candidate they can't stand, and Hill has spent the whole campaign making himself impossible to vote against — gun rights, oil and renewables both, fish sheds and school hallways — the second choice worth having rather than the lesser evil. The mechanism cuts both ways, too: ballots for eliminated candidates transfer, so the same field that lets Begich stand as the only Republican hands a consolidated anti-Begich majority a second round. Begich needs this to be a protest vote; the count is under no obligation to hand him one.

Alaska is not a fluke, and Hill is not alone. Mary Peltola, whom Begich unseated by 2.4 points in a state Trump carried by thirteen, has spent October making the same rural-cost case in her Senate run against Dan Sullivan — [we followed that campaign here](/articles/2026-10-22-mary-peltola-campaigns-against-dan-sullivan-over-alaska-s-rural-costs/) last week. Hill is one of [173 independents on House ballots this year](/articles/2026-10-13-173-independent-house-candidates-test-the-size-of-america-s-political-middle/), part of a wider wave of independents challenging Republicans in red states, including Senate candidates Dan Osborn in Nebraska and Todd Achilles in Idaho. He won't say whether he'd caucus with Democrats, and he shouldn't have to yet — he's part of a wave testing whether red states hold more political room than the two-party menu admits. Watch the word "long shot" while the coverage uses it. It is measuring name recognition, not capability. A school superintendent has already run a budget with children attached to every line. The state's budget is bigger. The skill is the skill.

So what do we build? The recipe is already in the state. Keep the dividend — it is the proof, cashed annually, that public ownership works in a place that calls such things socialism everywhere else. Extend the same ownership logic to the fuel bill itself: the canceled wind and solar projects were cheaper heat by another name, and villages pay some of the highest energy costs in America largely because nobody has ever pointed the machinery at them. Keep the bargaining structures that made this race close in the first place. Say the objection aloud — why isn't it easier here? — and notice what a strange sentence that is to write about Alaska, because here is where the machinery already exists: the fund, the density, the dividend. Rural electric cooperatives and credit unions do the quiet version of the same thing; my grandfather sat on the boards of a dairy co-op and a rural electric co-op in western Minnesota, and he would have laughed at the idea that any of this was exotic. Alaska has been social democracy without the word for half a century. We already do this. We just won't say it. The obstacle isn't invention. It's aim. Remind me what the obstacle is again.

The economy is a set of choices, not the weather. Tuesday does not un-double the price of diesel. It decides who reads the next bill before voting on it: someone who has priced a $200 fill-up in Naknek, or someone who cheered the stack and called it transformative. Alaska owns its oil. The election is a referendum on who the oil works for. Fifty years of dividend checks have already settled whether ordinary people can own things together without the sky falling — sending one of their own to read the fine print would be the least radical item on that list. That question was never the weather.
