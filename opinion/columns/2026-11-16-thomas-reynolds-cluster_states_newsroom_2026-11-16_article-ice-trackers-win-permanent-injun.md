The Trump administration silenced the citizens filming its immigration raids. It censored an American's app to hide what its agents were doing. The pressure went to Apple, and Apple took the app down.

The instrument is a permanent injunction — a court order that binds the parties until a court lifts it — which U.S. District Court Judge Jorge L. Alonso approved last week on a joint motion. The Departments of Justice and Homeland Security joined the request that they be barred from pressuring Apple. The app is Eyes Up, built by Brown County resident Mark Hodges so that people could share video of ICE activity. Apple pulled it from the App Store in 2025 after the two departments pressed the company. The suit also covered Kate Rosado of Chicago, whose Facebook group documenting ICE sightings was targeted as well, and the Foundation for Individual Rights and Expression, a First Amendment litigation firm, carried both claims. The order forbids pressure formal and informal — the written demand, and also the phone call, the meeting, the suggestion that arrives carrying the government's regulatory power. Lawyers call the practice jawboning: pressure applied where the government has no lawful command to issue.

The departments' lawyers get a version of this record too, and it is not frivolous. The government communicated with a private company; the company applied its own content policies; and the case ended by consent, so no court has found the pressure unconstitutional. A settlement in judicial form is not a merits verdict. The defense also starts from a line that is genuinely contested — where persuasion ends and coercion begins when the persuader regulates the persuadee — and the Supreme Court left that line undrawn when it disposed of a challenge to this practice on standing grounds. On this reading the government conceded nothing. It agreed to an injunction against conduct no court has ruled unlawful.

The First Amendment has read that sequence differently for more than sixty years. *Bantam Books, Inc. v. Sullivan*, 372 U.S. 58 (1963), involved a Rhode Island commission with no power to ban books: it mailed booksellers a list of titles it deemed objectionable and warned that selling them could bring prosecution. The booksellers pulled the books, and the Court held the scheme was censorship — notice backed by state power accomplishing what the state could not lawfully order. DOJ and DHS could not lawfully order an app removed. They pressed the company that controls the door to the iPhone, and the door closed. The departments did not circulate a list of objectionable videos. They had the storefront. The order before Judge Alonso forecloses the very category the government's best defense depends on: informal pressure. Where persuasion ends and coercion begins is genuinely contested, with government leverage sustained in some postures and condemned in others — but the First Amendment does not care whether the censorship arrives as a subpoena or as a phone call. It cares whether a man who films his government can still be heard.

Eyes Up was one of at least two platforms targeted at the start of President Donald Trump's second term — the same pressure [tech companies have begun pushing back against](/articles/2026-10-29-tech-companies-push-back-against-broad-ice-demands-for-user-data/) as ICE demands for user data widened, the same injury [a federal judge found in an ICE warning-notice case](/articles/2026-09-15-federal-judge-finds-first-amendment-injury-in-ice-warning-notice-case/), and the same machinery that has since produced [hundreds of subpoenas aimed at unmasking critics](/articles/2026-08-04-ice-s-social-media-surveillance-spurs-hundreds-of-subpoenas-to-unmask-critics/). Pressure routed through a private platform is the administration's working method for speech it cannot silence by command. Swap Eyes Up for any other platform and nothing in the analysis changes: the government leans on whoever controls the platform, and the footage of its own raids comes down.

The Supreme Court met this practice in 2024 and declined to grip it. In *Murthy v. Missouri*, 603 U.S. 43 (2024), a challenge to federal officials' pressure on platforms over content moderation foundered on standing — the doctrine governing who is entitled to sue — and the Court never decided whether the pressure violated the First Amendment. The complaint in *Murthy* was that officials had leaned on platforms to suppress speech the government disliked. The case before Judge Alonso is officials leaning on a platform to suppress speech about the government's own conduct. The theory has not changed hands. The target has.

Because the injunction came by consent, no circuit has spoken. What stopped the conduct was ordinary district-court litigation, and the doctrine that governs it is the oldest commitment in the free-speech canon. *New York Times Co. v. Sullivan*, 376 U.S. 254 (1964), promised that debate on public issues would be "uninhibited, robust, and wide-open." Citizens filming a raid on their own street are not an edge case of that promise. They are its center. Hodges said it in a news release on Friday: "People have a right to know what their government is doing." Until a judge made it stop, the government's answer to that sentence was pressure on the company that carried it.

The government may enforce immigration law and may tell a company what it lawfully knows. It may not spend the leverage of its own enforcement power making a private platform delete the record of what it is doing. An executive that respected this body of law would argue in the open: state the concern on the record, defend it in court, accept the answer. It would not work a company's back channels and then consent to an order barring it from working them again. The order's two adjectives — formally or informally — exist because informal pressure is what the lawsuit documented.

The government never commanded Apple. It pressured Apple, and an app that filmed ICE disappeared. It wanted the videos off the public's phones, so it went to the company that owns the door to those phones, and the door closed. Two citizens sued; the government agreed to stop; Hodges added that it should not take a lawsuit to make federal agencies respect the Constitution. A man built an app for watching his government; the Justice Department made Apple take it down; Judge Alonso has now written down, in a permanent injunction, that neither agency may do it again.

The suppression is barred now by consent. The filming was protected all along by right. One of those facts took a lawsuit. The other did not.
