The first customers through the door at SEK Community Food Shop's third Kent site were not statistics. They were Shirley Graves and her partner Rod Taffs, pensioners, working out the week's meals on the first floor of The Pentagon Centre in Chatham. Twenty samosas for £1.50. A big bag of chicken for £6. "Times are quite tight at the moment, being pensioners, it's cheap and the chicken is amazing," Graves said. Taffs sized the chicken up and decided it would carry them three or four meals. Walk in without an appointment. Pick up a £1 fruit-and-vegetable bag. Leave with three or four meals that did not need to be grown twice. That is the shop. It gives a pensioner couple on a tight week a week that is not as tight. I am not against it. I am against needing it.

It is also a hub. Gina English of SEK laid out the wraparound at opening: help with employment, CVs, interview skills, budgeting, money management, and signposting to other local services. At the back of the Chatham store sits a confidential, soundproofed office pod where a customer can sit down and talk through whatever they actually came in to talk about. The food is the front door. The advice is the building. The shop is open Monday through Saturday, ten till six. The food is the front door; the wraparound is the real building.

SEK's two earlier Kent stores, at Ramsgate and Ashford, have pulled 55,500 transactions and 170 tonnes of "perfectly good food" out of the waste stream in twelve months. Ramsgate has been running about three years; Ashford just over a year. That is not a pilot. That is a working answer to a question the rest of the country still treats as rhetorical. But call it what it is: those are not numbers to be proud of. They are numbers to be ashamed of by every actor further up the chain that produced the surplus in the first place. One hundred and seventy tonnes is not a trophy for the front wall. It is the spillover that fell out of a supply chain where supermarkets and processors wrote off edible food as a cost-of-doing-business line, leaving local charities and an initial public grant from Medway Council's Crisis and Resilience Fund to mop it up. A £1 bag is a confession that the pricing model upstream is broken.

Vince Maple, the council leader, put the case plainly: most people across Kent and Medway would rather see good food eaten than tipped, and the ability to put it on the table at a price that protects both the weekly shop and the planet "has got to be a good thing." He is right about the second half. He is wrong about the celebration. Read his own framing again: a shop that helps pensioners stretch chicken across four meals is also pitched as a general household shop for "all families." Normalise surplus shopping as a permanent fixture and the pressure to fix the actual supply chain gets weaker. English's wraparound — CV help, interview skills, budgeting, signposting — is the welfare state, contracted out to a charity on a first-floor unit of a shopping centre. The funding stream is called Crisis and Resilience, and "crisis" is doing a lot of work. It implies an emergency, a temporary state, a grant that closes when the crisis closes. It is not closing. And it is not closing because the councils absorbing this work are being cut at both ends — even at Westminster, [the shadow cabinet's own leaked deliberations](/articles/2026-10-03-griffith-leak-shows-shadow-cabinet-discussed-further-uk-conservative-cuts/) show further cuts on the table. The charity absorbs the surplus; the council absorbs the cut.

So what do we build instead? Not a wishlist. Two institutions, both named, both existing in the world.

The first is a statutory surplus-redistribution authority — the model here is France's Garot law of 2016, which took goodwill out of the question. Any food retailer above 400 square metres must hold a signed donation agreement with a registered redistributor — a food bank, a community shop, a cooperative — or face a per-infraction fine. Run it locally: Medway chartered for Kent, or a county-level body, licensing redistributors, auditing tonnage, publishing what each chain moved and what it binned. Fund it from the fines plus core council funding, with the grant money SEK currently spends on sourcing shifted into the advice pod where it belongs. What changes on the ground: SEK stops scavenging write-offs and gets contracted supply; suppliers get a legal duty instead of a press release; and the £1 bag stays, while the word "crisis" comes off the door. That last part matters. The food stops being penance.

The second is a tier the country does not have at all — a publicly funded cooperative grocery. Look at what the two existing tiers do. [Mixue just overtook McDonald's](/articles/2026-09-14-mixue-surpasses-mcdonald-s-as-world-s-largest-food-and-beverage-chain/) as the world's largest food-and-beverage chain on high volume, low margin, commercialised efficiency — a franchise machine that beats incumbents on price because it is ruthlessly ordinary about everything else. On the other side, SEK: low volume, donated margin, charitable goodwill, plus a council grant. Between them there is a hole. A member-owned municipal food cooperative, sourcing through the surplus authority and buying from publicly anchored wholesale markets on the Emilia-Romagna model — wholesale markets run as public bodies with producer price floors — would be owned by the people who shop in it. One member, one vote. If you have a credit-union card in your wallet, you already belong to a cooperative; you just have not been invited to think of your supermarket that way. A shopper who never qualifies for "crisis" support but cannot afford the supermarket could join the co-op the way they joined the credit union: as an owner, not a case.

This is where [London's pay-it-forward schemes](/articles/2026-08-22-london-shops-run-pay-it-forward-schemes-that-could-offset-small-costs/) belong. They are not a substitute for the architecture; they are the bottom layer of it. The neighbour who pays two pounds extra so the next person's shop costs two pounds less is already doing the redistributive work — informally, without a legal instrument, without an audit trail. Fold those schemes into the authority's redistribution points and the co-op's membership rolls and the goodwill becomes infrastructure. Mutual aid does what it does best: it shows you where the need is before the institution catches up. Then the institution catches up.

Graves is right that the chicken is amazing. What is missing underneath the goodwill is plumbing. The ask, stated so a council leader can be held to it: charter a surplus-redistribution authority with a legal donation duty for large retailers, and stand up a member-owned community food cooperative to buy through it. Neither is utopia. The Garot law exists in statute; Emilia-Romagna has run public wholesale markets for generations; the co-op form is in a hundred and forty-five million American credit-union wallets and in the Basque Country's eleven-billion-euro worker federation. Chatham already has the proof the model works — 170 tonnes of it. The next pensioner couple through the door should walk into a shop with a supply contract, an owner's vote, and a council that calls it public provision rather than crisis. The chicken will still be amazing. It will just be in a normal shop.
