It turns out that its principal author has not only done an enormous disservice to students in general. Now she's wrestling with the question of how much her politicized decision-making may have harmed her daughter. The catalogue's WSJ §4.12 euphemism cluster applies in full: opponent labels adopted in narrator voice, proponent labels scare-quoted. Operators call this the frame-loading move — pick the term that has already done the cognitive work for your audience and let the term carry the conclusion. "Anti-American" is not an argument. It is a frame that has done its work before the reader arrives. The piece hasn't argued anything yet. It has only named what it is against. The opening paragraph tells the reader who Hannah-Jones is in the narrator's voice, and the rest of the column never revisits the question.

> On the surface, Nikole Hannah-Jones's sad tale of sending her daughter to a failing school for ideological reasons may appear to be a rare and courageous case of journalistic humility. "In Fighting for Every Black Child, Did I Betray My Own?" is the title of her long piece in the Times magazine.

**Strawman — [Bad-Faith Catalog](/propaganda/docs/bad-faith-techniques-catalogue), representational variety — establishes the verdict before the column has engaged the artifact.** The piece concedes Hannah-Jones's article is "courageous" and a form of "journalistic humility" while in the same sentence characterizing it as the story of a mother sending her daughter to "a failing school for ideological reasons." The strawman is established so it can be knocked down. The construction is one the editorial page runs every Tuesday: when the substance cannot be made and the verdict still needs to land, set up the verdict and knock it down. The piece is performing concern for a child. It is not interested in the child. It is interested in what the child's story can be made to license.
