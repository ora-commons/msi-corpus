A federal film tax credit would buy the shoot without giving film workers the power to claim the gain. In [Congress can save America’s unique history and boost the economy with just one move](https://www.foxnews.com/opinion/congress-save-americas-unique-history-boost-economy-one-move), Charles Rivkin, chairman and CEO of the Motion Picture Association, argues that more than 120 jurisdictions use production incentives, that America’s share of global production has fallen from 65 percent to 39 percent, and that Congress should enact a federal credit to reverse the decline. He cites a study projecting that returning to 65 percent would add nearly $250 billion to local economies and create more than 143,000 jobs each year. It is a plan to make filming cheaper while leaving the owners of production companies free to decide what the public subsidy is worth to workers.

That is not a small thing, and the strongest true point in Rivkin’s case is simple: production is mobile. Governments compete. A targeted credit can tip a shoot that was close to choosing one location toward another. Georgia’s credit and Texas’s incentive have drawn measurable activity. Rivkin cites a 6.3-to-1 return in Georgia and $4.50 in local activity for every Texas public dollar. If the alternative is losing the production altogether, public money can improve the bid. Good.

That settles only the first question. Can a credit win the shoot? Yes. Can it win a worker’s claim on the gain? No.

A credit lowers the production budget. Then someone still decides what the remaining money becomes: higher day rates, more jobs, paid training, or retained profit. The subsidy does not decide. The company does, unless workers can bargain.

Take the extra camera crew the credit makes affordable. Without a negotiated wage floor, the studio can hire the crew at the old rate. The camera runs. The region gets the payroll. The people carrying the camera keep no claim on the saving. Then the season ends. The producer moves on. The workers chase the next job without benefits that follow them.

Film work is assembled project by project. That leaves an individual worker with little hold over the company that hires them for the next production. Rivkin counts 1.3 million workers as a constituency to be rescued by an incentive. A headcount is not a counterparty.

The op-ed does not show whether its estimates subtract production and spending that would merely move from one state to another. Nor does it show what happens inside a production after the money arrives. A state can count payroll, vendors, hotel nights, and shooting days. Those totals show that activity occurred. They do not show who received the margin, who carried the risk, or whether the people who built the production can bargain over the next one.

Rivkin’s local examples make the activity look impressive. An average feature film adds $22 million to the economy where it shoots. A television season hires 1,800 workers. Those figures measure the size of the production. They do not, by themselves, measure what the subsidy caused.

A state can win a shoot from another state while the national total stays unchanged. A production can receive a credit even if it was already going to happen. Then the public pays and the new-job count stays at zero. The missing comparison is simple: What would this production have done without the subsidy?

The industry’s more than two million jobs and more than $200 billion in annual wages show scale, not causation. Setting the existing $200 billion payroll beside projected production activity does not show what a new federal check adds. The op-ed says worker organizations are represented at the bargaining table. Fine. Endorsement is not evidence. Having worker representatives support a subsidy does not tell us which jobs will be additional, what each job costs, or how much money reaches workers after studios, financiers, and production companies take their share.

The credit lowers a production’s cost. The op-ed gives no project-by-project account of what changes after the check is written.

Now take the 65-to-39 statistic. Market share compares two things: what America makes and what the rest of the world makes. If the rest of the world makes more films, America can lose share without losing a single production. Hold the world’s total fixed, and moving from 39 percent to 65 percent requires American output to rise by about two-thirds. The arithmetic is 65 divided by 39, or roughly 1.67. Any growth in the world total makes the required American expansion larger.

So the $250 billion and 143,000 jobs are forecasts of a large expansion, not evidence that a federal credit will cause one. Returning to 65 percent is an assumption, not a description of what the credit restores.

The causal story is incomplete, too. If tax incentives pull production across borders, America’s own state incentives must be part of the story. Many states already offer what Congress is being urged to add. The op-ed points to more than 120 jurisdictions offering incentives, then treats the decline in the American share as proof that foreign incentives caused it. It does not show which productions moved because of which incentive, whether they would otherwise have stayed in America, or whether federal support would merely start a louder bidding war among states. Two things moving together does not prove that one caused the other.

The title also promises to save America’s unique history. Rivkin argues that foreign audiences who watch American movies and shows are more likely to visit the United States, buy American products, and engage with American culture and history. The subsidy cannot prove that link. It can pay for an American location, an American payroll, and an American production company. It cannot show that the story would not have been made here without the check. It cannot show that viewers will travel because of it.

A tax credit can buy production. It cannot buy proof of cultural inheritance. Congress should not turn a film subsidy into a guarantee of who Americans become.

The strongest conclusion the evidence supports is narrower: targeted incentives can tip some productions that were close to going elsewhere. Before Congress writes a broad check, the proposal must show how many productions were already planned, how many jobs would remain after counting activity that would have happened anyway, what each truly new job costs, and what happens when promised jobs fail to appear. If the evidence cannot answer those questions, the claim of 143,000 additional jobs should be withdrawn rather than protected by adjectives.

So what do we build? Not an open-ended tax-credit fog. Public money in film must purchase a claim for workers, not merely a shooting schedule.

Start with the credit. Any federal production incentive should pay only after a state workforce agency certifies a written project deal setting wage, benefit, safety, and scheduling floors. The deal must be negotiated with the people doing the work. Treasury should withhold payment until certification. Labor and Treasury auditors should check payroll and benefit records. If the producer breaks the deal, the credit should be clawed back and support suspended until the workers are paid.

Every covered worker should have a portable-benefit account that follows that worker from production to production and state to state. The account would carry money for health insurance, retirement, and paid leave. A subsidy that ends at the last day of principal photography has bought a shooting schedule, not a durable career.

Then build the ownership side. Congress should create a capped federal film-development bank, with a loan window run through state economic-development agencies for worker-owned studios and shared equipment. Start with repayable loans and guarantees. The worker-owners must hold the equity and share in the returns. Agencies could lend against owned equipment, production contracts, and cash flow. Shared equipment facilities could let small crews buy costly cameras together instead of renting them from distant owners. Public money should build assets the workers control, not temporary leverage for someone else.

The bank should finance scripts, production, and the work needed to finish a film when private capital declines a risky project. It should lend only to projects with no committed private financing and no overlapping state incentive. For every award, it should publish what the project would have done without the loan and what the loan changed. Every loan should stay in the public books.

A regional shoot has a script, a small crew, and no lender willing to bet on future ticket sales. The bank guarantees most of a loan against the verified budget. A community lender releases cash in stages as the crew and vendors bill for their work. The crew is paid through payroll when production starts, not months later after a distributor blesses the film.

If ticket and licensing receipts fail, the loan defaults and the public bank pays the promised guarantee. That loss must appear in the books. It cannot be renamed a tax credit.

Finally, bargain across productions rather than company by company. A multi-employer deal can set common terms for pay, safety, scheduling, and benefits when one production hires several companies and many contractors. The people doing the work and their representatives negotiate with all of them. Benefits follow the worker. Standards follow the job. The state agency certifies that the deal covers the work before the credit is released.

Film finance is risky. States already compete for shoots. A federal program can become another giveaway if its books are secret. So the fund should be capped, run through local film offices, public in its books, and relentless about repayment. Where a production company survives, employee ownership can give workers a stake. Where it does not, training and wage standards have to protect workers.

Build the film bank. Bargain the floor. Make benefits portable. Finance ownership where the camera comes home. Leave behind a loan that was repaid or a loss honestly recorded, a trained crew, a production company workers can share in, and wages set by the people who do the work.

Then Congress will have financed something durable. Not a bigger percentage point. An economy in which production can stay because the work is worth doing, not because workers should be grateful for a subsidy. Build that. The lights go down. The claim should not.
