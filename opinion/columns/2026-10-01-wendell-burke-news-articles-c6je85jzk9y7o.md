Nike was a name a kid in a town like this one could believe in. It meant somebody made a good shoe, and the people who made it got paid, and the kid who wore it was told — wrongly, but generously — that the shoe would make him faster. What has been done to that company over the last five years is not a brand turnaround, or a strategic reset, or a bold experiment botched in execution. It is what happens when a working maker gets handed to people who have never made anything, who run it the way I used to run other people's corn: abstracted, at a distance, on a screen, without once asking what the thing was for.

[BBC News](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c6je85jzk9y7o) reports what the accountants can see. Quarterly revenues of $11bn, undershooting expectations. Shares down more than 8% in extended trading. China revenue down 26%. A 20-year partnership with Kylian Mbappé ended, with the footballer walking to On. Lamine Yamal gone to Adidas. Ejection from the S&P 100 last month. A $2.5bn cost-savings target by 2031, with job losses attached. And a share price that has fallen roughly 75% over five years. That is the substrate. Now let me say what the accountants cannot.

The BBC piece's own reading deserves its due, and here it is honestly: the rot was real. John Donahoe, the former eBay boss who ran Nike for four years, turned a maker into a digital mall. He killed the wholesale partnerships that put real shoes in real stores where real people touched them. He starved research and development — starved the actual craft, the innovation that made the Swoosh worth believing in — while managing the brand like a marketplace listing. Veteran analyst Matt Powell is right that Nike over-produced Jordan editions, diluted its own scarcity, and forgot that scarcity was never the point; cultural devotion was the point, and scarcity was only its shadow. There is a serious argument that Elliott Hill's "Sport Offense" — throttling Jordan volume, cutting $2.5bn by 2031, admitting through his CFO that results are "below both our expectations and our potential" — is a firm punishing itself for sins it actually committed. I will not sneer at that. A maker that stops making well deserves to lose its market.

But the market is not the only thing that was lost, and the cure matters as much as the diagnosis. The people who made those shoes did not lose their jobs because Donahoe mispriced a share. They lost them because a corporation decided that the factory floor was an adjustable line in a model — that a supplier in a town three time zones away was a cost to be renegotiated, that a retail clerk who once put a kid in the right pair of shoes was a middleman to be deleted. I used to trade agricultural futures in Chicago, and the whole machine ran on one principle: never look at the field. Never see the man who grew the thing. That abstraction is not a bug of financialized management. It is the product. Donahoe did to Nike what the futures desk did to Adams County's grain — he made the brand tradeable, and everything that could not be traded on the screen got cut: the stores, the craft, the people.

And the people who got cut do not appear in the share-price story at all. There is no ticker for the contract workers in the factories that stitched the shoes — the ones whose livelihoods rise and fall on whether a brand decides to "reset its cost base," which is the polite term for moving production to wherever the wages are lowest and the compliance is thinnest. There is no ticker for the athletes at the bottom of the pipeline, the ones sponsored from age fourteen who are told they are family until the year the family decides the portfolio needs trimming. There is no ticker for the kid who saved forty dollars for a pair of Air Jordans the company has now decided to stop making in any quantity he could afford. That is the betrayal. Not a missed quarter. A movement that claimed to be about sport — real sport, played by real bodies in real towns — repurposed its whole apparatus to serve the quarterly return, and then called the wreckage a strategy. The share price fell 75% over five years, and the finance desk calls this the market "correctly pricing out the rot." I have been on that desk. I know what that sentence means, and what it leaves out. It means the market priced the company's value to the people who owned it — and the people who wore the shoes, sold the shoes, and made the shoes were never in the price at all.

So what would a different economics of sportswear look like? Not a nostalgia for the shoe of my boyhood — that shoe had its own sins, and the agrarian tradition I inherit is honest enough to name them where they lay, including in the parts of that tradition that wrapped itself in lost causes it did not deserve to borrow. But the distributist and cooperative tradition knows exactly what Nike lost, because it never had it: widely distributed ownership. The Rochdale Pioneers figured out in 1844 what Nike's board has spent five years rediscovering — that a firm owned by the people who work in it has a reason to keep the work here, keep the craft intact, and keep the promise made to the community that hosts it. Capper-Volstead gave American farmers that right in law in 1922 precisely because concentrated buyers kept treating them as a cost line. The Mondragon federation — seventy thousand worker-members in the Basque Country, a priest's idea in 1956 — proves it scales industrially. Organic Valley, run out of La Farge, Wisconsin, unites sixteen hundred family farms that would otherwise have been absorbed or gone under. These are not charities. They are firms that compete, and they compete without selling the town.

Applied to Nike, that means supplier factories where the workers own a share of the enterprise and a vote in its governance, so that "resetting the cost base" is a decision made by the people whose houses depend on the answer, not by an analyst three thousand miles away who has never seen the floor. It means reinvestment in grassroots leagues, school programs, and community sports where the brand earns its devotion by showing up — the way a co-op earns its members by being there at harvest, not by releasing a limited edition. It means treating the athlete's signature as a stewardship, held for the community that builds around it, rather than as extractable equity to be throttled and re-throttled until the scarcity machine squeaks out one more margin. It means, above all, a refusal to accept the premise underneath Donahoe's whole tenure: that the people who make, sell, and buy the product are inputs. The earth was given for all, and the goods that come from human labor answer to the same prior claim — the universal destination that stands over every title on every balance sheet ever filed.

None of this is a plan for Nike the corporation, and I am not foolish enough to pretend it is. Elliott Hill may genuinely be doing what a firm in Nike's position must do, and the S&P 100's verdict on the last five years was not entirely wrong. But a market that can only describe a broken promise as a mispriced asset is telling you something about itself, and it is not a compliment. The movement that once meant sport is now a line item, and the line item is being managed with real discipline toward an end that has nothing to do with any of the people who made it matter. The counter-model is not sentimental, and it is not new. It is old, and it is proven, and it is scattered across a hundred small towns that the finance desk has never priced: the cooperative, the mutual, the firm owned by the people who do the work. They know what Nike has forgotten. The name was never the asset. The people were the asset, and you cannot buy them back with a savings target. You can only stop selling them — and that decision, like every decision worth making, begins with somebody who still knows what the thing is for.
