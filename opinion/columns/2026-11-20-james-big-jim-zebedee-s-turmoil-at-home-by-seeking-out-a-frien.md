Trump is risking nuclear war and civilian deaths to secure his legacy.

Day seven of the administration's response to the outages that grounded flights, froze email systems, and delayed banking transactions, Trump flew home to Washington and, from Air Force One, announced a Friday afternoon press conference to tout his foreign-policy achievements. At the [Shenzhen summit](/articles/2026-11-19-trump-xi-and-putin-close-shenzhen-talks-without-nuclear-breakthrough/), Trump, Xi Jinping, and Vladimir Putin had agreed to continue discussing nuclear risks. The joint statement offered few details about next steps, and Putin offered no public concessions. The talks closed without the nuclear agreement Trump had sought. The press conference had a date. The nuclear agreement did not. That is the inventory.

Trump and his administration controlled the federal response, and on the seventh day it was still halting. A worker trying to reach a job could not count on flights or email functioning. A family trying to send a needed message could not get it out because email was frozen. A customer waiting on a banking transaction was left waiting by a system they did not control. These are not abstractions. They are the routes people use to get to work, send family messages, and move money. A country whose flights, email, and banking remain impaired does not become safer because its president receives a military flyover in Shenzhen.

Rep. Robert Garcia, the incoming House Oversight Committee chairman, issued document-preservation notices to Trump family members and administration members as Congress prepared for months of investigation. Those notices do not fix a frozen email system or release a delayed bank transaction. They do show Congress preparing to examine records kept by people at the center of power. The record matters. So do the outages.

The trip was staged as must-see television, and Friday's press conference was the set piece. Xi greeted Trump at the airport. Manila's marching band played “YMCA.” Shenzhen supplied the military flyover and an eight-piece ensemble playing “America the Beautiful,” while Trump held the head of the banquet table. At the Asia-Pacific leaders' banquet, he was the only world leader who stopped to pose for the cameras. His Truth Social video showed journalists jostling to enter the ornate room where he met Xi and Putin. There is no shame in a president receiving honors. There is shame in calling the ceremony the achievement. The flyover did not get a worker to work, unfreeze a family email, or clear a customer's transaction. Pageantry cannot make an arms-control agreement. A brass band cannot inspect a warhead. That does not pencil out as foreign policy.

He wanted a three-way deal that would bring Beijing into an arrangement aimed at curbing China's rapid nuclear buildup. He returned preparing to sell the meeting as a foreign-policy success. Continued discussion is not restraint. A promise to keep talking is not an obligation to reduce arsenals. The result lacked the pieces that change the outcome: written limits cap each side's arsenal; inspections test whether the cap is being kept; fixed dates say when the promises must be met; and a stated consequence puts a price on violation. Without them, the summit can hand him a historic claim while China's buildup remains outside effective restraint.

A nuclear exchange kills civilians in the blast and in the fallout. This is not a matter of wording. The flyover did not stop the violence, and the banquet did not restore the dead. Trump can take the bow after the summit. He cannot make a nuclear exchange leave civilians untouched. The human ledger was already open. Trump had struggled to end the pair of wars in Iran and Ukraine that had dogged his presidency, and civilians were dying in both while he staged a summit around a deal for his place in history.

Past arms controllers understood the hard part. SALT I, signed in 1972, set written limits and relied on each side's own satellite and other national monitoring rather than on-site inspections. SALT II developed inspections, and New START put inspections and fixed-term limits into force. The 1972 agreements and New START also had terms of years. They did not end rivalry. They made rivalry answer to rules. Shenzhen offered a paragraph and a procession. It was not an arms-control agreement. It was a televised conversation with military flyovers attached.

Thomas Schelling made the relevant point in *The Strategy of Conflict*, Chapter 3, “The Art of Commitment”: credible commitment often works by binding an actor before the bargain is complete, so backing out carries a cost. In *Arms and Influence*, Chapter 5, “The Art of Commitment,” he made the same point more directly: a government gives up some freedom to back out, or makes breach costly enough that the promise can be trusted. The Shenzhen statement did the opposite. It promised more conversation without requiring anyone to reduce a weapon, permit inspections, accept a date, or answer for a violation. A promise to keep talking binds nobody to disarm.

Bringing Beijing to the table was the thing previous presidents could not do, and Trump got that too. It is still not an agreement. A table without limits, inspections, or a date is a photograph with chairs.

After steep midterm losses, with no election of his own left and just over two years in office, Trump was chasing nuclear glory and foreign-policy breakthroughs that would secure his place in history. A president who needs a historic agreement before leaving office has reason to concede too much. A fixed deadline joined to a declared need for a historic agreement is not a bargaining strength. It is an incentive to concede. Xi and Putin can read his public appetite for one. Publicity magnifies the incentive because every staged meeting and Friday press conference becomes part of the price of failure. A rushed negotiator can mistake movement for settlement and pay for a photograph with a concession that cannot be taken back. That is how a president seeking a historic photo turns his own deadline into a security liability. Nuclear arsenals are not trophies, and summit photographs do not remove a single warhead.

The hunger for history did not stop with the empty communiqué. Trump floated another meeting with Kim Jong Un but left Asia without seeing him. He said he wanted Cuba “done” before he left office and repeated his promise to topple more than six decades of Communist rule. He called Putin “my friend” and received no public concession. A deadline to topple a government is not a diplomatic strategy. It is another search for an event to attach a victory to. A coherent strategy must set priorities, tradeoffs, and an endpoint. Each announcement puts a public demand for a dramatic result before the hard part, enforceable terms, has been settled. These were trophies on a calendar.

Any durable nuclear arms-control agreement has to come to the Senate as a treaty for its advice and consent under Article II, Section 2, and be ratified before it enters into force as a binding treaty. The terms, ways to verify compliance, duration, and consequences for violation must be made public before a president calls it a historic achievement. Congress keeps its oversight and appropriations power. The president can negotiate. He cannot supply the country's consent himself. A treaty the Senate has not approved and citizens cannot inspect is not an achievement. It is a press release.

Trump got the flyover, the music, the crowded room, and the video. At home, workers and customers were still dealing with the outages; in Iran and Ukraine, families were burying their dead. The cameras leave; the dead stay with their families.
