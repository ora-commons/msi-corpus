Donald Trump keeps taxing working families after the Supreme Court stopped his first program.

The administration’s strongest case begins with economics no serious trade lawyer ignores. Foreign subsidies can inflate manufacturing output, push more goods into American markets, and place subsidized products below the prices domestic firms must charge. The administration proposes a tariff—a tax on imports—to blunt that advantage. Its investigation covers China, the European Union, and 14 other economies rather than every trading partner. The statutory instrument is Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974, 19 U.S.C. § 2411 et seq. Section 301 authorizes USTR to investigate specified foreign trade practices, publish findings, and recommend a remedy before the President may impose a duty. The Constitution gives Congress power to regulate foreign commerce and impose duties: “The Congress shall have Power To lay and collect Taxes, Duties, Imposts and Excises.” A lawyer defending the administration can argue that the February judgment invalidated one program, not Section 301, and that the executive administers the border remedies Congress delegated through that statute. The economic case is serious. It does not establish that this administration may collect this tariff without making and using the findings Section 301 requires.

The legal record starts in February 2026. The Supreme Court held the broader April 2025 duties unconstitutional. The administration did not abandon its tariff policy. In July, the White House imposed [10% to 12.5% forced-labor levies on 60 trading partners](/articles/2026-07-23-trump-imposes-10-12-5-tariffs-on-60-nations-citing-forced-labor/), arguing that those countries had inadequately enforced bans on goods produced by forced labor. Those duties were separate from the subsidy investigation now underway. Bloomberg and The Wall Street Journal reported that the administration would announce subsidy tariffs on China, the European Union, and 14 other economies. The Associated Press, citing two people familiar with the rollout, said the schedule could still change. The White House and USTR did not respond to requests for comment.

The stated ground changed from forced-labor enforcement to foreign industrial support. The border tax did not. A remedy aimed at forced-labor goods does not, without more, authorize a levy on imports from 60 countries regardless of whether the particular goods involved forced labor. That is the question twenty-five states put to the courts when they [sued over the first replacement](/articles/2026-08-04-twenty-five-states-sue-trump-administration-over-forced-labor-tariffs/).

The subsidy tariffs carry the same gap into their announcement. USTR’s findings have not been published. Section 301 requires USTR to determine that the challenged foreign practice satisfies a statutory ground, including a violation of trade commitments or unjustifiable and discriminatory treatment burdening or restricting U.S. commerce, to recommend what action should remedy the violation, and to publish its report before the President acts. The President must then determine that the action is appropriate and necessary to remedy the violation. A foreign subsidy is not itself the statutory trigger. The levies themselves are already planned for 16 economies. The White House says foreign subsidies produce a flood of cheaper goods that undercuts American manufacturers. The planned duties are intended to answer that assertion. But findings and tariff announcement are expected in the same narrow window, and the legal foundation remains unproved.

Step back, because the pattern is the story and this instance is its newest page. Since February, the tariff strategy has carried three labels: the broad April 2025 duties the Court struck down, July’s forced-labor levies, and the subsidy tariffs now in motion. The instrument remains a percentage tax collected from importers. The administration changes the justification and the targets while preserving the power to collect.

Tariff law is not a presidential invention. Congress built the country’s first tariff schedules through revenue acts. The 1828 tariff helped push South Carolina toward nullification—its claim that it could refuse federal law it deemed unconstitutional. Congress later enacted the Smoot-Hawley Act of 1930. Delegation is real. The executive administers border remedies under statutes Congress has written. But delegation is Congress lending its power on the terms it wrote. The executive does not keep the power after each label falls.

*Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer*, 343 U.S. 579 (1952), supplies the governing boundary. Justice Jackson’s framework asks whether Congress authorized the President’s action; when the President acts against the express or implied will of Congress, his power “sinks to its lowest ebb.” A delegation can make presidential action lawful, but only within the statute that made the delegation. If the administration is using Section 301 as written, its steel-man remains strong. If it is using Section 301 to evade its limits or the February judgment, the President cannot borrow the statute’s words and ignore its conditions.

Two questions remain genuinely contested, and they should be named. First, whether the February judgment reaches a later levy invoking Section 301 rather than the authority invalidated in February. Second, whether Section 301 permits these countrywide percentage duties on this record without the findings, remedial fit, and procedures Congress prescribed. The relabeling has lived in exactly that open space.

A tariff is a tax collected at the border. The importer pays the government, and businesses and households commonly absorb part of that cost through prices. The administration’s account and the bill part ways there: the White House names the subsidy; importers and the households they supply pay the tax. Domestic manufacturers can recover market share. Buyers of affected goods, and businesses dependent on imported inputs, finance that recovery. The July duties took effect against a backdrop of elevated inflation and the affordability concerns the Associated Press tied to the midterm results. The administration calls this protection. The mechanism shifts income from importers and buyers toward the protected industry.

The replacement also widens the separation-of-powers fight. Congress now holds majorities in both chambers, but legislation overturning or watering down the tariffs still reaches Trump’s desk. A two-thirds vote in each chamber is required to override his veto. The institutional sequence is explicit: the Court invalidates the first program; the President substitutes another; Congress can try to alter it; the President can veto the alteration; the states are already litigating the replacement. No branch works alone, but the executive controls the instrument at each stage. The sequence describes one executive tariff strategy surviving judicial defeat by changing its ground and its targets.

An honest constitutional position would require the administration to publish USTR’s Section 301 findings, establish that the subsidy practices satisfy Section 301’s statutory predicates, tie each duty to the violation it remedies, use the process the statute requires, and keep the tariff within Congress’s limits. If the industrial policy needs authority existing statutes do not supply, Congress must grant it. Judicial defeat of one program is not a license for unilateral replacements.

The February judgment stopped the first tariff program. The second carries a new legal label, but the border tax still lands on working families. Trump changed the program. He did not change who pays.

Congress holds the power to tax. This year’s record shows the executive using it.
