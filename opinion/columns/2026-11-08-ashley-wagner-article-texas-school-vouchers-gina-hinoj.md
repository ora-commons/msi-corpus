Greg Abbott built a $1 billion voucher program that subsidizes private school tuition.

At my kitchen table, the first column is what comes in after taxes. The second starts with childcare for two children, before the mortgage, utilities, food, and debt. I am writing from a two-income household, which gives us options many families do not have; that privilege buys flexibility, not immunity. A voucher is not the same thing as an affordable school. In my house, a public subsidy can reduce one line. It cannot make the remaining private tuition, childcare, transportation, and hours of care disappear. The budget still has to balance.

The Texas Legislature appropriated $1 billion for a voucher program in 2025. ProPublica and *The Texas Tribune* reported that its 2026 launch served roughly 110,000 of more than 5 million Texas schoolchildren. Divide the appropriation by the participants and you get about $9,091 per child; divide the participants by the school-age population and you get about 2.2 percent. Those are scale checks, not a claim that every family received the average. They do tell us that private education receives public money while public schools lose enrollment and remain responsible for everyone else.

Parents should have real options, including when a public school has failed a child. Some private schools serve some children well. The strongest honest version of Abbott’s case is that families need an exit and that public schools can fail children. All of that is true. It is not an answer for what happens after the child exits.

An exit for one family can become a way of shifting costs to everyone else’s school. The voucher follows the child; the bill and civic obligation stay behind. This is not competition between schools. It is a public subsidy with an exit door, a subsidized exit from a shared system. The private operator receives public revenue; the public district remains responsible for every child left inside, including a child whose private placement does not work.

Anyone calling this “choice” has to produce the missing ledger: how much money each private school received, which public schools lost students, and what every district still carries. Until then, it is a demand for public trust without a balance sheet.

Some private school leaders were already counting on state funding to support their businesses and might lobby to grandfather in families already receiving vouchers. Scott Schneider, an attorney for participating private schools, told ProPublica and *The Texas Tribune*. Public revenue first; political protection if the public changes its mind. That is the concentrated beneficiary speaking plainly through its advocate. The concentrated benefit is public subsidy for private operators and families who leave; the diffuse burden remains with the public system and the children inside it.

By 2024, Abbott was angry at rural Republican legislators who had blocked vouchers because they argued public schools were vital to their communities. He spent millions trying to unseat them. Their replacements helped pass the $1 billion program the next year. That was more than an invitation to persuasion. It was campaign money used to manufacture a legislature, followed by taxpayer money used to finance private operators.

The 2026 returns supplied the next bill. In one race, Abbott’s campaign put at least $700,000 behind pro-voucher candidate Marc LaHood; LaHood lost to Zack Dunn. At least 13 Republicans Abbott had endorsed or financially assisted lost or trailed, according to *The Texas Tribune*. Its analysis found Abbott’s 2026 margin over Gina Hinojosa in rural counties was 16 percentage points narrower than his 2022 margin over Beto O’Rourke; in exurban counties, it was 20 points narrower. Dunn won 54 percent of the vote in his district.

Those numbers do not turn vouchers into the only issue in Hinojosa’s victory. Affordability, data centers, and other concerns contributed too, so this was not a one-issue verdict. But vouchers were a major contributing issue, especially where public-school funding had become part of the household-cost question. The [long-shot affordability campaign that carried Gina Hinojosa to victory](/articles/2026-11-04-hinojosa-s-texas-win-followed-a-long-shot-affordability-campaign/) turned the household ledger into the school ledger.

One University of Houston poll before the election found that two-thirds of Texans supported voucher legislation and as many believed vouchers would funnel money away from already-struggling public schools. Both things can be true because the program sells an escape and hides its bill. The contradiction is the program.

The House changed hands for the first time in 24 years, and Hinojosa won the governorship. She called the policy “the Texas voucher scam” and pledged to fire Commissioner Mike Morath, end Abbott’s public-school takeovers, and reverse the scheme. The Republican Senate majority survived, so repeal requires lawmakers, not a decree.

The new comptroller, Sarah Eckhardt, has promised an audit and greater disclosure: how much state funding private schools received and how many students public schools lost. Under the previous comptroller’s office, those two records were withheld on student-privacy grounds. That opacity is not privacy. It is the accounting being kept from the people paying it. An audit after the legislature put $1 billion behind the program is accountability downstream of the subsidy. The repair has to put the money and authority back into public schools.

Texas has not finished the experiment. Sen. Paul Bettencourt, who led the legislative push, said the state must review the first cohort’s outcomes. Some parents returned vouchers when their children required special-education services. Whatever else the first cohort proves, it does not prove that every child has a usable exit. The public system remains the place that must serve children after a private placement does not work out. That fallback is not an inconvenience surrounding “choice”; it is the safety net the program borrows while pretending the net is optional. Texas put $1 billion into the experiment before the first cohort’s results are known.

Anne Helen Petersen’s *Can’t Even* describes a generation taught to treat itself as human capital. Voucher policy demands that conversion at the kitchen table. A parent becomes a procurement officer: comparing curricula, vetting campuses, finding disability services, arranging transport, and watching what happens if the private plan fails. In the introduction, Petersen writes, “Burnout isn’t a personal problem.” Here, neither is the exhaustion. The state has made families perform functions the school system once performed together.

Taylor Swift’s “You’re On Your Own, Kid” is not about Texas, and it should not be forced to carry an argument it never made. Read here, though, its title line is the operating instruction of the voucher state: the family alone with a public obligation. A parent is told to choose a school while the state asks that parent to solve alone what public provision used to solve together—access, transportation, special education, staff, meals. The song is a parent speaking to a child; the policy speaks to the parent and leaves the child with the bill.

Pamela Druckerman’s *Bringing Up Bébé* is often mistaken for advice about better parenting. Its comparative-policy point is sharper: in the system she describes, French mothers are not subject to the same constant-service expectation because paid leave, subsidized crèches, and universal supports make care a shared arrangement. The point is not to turn France into a lifestyle. It is to expose the design. Texas asks parents to perform constant service, calls the failure a choice, and then sends them a coupon for the exit.

At St. Stanislaus, parish money and in-kind labor kept tuition within reach. My parents paid, and all three of us went through Catholic school and worked the fundraisers. We did not treat public schooling as a rival to be starved. The parish school was another path inside a common civic table; the public school was the table itself. Abbott’s program reverses the relationship: private school becomes the destination with the public check, while public school becomes the residual for every child with nowhere else to go.

Leo XIII’s 1891 *Rerum Novarum* tied wages to a family’s capacity to live and constrained private property by the common good. Read through that tradition, public education is one way a society keeps its promise to children who cannot buy a better deal. The Catholic working-class tradition I inherited understood the works of mercy through practice. When a neighbor’s child needed an education, the community shared the burden. The state now calls that burden a choice.

A genuinely pro-family agenda would pay the full child tax credit monthly, put federal money behind childcare and pre-K, fully fund IDEA, and restore Pell’s reach. Congress authorized federal funding equal to 40 percent of special-education expenditures in 1975; the Congressional Research Service reported a 12.7 percent federal share in 2021-22, a $23.92 billion annual shortfall. Sara Goldrick-Rab’s account of the federal financial-aid system notes that Pell covered roughly 80 percent of public four-year college costs when created in 1972 and about 25–30 percent now. Expand childcare subsidies enough that providers can pay teachers and families can work. Open the books. End the program. Put the money back into the public school every child can enter. Repair means public money returned to public schools, private tuition paid from private money, and no child assigned the cost of a policy failure.

At eleven at night, my spreadsheet still has the same two columns: what the month brings in and what it must pay. It does not care who won the election. It wants the money where the children are. Texas should stop subsidizing private tuition with public money. The last line should not be a voucher application. It should be a public school with its doors open, funded enough that no parent has to choose between a child’s classroom and the rest of the household bill.
