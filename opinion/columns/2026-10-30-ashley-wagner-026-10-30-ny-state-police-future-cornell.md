Cornell's campus cops had no business owning a felony case. Cornell had its chance. A student said seven members of the Chi Phi fraternity drugged her and raped her for hours in October 2024. Campus police were informed. Nobody was charged. And now the state has come to take the job away.

On Thursday, Gov. Kathy Hochul announced that [all future sexual offense complaints that would normally go to Cornell University Police will instead be referred to New York State Police](/articles/2026-10-30-hochul-sends-all-future-cornell-sexual-offense-complaints-to-state-police/), unless the survivor herself asks for different handling. The school signed the agreement voluntarily, which tells you exactly how badly it needed signing — a formal declaration of institutional failure, an Ivy League school telling the public, in effect, that its own campus police cannot be trusted with rape cases. Hochul said survivors "deserve to be heard, treated with compassion and know their allegations will be taken seriously." No press-release compassion changes that. The reason she had to say it is Jane Doe's case: a report of a drugged gang rape, campus police informed, and not one charge filed.

A campus police force exists to write up noise complaints and prop open broken dorm doors. It does not exist to investigate allegations that seven men drugged a woman and raped her for hours. Yet that is the case Cornell's own officers were handed in October 2024, and in more than a year they brought no charges against anyone. The woman, known in court documents as Jane Doe, left the school because of the trauma. The case became public in November 2024, but the fuller details did not surface until September, when she sued the university and her alleged attackers for compensatory and punitive damages — at which point the rest of us found out what the university had known all along. Cornell did not move quickly. It moved when it was cornered.

Here is where it lands on a kitchen table. This is the generation we are told to mortgage a house to send to college — the one we raise on the promise that the degree is the ladder, and that whatever else goes wrong, the institution on the other end of the check will function. I don't have a college line in my spreadsheet yet. I have a $2,400-a-month daycare line, and the arithmetic I run against it every month is the same arithmetic every family runs: what exactly am I buying, at this price, from an institution that has my kid for ten hours a day? The answer we are sold — for the daycare, and later for the tuition and the room and the board — is a baseline of competence. Someone competent answers. If the worst thing that can happen to your child happens to your child, a grown-up who investigates felonies for a living picks up the phone. Anne Helen Petersen's point about our cohort — that we were trained to understand ourselves as walking college resumes, as "human capital" — cuts both ways here, because the university read its own student the same way: not as a person owed an investigation, but as a liability item on a balance sheet. And Swift's title line, *you're on your own, kid*, is supposed to be the diagnosis of American care infrastructure, not the intake protocol at a university charging full freight.

Which is exactly how the case reached the public: through a lawsuit, because there was no investigation to follow. Swift's *no body, no crime* gets the mechanics of that right — the witness who can only tell it third-hand, through documents, because no one ever opened a case. Jane Doe had to sue to become a fact.

Now we are watching the choreography of a scandal. Hochul has already [appointed Attorney General Letitia James as special prosecutor](/articles/2026-10-01-hochul-names-letitia-james-special-prosecutor-in-2024-cornell-case/) to investigate the seven men, and former Deputy U.S. Attorney General Sally Yates is heading the review of Cornell's response, which the governor forced last month. The referral agreement is the third and most consequential move, because it removes the question of who investigates from Cornell's hands permanently, not just for this case. The Faculty Senate opened a no-confidence vote over the administration's handling of "the ongoing crisis of sexual assault," with ballots closing Nov. 6 — and Thursday's announcement landed one day after that vote opened. Senators are being asked whether they trust a leadership team that spent a year letting its own police department sit on the most serious allegation a university can receive. Hochul answered that question for them. Meanwhile the university has [banned fraternity parties for the academic year](/articles/2026-10-13-cornell-bans-fraternity-parties-for-academic-year-after-2024-rape-lawsuit/), as if the problem were the party calendar and not the conduct of seven accused men — a response that treats the symptom and skips the disease.

The deeper problem is structural. A campus police department answers to the university that employs it. When a student reports a sexual assault at an Ivy League school, the institution's reputation, endowment, alumni network, and Greek life are all in the room. The incentives are corrupted before the first question is asked. That is not how you investigate a crime. That is how you manage a liability. The New York State Police do not work for Cornell. They do not owe the fraternity system anything. That is the entire point of Thursday's transfer — and it is a damning verdict on the people who did owe Cornell's students something.

Here is the part that should embarrass everyone involved: the state did not need Cornell's permission to do this before. The agreement proves the transfer was always possible — the university simply never chose it while the case was live, and the governor never demanded it while charges were unavailable. A referral pipeline that exists only after the civil suit, the faculty revolt and a month of headlines is not a reform. It is an admission that the old arrangement was indefensible from the first day.

All necessary. All years too late. Every sexual offense complaint at Cornell now goes to investigators who handle felonies for a living, and that should have been the arrangement in October 2024. The transfer does nothing for Jane Doe — it is for the next survivor, not the one who had to sue to be believed. The seven men still face no criminal charges, Jane Doe still lives with what she alleges happened to her, and the university that was supposed to protect her is only now, under threat, giving the case to someone else. Cornell will survive this scandal. The next survivor may not. That is why the state had to take the cases away — and it is why the tuition check clears in the first place. Survivors were told their allegations would be taken seriously. This is the first week that was true.
