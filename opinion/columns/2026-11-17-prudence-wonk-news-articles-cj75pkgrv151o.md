Alan Milburn is handing the government a plan to rob sick young people. The proposed cut would extract support from disabled young people to police worklessness.

Ahead of the 241-page youth-unemployment review's publication, the [BBC's 17 November report](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cj75pkgrv151o) says Mr. Milburn would recommend ending the additional health-related payment for under-25s who cannot work because of illness, disability, or long-term illness. The review calls the condition of young people outside work, education, or training “both an economic and moral crisis.” Here is what the record says. Here are the numbers. The additional payment is £217.26 to £429.80 a month, on top of a Universal Credit standard allowance of £338.58 a month for under-25s. Universal Credit is the United Kingdom's main working-age benefit; NEET means not in employment, education, or training. Anyone who has read a benefits schedule knows what £338.58 a month is: about eleven pounds a day. The diagnosis and remedy point in opposite directions. The moral crisis is made to pay for itself by taking money from the people inside it.

The Department for Education's 2025 data show that about one in five NEETs has a mental-health condition, more than double the 2012 rate; 12% have learning difficulties, including autism; and roughly one in ten are university graduates. These are employment statistics. They are not a census of laziness. Disability makes employment harder. It is not evidence of an appetite for benefits. That is the population the reported recommendation reaches.

Set the arithmetic beside the testimony. Mr. Milburn's summer interim report records that 45% of NEETs reported having a disability; he called their situation “devastating.” That finding sits beside the reported recommendation, not against it. The review cannot cite disability to explain exclusion and then treat the excluded as if disability were irrelevant to what support they need. Nothing in the record shows that the population's health and disability data changed between the summer report and the winter recommendation. What changed is the proposal now on the table. The report and the record describe the people that recommendation would strip. For the young people who cannot work because of illness or disability, the recommended subtraction begins at £217.26 a month; the standard allowance is about eleven pounds a day.

A companion BBC report puts the number of young people out of work or education at roughly one in eight and records Mr. Milburn's line that young people “aren't snowflakes” and mental distress is rising. The intellectual laundering traces cleanly: attach moral judgment to a group, record the health and disability inside that group, then remove money from the group on the theory that moral improvement is the fastest route to employment. The document need not resolve whether every claimant is sincere. It only needs the moral frame. The moral frame is doing the work.

The four young people make the mechanism plain. Michael, 19, left college after social anxiety worsened; a Merseyside Youth Association placement gave him work experience at a supermarket. Maariyah, 22, a history and politics graduate, submits at least 20 tailored applications a week and has been rejected from unpaid heritage placements, including because she lacks the experience those placements were supposed to provide. Matthieu, 19 and autistic, wants factory, warehouse, or delivery work and says employers need to take time with him. Alex, 20, shares care of her mother, wants work with animals, and says businesses in her home town have “gone or moved or shut down.” The documentary evidence points one way.

Their evidence is not about character. It is about a hiring system that asks applicants to supply experience before granting them the means to obtain it. Michael put it plainly: “Everything is experience, experience, experience.” That is not a request for subsidy. It is a description of a circular gate. Jobs requiring experience exclude people who cannot acquire experience without work, and the system then calls the excluded “not working.”

Employers say higher National Insurance contributions—the payroll tax employers pay on wages—the minimum-wage increase, and energy bills make it unaffordable to accommodate disabled applicants. Some businesses say they are struggling to provide new jobs as a result. An employer's higher payroll cost does not become a claimant's burden merely because the employer reports it. An employer may decline to hire someone without training, demand experience the applicant cannot acquire, and leave the cost of adaptation where it already sits: on the young person and the public. The reported cut would complete the transfer by reducing the income available while the job search continues. That is cost externalization—moving the cost of adaptation from the firm choosing not to train to the applicant and the benefits system.

Michael's acknowledgment that some young people may be “taking advantage” cannot carry that policy. He says most use the system to get by until they can find work. Fraud can be prosecuted. Disability is not fraud. Combining them converts enforcement into a spending cut and leaves the labor market untouched.

The belief Alex hears most often is the oldest one in the file, and she quotes it: “Lots of people think, ‘well, young people just don't want to work.’” Nothing in the record attributes that belief to Mr. Milburn or to the review, and no such attribution is made here. What the record contains is the people the belief is about. The graduate sending twenty applications a week from her local library is in the record. The autistic nineteen-year-old volunteers at a gardening club and a computer repair shop while he waits for an employer with the patience to train him. The nineteen-year-old takes a five-in-the-morning work-experience shift to collect the experience every employer demands. Alex applies for “anything and everything.” A recommendation that strips income from the people in that record facing the greatest health barriers is not supported by it. It is arithmetic with a press release attached.

The same government has raised the cost of hiring for some employers at one end of the pipe; at the other, the review it is about to receive is reported to recommend cutting the income paid to young people whose health keeps them out of that market. The young are being squeezed from both ends, and the second squeeze is being sold as reform.

At the scoring desk where I spent my career, the first question about any cut was where the money comes from and where it goes. Where the savings from this recommendation would go is not established in the record, and no claim about their destination is made here. The interventions in this record that moved someone—the Merseyside mentoring that took Michael from a year in his bedroom to a five-in-the-morning shift, and the supported internship that gave Matthieu a year at the Whittington and a team to be part of—cost money and work. A cut does not create a vacancy. A funded placement can create a route to one. Parliament should preserve the health-related addition for any under-25 unable to work because of illness or disability, prosecute fraud claim by claim rather than against a generation, fund mentoring and supported placements at scale, and require employers to share the cost of training and accommodation. That is the expansion of youth-employment support [the TUC put to the government earlier this month](/articles/2026-11-09-tuc-chief-urges-uk-to-expand-youth-employment-support/). The record supports that program; the reported recommendation cuts the other way.

A recommendation is not yet a rule. This one can be refused. Enacted, it is a transfer from the young people with the least capacity to absorb it and the least power to object, and the welfare overhaul it sits inside calls that reform.

On the review's arithmetic, cutting a disabled 19-year-old's income creates no vacancy, no training place and no employer. It creates one more young person whom the state would make poorer and less able to compete for work. Milburn's 241-page ledger can call that discipline. It is extraction with a spreadsheet. Mr. Milburn warns of a “lost generation.” A lost generation is not rescued by taking its money. The recommendation is to make it cheaper to be devastated.
