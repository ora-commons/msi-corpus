Half a billion dollars changed hands this week on the promise of a machine that learns the trades of working people, and the report discloses no share, no stake, and no vote for the people whose work it learns. One of two economies sits in [The Wall Street Journal’s Logistics Report](https://www.wsj.com/logistics-report/electric-tugboats-get-l-a-audition-4168b93d): Perry Cleveland-Peck reports on the electric tugboats entering California’s ports, while Steven Rosenbush reports that barely two-year-old Genesis AI raised $500 million in a Series A at a $3.5 billion valuation. The money buys data acquisition and large-scale training; Genesis says its methods scale by “digitizing the work of large groups of people,” and chief executive Zhou Xian promises, “I think next year, sometime next year, we’ll have a robotics model that just works out of the box and generalizes across all different kinds of environments and tasks and skills.”

The other economy is made of steel, salt, and a battery. Curtin Maritime and the marine-technology startup Arc are developing eight battery-powered tugs; the first is in sea trials with the Coast Guard ahead of planned delivery later this month and would be the first fully electric tug at the Port of Los Angeles. Eight would be roughly a third of the fleet operating across Los Angeles and Long Beach. The boats are designed and built by Snow & Co., a Seattle shipyard part-owned by Curtin. Arc co-founder Ryan Cook says their operating costs could run 50 to 60 percent below conventional tugs, chiefly because fuel and maintenance expenses fall. Martin Curtin says he began developing them after growing frustrated with the cost and complexity of diesel engines built to meet California’s stringent emissions rules. We [followed the first hull into sea trials](https://www.msi.org/articles/2026-11-17-electric-tug-enters-coast-guard-sea-trials-ahead-of-port-of-los-angeles-delivery/) earlier this week.

Two economies on one page: the producer’s and the paper’s. The paper’s got the half billion. The producer’s has to put a hull in the water.

Give the port its due before anything else, because the port is where the actual boats are. No federal grant, panel, mandate, or virtue appears in the account. A Seattle shipbuilder and a marine-technology startup got the economics right: the cost of moving a containership into a berth in San Pedro or Long Beach falls by half or better, and the harbor comes out cleaner as a side effect. That is how an improvement is supposed to arrive—somebody notices a cost, somebody builds the better thing, and the price falls out the other side. Loaded container imports into the two ports fell 5.2 percent from September but stood 20.4 percent above a year earlier, so there is plainly work for the boats to do.

When the ports have reached for public money to solve their own diesel bills—their proposal of a $60-per-trip subsidy for zero-emission trucks—I do not need to be told that I distrust the instrument. The private route is the better route, and I will not pretend otherwise. The robotics bet deserves its strongest hearing too. A method that records skilled work can, in principle, carry scarce judgment to places where judgment runs short and lift dangerous, deadening repetition off people’s backs. No one should mourn a lung full of bunker exhaust. That is the strongest version of the case, and I concede it whole.

The quarrel is not with the machine. It is with who will hold what the machine learns.

Michael Polanyi put the old truth plainly: “We can know more than we can tell.” A workingman’s trade lives partly in what he can demonstrate and largely in what he cannot—the weight of a line coming taut, the set of a current under a hull, the judgment that says not yet. The recording takes the tellable part. The untellable part stays ashore in the men. Then the valuation is placed upon the tellable part, the tellable part is called the skill, and the skill is called redundant.

The old political economy had a name for what happens next: enclosure. A trade handed down from grandfather to father to son, from journeyman to apprentice, across a hundred years of hands, is a trust built by the dead, held by the living, and owed to the unborn. Edmund Burke called society “a partnership between those who are living, those who are dead, and those who are to be born.” When a firm records that inheritance and sells access to it, it is fencing the common field and charging a toll at the gate—income from owning access rather than making anything. Hilaire Belloc warned where that road runs: a class of men who own nothing but their wages, dependent forever on whoever owns the means. He called it the servile state. It is arriving by Series A.

I cannot claim to have made a number like $3.5 billion; the desk I worked made different numbers. I spent years in Chicago trading paper claims on grain I never grew—futures on corn, cattle, and beans raised by men exactly like the neighbors I had left. The whole architecture of that trade was the distance between the paper and the field. The men upstairs did not think much about the men in the field because they did not have to. I was the man upstairs; I can report the size of the thought. John Kenneth Galbraith had a name for the gain that feels weightless because the loss has not yet landed: “that magic interval when the embezzler is richer by the sum he has taken and the victim does not yet feel poorer.” Three and a half billion dollars for a two-year-old firm on a promise about next year is not yet an asset. It is the interval, priced and celebrated.

Now watch where the 50 to 60 percent lands. It lands first on whoever runs the biggest fleet, because the biggest fleet can spread the price of a battery across the most work. I could be wrong about what follows—if the electric fleet adds tugs instead of replacing owners, then more people have work, and I will be glad to have been wrong. But the direction of the money is not in doubt.

A diesel hull could be financed against the work and the note; a battery hull is a different order of purchase. Unless credit reaches the independent operator, the threshold moves from a man with three boats and a note on the wharf to a company with eight boats and a shipyard it partly owns. The cheapest boat in the harbor is not the end of the work. It may be the end of the small owner.

A tug is not an abstraction. A tug is a hull with a crew on it that takes a ship into a named berth, holds her there, and puts her back out; the men sleep in the port city; the owner’s name is on the transom; the customer is a mile down the road. To the independent operator, the tug business is the closest thing a waterfront has to the family farm. What the new propulsion does to that business is not merely to make it more efficient. It raises the price of the ticket. The savings do not arrive where the work is. They arrive where the fleet is.

One accountable owner is nearer the mark than an absentee fund. Curtin’s part ownership of the Seattle shipyard is property integrated down to the keel: a hull with a name on it, an owner with a slip to answer at. But the crew’s claim still waits. If an electric hull comes to want different hands than a diesel—and it may—those are hands that took lifetimes to train. A motive is not a man. Watch the crew ledger.

The crews have not been idle where the law lets them be more than equipment. In October, a [watchmen union local at the Southern California ports authorized a strike](https://www.msi.org/articles/2026-10-28-watchmen-union-local-authorizes-strike-at-southern-california-ports/)—the oldest lawful counterweight there is, men pooling their standing against men pooling capital, the same instrument by which farmers once bargained with the grain elevator, though the charters differ. The Logistics Report does not tell us what lit that fuse, and no honest column will guess. But a capital market prepared to stake billions on skill detached from the people who hold it cannot complain when workers use lawful leverage. Hardware gets financed. Crews get managed.

The small-owner conservatism I grew up with—I am one, from the inside, God help me—used to say that the small owner is the spine of a free people, because the man who owns the boat and the man who crews it answer to each other and to nobody in New York or Chicago. I believe it. Much of the movement that calls itself conservative defended the paper—the fund, the valuation, the buyback—against every person whose work could be inventoried, and called it the free market. Earl Butz told the farmers to “Get big or get out.” My county obeyed until the working families that made it were gone.

Mellencamp put a scarecrow in a field for this, and he was not being nostalgic; he was reading a bill. Wisconsin’s dairy farms fell from 16,886 in 2002 to about 6,216 in 2022, while the average herd rose from 55.6 cows in 1997 to 203.4 in 2022. Heartland Farms works something near 27,000 acres across five counties, about 9,000 acres of potatoes a year for Frito-Lay, on roughly 100 center pivots. The rail spine snapped in the other direction: on May 19, 1932, the railroad cut thirty-nine men from the Adams railyards and left twenty-seven; the last Twin Cities 400 ran on July 23, 1963; diesel came, the line was single-tracked, and Union Pacific took over the C&NW in 1995. The parish founded in 1884 was consolidated with the rest. The VFW post emptied. Nobody digitized those men’s hands. That was the only mercy they got: the knowledge went into the ground with them, unsold. The harbor’s men will not be granted that mercy by default.

By 2017, Adams County’s labor-force participation was 48.6 percent, the lowest in Wisconsin. The county’s best available future turned out to be a golf course on the sand and somebody else’s second house on the lake. Berry called that work “solving for pattern.” Can a place feed the people in it? We could not, so we became a place other people come to. I have lost most of that fight. What I have not lost is my eye for the arithmetic when it changes costumes.

Conserve what, exactly?

Meanwhile, the same newsletter records that October import prices rose 1.1 percent from September, a third consecutive monthly increase; import fuel prices rose 3.6 percent, with petroleum up 3.7 percent and natural gas up 5.1 percent, while nonfuel imports rose 0.8 percent. The Bureau of Labor Statistics could not provide a year-over-year reading because it had not collected October’s survey data in 2025, the government shutdown having swallowed the month. The record on what things cost is one year short. The record on what we might build next year has no such gap: the new capital alone is one-seventh of Genesis’s valuation. Only one of those is a boat.

Before I name a remedy, there is another temptation I have to refuse. I have friends who want the harbor simply run by the city—a municipal tug fleet, a public option on a boat, a tariff and a clerk and a hearing in Sacramento. I say that without any wish to score a point, because I am fond of them; but I am certain of one thing, and I learned it inside the conservative tradition rather than against it: the cure for concentrated ownership is not concentrated ownership of a different flavor. A working boat does not belong to the harbor. The moment the tug is owned by a port authority, the port has lost the one thing it was holding: a business a man in the city could join, buy into, and vote out if it went bad. Concentrated capital and a concentrated government are the same disease in two coats, and I will not cure a company with a commission. I have no quarrel with the state that builds the road. I have a quarrel with the state that takes the work away from the town.

An argument that has spent thirty years defending the dignity of workers has been curiously quiet about the dignity of owners. Ownership is not an absolute—the earth was given for all—but a man with no stake in the boat he runs is a wage-slave with a pension, and that is the servile state in miniature.

The answer to the machine is not a bureau; it is a charter.

So here is what gets built, and it is not a plan out of Washington. It is the oldest instrument in this county: members who own the means, the risk, and the vote. The co-op I manage in this county is exactly that—member-owned, one member one vote, and the answer to any question about what is fair comes from the membership rather than a committee.

The independent tug companies would buy their hulls together, pool maintenance and training, borrow against the fleet, and take an equity stake in the yard where they can. A credit union in the harbor town would lend against a boat. The member-owned electric cooperative in this county, Adams-Columbia, serves roughly 31,560 member-owners across twelve counties from Friendship; when the Rural Electrification Administration came along, some 350 cooperative projects in 45 states were serving about 1.5 million farms by the end of 1938. Members buy the boats. The cooperative maintains them, trains the crews, and holds part of the yard. The boat keeps the name of the member whose money is in it. One member, one vote, one boat, and a vote in the harbor’s business. Practical knowledge is not the kind of thing an office can buy, and the man who knows the harbor by working it is the man who ought to own the hull.

Whatever work a trade records, the trade’s cooperative should hold: the old Rochdale form carried down to whatever work the recording touches. Members, not shareholders; one member, one vote; the surplus going to the people whose work made the training possible; a royalty on the model flowing back to the hands that taught it; a member seat on the board deciding what the model may be sold to do; and patient local capital in credit unions and cooperative lenders capitalized to finance it. Call it what it is once it has members, rules, and a board that can be voted out: a commons of skill, governed. Mondragon’s worker-owned firms prove that democratic ownership can run heavy industry at scale: 70,085 workers, €11.213 billion in sales in 2024, and an average pay ratio near five to one. It centralizes nothing. It asks no permission from either coast.

It is harder here than it was in the country, and I will not pretend otherwise. Capper-Volstead gave farmers a limited antitrust exemption for their marketing cooperatives in 1922; it does not answer the harbor’s legal problems. The lawyers who would write those papers would spend their time in Washington instead of on the water. A battery is not a used asset with a used-asset market behind it. Nobody yet knows what a pack is worth the second time around, and the lender who used to take a note on a tug wants something he can resell. The credit union has to exist before the loan does, and there are harbors where it does not exist and never has. Worse, a model built to generalize across all kinds of work has no single dock to stand on. That is precisely why the ownership must be chartered before the recording begins, not sued over afterward.

Nor will I tell you the form never fails. Beatrice Potter saw the danger in 1891: a cooperative can drift from member control into managerial oligarchy. A 2022 review of 83 enterprises found that 63.5 percent either resisted that degeneration or regenerated, while only 9.5 percent fully degenerated. REI, still a member-owned store, lost $156.4 million on $3.53 billion in 2024 sales. Mondragon is proof, not a guarantee; the REA is proof, not an immortality charm. The instrument is not certain. It is the only instrument I know that lets a man keep his name on his own boat.

Members buying hulls together. A credit union lending against the boat. A yard partly theirs. A crew trained by its own. A name on the transom that belongs to the port city, not to a quarterly report.

The first hull is already in sea trials. The machine is already learning. The only thing left to decide is whose name is on it, whose vote governs the fleet, and who owns the skill.

A harbor can survive a battery. It cannot survive being worked by men who own nothing.

The machine will learn the motion. It will not learn the men.

Leave the harbor its hands.
