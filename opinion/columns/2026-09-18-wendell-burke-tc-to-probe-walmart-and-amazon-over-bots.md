The man who bolts a final component onto a washing machine in a plant somewhere in Ohio or Wisconsin has done his job honestly. The product carries a label, the label names a country, and the country owns the claim. He did not choose the labyrinthine legal standard behind that label — the FTC's own "all or virtually all" threshold, litigated, amended, and re-litigated across decades — and he has no say in what comes next. What comes next is two senators, one from each party, asking the Federal Trade Commission to investigate how Amazon's and Walmart's AI shopping assistants answer when a customer types a simple question: *Is this American?*

The complaint — dispatched by Sens. Tammy Baldwin (D., Wis.) and Rick Scott (R., Fla.) to FTC Chairman Andrew N. Ferguson and Commissioner Mark R. Meador — says the bots may "obscure and suppress" information about which products genuinely qualify as American-made and which do not. [As Main Street Independent documented when the letter first surfaced](/articles/2026-09-17-baldwin-scott-ask-ftc-to-investigate-amazon-walmart-chatbots/), the senators want an inquiry. But the charge is not that the chatbots are lying. It is that they are being precise about a question the law itself cannot answer cleanly — and that precision is politically inconvenient.

Here is what is actually happening. Walmart's and Amazon's shopping assistants do not suppress provenance. They surface it. They ask clarifying questions, qualify assertions, and decline to certify claims the underlying product pages do not support. The law says a product is "Made in USA" when it is "all or virtually all" domestic — a standard the FTC itself has spent years trying to operationalize because Congress could not be bothered to define it clearly. A chatbot that navigates this tangle with care is, in a real sense, doing the honest work that the legislative branch abandoned.

What Baldwin and Scott are asking the FTC to do is not enforce a labeling standard. They are asking the agency to compel these bots to declare a toaster patriotic when its heating element ships from one country, its housing from another, and its circuit board from a third — because manufacturers in their states would benefit from a regulator-imposed halo on goods that cannot win the argument on the merits in the marketplace. That is not consumer protection. It is a sectoral subsidy laundered through an enforcement action, the rentier's ancient trick: when you cannot compete, capture the referee.

The bipartisan packaging does not change the substance. Both senators represent states with domestic producers who want federal authority to do what advertising cannot. [A growing list of lawmakers](/articles/2026-09-14-dozens-of-lawmakers-push-new-ai-regulation-proposals/) is pressing for AI guardrails across a range of issues — [some genuine, some self-serving](/articles/2026-09-17-wiles-bessent-press-trump-on-ai-guardrails-as-tech-ceos-resist-oversight/) — and the Baldwin-Scott letter rides that current toward its own particular harbor. The letters arrive from across the aisle because the subsidy arrives from across the aisle. The same week, the SEC cleared a path for tokenized U.S. equities and Mastercard, through its Alchemy partnership, rolled out an agentic payment system that issues a virtual credit card to a bot with hard spending caps and product-category restrictions. The real economy of automated commerce is rearranging itself around these senators, and they have chosen this moment to insist that a chatbot's careful answer to an impossible question requires federal investigation.

The honest answer — the one a Catholic communitarian owes to the worker who actually makes things — is that this is not about the bots. It is about a Congress that has outsourced its own lawmaking to agencies, and agencies that have turned those mandates into labyrinths, and now everyone downstream — the manufacturer, the retailer, the AI, the shopper — is punished for the original failure. The man on the plant floor deserves a clear law, not a regulator policing the speech of a machine that is already trying to tell the truth. He deserves a legislature willing to sit down and define what "Made in USA" means in 2026, when a domestic final assembly counts even when every internal component is foreign, and then to hold that definition steady long enough for businesses and consumers alike to trust it. He deserves the ancient principle that law should bind the powerful and not the instruments of their speech — that Congress write the rule, and the bot obey it.

What he will get, instead, is an FTC inquiry into an AI's tone of voice on a question Congress itself refuses to answer clearly. Ferguson and Meador should return the letter with a polite note: the agency already regulates labels; the senators are welcome to draft a bill. The machines do not need a chaperone. The congressmen need a desk, a calendar, and the willingness to do the work they were sent there to do — the quiet, unglamorous, constituent-serving labor of legislating instead of grandstanding on someone else's algorithm.
