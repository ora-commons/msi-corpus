The premise of the defenders fails on its own terms. Raising revenue for homelessness programs is a legitimate public purpose, and a city cannot conjure that money from nothing. But a tax that destroys the base it is levied on does not raise the revenue — it destroys the future revenue. A tax that subtracts 4% to 5% off the top of a building's sale price is not a rounding error. It is a subtraction of the entire profit margin on a typical project. If the goal were genuinely to reduce homelessness, the fix would target the cost of building, not add to it. A land value tax, with improvements untaxed, would make it more expensive to sit on an empty lot than to build ten units of housing on it. That is the reform that would actually produce homes. The fact that the city instead chose a transaction tax that punishes the highest-value additions to the housing stock tells you everything about whether the goal is housing or headlines.
