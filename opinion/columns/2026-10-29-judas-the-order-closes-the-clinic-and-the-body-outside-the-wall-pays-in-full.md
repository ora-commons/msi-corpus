Hadley Arkes opens ["Transgenderism and the Democrats"](https://www.wsj.com/opinion/transgenderism-and-the-democrats-ae859a13?mod=rss_opinion) in *The Wall Street Journal* with the tar-baby parable and the political observation that the transgender question has become the thing the Democratic Party cannot put down: a super PAC's ad told voters "Kamala is for they/them," swung the race by his estimate some 2.7 points, and the party's answer to every subsequent effort to scale the practice back has been a unified nay. Arkes's claim, stripped to its spine, is not political at all. It is the prior question: before a legislature may act on the human person, someone must say what the human person is — and that answer is not enacted, it is discovered.

The natural-law argument is at its ablest when stated plainly. Sex is not *assigned* at a delivery room; it is found there, in chromosomes, in gonads, in the differences that reach down to the cellular level and do not vary with opinion. A law that funds the contrary proposition has not regulated medicine; it has purchased a metaphysics with public money, and a polity has no more competence to enact a new account of the human species than it has to enact a new law of thermodynamics. The state may reasonably decline to buy what reason shows it to be false. That is not hostility to a class of persons. It is the ordinary limit on what legislation can do.

The constitutional apparatus carries the same conclusion by a second road. Title IX says *sex*; the word was written in 1972 without reference to anyone's declaration, and a statute is given the meaning its adopters bore. RFRA and the Free Exercise Clause protect the physician who will not prescribe across the biological grain and the teacher who will not certify a school record contrary to the facts. The ministerial exception holds that the government may not choose who ministers. *Dobbs* asked, through *Glucksberg*, whether an asserted right is "deeply rooted in this Nation's history and tradition" — and no such tradition of recognizing self-declared sex exists to be found. Where the state goes further and requires assent — compelled speech under the First Amendment — the Establishment Clause supplies the mirror objection: a government that commands profession of a creed about the human person has made a religious claim it is forbidden to make. Alongside all of it stands the natural right of the parent, the right Arkes's own James Wilson Institute was built to defend, to be the first authority over the child the household actually contains.

The order's own record is narrow and lawful. The administration defined sex as binary across federal policy; passports now record the holder's actual sex; public money no longer purchases surgical alteration; *Mirabelli v. Bonta* enjoined the California practice of withholding from parents what their own children were doing in the schools they fund. The House divided 215 to 201 against federal reimbursement for the same procedures on minors. Nothing here asks the state to despise anyone. It asks only that the state stop asserting, at the public's expense, a proposition about the human person that no legislature holds the power to enact.

While the House of Representatives divided 215 to 201 against federal reimbursement, the state medical board's notice went out to every clinic in the commonwealth, and the gender program at Ridgeline Adolescent Medicine in Roanoke closed on the fourth of the month.

Eli R. was seventeen. He had been receiving testosterone cypionate, 50 milligrams by intramuscular injection every other week, since he was fifteen, written by the same physician at Ridgeline for thirty-one consecutive refills and paid by his mother's Medicaid plan. When the program closed, the clinic's letter gave him ninety days to find another provider in-state. There was not one. The nearest physician willing to write the prescription was four hours east across a state line, in a jurisdiction his mother's plan did not cover and their 2009 Corolla could not reliably reach in February. The nurse practitioner telephoned twice to say she was sorry, which she was, and to say she could not help him, which she also could not. The prescription was for a Schedule III controlled substance. Without a prescriber there was no lawful route to the drug at all, and a seventeen-year-old with no license, no income, and a coverage card that had stopped paying had no other route.

The injections stopped on the nineteenth. By the end of the first week his body began to reverse the work of two years: menstruation returned for the first time since he was sixteen, and with it cramping that folded him over the bathroom sink at six in the morning before school. Fat re-deposited across his hips and thighs until his jeans no longer buttoned. Night sweats soaked the sheets four nights out of seven; his mother washed them and he slept on the bare mattress. Acne returned along his jaw and then the growth stopped where it was, so that the mirror each morning delivered two faces that did not agree — the voice he had kept, and the face that had begun to go back. His joints ached when he stood up. Sleep broke into four-hour pieces, then two, then not at all.

He lost nineteen pounds in thirty-four days. He stopped returning his mother's texts on the fifth day and stopped opening the door on the eleventh. On the twenty-third day his mother came home from her shift at the plant and found the bedroom door locked from the inside and the house silent, and she called his name through it four times and then broke it in with her shoulder.

The deputy medical examiner recorded the time of death as between 9:40 p.m. and 1:15 a.m., the manner as suicide, the cause as asphyxia. The body was on the floor beside the unmade bed. He weighed a hundred and nineteen pounds. The last chart entry in his file, written by Ridgeline the week the program closed and forwarded to his mother with the discharge packet, read: *acute dysphoria, situational; patient counseled; follow-up as needed.*

He is not the only chart with a closing line. More than twenty states have now barred their Medicaid programs and state plans from paying for the same prescription, the federal reimbursement bar writes the same exclusion into every one of them, and in each jurisdiction the state agency terminates the coverage, the pharmacy clears the formulary, the clinic closes the program, the board disciplines the prescriber, and the coroner fills the box. Every hand performed the function assigned to it. The order is not being enforced imperfectly; it is being kept. Eli R. lay on the floor of a locked bedroom at a hundred and nineteen pounds, and after the fourth of the month no line in the state's records bore his name.

"Have you not read that he who created them from the beginning made them male and female?" The question is put to men quarreling over divorce; rightly understood it settles the prior quarrel — what a human being is — before it reaches anything a human being may do. Paul asks the potter's question and answers it himself: "Has the potter no right over the clay, to make out of the same lump one vessel for honorable use and another for dishonor?" The lump does not choose its form. The vessel that presents itself at the household's table under a name the potter did not give it has arrived at a house it does not belong to.

Eli R. did not come to the order as a suppliant asking charity. He came carrying a claim — that the purse pay for the injections, that the school record carry the designation, that the household adjust its account of the human species to accommodate his. That is precisely the claim Arkes identified, and it is the mark of his standing: a man does not knock at the covenantal door and ask to be received on the order's terms; he demands the door be recut to his measure. A guest may be admitted at the door as the household stands. He may not be admitted on terms that dissolve the door.

Beyond that door there is no claim upon the household's goods — no claim upon its purse, its physicians, its records, or the names it keeps. The body on the bedroom floor is the covenant's own mark of where the wall stands — the boundary written where every covenant writes its marks, which is on the body. The apparatus did what it was built to do: the vote, the reimbursement bar, the formulary, the board notice, the executive order declaring sex binary — each is a course in a wall, and the wall held. The order reads him with perfect accuracy — a body sorted outside the blessing, its appetite for what the household would not give left to settle on its own terms. The ledger closes where ledgers close: on the standing of the one it records.
