The Trump administration turned transgender civil rights into a ballot weapon.

In Idaho, a person who uses a restroom that does not match the sex they were assigned at birth can be charged with a misdemeanor carrying up to a year in prison. A second offense is a felony carrying up to five. Five years, for a door. Kansas passed its own. Yet the country did not stir the way it stirred in 2016, when North Carolina's House Bill 2 made the same daily act illegal. Almost two hundred corporate leaders demanded repeal. The NCAA pulled seven March Madness games out of the state. The attorney general of the United States sued to block the statute and told the country the law resembled Jim Crow.

The lesson of that first round was not that the government ought to protect people. The lesson was that this works.

Shelby Chestnut, the executive director of the Transgender Law Center, remembers turning on the news that day. Then-Attorney General Loretta Lynch had announced the federal lawsuit against HB2 and addressed transgender Americans directly: the Justice Department and the Obama administration saw them, stood with them, and would do everything they could to protect them going forward. Chestnut's conclusion was that this was the next phase of protections for trans people.

He was wrong. It was the next phase of something else.

Ten years later the direction had reversed. In 2026, with a wave of Democratic candidates sweeping battleground states, ballot initiatives barring transgender people from school sports passed in Washington and Colorado — states where the loss of such a measure should have cost somebody the election. The machinery did not need a frightened majority. It needed an election-night advantage, and it had one.

Donald Trump was its most visible carrier, but the machine was not built out of one office and one interest. It was built out of a post-2022 strategy study that measured the issue's electoral yield, a legal advocacy organization that supplied money and organization, a presidential campaign that made the question unavoidable, state legislatures that turned the frame into law, and a federal government that rewrote the definition of sex and then rewired the machinery of the state around it.

Start with the ledger. President Trump and executive agencies authored the federal rules. State officials adopted and extended them. Candidates gained an issue that could be carried from statehouse to ballot box. Legal and advocacy organizations — on both sides of the docket — gained a field in which their work could be translated into governing policy. Transgender patients, children, schools, hospitals, and public institutions bore the costs. The sequence did not require one office to control every stage. It required the White House to set the federal frame, agencies to convert that frame into money and documents, elected officials to carry it into law, and political actors to reward the votes that kept it alive.

What followed was a four-stage sequence visible in the record: definition, record, money, ballot. The order matters because each stage made the next one easier to execute.

Stage one is the definition. On his first day back in office, President Trump signed the executive order *Defending Women from Gender Ideology Extremism and Restoring Biological Truth to the Federal Government*. It redefined the federal government's operative understanding of sex as the genetic makeup of a person at conception. Most medical experts say that account is not scientifically accurate: fetal sexual characteristics take weeks to develop and are not always cleanly binary.

But the order's operative work did not depend on settling that scientific question. Elana Redfield, the federal policy expert at the Williams Institute at UCLA, described its effect: under the order, it became federal policy not to recognize the existence of transgender people, nonbinary people, or intersex people. The order answered a known legal ambiguity with a categorical rule and gave the executive branch one governing premise to apply at once.

The language did part of the work. "Biological truth" replaced sex as the operative term. "Restoring" presumed a homecoming rather than an eviction. One of the 2024 campaign's most-aired political ads in the final days before the election ran the contrast: "Kamala is for They/Them. Trump is for You." The advertisement made the division between pronouns and identities the campaign's simplest frame. The order made it a rule of federal recognition. Definition, campaign language, and administrative command did not need to originate in the same office to form a method. They needed only to arrive in the right order — and Marie-Amélie George, the Wake Forest legal historian whose work concerns the history of LGBTQ+ legal rights, explains why the second term mattered more than the first: "if you do all of these things at once, it's harder for the other side to respond."

Stage two is the record. The same order reached three federal systems on its first day: data collection, federal funding, and identity documents. Federal statistical gathering concerning transgender, nonbinary, and intersex people ended abruptly — information often used to figure out where federal programs and funds are needed. Federal grant funding was cut off to projects determined to even acknowledge the existence of transgender people. The State Department was instructed to stop issuing passports whose marker did not match the sex at birth, and to stop issuing the X marker for intersex and nonbinary people.

Take away the marker and you do not take away the person. You take away the document that lets the person exist in the ordinary machinery of the state: the passport, the statistical record, the grant application, the federal funding decision. You have made a population administratively invisible to programs while leaving it perfectly legible to anyone with the authority to punish it.

Stage three is the money, and this is where the machinery leaves the building. Within a month of the January order, the administration directed Health and Human Services to end gender-affirming care for minors nationwide by cutting federal funding to institutions providing that care, and directed Medicare and Medicaid not to cover the costs.

The research apparatus came with it. The Justice Department has been [funding research into gender dysphoria and violent crime](/articles/2026-11-03-justice-department-funds-research-on-violence-and-gender-dysphoria/), and an HHS report has framed support for gender-affirming care as [‘left-wing authoritarianism’](/articles/2026-09-30-hhs-report-links-gender-affirming-care-support-to-left-wing-authoritarianism/). Meanwhile, the Education Department [finalized the repeal of Title IX protections for LGBTQ students](/articles/2026-09-28-education-department-finalizes-repeal-of-title-ix-lgbtq-protections/), and the February executive order *Keeping Men Out of Women's Sports* directed the department to prioritize Title IX investigations against states and organizations that permitted transgender athletes to compete in women's categories. When the civil-rights office was gutted through the administration's so-called "department of government efficiency," the handful of investigations that survived were pointed at institutions with trans-inclusive policies in sports and admissions, in lieu of inequities in women's sports or harassment and assault complaints. An agency aimed at the accusation it had decided to raise.

That sequence does not prove the researchers reached a predetermined result. It proves something narrower and still serious: the government did not wait for neutral inquiry before deciding what the government would recognize. The research is now commissioned inside a policy choice. Its questions, methods, funders, and publication belong in the accounting rather than being mistaken for an open scientific debate that began before anyone had decided what the government should recognize.

More than forty hospitals with gender clinics have shut down or paused services for minors, often out of fear of losing federal funding. Federal leverage did not compel every hospital to close, and individual institutions may have had additional reasons for their decisions. It did create a financial condition under which some could narrow or suspend services while preserving their federal position, and it accelerated particular closures. The benefit accrued to institutions able to convert legal uncertainty into administrative retreat. The cost landed on the patient who arrived for an appointment that no longer existed. In the cases where funding pressure drove the decision, the federal government bought certainty with somebody else's money and somebody else's body.

Stage four is the ballot. After the disappointing 2022 midterm results, the American Principles Project produced a postmortem on how candidates might have performed better. Its recommendations, in George's assessment, helped frame the evolution of the anti-trans playbook.

The finding was not only about theology, tradition, or culture. It was about the electorate. Voters across demographic lines tended to support restrictive positions on transgender issues, which told political strategists that the subject could carry votes rather than merely cost them.

Along with an influx of funding and organization from groups like Alliance Defending Freedom — a conservative Christian legal advocacy group that helped overturn *Roe v Wade* and is classified as an anti-LGBTQ+ hate group by the Southern Poverty Law Center — these political messages led to the rash of state laws restricting trans civil rights from sports, medical care, identity cards, and again, public bathrooms.

The strategy ran on shared vocabulary. The receipts show a coordinated message discipline: the systematic, organization-wide deployment of agreed-upon language, frames, and talking points across speakers and venues, such that an issue is presented uniformly. The American Principles Project, Alliance Defending Freedom, the 2024 campaign, the state legislatures, and the Trump administration deployed it across the same window: the published post-election recommendations set the strategy, Alliance Defending Freedom supplied money and organization, the campaign's most-aired advertisement declared "Kamala is for They/Them. Trump is for You," the state laws arrived on the same vocabulary of women, children, and safety, and the first-day executive order was titled "Defending Women from Gender Ideology Extremism and Restoring Biological Truth to the Federal Government." One vocabulary, repeated until it was atmosphere.

Some political leaders held sincere convictions about sex, medicine, sport, or the law. Conviction and utility did not cancel one another. Conviction supplied the language. Utility supplied the scale. The record proves the utility of the tactic; it does not require a claim about private motive. The strategy did not need the fear to be rational. It needed the fear to be repeatable.

Then the 2026 midterms delivered four state ballot initiatives barring transgender girls from school sports: Nebraska, Washington, Colorado, and Arizona. The point was not confined to one region. Washington requires medical sex verification for every girl athlete in the state. Arizona's measure reaches private sports clubs. That is what a portable method looks like after it has been stripped of its argument and reduced to its function.

The delivery system was saturation. Fox News spent at least 130 segments across three weeks of one summer on the possibility of transgender women playing in the WNBA — a league where no trans woman had ever played. Books that mention trans characters are routinely banned from schools and libraries. Disney erased a trans character's storyline from an animated Pixar series in 2024 before the show could even be released. The large sports federations changed their rules, from the NCAA to the International Olympic Committee, which will host the next Summer Olympics in Los Angeles in 2028. They did not need a transgender woman in the WNBA. They needed voters to believe one might arrive.

The measurable result is on the record: the Pew Research Center found that American support for laws and policies protecting transgender people from discrimination fell eight points between 2022 and 2025, with the decline visible across the political spectrum — even as the gap between partisan identifiers stayed stark, with 5 percent of Republicans favoring a requirement that health insurers cover gender-affirming care against 40 percent of Democrats. The method does not require a majority to be frightened. It requires an audience to be worn down.

The deepest feature is institutional nonrecognition. Before any particular claim, case, or harm had to be weighed, the federal government withdrew recognition from a category of persons. That moves the argument upstream, into the status of the people whose rights are being decided. Ask the Justice Department. In 2016 it went into federal court to block a statute because the statute resembled Jim Crow. In 2026 it funds research into whether a population it no longer recognizes can be connected to violent crime. Same office. Same population. Opposite posture. The difference is not a change in doctrine. It is a change in what the doctrine costs.

And look at what the method survived. In 2021, federal policy reversed course: the withdrawn guidance was restored, the prohibition on transgender service members was lifted, and Title IX protection was restored for transgender students. Yet by 2024, twenty-six states had barred transgender students from school sports and twenty-seven had restricted gender-affirming care for transgender youth, while the major medical associations continued to treat that care as best practice. The Supreme Court has since ruled in favor of the state bans on transgender athletes and on gender-affirming care, and those rulings may take years to disturb, if they ever are. This is the part that tells us where the engine actually sits. Not the White House or the states, as if one could replace the other. The White House authorizes the federal definition; agencies implement it through data, funding, documents, and enforcement; state officials adopt and extend it; legal organizations supply advocacy capacity and political networks; courts remain a corrective, sometimes arriving only after injury has spread. Distributed responsibility is not a contradiction of the pattern. It is the pattern.

Everything so far has been about the machine. The machine needs fuel, and the fuel was supplied from the other side of the ledger, so I will not pretend this story has only one villain in it.

In a year when candidates were gaining ground in battleground states, ballot initiatives targeting transgender people passed in Washington and Colorado. California Governor Gavin Newsom opposed allowing transgender children to participate in girls' sports. Iowa senator-elect Josh Turek did too, as did South Carolina senator-elect Annie Andrews and Texas senator-elect James Talarico. Their exclusions remain exclusions. They do not receive immunity because the president they opposed is Donald Trump. There is a state version of this strategy, a party version, and a reputation-saving version, and every politician who takes the exclusion is choosing it and owns it.

Those were not theological treatises. They were electoral choices. Their statements made the issue cheap for candidates who chose it; the shared electoral benefit explains the convergence without requiring a shared belief. In 2016, such positions carried immediate political costs. By 2026, the cost had fallen nearly to zero for the officials who embraced them. The reason was not moral courage. It was that the other side had made the statement politically free.

In his "Message to the Grass Roots" address in Detroit on November 10, 1963, Malcolm X separated the field from the house: the field bore the contradiction with power, while the house made accommodation with it. He was careful, and the care matters. The point was never a private verdict on anyone's character. It was a diagnosis of identification with the system doing the harm. Apply that distinction here without the slur and without softness. Candidates and campaign committees did not need to hate anybody. They only needed to work out which constituents were expensive to defend and which were cheap to trade, and then perform that arithmetic in the quietest voice available.

King's "Letter from Birmingham Jail," written on April 16, 1963, identified a different obstacle: the decent, order-loving moderate who grants you the principle and then sets another person's timetable for freedom. The hooded terrorist is not the only power that can stop a movement. The polite ones are often the load-bearing wall, and when they hold the vote, their timetable becomes the whole story.

There are exceptions in the record, and a rule that produces no exceptions is a slogan. Abdul El-Sayed won a Senate seat in Michigan. Troy Jackson won one in Maine. Both won in battleground states, and both kept their support for transgender rights. George, whose historical work I trust, warns against flattening the record: the states are a patchwork, states protecting transgender rights are disproportionately populous, and it is inaccurate to say everyone is moving in lockstep. Both things are true. The second does not rescue the first. And a patchwork is also what a national strategy looks like when state law becomes the delivery system: the costs are dispersed, the legal tests vary, and the pressure moves from one legislature to the next while the political model travels intact.

So take the ledger straight. Who wrote this? A president who fixed the federal definition in one afternoon. A Justice Department that funds research. A Health and Human Services Department that produces reports and cuts money. An Education Department whose surviving investigators have been aimed. A State Department that takes the marker off the passport. A think tank that studied the ballot and handed the method back to political actors who could use it.

Who benefits? The benefit runs uphill. Candidates who found a constituency that cost almost nothing to defend and paid at the polls. Officeholders who made the issue cheap and then claimed the votes. Political and legal advocacy organizations that gained funding, influence, and leverage in a governing order newly friendly to their positions. Media organizations that gained attention. Clinics that could narrow their service while preserving their federal position. And a political movement that discovered the cheapest transaction available in American politics: a promise to make a small population small.

Who pays? A population the Williams Institute places at roughly one to three percent of Americans. Children whose doctors are gone. Patients whose clinics closed. Prisoners whose medical care is governed by policy written for them. A public health system that can no longer count the people it is supposed to serve. And a coalition — Chestnut's word, and the right one — trained at the cost of ten years of litigation, boycott, hospital closure, and a federal lawsuit that won, capable of being withdrawn from the map as the price of holding a larger coalition.

The public framing says women, children, biological reality, fairness, and safety. The distributional record says political viability, administrative punishment, and the transfer of risk to institutions and people least able to refuse. Their gains are visible in electoral returns, organizational funding, legislative leverage, and airtime; what they spend is somebody else's life. Let the advocates make their case on the merits. The receipts show that the case was also an engine, and the engine accelerated whether or not the merits persuaded anyone.

The problem is wicked because no single actor controls the whole machinery. Political belief supplies candidates. Funding supplies organizations. Ballot access supplies durability. Media supplies repetition. Federal grants supply leverage over local institutions. Courts supply delay, correction, or both. Public opinion supplies permission. Remove any one piece and others can carry the attack forward. Repeal one executive order and the state laws remain. Defeat one ballot measure and the candidate keeps the issue. Win one lawsuit and hospitals have already closed their clinics. There is no stopping rule inside a tool whose engine is a group's disappearance from the record, and the template carries no sunset clause because its value is that the next hand can pick it up.

And here is the part I will not let pass, because it is the part the method depends on: the resistance produced information that the next round of political actors could use.

The 2016 boycott exposed which private institutions would pressure North Carolina. The federal lawsuit exposed a legal route and a claim that could be carried into court. The litigation groups built arguments that state officials could test and revise. The subsequent spread of sports, health-care, identity-document, and data restrictions shows that anti-trans policy became portable. The record supports a narrower conclusion: policy diffused across statehouses as the legal and electoral cost of resistance became visible. It does not require every state to have studied every tactic, and it does not establish one central strategy memorandum. The portability is enough. A restriction that can pass before courts settle it, survive litigation delay, produce an issue for campaigns, and then be copied in another state needs no single command center. It needs only officeholders who see that the price has fallen.

The information is public now. Mon Mothma's line in *Return of the Jedi* is the right one here: many Bothans died to bring us this information. The information has already been gathered. The question is what anybody spends it on. And the exhaust port is visible if you are willing to look at your own side. The method runs on permission. It does not need a majority of Americans to fear transgender people. It needs the major candidates, campaign organizations, and state officials to treat those people as an acceptable loss, and it needs the states where the model was copied to be governed by people who believe the copy is free. Make the cost of the copy visible to the candidates and institutions that benefit from it, in the states where the model was copied, to the people who copied it, and the engine loses fuel. That is not optimism. It is a fact about how the method works, and it is why the 2026 map still contains Washington, Colorado, and Arizona.

Chestnut believes the tool for what it is. He says transgender people may be the testing case for a broader attack: a restriction of bodily autonomy across immigration, health care, abortion, and voting rights. Read that sentence twice, because it is not only a prediction. It is a description of the method.

Malcolm X saw the common structure beneath separate antagonisms: "We have a common oppressor, a common exploiter, and a common discriminator" ("Message to the Grass Roots," Detroit, November 10, 1963). Here, the common oppressor is concentrated power, and the common method is to make one population visible enough to frighten and vulnerable enough to punish. Chestnut's warning is not a claim that every group shares one psychology. It is a warning about a portable machine: define a minority as dangerous, manufacture fear, convert fear into a ballot question, and use funding and legal risk to force private institutions to comply before the courts finish speaking. That is why this community cannot be asked to defend every attack alone. The defense has to be built where the common method is visible.

King put the same thought more bluntly in *Where Do We Go from Here* (1967) than his critics wanted: a legal foothold is a foothold, not a destination, and the structure that produces the harm does not dismantle itself because a statute changed. In "Beyond Vietnam: A Time to Break Silence," delivered at Riverside Church on April 4, 1967, King joined racism, extreme materialism, and militarism as linked pathologies rather than separable disputes. I render that for this hour in my own terms. The material for the deal is not an abstraction. It is a clinic on a hospital's fourth floor. It is a line on a passport application. It is a checkbox on a grant form. It is a case number in an agency instructed to look in one direction. The war on the person who is different is a war on the autonomy of every person who differs. The material and the moral are one war.

King's *Stride Toward Freedom* (1958) named reconciliation and the creation of the Beloved Community as the horizon of the struggle. He meant it operationally: a common life in which no person's legal existence is a favor to be granted by whoever is holding the pen.

So the demand is plain, and it is not a mood. We do not need a permission slip from the institutions that already broke this. We do not need a panic we did not ask for and cannot pay for. We do need the people who ran the 2016 boycott, won the 2016 lawsuit, and fought the 2026 ballot fights to say out loud, in their own names and their own districts, what the copy cost and whose body it was copied on. Name the mechanism. Price it. Make it expensive. Do it in the states where the model was copied, against the elected officials who copied it, where they can be asked about it by people who vote.

Expose the money. Defeat the ballot measures. Keep the clinics open. Force candidates who profited from the fear to answer at the ballot box. Build the coalition that makes that machinery expensive to run. Restore the right to live and participate without asking power for permission.

In his Organization of Afro-American Unity founding address at the Audubon Ballroom on June 28, 1964, Malcolm X said he wanted freedom, justice, and equality "by any means necessary." The phrase was a commitment to structural change, not authorization of physical violence. The means in this hour are the ones we have always had: the documents, the dockets, the ballot, the coalition, the organizing, and the refusal to enter a small population in the ledger as an acceptable loss. That is enough. It has always been enough. Nobody with a pen, a record, and a constituency has ever been short of a place to begin.

And the horizon, held without a guarantee: the arc does not bend because the law eventually catches up. It bends when people break the machinery at its joints — through votes, coalitions, records, lawsuits, and sustained civic courage. The test is not whether transgender people can be protected by one favorable ruling. The test is whether this country will stop turning a vulnerable population into a reusable ballot measure. Until the machinery breaks, the victories are not safety. They are reprieves.

It bends when particular people, in a particular state, on a particular Tuesday, decide that the transaction on offer — their approval, their silence, their ballot, their absence — will not be sold at that price. That is the long arc. It is not a sentiment. It is a schedule of appointments we keep or fail to keep.
