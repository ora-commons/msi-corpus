COP31’s consensus rule turned the fossil-fuel question into a veto over an overarching climate agreement.

In Peru, Pope Leo XIV had recently demanded justice for environmental defenders killed for protecting the Amazon. That demand keeps the Antalya failure in its proper scale. The dead are not scenery in a diplomatic dispute. They are the human face of an arrangement that can preserve profit by making somebody else absorb the danger.

On Wednesday in St. Peter’s Square, Leo said extreme environmental events linked to the climate crisis were becoming increasingly frequent. He appealed to “the international community and to each individual” for “fundamental changes in behavior, socioeconomic systems and the governance of our societies.” He told the world not to forget a crisis that affects millions of people, strikes entire communities, and undermines the prospects of future generations.

COP31 had gathered in Antalya, Turkey, to address that crisis. On Sunday, the talks ended without an overarching agreement. The immediate dispute was whether the text should mention “the fossil fuels that cause global warming.”

The account of that result is compact, but its passive construction is not neutral upholstery. Strip the procedural fog away and the failure stands plainly: a dispute over whether to name the cause of the harm became a dispute over whether the agreement could be adopted at all.

The question that explains the result is not why diplomacy failed. The question is *cui bono*—who profits from a governing text forbidden to name its own subject?

The answer follows the veto straight to the rent.

A treaty that cannot name fossil fuels cannot make the obligations attached to them legible. It cannot say plainly which production must decline, which subsidies must disappear, which extraction must stop, or which wealth must be surrendered. Delay becomes the agreement. The absence of an agreement becomes permission to continue as before.

Start with the true half, because the truth of it earns the standing to say the rest. Multilateral negotiation is genuinely hard. Consensus among sovereign states honors a real principle. It can prevent stronger actors from steamrolling weaker ones and forcing them into terms they had no power to resist. Agreement among delegations with different coastlines, grids, treasuries, and histories was never going to come cheap in the best of faiths. Diplomatic exhaustion is real. Disappointment is real.

That is not the defect.

The defect arises when a rule designed to protect vulnerable participants allows interests that benefit from delay to convert refusal into a veto over everyone else. A disagreement can be justified. A refusal may have legitimate grounds. But when unanimity gives one interested party the power to stop the entire text, the process is no longer merely protecting consensus. It is making delay an asset.

The first-order beneficiary of delay is continued fossil-fuel production. Every additional period of permissible extraction preserves demand and allows existing producers to postpone the costs of changing how energy is produced and consumed. The broader beneficiaries are the actors who can convert waiting into leverage: those who gain market position, investment certainty, or political power while agreement remains unreachable.

The costs land elsewhere.

Leo named millions of people affected, communities struck by extreme events, and future generations whose prospects are diminished before they arrive. The environmental defenders he honored in Peru belong in the same ledger. Current benefits are concentrated and immediate; the costs are dispersed and delayed. That asymmetry is why delay can be profitable. It is also why moral urgency alone cannot defeat it.

Not every person connected to fossil-fuel production has the same power or the same responsibility. The standard falls hardest on those who control the terms, block correction, and retain the gains. It does not make workers who lack those decision rights the authors of the rule. Distinguishing power from mere association is not a courtesy. It is accuracy.

The apparent problem was the failure to agree. The underlying cause was the interaction between the consensus rule and concentrated benefit from delay.

Consensus lets a refusal block the whole text while the cost of waiting falls on those with less power to impose a settlement. When an actor stands to gain from no agreement while the rest of the world bears the cost of waiting, refusal becomes leverage. No conspiracy is required. Separate actors can preserve the same arrangement without ever coordinating: each decision protects one piece of the benefit, and each refusal pushes the cost toward somebody less powerful.

That is why earlier MSI reporting on [the collapse of the talks in Turkey](/articles/2026-11-22-un-climate-talks-collapse-in-turkey-without-agreement-on-fossil-fuels/) and the subsequent [calls for consensus-rule reform](/articles/2026-11-23-cop31-ends-without-pact-drawing-calls-for-consensus-rule-reform/) identified the hinge rather than offering procedural housekeeping. A rule that can be used to preserve harm is not neutral procedure. It is a transfer of power from those exposed to the consequences to those who gain from postponing action.

This is a wicked problem in the technical sense. The stakes are planetary. The causal account is not scientifically ambiguous here: the dispute concerned whether to mention the fossil fuels that cause global warming. The uncertainty lies in the distribution of benefits and costs, in the competing claims of states and institutions, and in the absence of a stopping rule that makes everyone whole. No conference can produce moral unanimity by decree.

But a wicked problem is not a license for procedure to hide. The world does not resolve the conflict over who pays by making the name of the cause unavailable. Every participant may have reason to demand protection from an unfair settlement. That does not entitle the participants whose power and retained gains make delay valuable to convert the whole agreement into a hostage under the pretence of consensus.

Cardinal Pietro Parolin, Leo’s No. 2, told COP31 that the talks were not “merely technical” but concerned people’s lives. His sentence made the mismatch sharper. A process in which human consequences remain visible in the preamble while the causal term is blocked from the operative text has reduced lives to the preamble and power to the operative text.

Trace the mechanism one level deeper. The refusal to name the cause protected an arrangement that lets the price of fuel remain separate from the price of the damage. The costs did not disappear when they were kept off the ledger. They moved. They moved onto communities already exposed to extreme events, onto public health, onto harvests and coastlines, and onto the future generations the pope invoked.

Externalized cost is not an accounting curiosity. Under this arrangement, it is part of the return. The business can remain profitable because the damage lands on people who did not set the price and cannot send the bill.

That is why the language mattered. Every text that cannot name the fuels cannot touch the ledger by which their damage is counted. Every text that cannot touch that ledger cannot end the harm. “Fossil fuels” was not ornamental language. It was the route from abstraction to accountability.

The pope had already shown where the ledger’s earliest payments land. During his recent trip to Peru, he demanded justice for the environmental defenders killed for protecting the Amazon. They were people who stood between extraction and the forest and were killed for taking that stand.

I want that sentence to sit without ornament, because it needs none. Those defenders are the first line of the invoice, and the invoice is addressed to all of us. Justice for them is not charity. It is the minimum the arrangement owes the people it has been spending.

Malcolm X wrote in *The Autobiography of Malcolm X* (1965), “We didn’t land on Plymouth Rock; Plymouth Rock landed on us.” The warning is useful here because the fossil-fuel economy is not weather that happened to us. It is an arrangement assembled through choices about extraction, ownership, infrastructure, finance, law, and enforcement. To describe it as natural erases the authors. To describe it as inevitable denies that choices can be changed.

The beneficiaries do not have to share one secret plan. Systems can be maintained by separate decisions, each preserving a piece of the gain, and by rules that make delay more tolerable for the powerful than for everyone else. The more exact we become about responsibility, the less need there is for the comforting fog of “the system.”

Leo directed his appeal at “each individual,” and the same standard reaches the societies that consume what the fuels produce, subsidize the extraction, and tolerate the veto for the comfort it purchases. Seller, burner, voter, bystander—the network binds all of us, which is why the appeal went out to each individual and not to a designated guilty party.

Any government demanding climate action from households should identify the institutions that still reward extraction while making those households bear the burden. Any delegation or government that sought weaker fossil-fuel language should be willing to place its position, its instructions, and its material interest on the record. The source does not name the delegations behind the disputed language, and this column will not invent them. That evidentiary limit does not make the rule neutral. It makes the rule itself the necessary object of analysis: a process that can stop a multilateral text because of the phrase “fossil fuels” gives delay to interests that gain from delay.

King’s late analysis is the relevant inheritance. In *Where Do We Go from Here?* (1967), he argued that poverty had to be abolished directly—including through a guaranteed income—rather than managed indefinitely at the margins. His point was structural: personal restraint could accompany change, but it could not substitute for a just distribution of power. The climate application is direct enough. A people told to change their behavior while institutions preserve the benefit of delay are being asked to carry a burden designed for them.

Preaching at Ebenezer Baptist Church on Christmas Eve 1967, King described the world as a network of mutuality into which every human life was woven. The Antalya outcome demonstrates what the process preferred not to hear: the atmosphere is the garment, and the garment has no consensus clause. A delegation can keep the words “fossil fuels” out of a treaty; it cannot keep the molecules out of the sky.

The moral tradition and the cost ledger agree on the finding. The garment binds, and no revenue stream exempts anyone from the binding.

Leo has moral authority. The question is whether he will use it to move from principle to power. Moral language opens the door. The rule decides what the room does once everyone is inside. The pope named systems and governance. Now the system must be made specific enough for the people inside it to answer.

The criticism cannot be that Leo ignored structure. The criticism is that he left structure as a category rather than a map.

Who controls the extraction of the resource? Which governments can block an agreement? Which companies retain the gains when fossil-fuel dependence continues? Which institutions convert delay into leverage? Which communities absorb the damage while decision-makers postpone the cost? A call for “fundamental changes” can be morally forceful without answering those questions. It can set the moral direction while leaving the beneficiaries exactly where they were.

The immediate demand is not a perfect treaty. It is legibility. Name the cause. Name the beneficiaries. Name the rules that let delay pass as consensus. Protect environmental defenders. Give affected communities standing in decisions that determine their exposure. Build a process capable of producing binding obligations without pretending unanimity is the only route to action. Consensus-rule reform is already on the table; pressure on the delegations is the specific work this week demands.

No agreement can erase every conflict of interest. No conflict of interest can erase responsibility for the harm caused while the conflict remains unresolved.

King was right about the arc of justice, and wrong for anyone who hears him as saying the arc moves by itself. The arc bends when people with the power to alter a mechanism decide that preserving the mechanism is no longer acceptable.

The Beloved Community is not a mood that arrives after a failed conference. It is a society in which no community’s prosperity is purchased through another community’s exposure, and no generation’s future is treated as collateral in another generation’s bargaining.

Future generations cannot pay for adults’ verbal caution. COP31 ended without a pact. That is a failure, not a prophecy.

The refusal chose delay; delay chose the rent. The next choice remains open, and it must be made in public—with the words in the text this time.

Name the cause. Name the beneficiaries. Change the rule.
