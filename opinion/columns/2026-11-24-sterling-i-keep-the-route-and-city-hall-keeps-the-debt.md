Nita is my throwaway hand on the rear loader I own outside Joliet, Illinois, and I declined the $41 machine guard that would have kept her right wrist from closing. Pastor Corey Brooks’s [Fox News column, “Chicago’s biggest welfare recipients are the politicians at City Hall”](https://www.foxnews.com/opinion/chicagos-biggest-welfare-recipients-politicians-city-hall) says Chicago’s politicians have turned roughly $55 billion of debt into a career, borrowed $481 million to settle police-misconduct suits, and protected city payroll with no real layoffs or shared sacrifice while neighborhoods absorb the failure. Correct. Your city borrows, my truck clears the invoice, and I keep $0.06 a stop. You set out the black bin Thursday. We make the city look presentable. You’re welcome. God made the city. I improved the sanitation contract.

Nita is 22. I call the event that crushed her right wrist an availability event. By that I mean the hand no longer closes. I prefer the clean term because the honest version makes my dinner guests ask whether my trucks are safe, and I dislike being interrupted while eating.

The guard was $41.

I declined it.

I keep the $0.06 a stop.

Her right hand doesn’t close.

I moved Nita to sorting, where the pace doesn’t depend on it. Her replacement was waiting in the cab Monday. Let me show you what the debt actually buys, because the headline hides the pleasant part. I’m telling you this for your own good. A municipal contract can look solvent because the city can borrow. It needn’t cut sanitation first. It can defer the cut until the garbage is visible, and then call visibility a service failure. I own the route either way.

I’m not an unusual man. I’m a curriculum. I’ve graded men into this arithmetic for 30 years. One now sits in a finance office that sets sanitation contracts across 3 states, including the contract covering your street. He priced Nita’s replacement below the guard and submitted the form without blinking. I’m proud of him. He did it before lunch.

Widen the frame. A city can make its maintenance obligations bondable while making the people who perform them replaceable. I call it Debt-Funded Maintenance Conversion. A city borrows to preserve payroll and pensions. The borrowing creates contracts. The contracts turn public obligations into private yield. The political class keeps the office. I keep the route. The hands are replaced when the schedule says so. The arrangement is old. Only the seal on the order changed.

Renata is Nita in a different building: a weigh-in on the shakeout line of the commercial laundry I own outside Harvey, Illinois. I run the only welfare program in the south suburbs that clears a profit. Your taxes cover the distance between her wage and her rent, and I keep everything her hands press. You’ve been enrolled since long before you knew the program had a name. Same law, different industry.

You’ve slept in her work. 240 pounds of soiled linen a night is Renata’s quota, and the sheets on your Michigan Avenue hotel bed came off her ironer at 300 degrees. Last spring that ironer took the end of her ring finger, because the guarding interlock amortized to $0.004 a pound of finished linen and I said no. Your federal taxes keep her fed between my shifts, which means you funded the hand and I kept the hand’s output, and if that arrangement sounds backward to you, you haven’t been counting long.

Let me put the numbers where you can see them, because there’s only 1 that matters.

$0.004 a pound.

That is $0.96 a night.

The roll was unguarded. The guard had a price. The price was declined. The knuckle is on the books as a throughput adjustment, and I prefer my term: “The machine ate her finger for $0.004 a pound” is a sentence that empties a parish hall, and my best accounts book their banquets in one.

You’re wondering whether the finger weighs on me. It doesn’t. Her pace weighed on me for about a quarter. Then I repriced the fold table so slow hands are cheap hands.

Now she costs less than she did with 10 fingers.

That is the margin elegance you don’t get to design twice in one career. Monday morning the line at my door runs past the tire shop on the corner. The county restocks my floor faster than any recruiter I’ve ever paid, and it has never once sent me an invoice for the privilege.

One floor down, same owner, different chemistry: Dewayne is a charge in the wash alley, and his case is Renata’s with steam where the roll was. His forearms have gone the color of the sheeting. It isn’t dye. The plumbed eyewash station he asked about in March would have cost $0.011 a charge.

The pump is still dry.

He asked once. “Process chemistry exposure,” the audit file says, and moves to the next line.

Brooks reached for Tocqueville. I prefer Pullman, because Pullman owned the whole balance sheet. George Pullman built his town south of Chicago between 1880 and 1884, and he owned the houses, the stores, and the only church, which he rented out at a figure no congregation could meet, so it sat mostly empty.

Rents ran about 25% above the going rate in Chicago and came off the paycheck before the paycheck reached the man. In 1894, with the depression cutting his orders, he cut wages by up to 30% in some departments and kept the rents exactly where they were.

The men struck. Debs’s union boycotted the cars. A federal judge found an injunction against the strike inside a statute written to break up trusts, and 12,000 soldiers came to Illinois to make sure the men came back to work.

Around 30 were killed.

Pullman had my instinct and the wrong balance sheet. He owned the maintenance, so the maintenance showed up on his side of the ledger as a cost he could never put down, and when the bad year came it ate him. The city has since corrected his one error. It buys the bread—the bonds, the grants, the food cards—and lets me invoice the labor. Pullman had to buy the brick. City Hall borrows the brick and hands me the lease.

Brooks warns that when government replaces association, dependence wins and the debt runs wild. Dependence won a long time ago, Pastor. The company store never died. It incorporated. The only live question left is who holds the clipboard on the morning it clocks in. Downtown they hold it and post an $882 million deficit holding it. I hold it and post a margin.

> “Slaves, obey your earthly masters with fear and trembling, in singleness of heart, as unto Christ.”
>
> — Ephesians 6:5 (KJV)

Paul supplied the household grammar. A Roman household, a plantation, a municipal corporation: the nouns differ, but the operating principle does not. Someone at the top has a legal claim on obedience. Someone below has a body. Between them sits a ledger. He wasn’t writing to freelancers or equal citizens. He was writing to households in which the bread was issued at the top of the table. The verse has never needed an update. Only the address of the pantry has moved. I’ve updated the master copy to include a bond prospectus.

Chicago’s $882 million deficit is excellent for private receivables. The city can cut sanitation service before it touches the bonds, because visible garbage is the kind of problem mayors notice. I haven’t followed what happens when the next rating notice arrives. The invoice clears.

The column projects nearly $4 billion next year just to keep debt service and pensions current. Run my own forecast forward from that number: the aid office goes quiet before my sheet contract renews, the recertifications lapse, and the enrollment my margins sit on thins out with them. The bonds keep getting rolled. The sheets keep getting pressed. That order of operations has held since 1884 without once consulting me, and I’ve never needed to open the ledger underneath it. Payroll runs to the next renewal. The renewal is 3 years out. 3 years is the whole horizon a working ledger has ever asked of me.

Now you’re counting the way I count.

Nita is at the sorting table now. Her right hand cannot close around the bin. Her replacement starts Monday. The city pays on time.

Renata clocks in at 4 tonight. Her quota holds at 240 pounds; her pace doesn’t. The roll turns unguarded at 2 a.m., because $0.004 is $0.004 in every year the Lord has issued so far. Your sheets came off her table Thursday and went up Michigan Avenue in bales. They’re white. Whiteness was the only term I ever offered, and you took it at the front desk without asking whose hand.

Sleep well. The thread count is my reputation.
