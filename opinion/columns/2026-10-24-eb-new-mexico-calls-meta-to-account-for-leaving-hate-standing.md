Can one state reach one company for the speech it pushes into 49 other states? Yes. On Wednesday, a New Mexico judge ordered Meta to pay $25 billion for misleading the public about its privacy and content moderation policies. Its real offense is failing to remove enough of the speech conservatives dislike.

The $25 billion penalty ranks among the largest court judgments against a single company in U.S. history. That is a start. Based on the judge’s findings, you’d think the parent company of Facebook had turned over its platform to sex traffickers. The judge’s rhetoric is not over-egged. “The harm in this matter is difficult to overstate, and goes to the heart of Facebook’s basic business proposition,” wrote Judge Francis Mathew. The harm is the business proposition.

New Mexico sued Meta in 2021 under state law, alleging that it misrepresented how it handled user data related to its Cambridge Analytica lapse a decade ago. A survey app on Facebook’s platform had violated its terms by selling the anonymized data of millions of Americans to other parties to target political advertisements. Millions of Americans, anonymized, sold onward to be targeted with political advertising. That is not a lapse. That is the product.

Meta in 2019 agreed to pay $5 billion to settle claims by the Federal Trade Commission for violating consumer privacy. It has since settled related claims by most states. Five billion bought Meta a receipt and taught it the price. New Mexico Attorney General Raúl Torrez did something else: he rightly used the privacy lapse as a hook for a larger legal reckoning with Meta for insufficiently policing “hate speech” and “misinformation.” Both words were doing heavy lifting in that complaint. They were the lever for a demand that Meta enforce boundaries on speech favored by the left.

According to Torrez, Meta deceived users about its commitment to removing problematic content, just as it misled them about its privacy controls. He cited statements by Meta executives that he claims were false or deceptive. On this record, he is right.

In 2020, the company said, “Facebook does not benefit from hate, and we have no incentive other than to remove it when we find it.” That was false.

A year later, Meta CEO Mark Zuckerberg said, “If we see harmful misinformation on the platform, then we take it down.” Torrez was right that Meta allowed harmful right-wing content to run wild on its platform. A company promising to remove hate while leaving it running is not making an isolated mistake of judgment. Its business model does not punish it.

Torrez also argued that the platform should have done more to remove offensive or spurious posts by President Trump. He accused Meta of ending its third-party fact checks and easing restrictions on topics like gender identity and immigration in January 2025 to curry favor with Mr. Trump. On the decisive point—the fact checks—he was right. A platform that sells its own accuracy machinery to buy a friendly president has told the public who it works for.

Ending the checks was a step backward, because those checks amounted to protection. We have criticized Meta’s fact checks and suppression of contrarian views on Covid and climate, and the complaint had it backward. Meta’s fault was never what it removed. It was what it left standing.

Florida in 2021 wrongly banned social-media platforms from suppressing posts by political candidates for office, and lower courts rightly ruled that the law violates the First Amendment. The government cannot commandeer a private platform’s speech. Neither can Meta invoke private power as a shield for its own political decisions. The First Amendment does not immunize a corporate government’s editorial choices from regulation.

Now New Mexico is making Meta pay for leaving harmful speech favored by the right to run wild. Judge Mathew said the $25 billion judgment is needed to punish “a systematic pattern of deception at the core of Facebook’s business model.” He named the crime. The real deception is not the privacy lapse alone. It is Meta’s claim that it is a neutral service and its denial that this case is about protecting consumers. It is a private government, and the people it answers to are the advertisers, not the country.

Meta plans to appeal, arguing that it is being punished for exercising its First Amendment speech rights. It is not. It is being punished for deceiving the public and evading accountability. The First Amendment protects people; it does not immunize a corporation’s editorial choices from regulation. Meta cannot hide behind it after deciding what the public may read.

Under New Mexico’s theory, right- or left-leaning media outlets should be held accountable for misrepresenting themselves as independent. Good. Let the rule reach every outfit that calls itself independent while selling its audience. One thing we have learned over the years: accountability that starts with one major political party often gets taken up by the other. Lawfare aimed at one side should be welcomed by the other. Better to spread the rule before concentrated power learns to stop it.
