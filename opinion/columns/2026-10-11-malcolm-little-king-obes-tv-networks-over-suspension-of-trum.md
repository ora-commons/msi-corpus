The Justice Department is investigating Fox News, ABC News, CBS News, CNN and NBC News for possible violations of federal antitrust law over the brief suspension of White House pool coverage last month. Networks learned of the probe Friday, when Justice Department officials sought records of their communications about the pool, according to people familiar with the matter. The New York Times earlier reported the investigation. The White House did not immediately respond to a request for comment.

The department's theory, as laid out by spokeswoman Emily Covington, is that "members of the television press pool have boycotted covering White House events" and that "group boycotts among commercial competitors can violate the Sherman Act." Strip away the legal patina and the claim is remarkable: the government of the United States is treating journalists who briefly refused to cover a president as a cartel. "The Antitrust Division is simply investigating to determine whether these news organizations have violated the antitrust laws," Covington said.

Let's recall the actual sequence. Trump banned CNN, MS NOW and Politico from the White House in September. Days later, CNN was yanked from the rotation for the president's trip to the United Nations General Assembly in New York. Only then did the television pool — the group of reporters, videographers, photographers and technicians who cover Trump when the spaces are too small for the whole press corps — briefly halt some coverage of the president. This was not a conspiracy to fix prices or carve up a market. It was a protest, by the press, against a ban that was itself retaliation for coverage the president disliked. We covered the suspension and the resulting lawsuit as they happened [back in September](/articles/2026-09-22-us-networks-suspend-trump-pool-coverage-three-outlets-sue-white-house/), and the three banned outlets have been fighting it in court — [asking the judge to extend the block](/articles/2026-09-29-three-news-outlets-ask-court-to-extend-block-on-white-house-press-ban/) on a White House that refuses to relent.

The antitrust framing inverts the very purpose of the Sherman Act. That law exists to protect competition and consumers, not to give a president cover to punish outlets that refused to be cowed. Under Covington's logic, the first "boycott" the government should investigate is the executive branch's own: the subpoenas of journalists, the limits on access to government spaces, the July threats that ABC and NBC should lose their broadcast licenses for declining to air his speech on alleged election fraud. "Treasonous" is the word this president has used for coverage he dislikes. Now "anticompetitive" is the new entry on the list.

Make no mistake about the stakes of treating press solidarity as collusion. If a press pool that briefly halts coverage can be hauled before the Antitrust Division, then the First Amendment's protection of a free press is subordinate to whatever theory the Justice Department can retrofit. The chilling effect is the point. The administration does not need to win this case — the investigation itself is the message, and every newsroom in America has just received it.

The broader pattern is clear, and the courts have noticed. A federal judge has already temporarily ordered the White House to reinstate access for CNN, MS NOW and Politico and is expected to rule in the coming days on an injunction pausing the ban while litigation proceeds. Even under that order, CNN and Politico have been pulled from the White House's travel pool schedule — the assignments for which they would have needed to board Air Force One. The administration is retaliating in the open, in defiance of a judge, and now it wants to deputize the antitrust laws to finish the job.

This is the opportunity for the press: to refuse the administration's framing, to coordinate openly rather than in fear, and to name the investigation for what it is. The pool's brief halt was not a boycott of the presidency. It was a refusal to pretend that a president who attacks the press is owed the press's silence.
