The real cause of this market scare is concentrated ownership, not AI hype, and the bill is landing on workers and PC buyers who never got to vote on the buildout. The Wall Street Journal’s Editorial Board tells the fear version in [“An AI Halloween Market Scare”](https://www.wsj.com/opinion/market-scare-ai-binance-nasdaq-google-alphabet-94e31518?mod=rss_opinion). Its case is that worries about AI costs, geopolitical uncertainty, disappointing earnings, and next week’s midterms have made stock prices stop shrugging off bad news. It calls the resulting correction probably inevitable after capital chased anything wearing an AI label, warns that more weeks like this may come, and warns that a pullback by the hyperscalers will be felt throughout the AI ecosystem. It treats the Binance theft as another AI-assisted warning. Every loss gets filed in the same haunted house.

The board has one true point. Capital can chase a story, overbuild, and mistake a rising share price for proof. When customers have not paid for the promised service, a correction is not a personal attack on progress. It is a price tag. Markets are allowed to say no.

But the board has the order backwards. It lists real facts—earnings that missed, costs that climbed, billions raised—and then folds all of it into one word: scare. The facts are decisions. Earnings missed because somebody chose to spend ahead of them. Memory chips got expensive because somebody placed enormous orders. The billions got raised because the companies decided to raise them.

The mania was not discovered on Wall Street. The firms making the bets amplified it. They issued the securities, bought the chips, drove memory prices higher, and sent a wave of capital toward anything wearing an AI badge. Their scale let them commit on terms smaller firms could not match. When the payoff failed to arrive, the board called the result a change in mood. The corporations made the bet. The market marked it down. A bill is not a mood.

The sequence matters. Capital poured into anything with an AI angle while the story was hot. When prices fell, that same pouring got renamed investor nervousness. Both the companies and investors allocated capital, but only the companies decided how much to raise, what to buy, and which orders to place. They set those numbers. You cannot turn the spending into a bad mood after the price falls.

Take the $500 billion. It is not cash that walked out of Alphabet’s bank account. It is the market’s estimate of future profits being marked down. A stock price can fall before one chip order is canceled. That makes the loss evidence of changed expectations, not proof that AI itself has become a haunted house.

Start with the bills.

Alphabet fell 14% in two days and erased some $500 billion in market value. Intel fell 29% in one. Amazon reported more spending than expected and negative cash flow. Meta floated $25 billion in new shares. Microsoft announced a $25 billion bond offering. The Nasdaq fell 3% as the rout spread to Nvidia and Micron Technology.

Now connect those bills. Google and OpenAI were among the giant buyers snapping up scarce memory chips. That demand pushed memory prices higher. Higher memory prices raised the cost of PCs and smartphones. Intel’s legacy PC business got squeezed, and its stock fell 29% on Friday. Alphabet’s advertising revenue undershot expectations while investors worried that AI search would consume the search ads that finance the search business.

One corporation’s capital plan can push costs through an entire supplier chain. When the hyperscalers buy more, suppliers expand. When they slow down, suppliers absorb the canceled orders and inventory. Intel did not get hurt because some engineer mistrusted a chatbot. It got hurt because demand from a few enormous buyers raised input prices Intel could not pass on. The board sees a bad week. The market structure created the bad week.

Again, concentration does the work. The firms are not isolated buyers. Their capital choices set demand for suppliers that also serve their competitors. Their financing choices spread uncertainty through the same ecosystem. When several enormous balance sheets move at once, risk passes through the chain. AI enthusiasm amplified the capital rush. It did not create the concentration that made the rush fragile.

Now look at financing. A share can be sold. A bond must be serviced. If profits disappoint, debt payments do not shrink because the technology looks promising. Bondholders hold a riskier IOU. Chip suppliers sit on inventory built for enormous orders. PC buyers keep paying the memory prices those orders created. The companies that authorized the gamble can retreat behind a market-wide scare. The PC buyer cannot diversify away from the bill.

That is what concentration buys: the right to make a bet, spread the invoice, and complain that the weather changed.

Here is the plain test. If a company can call a project profitable only while somebody else absorbs the loan payments, the supplier risk, or the security failure, the project is not profitable. It is financed by pushing the bill elsewhere. The cost did not disappear. It moved.

Concentration is not a side note to this correction. It is the structure that made the correction possible and sent the bill outward. The board cannot wave at market “nervousness” and pretend the structure does not matter. The market is not revealing a change in human nature. It is revealing who made promises and who gets the bill.

The ownership problem is larger than the bond market. Alphabet, Amazon, Meta, and Microsoft are shareholder-controlled. Their boards decide what to build. Their shareholders can capture the gains and sell their shares. If AI raises productivity, workers do not automatically receive the surplus. If AI changes work, workers do not automatically own the machine doing it.

A capital raise does not repair that gap. It gives more money to the company that already controls the decision. The board wants investors to watch the spending more closely. Fine. Investors should watch who controls the spending too. More finance for the same concentrated control is not the answer. Broader control is.

Then there is Binance, the world’s largest crypto exchange, which reported the biggest crypto heist on record: $6.3 billion stolen from its digital wallets. The theft is a theft. AI may have made the attack easier, but that is a possibility, not a finding. The article itself concedes North Korea might have broken in without AI.

Binance suspended withdrawals and says reserves and company assets will keep customers whole. Markets reprice Alphabet overnight. Crypto customers wait to learn whether one private balance sheet can make them whole. That is concentrated custody: the customers’ claim is collective, but the risk-bearing decision is private.

One private exchange became custodian, operator, risk manager, and emergency source of replacement funds. A reserve is a buffer. It is not a guarantee. It is not insurance either.

Alerts are useful. They tell a company where the fire is. They do not tell customers who owns the bucket, who must keep it funded, or who answers when the money is gone. Cybersecurity matters. The ownership arrangement underneath it matters too.

I am not against the AI buildout. I am against letting a handful of companies privatize the gains while the costs travel everywhere else.

So what do we build?

Not another caution committee run by the companies whose capital plans require caution. Build shared capacity. Give the people exposed to it a claim on the result.

Start with a public option for computing capacity and AI services. Publicly supported capacity could finance shared computers and common data resources, serve schools, hospitals, researchers, small firms, and workers at transparent cost, and let private providers use the infrastructure on published terms. The public need not own every model. It must own some capacity, keep the road open, and have a say in access and price. A public option does not require government to be the only operator. It requires government to own part of the floor.

Then build member-owned compute and data cooperatives. When private utilities would not wire the countryside because there were too few customers per mile of line to pay for the poles, roughly 900 rural electric cooperatives built the service themselves. They now serve some 42 million Americans across 56 percent of the country’s landmass.

AI has a similar scale problem and a stranger commodity. A cooperative can let a hospital, a university, a small firm, and a worker-owned manufacturer buy chips together. They can own the machines, set the fees, vote on the data rules, and share security costs instead of leaving every small buyer alone. Private providers can compete with the cooperative. They just cannot own the only entrance.

Then use the bargaining machinery already available. Some states allow sectoral wage boards. Give AI-intensive industries a board that bargains over pay, monitoring, productivity gains, and retraining before deployment becomes fact.

Where a company uses AI to reorganize work, worker ownership can go further: the people affected by the machine get a claim on the enterprise using it. About 15 million Americans already participate in employee stock ownership plans, so this is not an imported Scandinavian fantasy. Employee shares without bargaining can leave management in charge, however. Public capital should buy ownership, not another round of voting shares for distant shareholders. Workers should get a stake in the machine and a say in how it is used.

Publicly supported capacity also needs patient finance. Private capital is excellent at finding the next hot thing. It is less reliable at financing a hospital’s computing commons or a small firm’s access to models that will still be there in ten years. Public capital can sit beside private capital without replacing it. It can insist that the public keeps a stake and that access rules do not travel entirely to whoever owns the largest server farm.

Digital assets need the same treatment if they are going to function as money. Build public or cooperative accounts that compete with private exchange accounts. Separate customer assets. Set reserve standards. Report breaches. Share threat information. Create a public backstop for systemically important custodians. Give users a practical way out when withdrawals freeze. A public alert system helps. It does not replace the owner carrying the risk.

Private custody needs those rules too. Firms holding customer money should segregate customer assets, meet reserve standards, report breaches, and share threat information. One company’s board should not be the final backstop for everyone else’s money.

Each measure fixes a failure that produced the bill. The public option breaks the single-owner bottleneck on computing capacity. The cooperatives let smaller buyers own the machines and share the security bill. Sectoral bargaining divides the productivity gains before deployment, not after. Worker ownership gives the people affected by the machinery a claim on the enterprise. Custody rules put exchange losses on the balance sheet that controls the wallets.

This is harder here because compute is capital-hungry, patents charge royalties, and giant platforms get harder to dislodge as they gather customers. Remind me what the obstacle is again?

Capital. Scale. Politics.

Those are design constraints, not laws of nature. The economy is a set of human choices, not the weather. The ownership map was drawn. We can draw another.

America has built shared institutions before. The Bank of North Dakota has been state-owned since 1919 and has paid a profit back to the state in most years. We know how to build shared ownership. We just get uncomfortable when the shared asset wears a server instead of a hard hat.

So build the public option, the member-owned cooperatives, the sectoral bargaining, and enforceable custody rules. The AI buildout is going to happen. The question is whether the people living with its prices, work, errors, and infrastructure will own any of the institutions beneath it.

The boom is coming. This time, the institutions should answer to the people beneath them.
