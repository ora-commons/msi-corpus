Donald Trump just sold Jensen Huang immunity from consequence. The instrument was a CNBC interview; the market supplied the receipt: about $1 trillion in NVIDIA value disappeared while Huang was gone, and the stock rose 31.48 percent when he returned. The interval was eleven days. On Monday, after Trump said Huang “did nothing wrong,” declared “I believe that you have to protect our geniuses,” and added “We don’t have enough of them,” [NVIDIA’s shares jumped](https://mainstreetindependent.com/articles/2026-11-23-nvidia-shares-jump-more-than-25-after-huang-s-reinstatement/). The market did not merely applaud a personnel decision. It repriced the relation between one executive’s conduct and a trillion-dollar enterprise.

The Wall Street Journal’s account was unusually spare. NVIDIA’s board had removed Huang after revelations of two extramarital affairs—one with a subordinate at the company and another with a customer suspected of links to Chinese intelligence—and after Huang failed to disclose the indiscretions to the board. The company was also under federal investigation. Yet the paper reported that “the real catalyst for change seemed to be the market selloff,” and that Trump had offered Huang “a kind of market bailout.”

Nothing in the record indicates that a chip, a compiler, a customer contract, or a product roadmap changed during those eleven days. The government intervened. The board reversed. The market reversed. Neither the reversal nor the stock rebound resolved the federal investigation or the export-control concerns.

What NVIDIA sells is not merely silicon. It sells a computing environment: CUDA, its proprietary programming platform; the libraries, compilers and debugging tools built on it; and the customer code written against it. CUDA’s price to a customer is not the price of a licence. It is the years of tests, training pipelines, staff knowledge and operating assumptions built around it. A customer does not switch merely because a rival offers a discount. It switches after porting code, retesting systems, rebuilding tools and carrying operational risk.

That is a switching cost—the cost of leaving—and it is enormous. It is also a moat. But a moat is not a man. The market held the moat and treated Huang as its spokesman.

It is true that a chief executive is not interchangeable with the job. A company NVIDIA’s size does not run itself from a filing cabinet. There are decisions—how much capacity to buy, which architecture to bet on, which customer to disappoint—on which a founder with ten years of context may be better than a board reading a file. For eleven days, the directors had been deciding without that context. That is an argument for retaining an experienced executive. It is not an argument for suspending disclosure, board independence or export-control law.

The trouble is that none of that concession describes what happened. NVIDIA’s own account of the reversal, as [MSI has reported](https://mainstreetindependent.com/articles/2026-11-23-nvidia-s-board-cites-u-s-government-input-in-huang-s-return-amid-sec-probe/), cites United States government input in the decision to bring Huang back. [Trump said he had persuaded the board](https://mainstreetindependent.com/articles/2026-11-23-nvidia-reinstates-jensen-huang-after-trump-says-he-persuaded-the-board/). Vice President Vance helped, according to MSI’s earlier reporting.

The documented sequence supplies no new product, disclosure or export-control finding. It supplies government input and a trillion-dollar market loss. A board that reinstates a chief executive because the executive branch of the United States asked it to has not exercised judgement about its chief executive. It has been relieved of the judgement and handed a receipt.

The export-control gap deserved better than the sentence it received. Advanced-chip export controls are not a property of the metal. They are a licensing regime: applications, end-use declarations, end-user lists and enforcement decisions made by people. The Journal reported that Huang “oversaw a company that exploited a gap in U.S. export controls to the benefit of China.” The gap was therefore not a hole in physics. It was a run of decisions not to look, taken by people with the authority to look, and every one of those decisions was priced by somebody else—by the national-security agencies that must still defend the control and by competitors denied access to the same supply.

Trump’s reply was shorter: “He’s in the business of selling chips.” A regime that holds because the chief executive of one licensed exporter is personally taken to be trustworthy is not a regime. It is a favour, and a favour has to be renewed. Rules outlive their authors. Favour does not; it lapses when the granting stops, and the granting stops whenever the mood changes.

Cui bono is not difficult to answer. Huang gets his office back. NVIDIA gets its market-preferred custodian. Trump gets a public demonstration that presidential intervention can reverse a board’s governance decision. The export-control gap gets nothing, because nobody closed it.

The doctrine has a name because it is a doctrine. The Journal described Trump’s successful campaign as effectively endorsing “Founder Mode”: the belief that founders hold a unique power to create value and that their foibles should be overlooked because of what they create. That is not a method of creating value. It is the removal of every constraint between one person’s judgement and a trillion-dollar balance sheet.

The paper also noted that investors “largely support the philosophy when the returns go in their favor.” The operative criterion was never the genius. It was the share price. The board removed Huang after the disclosures; the market erased about a trillion dollars; and the public case for putting him back became a price case.

The Journal put Huang among the “Imperial CEOs,” reporting that “He’s now bigger than a board of directors, than shareholders, than the norms of corporate governance.” Huang, unlike Elon Musk at SpaceX and Mark Zuckerberg at Meta, lacked a dual-class share structure—stock rules that give a founder voting power ordinary shareholders cannot outvote. That absence mattered: without special voting rules, a board could remove him. With powerful friends, even that did not matter.

His friends in what the report called the “New Boys Club”—Musk among them—made the calls. Musk had called Huang “effectively the Michael Jordan of AI.” In the register in which that sentence was spoken, it was a job description: irreplaceable, unaccountable and priced like a franchise.

To be fair—and here the phrase is doing real work, not Letterkenny work—the “delicate supply chain” defence is not empty. CUDA’s lock is real. The hyperscalers have chained capital to the buildout: Microsoft’s twenty-year agreement with Constellation to restart a reactor at Three Mile Island, and Meta’s twenty-year agreement for the Clinton plant’s entire output in Illinois. That is infrastructure with political weight.

The supply chain is delicate, though not because one man can assemble it. It is rigid because one ecosystem has trained much of the market to depend on it. Indispensability at that scale is the case for breaking the lock, not exempting its custodian. The national-security gloss said the country was “at war for the future”—a zero-sum contest. The gloss did real work: it converted a governance question into a wartime question, and wartime questions arrive pre-answered.

John Kenneth Galbraith had a name for the interval between appropriation and discovery, when the promoter has his gain and the victim does not yet feel poorer: the bezzle. Much of the AI buildout has resembled that interval for two years, with enormous capital commitments made before the promised returns have arrived. This is not to say every company in the buildout is engaged in fraud; the analogy is narrower. Capital is committed, expectations harden, and the market continues paying today for returns the income statements do not yet show.

Monday established something the bezzle literature had not contemplated. The interval now has a bodyguard. When the market’s confidence in the promoter wobbled for eleven days, the executive branch did not move to protect investors, disclosure duties or the governance process. It moved to protect the interval. The market’s bezzle now has a federal bodyguard, priced at 31.48 percent in a single session.

Cory Doctorow’s account of enshittification is a theory about constraints rather than character. Once a technology company has enough scale, four forces tend to check it: competition, regulation, self-help through independent software, and the labour power of people who refuse to ship something they are ashamed of. Run this week through the checklist.

Regulation was suspended. The White House intervened with the directors of a public company in the middle of a governance process and while that company sat inside a federal investigation. Competition was exposed: a 31.48-percent swing on one employment decision announced no practical substitute for the person. Self-help interoperability had been disabled years earlier by the CUDA lock and the legal barriers around independent tools. Labour’s leverage had been weakened by the 2023–24 layoffs that stripped the industry of much of its power to refuse.

That left the board as the last check operating inside the firm rather than on it, and eleven days in which it functioned were treated as a market emergency requiring federal response. Doctorow put the related danger plainly: “When a company is bigger than the government, it gets damned hard to credibly threaten to punish that company.” NVIDIA is not beyond the reach of government action. The investigation did not appear to matter. That was not a failure of the investigation. It was the finding.

Tim Wu’s warning in *The Curse of Bigness* was never merely that large companies charge too much. It was that size itself concentrates power past the reach of institutions supposed to check it. “Too big to fail” arrived in 2008 as a retrospective confession: the banks had outgrown their regulators. The genius doctrine is the same arrangement moved earlier in the life cycle. Immunity is granted prospectively, at the first sign that governance might actually reach the indispensable man. It is a protection racket with a growth mandate. The protection arrives before the fire, and the stock prices the exemption the day it is announced.

Wu’s *The Master Switch* described the same drift one layer down, where a network built so that anyone can speak to anyone becomes a network with a switchboard in the middle. Nothing about NVIDIA’s switchboard is metaphorical. The company owns the toolchain, and much of the switching cost is contractual as well as technical—a matter of terms, licences, accumulated code and what a supplier will do for a customer bringing money and a queue. That kind of cost was always a policy choice, which means it can be unmade by a policy choice, provided somebody has an interest in making it.

Formal verification has a rule that fits this case with uncomfortable precision: you cannot verify a system whose specification you cannot write down. A specification is an explicit statement of what the system must do and under which conditions. “Genius” is not a specification. It cannot be tested, falsified or audited; it can only be invoked, and it becomes most useful at precisely the moments when disclosure, competition and the law would otherwise apply. “Protect our geniuses” is not a policy argument. It is an immunization document issued by the one office with the power to make it self-executing.

To be fair, boards are clumsy instruments. Eleven days is not a deliberative arc, and boards do remove chief executives for reasons that rhyme with embarrassment as much as fiduciary duty. The trouble is the referee. The directors reinstated Huang after the vice-president worked them directly and the president claimed a personal role in the result. A personnel decision at a public company under federal investigation was taken with the government’s fingerprints on the announcement.

Strip away the euphemism and the arrangement is a chief officer holding his desk at the pleasure of the residence. The securities laws have no column for that because until this week nobody had tried to install one. This is not board deliberation. It is external command translated through a nominal corporate decision.

My father was a journeyman millwright on the bar mill at Manitoba Rolling Mills in Selkirk for thirty years. When Gerdau of Porto Alegre bought the place in 1995, the new owners kept the men who could run the modernised line, laid off a third of it, and paid the pension at a fraction of what had been promised. Nobody in Ottawa was asked, and nobody expected Ottawa to be asked.

The mechanism is older than the chip: keep the operator, treat everyone else’s claim as a cost to be carried, and let the promise to the rest lapse. NVIDIA’s version keeps the operator too. The promise that has lapsed is the one made to the public—that a licence means something, that a gap gets closed because it is a gap, that a company’s incentives are not the regulator’s. The benefit runs to the share price, the founder’s control and the incumbent in the AI buildout that is most expensive to displace. The cost runs to the national-security agencies that must now defend a control they have been told is somebody’s private matter, and to the competitors outside the gap who were not given the same licence. This is the inheritance of extraction, updated.

Which is where the Canadian record is instructive. Michael Geist’s *Law Bytes* podcast has spent years taking Canadian platform policy apart line by line, and the useful distinction is between a regulator acting for the public and a regulator acting for somebody’s client. The actual Canadian record on concentrated infrastructure power is duller and more useful than the American one. The CRTC’s answer to a chokepoint has been an order for disaggregated wholesale access—smaller competitors must be able to use parts of the fibre networks on regulated terms—under Telecom Regulatory Policy 2024-180 of August 2024.

The Competition Tribunal has never blocked a merger outright. In *Commissioner of Competition v. Rogers and Shaw*, 2023 Comp Trib 1, approval came with a divestiture condition attached. Ottawa’s tool against a bottleneck is a written obligation with a schedule. Washington’s tool this month was a personal favour to one man. And favours, unlike obligations, are not renewable.

The lever, then, is not a different president; a different president is a variable, whereas an exemption is a structure, and structures need documents. NVIDIA’s board should publish the minutes and reasons for its reversal; federal law should bar officials from lobbying a public company’s board about personnel while their agency has an open investigation into that company; and law should require competing hardware and software to interoperate with NVIDIA’s systems while protecting independent developers who make those systems work without permission. As the Copyright Act’s anti-circumvention rules show, a digital lock can make a lawful repair, audit or competitive tool a federal offence; without an interoperability mandate, there can be no meaningful audit of one’s own equipment when the person who owns the stack stands above the law.

Nothing here requires a lecture about founders or about the market’s capacity for affection. It requires three documents filled in honestly: the minutes of the board meeting that reversed its decision, the Commerce Department licence files for the shipments that used the gap, and the SEC’s open case file. Two of the three are the government’s to insist upon. The third is NVIDIA’s.

The waiver clause now sits in the rule of law, and the market has already priced it: roughly a trillion dollars out, a 31.48-percent rise back in one session, eleven days. It is written in no statute and carries no expiry date. The market has put a price on the presidency—and on presidential immunity from consequence.

In my father’s workshop, a machine that ran beautifully until you removed the cover was not a genius. It was the reason for the manual. Nobody asked for a second opinion this week. The probe is open, the export-control gap is open, and the dogma has its first confirmed customer. A machine that runs only while nobody opens the case is not a genius. It is the reason the manual exists.
