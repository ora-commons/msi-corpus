Retailers shift pricing risk onto shoppers by making algorithmic pricing opaque.

Nick Nadeau, a product manager in Belleville, Illinois, found a hard drive priced at $369 in Walmart’s app. His wife’s phone showed the same drive for $110 less. He bought at the lower price and remained alarmed, suspecting stored data or cookies had been used against him. Walmart said the discrepancy was an error that had been corrected.

That explanation may be true. The public cannot verify it. And that verification problem—not proof that artificial intelligence secretly rigs every grocery bill—is the real centre of the fight over algorithmic pricing.

If two phones can display different prices, shoppers have no visible way to know whether the difference came from a mistake, a routine price adjustment, a coupon or a price individualized after a company inferred something about the buyer. Retailers now possess digital instruments that can make prices more responsive, more granular and harder to inspect, then ask shoppers to believe denials no consumer can test.

The AI mass-pricing apocalypse remains unproven. The opacity is real.

Shoppers are primed to hear betrayal because the price itself has become a moving target. Airlines, hotels and ride-share companies such as Uber have used dynamic, demand-based or surge pricing for years. Retailers have long adjusted prices to meet costs or maximize profits. The newer fear is that a business can infer a shopper’s identity, urgency or immediate need and use that knowledge to demand more money.

Dynamic pricing, by itself, is not new. What is new is the volume of data companies can collect about individuals and the speed at which they can alter prices online. As Jameson Spivack of the Future of Privacy Forum put it, that combination is prompting shoppers to ask whether a business might respond to a profile or an immediate need with a higher price.

The inflation backdrop makes the question more combustible. Overall prices, as measured by the consumer-price index, have risen more than 29 per cent over the past six years. Consumers already resent paying more for groceries, dining and basic goods. Then came AI.

This adds a new fear on top of familiar cost pressures: not merely that prices are rising, but that the system may be deciding how much each person should pay.

The most alarming theory has also attracted the weakest evidence. Posts across social media have claimed that stores are combining AI with electronic shelf labels to follow a shopper’s interest and trigger sudden price changes. Research in the United States and Europe, where grocers have used digital shelf labels for more than a decade, has found that the technology does not automatically produce surge pricing. Part of the reason is that price changes remain obvious to anyone walking through the store.

Grocers have instead used electronic labels to discount food approaching expiration, cutting waste. Some grocers in certain European markets now offer such discounts hundreds of times a week. More commonly, prices move overnight or on regular schedules, such as Monday mornings. Walmart said it typically changes digital prices while stores are closed.

Electronic shelf labels are plumbing, not a smoking gun. But plumbing can be rerouted.

Walmart, Kroger and McDonald’s all say they do not use technology to set prices according to who is buying or what a shopper suddenly needs. Walmart chief executive John Furner put the corporate promise plainly: “We price the product, not the person.” McDonald’s said its franchisees do not use AI to set menu prices or change them according to real-time conditions or demand.

The dispute has outlasted the assurance. The nonprofit Groundwork Collaborative believes Walmart’s patents and technology could enable personal data to be used in raising prices, and called on the company to withdraw some of them. Walmart answered that “a patent isn’t a plan” and said it would not use or renew one of the patents that drew scrutiny.

That answer may be technically true and materially incomplete. Possessing a patent does not prove a plan to use it. Neither does refusing one patent settle what a retail pricing system can do, what data it can draw upon or whether shoppers can inspect the result. The distinction between what a company says and what its architecture permits is precisely what the public cannot presently audit.

Absence of proof is not proof of innocence. It is also not proof that every grocery bill is being individualized. Consumers do not need to establish that every retailer has built a personalized-price machine to know that the capacity exists, the data is growing and the pricing decisions remain invisible.

Under scrutiny, Instacart, the grocery-ordering service, backed away from experiments with variable pricing. Consumer advocates and politicians are also challenging the accumulation of shopping data and the patenting of technology capable of individualized prices.

Distrust has already reached the courts. A customer has sued McDonald’s over alleged algorithmic price-fixing of menu items; the company says the lawsuit is “filled with inaccuracies.” Whether that case succeeds is beside the point. Shoppers do not have to win a lawsuit before deciding whether they should trust an opaque pricing system.

Lawmakers are beginning to force the issue. New York passed a law last year requiring companies to disclose when they have used algorithmic pricing on a product. Maryland, Connecticut and New Jersey enacted laws this year restricting certain types of personalized or data-driven pricing. Dozens more states are considering bills governing the use of personal data, real-time demand and even conditions such as weather to set prices.

That movement began well before shoppers’ viral shelf-label fears, as [Main Street Independent reported on the growing push for limits](https://mainstreetindependent.com/articles/2026-06-03-state-lawmakers-move-to-ban-personalized-pricing-as-consumer-data-tools-expand/). This summer, the Federal Trade Commission issued a proposed enforcement policy statement targeting “any deceptive or unfair personalized pricing,” particularly cases “where consumers reasonably expect that prices for a product or service will not vary based on their personal data.”

The FTC’s [proposed personalized-pricing policy](https://mainstreetindependent.com/articles/2026-08-19-ftc-proposes-policy-on-companies-that-vary-prices-using-shopper-data/) would draw a line around prices consumers could not reasonably anticipate. It is a proposal, not an operative rule.

Retail pricing was opaque enough without AI, but algorithmic pricing magnifies that opacity. A company may say it is responding to demand, reducing waste or offering a discount. A shopper may experience the result as something more personal and unfair: the same hard drive costing $110 less on another phone, or a menu item carrying a different price from one restaurant location to another. Neither side of the transaction can see the rule that produced the difference.

The next step is auditability, not another press release. If algorithmic systems can influence what a consumer pays, companies should have to explain the rules in plain language: what information is used, which factors can change a price, how long a change lasts and what the customer can do about it. They should disclose when a price has been individualized, identify the data driving the decision, submit their systems to independent testing and face penalties when they defeat reasonable expectations.

An independent test could compare identical baskets across different accounts, devices and times while keeping the underlying shopper data confidential. That would not make every price popular. It would make the machinery visible, which is the difference between pricing technology and a secret auction over each shopper’s profile.

Show the receipts. Until the pricing system can be inspected, a $110 gap between two phones will keep looking less like an isolated error and more like evidence of a system shoppers were never allowed to inspect. Corporate denials will remain the quietest thing in the room.
