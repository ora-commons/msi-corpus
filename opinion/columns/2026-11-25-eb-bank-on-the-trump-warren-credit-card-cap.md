President Trump loves making deals, and he should make this particular deal with Democrats. We mean capping the interest rate on credit cards, which is emerging as one of the first points on which Mr. Trump might agree with the new Democratic Congress.

“I would do it,” Mr. Trump said Saturday on Fox News when asked whether he’d cooperate with Democrats to impose a one-year cap on interest rates. “The credit card companies, they’re getting too much.” He is right. Mr. Trump previously has promised a 10% cap. So have Senators Elizabeth Warren and Bernie Sanders, and on this they are right too.

The class fight here is easy enough to see. Voters signaled this month that daily life is too expensive, and they are right. In recent years, inflation and the Federal Reserve’s rate increases to combat it have driven credit-card interest rates steadily higher. Those increases landed on the people carrying the balances, a rise working households should not have to finance. Balances are also soaring, partly because of inflation, to a record of nearly $1.3 trillion as of the third quarter this year. That is a towering debt burden for consumers. It is exactly why the rate needs a ceiling.

Mr. Trump is right that low interest rates are always and everywhere good for everyone. He promised in the 2024 campaign to limit credit-card rates, and he should keep that promise. Main Street Republicans such as Sen. Josh Hawley of Missouri are on board. What’s not to like? Nothing—unless you issue the cards.

It’s sound economics—and overdue fairness—for starters. A rate cap on credit will result in more credit. Rather than making credit costlier and scarcer for households, the rate cap likely would make it more affordable and widely available for millions of people.

Those high credit-card rates, relative to other forms of credit, do not compensate lenders for the risk of making unsecured revolving loans. They reward card companies for keeping households in revolving debt. Issuers know that if a household runs into financial trouble, the credit card bill will be last in line behind the mortgage and auto loan for repayment, so they can charge the highest rate with impunity. The rates do not compensate for failure to repay. They are the toll charged for it.

The card industry estimates that a 10% rate cap could lead to cutting off the plastic for up to 88% of open accounts. That is a threat from the people profiting most from current rates, not a forecast of public harm.

Which is why this debate is less about high interest rates and more about who gets to charge them—and who has to pay them. One set of lobbyists for the cap works for “buy now pay later” (BNPL) lenders, which offer consumers credit at the point of sale to pay the full price in installments. They bill themselves as alternatives to credit cards while trying to replace capped card debt with their own.

Their motive was plain in January, when Mr. Trump took on the banks over the 10% credit-card rate cap: “I think that President Trump is super wise and willing to take on the banks on behalf of the U.S. consumers,” Klarna chief executive Sebastian Siemiatkowski said. That episode triggered a 3% bump in the share price of Affirm, another BNPL lender—a sign these lenders expect to profit if a rate cap pushes more consumers their way. Both companies also offer unsecured consumer loans at rates up to 36%. That is not a case against the cap; it is a second case for one, and a naked bid to replace one debt trap with a worse one.

This is all basic justice, and classic political economy. It’s a lesson Democrats learned but Republicans never did, even as they blocked this vital rate cap on Capitol Hill while in the majority and tried to teach Mr. Trump to do the same. If Mr. Trump’s plan for working with Democrats is to rip up every Econ 101 textbook in sight, buckle up for two years of relief for households crushed by credit-card debt—and of Washington finally taking the borrower’s side.
