The World Trade Organization's trade forecast more than doubled because the AI assumption moved. Geneva now projects this year's growth in global goods-trade volume — the quantity of physical goods crossing borders, stripped of price effects — at 3.9 percent, against the 1.9 percent it projected earlier this year, and next year at 4.1 percent against 2.6 percent. Those figures sit on top of 4.2 percent growth recorded in 2025. At that pace, volumes in 2027 would run more than 12 percent above their 2024 level. Robert Staiger, the WTO's chief economist, attributed the revision to the AI investment boom and observed that a three-year run of this size has been uncommon since the financial crisis. A doubling inside a single projection cycle is a statement about a capital-spending assumption and should be read as one. The world's ports did not double their capacity in nine months. The projection's driver changed.

Why would AI capital spending move trade volume that much? Because compute hardware is assembled from inputs that cross borders several times before it arrives as a finished server. Leading-edge logic and memory are fabricated in a small number of places; the lithography and deposition equipment that make them come from a small number of others; advanced packaging, boards, racks, cooling systems, transformers and switchgear each add a crossing of their own. Merchandise trade statistics count gross flows rather than value added — a component counted at each crossing instead of once at final assembly. A build-out that concentrates income in three or four economies therefore registers as broad trade growth in the aggregate. The 3.9 percent and the phrase "a small number of economies" come out of the same release. They describe one event at two levels of resolution, and only the first level is a single number.

There is a second mechanical point. The trade intensity of a build-out is front-loaded. Fabrication plants, data centers, substations and cooling systems are purchased once and then operated. When construction slows, the ratio of trade growth to economic growth falls back toward trend even if the installed computing base keeps running. A three-year surge driven by a capital-spending schedule is a surge with a visible end date. The same release states that services trade will grow more slowly than previously expected because of the war between the United States and Iran. Goods up, services down, concentration up. That is a more complicated finding than a boom, and it is the finding the release actually makes.

The fiscal backdrop settles neither side of the argument. The federal deficit for the fiscal year that ended September 30 came to $1.993 trillion, 12 percent above the prior year in nominal dollars and the highest since 2021, according to the Congressional Budget Office. That comparison is nominal. Without the decomposition — revenue measured against the size of the economy, spending before interest, and the interest bill separately — a 12 percent nominal increase is not a 12 percent real deterioration, and the figure as reported does not carry that decomposition. What the number does support: private capital spending on compute does not depend on fiscal space, so widening deficits and rising trade volumes can occur together without one causing the other. What it does not support is the claim that the deficit is unconstrained. Whether it constrains turns on the interest bill and on what the borrowed money bought, and neither is inside the figure that was released.

Taylor Farms has suspended sales to the United States and Canada of iceberg lettuce grown in central Mexico between May 1 and August 31 of next year, closed a regional processing facility, and cut ties with a local grower. A company spokeswoman said the vast majority of its lettuce is grown in the United States and Canada, making the suspension manageable. That is a cost statement addressed to investors reading a CFO newsletter. It is not a safety finding, and it does not answer the question the outbreak raises. Twelve thousand people in the United States were sickened and two died. A four-month calendar suspension removes the exposure from the peak season window; it does not establish that the contamination vector is gone, and cutting ties with one grower addresses an instance rather than a class. The Food and Drug Administration's ten-part plan includes food-safety training in Mexico's produce industry and expanded Mexican laboratory capacity to collect and process cyclospora samples. Two of the ten parts have been described publicly, and both sit inside another country's institutions. The other eight are the ones a reader should ask for.

The eurozone is where the counter-argument has real teeth. Inflation there reached a three-year high in September, and investors expect the European Central Bank to follow the two increases it has announced since June. Philip Lane, the bank's chief economist, said this week that wages are not rising at a rate that would trigger inflation alarms. The open question is whether the energy-price shock from the war that began in late February indexes into wage settlements — whether workers made poorer by energy costs ask to be made whole, and whether employers grant it. If they do not, the shock is absorbed and inflation returns toward target without a wage-price spiral. If they do, the bank faces a supply shock that tightening cannot repair and a credibility problem that waiting cannot repair. Nothing released this week distinguishes between those two paths. The coming wage-settlement rounds and services inflation outside energy will.

The forecast and the food-safety plan are one discipline problem in two registers. In both, an aggregate was published with a distribution attached, and the aggregate is the part that travels. The useful questions are the ones the number does not answer: which economies, at what value added, on whose schedule; which ten parts, described how, enforced by whom.
