The structure is old, though resemblance is not proof of historical transmission. The question changes from what U.S. policy does to what the regime has done, and the policy accounting leaves the room. That is the shell game at the end of the chain: no matter which villain is named, somebody else’s accounting disappears.

So here is what the six passages amount to, taken together.

The title asks why the lights go dark. O’Grady tries to make the answer arrive through atmosphere—dictatorship, cruelty, buying in—before it reaches the machinery. Fuel is constrained by sanctions and by the sanctions’ exceptions. The grid is broken by decades of management choices and elite priorities. The Cuban authorities retain the power to decide where fuel goes, where investment goes, and who keeps the benefits of both. The United States retains the power to open and close legal fuel routes and then describe the result as strategy. The article can hold those truths together. Instead, it lets the nearer moral vocabulary keep the policy question out of view.

It assembles a real case against Havana’s blaming of sanctions for the darkness, then turns that case into a permission slip. Because the grid is old, the reader is told not to ask what sanctions tightened. The piece’s answer is not that the grid is broken. Its answer is that the grid is broken, so stop asking what sanctions did.

That is the face in the mirror: the piece warns against Havana’s all-purpose propaganda apparatus, then builds a whole-apparatus operation for Washington. The missing relation is simple. A decrepit grid and restricted fuel can exist together. A regime can be culpable for its own decay while a policy remains relevant to the suffering. The article offers one cause as a verdict because a verdict closes the inquiry; a mixed record keeps it open.

Put the column beside its own evidence and the mirror appears. A column about sanctions must eventually say what the sanctions change, what they do not change, what they cost, and what happens if they continue. O’Grady gives us regime facts and then treats the list as a substitute for that accounting. She does not erase the fuel facts; she puts them in a frame where the reader’s conclusion arrives before the arithmetic does. The scam is not in the facts. It is in the routing. Call the operation what it is: sanctions laundering. Take a policy whose effects must be measured, turn the effects into scenery, and make the regime’s crimes the substitute for measuring it.

Two beneficiaries explain the architecture now. The Trump administration is the immediate political beneficiary: it gets a shield from scrutiny and a clean policy conclusion. The Castro family and its inner circle are the material beneficiary of continued delay. Cuban workers and households are the diffuse cost-bearers: they get the darkness as the bill. Prospective investors are only the article’s claimed beneficiaries of any eventual rules; that promise has not earned the place of a receipt.

The remedy is not a louder propaganda counter. It is the missing sentence, restored and made hard: the grid’s decay is real; the sanctions’ causal effects must be tested, not dismissed; every beneficiary must be named. Until then, this is not an inquiry into Cuba’s blackout. It is a blame-shift scam built on top of real evidence.

The lights do not go dark because a television special won an argument. They go dark because power is routed through institutions that have made scarcity useful. The question is not whether Havana is a dictatorship. It is. The question is whether a policy defended through that undeniable fact leaves the machinery of dictatorship any less protected. A reader can reject Havana’s preferred story without granting Washington a free pass. A reader can support one policy and still demand the accounting for another. This column asks us to do neither.

The label was the protection. The mirror is the machinery.
