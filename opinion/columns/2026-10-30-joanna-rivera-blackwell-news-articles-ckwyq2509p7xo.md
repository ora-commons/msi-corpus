Trump attacked the pope for preaching peace because his movement needs a church blessing war. The Christian-Nationalist apparatus has made voting a sacrament and peacemaking a heresy — and one ordinary ballot in Illinois just made the contradiction impossible to look away from.

So here is the ordinary thing that happened: a seventy-one-year-old man, born in Chicago, requested his mail-in ballot from the Will County Clerk's Office on the third of October, and by the end of the month he had sent it back. That is all. That is every unremarkable civic act a hundred million Americans are doing this week. It is news for exactly one reason — the man is Leo XIV, the first American to hold the see and the first pope in the history of the Church to cast a ballot in a United States election. And the movement that spent nearly fifty years telling the American pew that the ballot box is a holy of holies cannot decide whether to worship him or to run him out.

Start with what he did to earn the running-out. In April, after the US-Israeli strikes on Iran, Pope Leo XIV called for peace. Donald Trump answered: "I don't think he's doing a very good job." Then: "We don't like a Pope that's going to say that it's OK to have a nuclear weapon." Then: "I'm not a big fan of Pope Leo."

Set the two statements beside each other in the order they were spoken. A man says the mission of the Church is to preach the Gospel, to preach peace. The president answers with an appraisal of his competence, an admission of personal distaste, and an attribution — a pope "going to say that it's OK to have a nuclear weapon" — that appears nowhere in anything the pope actually said. The reply carries the discipline the exchange lacks: "The mission of the Church is to preach the Gospel, to preach peace. If someone wants to criticise me for preaching the Gospel... I hope simply to be listened to because of the value of God's words." What he said was peace. What he got back was the bomb.

Pope Leo is not of my tradition, and this is not a column about his. I am writing about mine. I have stood in a church foyer on the Sunday before an election and handed out the voter guides. I have heard the sermon that was not quite a sermon, the one that named no candidate and left no doubt. I taught the lesson out of that workbook that sorted the world into the party of God and the party of the enemy, and I did not notice, for years, that I was being taught to read the Bible the way a precinct captain reads a map.

I never once heard that same pulpit say plainly what the twenty-fifth chapter of Matthew says plainly. Let me show it to you, because the chasm is the whole point.

The plain language of Matthew 25:31–46 is not complicated. The Son of Man separates the nations, and the criterion of the separation is not doctrine, not party, not a single sexual ethic, not a single vote. It is this: "I was hungry and you gave me something to eat, I was thirsty and you gave me something to drink, I was a stranger and you invited me in, I needed clothes and you clothed me, I was sick and you looked after me, I was in prison and you came to visit me." And when the righteous ask when they ever saw him in that condition, the answer is: "Truly I tell you, whatever you did for one of the least of these brothers and sisters of mine, you did for me."

Now watch what the apparatus does with it. It narrows "the least of these" until the phrase means only persecuted Christians. It softens "I was a stranger" until the stranger is a fellow church member and not the one at the border. It moves the whole passage from public policy to private charity, so that a nation may strip the immigrant of protection and still feel righteous because a food pantry served a Thanksgiving turkey. The text has not been edited. The reading has been cropped — cropped precisely where the plain words would cost the apparatus a vote.

The man at the center of this has spent his first year and a half clashing with this administration over immigration enforcement and over the American and Israeli strikes on Iran. He is not a bystander to the argument. He is what the argument keeps having to explain away.

Which brings me to the verse the movement actually cannot bear. Matthew 5:9, red letters, and the King James and the NIV differ by a verb and an article and disagree about nothing: "Blessed are the peacemakers: for they shall be called the children of God." Plain English does the work here. The peacemaker is not warned about, not pitied, not ranked below the careful or the shrewd. The peacemaker is named a child of God.

Now run that against April. The movement that drapes itself in the Sermon on the Mount heard a call for peace issued days after its own side's strikes, and it did not answer the text. It answered the man — his job performance, his likeability, and, reaching past him, the bomb. The Bible's plain language makes peacemaking the criterion. The operative reading, the one that decides which politicians get blessed, is that peacemaking in public after your side has fired is a liability, and the preacher who does it has to be discredited. The Sermon on the Mount, read plainly, is a blessing on the man the apparatus finds suspect; read through the legalist lens it is a naïveté, something a serious Christian sets aside when there is a war to win and a border to hold. The text has not been edited. The reading has.

So: the peacemaker is blessed, and the stranger is the test, and the man who narrows the test to protect a coalition has already answered the only question the passages ask. I can read the red letters in the translation I grew up on, and I can tell you what they say.

And there is the other favorite verse, the one that gets quoted at me every time I say any of this. Romans 13:1, King James: "Let every soul be subject unto the higher powers." I can still hear it read aloud in a classroom I sat in for years as a command to stop arguing with whoever held the office — and then, as always, we stopped at verse one, though the Greek runs clear through verse seven, where the authority is described as God's minister bound by the justice the chapters before it laid down, and the chapter before it says it plainly at verse fourteen: "Bless them which persecute you: bless, and curse not." The apparatus reads verse one and stops before the verses that would make the magistrate accountable. It quotes submission to power at the immigrant and the peacemaker and never once at the president who insults a pope. That is the machinery I was taught to operate, and I am not writing this from above it.

It did not fall out of the sky. It was built. In June 2018 the Attorney General stood up Romans 13 to defend family separation — the same verse I was handed as a command to keep quiet. In 1979 the conservative resurgence took the Southern Baptist Convention by capturing its presidency, its seminaries, its agencies. In 1980 in Dallas a candidate told that room, "I know you can't endorse me, but I want you to know that I endorse you," and the fusion was sealed. In 2000 the convention rewrote its confession and quietly removed the clause that said the criterion by which the Bible is to be interpreted is Jesus Christ — removed the Christ-criterion, mind you, from the confession of a church that bears his name. And alongside it grew the stranger thing: the men who laid hands on a president and anointed him a Cyrus, who told a movement that a man of the Lord now occupied the Oval Office and that his enemies were the Lord's enemies. That is not the Bible's plain language. That is a coronation rite dressed in it.

Kristin Du Mez's *Jesus and John Wayne* documents what the coalition was built to produce: evangelicals did not betray their values, because Trump was the culmination of the half-century-long pursuit of a militant Christian masculinity. Andrew Whitehead and Samuel Perry, in *Taking America Back for God*, name the mechanism — Christian nationalism co-opts Christian language and iconography in order to cloak particular political and social ends in moral and religious symbolism. A coalition assembled that way does not merely tolerate a compliant church. It requires one. That is the accusation, and it is why a call for peace has to be answered with contempt rather than with argument: argument would mean engaging the text, and the text says what it says.

Meanwhile, the man under that contempt voted. He requested a mail-in ballot from the Will County Clerk's Office on 3 October, less than two weeks before the Vatican announced surgery to remove the lower lobe of his right lung, and became the first pope ever to cast a ballot in a US election — having voted, the Holy See notes, "whenever he was eligible to do so," including his years in Peru. The Press Office's wording is worth keeping: "the right and the duty of citizens to participate in democratic processes." Duty, not dominion. That is the whole difference. The Seven Mountain Mandate does not ask Christians to vote; it calls them to dominate government, and education, and media, and the rest. Jesus, asked about the coin, gave a split answer in Matthew 22:21 — Caesar what is Caesar's, and to God what is God's — and handed Caesar no altar. A ballot offered as a duty and a peace preached as a mission are the same posture. A pope with one lung, voting by mail, going anyway: he is keeping the [November travel schedule to Uruguay, Argentina, and Peru as planned](/articles/2026-10-27-pope-leo-keeps-november-trip-gallagher-calls-diagnosis-a-bit-of-a-game-changer/), and on the same Latin America journey he is scheduled for [meetings with clergy abuse survivors](/articles/2026-10-27-leo-xiv-plans-several-meetings-with-clergy-abuse-survivors-in-latin-america/).

Ask who benefits. With his party's prospects gloomy days before 3 November and Democrats favored for the House, ridiculing a pontiff is a cheap way to stand beside something his coalition treats as holy while making the man preaching peace look like the intruder — and it costs him nothing among the people who already approve, among US Catholics [Pew puts that at 30 percent](/articles/2026-10-26-pew-finds-30-of-us-catholics-approve-of-trump-s-job-performance/). The Catholic pew itself is divided over this president, which is a reminder worth keeping. It tells them precisely which authority is in charge here.

I will say the thing this column says plainly, because I will not be a counter-machine: the religious left runs the identical operation on its own party platform and I name it in the same breath. The moment a bishop agrees with your politics, your politics wants to make him a mascot, to parade him, to reduce his office to a prop in your fundraising email. The temptation is not conservative; it is human. It is the same con at a smaller scale, the same sin in two costumes — and if I refuse it in the pulpit I must refuse it in the party I am tempted to cheer.

I love the church I came out of these lessons in. I am still in it. And I am telling you plainly: a country that says it loves the Gospel cannot long resent a man who preaches it — and the Sunday you hear Matthew 5:9 read aloud, you will be able to see from the pew exactly who in the room wishes the verse said something else.

I do not know what God thinks of my ballot. I have voted badly and I have voted well and I could not always tell you which was which. What I know is smaller than that and firmer than that: the Bible does not say what the apparatus has been telling you it says. The text has not changed. The reading has. So on Tuesday, if you are in the pew where I used to sit, do the ordinary thing. Vote, as the pope voted, as a citizen and not as a sacrament. And then read Matthew 25 again, all of it, out loud, and notice who the stranger turned out to be. "I hope simply to be listened to," the pope said, "because of the value of God's words." That is not much to ask. It is, at the moment, more than his countrymen are prepared to give.
