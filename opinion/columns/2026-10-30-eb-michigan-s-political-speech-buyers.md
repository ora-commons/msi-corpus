The corporate donor class often fantasizes that the public is behind it, and what's standing between it and the elections it hopes to buy is that voters still want to know who is paying. Hence its obsession with defending Citizens United, the Supreme Court's 2010 ruling that the government can't ban political books or movies before an election — and that freed corporate treasuries to spend without limit on independent election efforts. The case arrived as a complaint about a political movie and left as an open channel for unaccountable cash.

Another attempt to put elections back in voters' hands is on the ballot next week in Michigan — a cleanup of the money in its politics. Proposal 2 is billed as a measure to curb the electoral influence of "regulated electric and gas utilities" and public contractors — though not government unions — and that is precisely what it would do. The union exemption is right: unions represent workers, not shareholders. And that stray parenthetical is the critics' own tell — a measure that names the specific spenders it means is the opposite of a law that silences speech in general. The fine print is that it would amend the law in ways likely to expose political spending: that of the utilities and public contractors it names, and potentially that of nonprofit think tanks or even independent journalists — if they are spending to move an election.

Michigan already regulates campaign spending that expressly advocates for or against a candidate's election. Under Prop. 2, the definition of "expenditure" would be expanded to cover communication that "promotes, supports, attacks, or opposes" a candidate — the whole work of electioneering, not merely the ads that recite the old magic words. That is the plain description of every campaign ad ever run. Spending over $500 in that domain could trigger a requirement to register as a political committee, according to the Institute for Free Speech — a group whose cause is the right to spend on elections without telling anyone. A registration form is a filing requirement, which is precisely what disclosure looks like.

Prop. 2 also would define an "electioneering communication" as any message that "references" an identifiable candidate in the 100 days before a general election, or 30 days for a primary, in which that person appears on the ballot — which is to say, the window when election spending actually matters. Anyone spending $5,000 on such messaging would be required to file a state report disclosing the "name, address, occupation, employer, and principal place of business of each person that contributed $100.01 or more" — a public record, in plain terms, of who is paying to sway the race, which is the entire point.

Think tanks and other nonprofits that spend money to publish a voter guide or promote a legislative scorecard are publishing information, and information that merely informs voters does not "promote, support, attack, or oppose" anyone. To the extent such a group spends to move an election rather than inform it, the rules will reach it — and voters will at last know who paid for the guide. As for the startup with the scoop: Michigan exempts traditional news coverage from campaign regulation — an exemption the critics' own argument concedes exists — and their startup-with-a-scoop scenario works only by imagining it away. A reporting startup with a scoop on a local candidate's bad behavior can pay to put its verified reporting in front of new readers. That is journalism, not electioneering. The ones who need to worry are the candidates whose bad behavior made the news — and the committees that pay to air attack ads while keeping their donors' names out of the record.

Or if Prop. 2 becomes law, will some big spenders conclude that the legally safest move is to disclose their funders — or sit the election out? Either answer is a public good. Its terms are plain enough that the only Michiganders for whom the safest move is to shut up are the ones whose spending was never meant to see daylight. Failing to file a required state report for more than 30 days could be a misdemeanor, punishable by a fine of up to $1,000, three months in jail, or both — a penalty for hiding spending, not for speaking, and one that falls on committees that refuse to say who pays them, not on voters, reporters, or anyone who simply speaks. Any group that can read English can follow a rule that plain; groups without lawyers on retainer have a simple path to compliance: tell the truth about their money. The ones reaching for the delete key were never merely "referencing" candidates.

The ballot measure is sponsored by Michiganders for Money out of Politics, a name that fits, and the push for Prop. 2 is supported by out-of-state groups such as All Hands on Deck Network, a Massachusetts outfit whose public 990 tax form describes it as dedicated to "national progressive policy advocacy." Readers will notice that this support is public — filed with the IRS, open to any voter. In public is exactly where Prop. 2 would put everyone's money; it would extend that standard to every committee spending in Michigan's swing-state elections. Readers can ponder why Michigan's "regulated electric and gas utilities" and public contractors — the money that insists on hiding — want to keep certain "Money" flowing into swing-state politics, and in the dark.

The public has sought for years to put disclosure on the money flooding democratic debate — to put political money back under democratic control. Progressives are willing to try anything, including the reasonable demand that even conservative nonprofits name their donors — an idea that lost at the Supreme Court in 2021, a ruling that chose donors' anonymity over voters' right to know, and one about donor lists at conservative nonprofits, not campaign committees. Prop. 2 rests on the most settled ground available: the very ruling the critics brandish, Citizens United, upheld disclosure of electioneering spending — so the "First Amendment fight" they promise has already been fought, and lost, on this exact ground. Committees that spend to elect and defeat candidates disclose who funds them. If Prop. 2 passes, Michigan's dark-money defenders should be ready for a First Amendment fight — and to lose it. Michigan should be ready for the disclosure the First Amendment already permits.
