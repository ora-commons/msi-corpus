Rich nations abandoned vulnerable countries to climate catastrophe.

At 5:10 Tuesday morning, I had a customer’s Stihl MS 250 on my bench, its carburetor varnish-locked with last season’s gas. I gave him the honest price: a new fuel line and carburetor kit, paid before I put the saw back to work. He could repair the failure or carry a broken saw into the woods. Nobody gets a working machine and leaves the cost of making it work somewhere else. I know what it looks like when the people with the most machinery decide somebody else must carry the risk.

After [the longest climate summit in history ran into overtime with fossil fuels outside the draft deal](/articles/2026-11-21-cop31-talks-run-into-overtime-as-fossil-fuels-stay-out-of-draft-deal/), COP31 [closed without agreement on fossil fuels](/articles/2026-11-22-un-climate-talks-collapse-in-turkey-without-agreement-on-fossil-fuels/). The Associated Press report from Turkey records what the summit finally produced: no consensus. The final text did not mention fossil fuels—primarily coal, oil, and gas—and did not increase financial commitments for nations already suffering the worst effects. Dozens of nations objected. Delegates failed to agree on who would pay what, whether wealthy nations would deliver the funds they had already promised, or how much more vulnerable countries would receive.

Those omissions were the policy. No fossil-fuel obligation. No new finance. No agreement even on delivering the old promises. Everything around them was process.

Climate Action Tracker found that progress in slowing global warming has essentially stalled for five years. Even if every country delivers every pledge it has made—an outcome the report called increasingly unlikely—the globe still reaches 2.6 degrees Celsius above pre-industrial temperatures by century’s end. The Paris Agreement’s 1.5-degree limit is almost certain to be overshot. This is not a shortage of plans. It is a shortage of money and enforcement. The distance between the 1.5-degree goal and the 2.6-degree projection is a political distance. Wealthy countries made commitments. The record shows governments serving up the appearance of movement rather than meeting the obligation of payment.

The talks failed in the same season that the bill came due. A summer heat wave in Europe killed more than 50,000 people, with nearly two-thirds of those deaths blamed on human-caused climate change. In Nepal, a partially collapsed Himalayan glacier triggered flooding that washed away entire villages and left thousands dead or missing. Smoke from Canadian wildfires made air quality in several American cities among the worst in the world. The bill for carbon lands downwind and downstream. The countries least responsible for the climate crisis are carrying some of the heaviest consequences. These are not abstractions behind negotiations. The people who lost their villages and the people breathing that smoke cannot wait.

In Washington, abandonment took a sharper form. The Trump administration’s abrupt 2025 cuts to United States foreign aid eliminated or sharply curtailed programs serving millions of people worldwide, including disaster-preparedness work. President Donald Trump pulled the United States out of the Paris Agreement, and the country did not participate directly in the COP31 talks for the second consecutive year. A United States delegation that included California Gov. Gavin Newsom attended anyway and pledged climate action. National governments left the obligations unassigned.

The Cottonville Fire of May 2005 burned 3,410 acres across Big Flats and destroyed thirty homes. It did not come from a foreign-aid cut, but it shows the size of what one bad fire can do to a county. Disaster preparedness is the work of getting warning and help to people before weather turns a hard night into a regional emergency. The 2025 cuts removed capacity for that work. That is the cut working as a cut.

My county has a useful piece of evidence pointing the other way. The federal rural-electrification program created in 1936 made long-term, low-interest capital available to member-owned rural electric cooperatives when private utility economics would not serve scattered farms. Adams-Columbia Electric Cooperative in Friendship is what that decision looks like on the ground today. The co-op model did not wait for a private company to discover a market dense enough to pay for the wire itself. Public patience put wire on the land; local ownership kept the system answerable to the people using it.

COP31 inverted that test. Rich countries asked vulnerable countries to move faster while keeping their own energy policy open and their money close. The nations with the least room to pay were told the transition could not wait. The nations with the most room to pay refused to put the money on the table.

In *The Unsettling of America*, Wendell Berry described extraction destroying the membership before it destroys the economy. The fossil economy takes wealth from one place and leaves damage in another. COP31 then asked the damaged place to finance the transition away from the system producing the damage. That is not aid. It is the bill assigned to the wrong people.

This is the Community Collapse contradiction named in Chapter 16 of *We Too*: governments invoke a shared climate future while arranging for the least powerful communities to absorb the physical and financial cost of collapse. COP31 did not fail for lack of a sentence about responsibility. It failed because responsibility had no bill attached.

Aldo Leopold, writing in “Thinking Like a Mountain” in *A Sand County Almanac*, gave the shortest good definition we have: “Conservation is a state of harmony between men and land.” The text that came out of Turkey failed because the governments that wrote it treat the land as a ledger they never have to balance, and the weather on the losing side of that balance as somebody else’s weather.

On the south side of my county, wells have tested above the drinking-water standard for nitrate, and the Adams County Land and Water Conservation Department has published the data. The cornfields around town now carry three corporations’ names instead of the family names I grew up with. The people drinking that water are not the people who spread the manure. Same ledger, smaller geography. Different disasters, same accounting arrangement.

States, provinces, Indigenous groups, and local officials kept acting because there was work to do. Almost 60 countries participated in the first International Conference on Transitioning Away from Fossil Fuels in Colombia, a voluntary initiative outside the United Nations. The second is scheduled for next year in Tuvalu, an island nation already feeling the rising water. Katharine Hayhoe said countries are “building new networks to work together.” She said most climate action takes place below the national level, including in the United States.

This is evidence the work continued. It is not proof the summit was irrelevant. Summits set the money, the rules, and who gets to build. Local governments cannot finance every seawall, early-warning system, heat plan, and upgraded grid themselves. When rich governments withhold money, they do not cancel climate work. They push it down to the places least able to carry it. The membership does not need the summit’s permission. Down here it looks like the co-op that keeps the wire up to my shop, the county staff who keep the well-water data, and states doing the work Washington walked away from. What the ground needs from the summits is narrow and plain: the money already promised, actually paid, and the warning systems funded before the next glacier lets go. They could not manage that with the longest climate summit anyone has ever run.

The Wisconsin records give the failure a local scale. The Wisconsin Initiative on Climate Change Impacts reported in its 2021 assessment that Wisconsin has warmed about 3 degrees Fahrenheit since 1950 and that 2010 through 2019 was the state’s wettest decade on record. Winter warming has been most pronounced in northwest Wisconsin, at 4 to 6 degrees. Lake Mendota has the longest continuous ice record in North America, beginning in 1855, and now runs about a month lighter on ice than it did in the nineteenth century. The Wisconsin DNR’s long-term lake-ice and deer-population series track the same changing conditions.

I wrote the date the ice went off Petenwell last April into the same spiral notebook I have kept for twelve winters. It was eleven days earlier than my first entry. The entries do not flatter anybody, including me. My notebook is one local observation and cannot substitute for the state’s records. When the two point in the same direction, the weather has entered the work. It arrives as repair bills after storms, smoke against the shop windows, flooded field roads, longer stretches of heat, and the fuel used to move grain and make repairs. A county cannot solve a global finance failure by being thrifty. It can see exactly what that failure costs: less capacity to prepare, more money spent repairing, and fewer choices after the weather turns violent.

A serious compact would put enforceable dollars and deadlines into the hands of affected governments, Indigenous nations, and local institutions, with public accounts and local authority deciding what must be built. The cooperative model does not solve climate change, but it supplies a governing pattern: public capital where private returns will not serve the public, ownership close to the users, and responsibility that cannot be passed to the next summit. COP31 did not produce an imperfect agreement. It produced unequal terms: transition demanded, fossil fuels untouched, money optional. Tasneem Essop, executive director of Climate Action Network International, said rich nations “gutted the very measures that could have made a difference.” The current fossil-fuel economy keeps its market. Rich governments keep their freedom from payment. Vulnerable governments keep the deadline. That is abandonment, and the 2.6-degree projection already shows the cost.

Tomorrow morning I have a county-fair mower on the bench. I will cut the grass at the fairgrounds, where families from across the county stay for a day they can afford. That is what functioning looks like from the ground. Mike and Quinn should inherit a place where people can repair what comes. We will keep it by making rich nations pay what they owe and putting climate money into the communities doing the work. The dates are the dates. I’ll keep writing them down.
