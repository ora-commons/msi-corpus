A market can drift lower for honest reasons. It cannot crash for honest ones. The thing that turns a decline into a wreck has a name, and it isn't wisdom — it's borrowed money. The Wall Street Journal's Jeff Yass, co-founder of the options-trading firm Susquehanna International Group, disagrees at length in [There's No Such Thing as a Stock-Market Bubble](https://www.wsj.com/opinion/theres-no-such-thing-as-a-stock-market-bubble-159ea117?mod=rss_opinion). Every price is just the market's best guess, he argues, so when prices collapse nobody was irrational — the uncertainty simply resolved. There is no "true price" for investors to lose sight of, and anyone who calls the AI trade a bubble is arrogant enough to think he can out-guess millions of people with money on the line. His closing challenge to the doubters: put your own money down and buy puts. It is a genuinely clever essay, from a man who makes his living selling insurance on price swings — the crashes included. Let's run the arithmetic it skips. Because the argument runs on a quiet assumption: that every bet in the market is made with cash the bettor owns. Remove that assumption — as 1929 did, as the telecom bust did, as 2008 did — and "uncertainty resolving" becomes a chain of forced sales that doesn't care what anyone believed.

He earns part of the concession, so let me pay it in full. Prices under real uncertainty are guesses, not verdicts; no pundit holds more information than the tape. Amazon really did fall more than 90 percent from its 1999 peak, and the people who held were vindicated. His citation is real: Hendrik Bessembinder of Arizona State found that most stocks have returned less than Treasury bills over their lifetimes, with roughly 4 percent of companies producing the market's entire net gain since 1926. A few winners pay for a graveyard of losers. Bubble-calling is a free option for pundits — wrong, forgotten; right, famous — exactly as he says. A losing bet is not proof of stupidity; a random day is a Tuesday about 14 percent of the time. All true. Now watch where the argument stops.

His claim can't lose. If prices fall, uncertainty resolved. If prices rise, the market knows something. Every possible outcome confirms the theory, which means the theory rules nothing out — and a rule that can't be broken isn't about the market at all. It's a guarantee. The real question was never whether prices are guesses. It's whether the guess is honest — whether the 15 percent in his own example is a real fifteen percent, or a story wearing probability as a costume. Take that example at face value, the way he asks. Pets.com went public in early 2000 and was shut down before the year was out. It famously sold dog food for less than the cost of shipping it. There was never a 15 percent chance of that business becoming Amazon. There was a pitch — eyeballs, first-mover, the future — and the pitch got priced as if it were math. When a whole sector prices the story instead of the arithmetic, that has an old name. It isn't uncertainty. It's the bubble, doing its work.

But notice what the whole architecture rests on. His Pets.com buyer owns his hundred dollars outright, so he gets to watch the bet resolve; the speculators, Yass writes, are in it "with their own money at risk." He wrote the assumption down himself, and the argument follows from it. The people a bubble actually breaks are the ones that clause excludes: the retiree who bought near the top, the fund with Friday redemptions, the trader on margin who must sell whatever he believes. Bessembinder's 4 percent is a statistic about a diversified holder across decades. It is not a consolation for someone who bought in March 2000 and needed the money in 2001. The aggregate recovers. The person holding at the resolution moment eats the resolution. That is who really pays, and the essay never mentions them.

Walk me through it slowly, since he likes arithmetic. In 1929 you could control a hundred-dollar share with ten dollars of your own and a broker's loan behind the rest. The price dips ten percent and the loan is impaired, so the broker liquidates you at whatever the tape will take. Your sale pushes the price lower, which drowns the next borrower, and down it goes. No new information arrived. No delusion was corrected. The debt did arithmetic. The same loop runs in reverse on the way up: rising prices raise the collateral, collateral funds more borrowing, borrowing buys more stock. That's a financing loop, not a belief loop, and it can carry prices far past what most of the buyers privately think is sensible. The current AI trade has the same engine, already running: the chipmaker has said it will invest in its biggest customer, who will spend the money on chips. Every sale manufactures its own demand. And it's why Yass can't tell you how far prices will fall. When the decline is financed, "how far" isn't set by beliefs at all. It's set by the margin calls.

Then notice what his own instrument says. He praises options as the market announcing the crash "in public, on an exchange," so no one can claim they weren't warned. True — and it stops one sentence short of the interesting part, which is who gets the bill. Insurance gets expensive when the insured event is likely enough to charge real money for. So if options on AI stocks are expensive, the market is saying a crash is a real chance, which means today's stock prices carry that same chance too. That is exactly the bubble callers' case: prices this high stay this high only if the enormous payoff arrives. And the people selling the insurance collect a premium every year the crash doesn't come. He hasn't refuted them. He has restated them in his own dialect, at prices his industry sets.

Then the column walks into its own trap. Two paragraphs from the end, believing you know more than the market is the definition of arrogance. The final paragraph's advice to anyone who thinks AI is a bubble: buy puts — bets that pay off when a stock falls. That is a wager against the market's own price, which by his definition is dumb and arrogant. The essay's cure for the sin is a demonstration of the sin. And note who writes that insurance. Put premiums are set by sellers, priced to profit on average, so "if you're so sure, buy puts" is a polite way of saying pay my industry for coverage against a risk the essay insists doesn't exist. The reader who takes his closing advice pays a premium to — well. He would know exactly who. His firm is a market maker: it stands ready to buy from every seller and sell to every buyer, all day, and earns on the swing whichever way it goes. It's easy to believe the market is never wrong when your paycheck rides the swings in both directions. None of that makes the argument false. It makes it well-paid.

Watch, too, where the money is standing when the fall comes. The gains were booked on the way up: by early sellers, by the exchanges, by the dealers positioned on both sides of every swing. The losses land later, at addresses the essay never mentions — the 401(k) that bought the top because the story was everywhere and the arithmetic was nobody's hobby. "Uncertainty resolving" describes the weather. The flood still has an address. That is the whole trick: a theory in which the boom is the market's wisdom and the bust is nobody's fault, so nobody owes anybody anything.

In 2008 the country found out who "nobody" is. The housing bets went bad, and the same system that had been collecting the upside arrived at the public window holding the downside. Congress authorized seven hundred billion dollars for TARP to backstop private wagers. The gains were private. The resolution was public. That backstop is the real reason "there's no such thing as a bubble" can be said from a trading floor with a straight face: the floor has never had to eat a full resolution alone. As for his challenge to produce a bubble caller with a track record — the forecasters who saw 2008 coming early weren't out-guessing anyone's beliefs. They were reading the debts.

So here is my standing offer, and I'll honor it either way. Someone will reach for October 1987 — no margin mania there, just portfolio insurance. Look at what portfolio insurance was: funds that had programmed themselves to sell stock automatically every time the price slipped, to cap their own losses. The rule did the loan's work. Selling became compulsory exactly when everyone else was selling, and compulsory selling is what turns a decline into a crash. Borrowed money is the usual engine; 1987 shows a rule can drive the same machine. So find me a famous American crash — 1929, 1987, the telecom unwind, 2008, take your pick — where the holders were free to sit still and the market broke anyway, and I will retire the word bubble with an apology. Until then, "bubble" is not a superstition. It's a description of what leverage does to a market full of individually correct guesses.

So take him at his word, and build for the swing. If prices are guesses, stop handing households lottery tickets and calling it a pension. If 4 percent of companies make all the gains, the honest retirement plan is broad, boring, cheap index ownership — not one man's conviction in one stock. Better, let the public own a slice of the upside on purpose. Norway parks its oil money in a fund worth well over a trillion dollars, one that owns about 1.5 percent of the world's listed companies — more than $175,000 a citizen — and Alaska mails a small version of the same idea as a yearly check. If the market is just an instrument, instruments can be owned by everyone.

What gets built instead is not a smarter forecast. It's a market whose guesses resolve the way his cash bet does, instead of the way 1929 did. That starts with less borrowed money: honest margin rules, and a finance sector that holds loans rather than stacking them. America already owns boring versions of the alternative — about 140 million of us belong to credit unions that don't need tomorrow's price to survive the week, and North Dakota has run a state-owned bank since 1919, holding loans through the cycles the speculators kept breaking. Then put floors under people and weld the trap doors shut, the way Denmark did: its unemployment insurance follows the worker, not the job, so losing a paycheck doesn't mean losing the floor. Easy to fall. Impossible to be ruined by the fall — so that the resolution of somebody else's uncertainty doesn't cost a nurse her retirement. Health care that doesn't vanish with the job. Retirement security that doesn't ride the ticker. A bad decade becomes a setback instead of a ruin.

Why it's harder here: America runs retirement on 401(k)s, individual accounts riding the same swings, with fees skimmed at every layer by the same industry that funds the argument that no guardrails are needed. The institutions don't exist because the people profiting from the swings wrote the rulebook. Building them is slow, unglamorous work, and it starts with admitting the fall is coming. We even have fresh proof the floor works here: the 2021 child tax credit cut child poverty nearly in half in a single year, and the poverty came right back when the check stopped.

That is the line between a market and a casino. A casino resolves its uncertainty on the bettor. A decent economy absorbs the resolution together, so nobody's life depends on whether the 85 percent arrives during their particular decade. That's not anti-market. It's anti-ruin. Somebody chooses which one we have.

And Yass is right about one thing more than he intended. Rational isn't about being right; it's honesty about the odds. The honest odds include this: millions of people can be wrong in the same direction at the same time, and when the guess comes due, the bill lands in retirement accounts. That isn't a paradox. By his own arithmetic, it's Tuesday. The market is a guess. Guesses swing. Build the floor before the fall, not the eulogy after.
