The Justice Department sued Alaska to keep a Republican Senate loss from becoming final.

The complaint was filed in the U.S. District Court for the District of Alaska late last Thursday, before the Nov. 3 ballot. It asks the court to bar Alaska from certifying "any election results that may be impacted by voter confusion" while the government investigates whether votes cast for Daniel J. Sullivan, a retired schoolteacher from Petersburg, were intended for the senator. The filing invokes the National Voter Registration Act of 1993 as the source of its federal authority. Trump removed whatever ambiguity remained about whose initiative this was. "I'm the one that said do it," he said Friday.

Attorney General Todd Blanche defended the suit Sunday on CNN's *State of the Union*, insisting that the president "is not trying to take over a state election in any way, shape or form," and calling the possibility a "doomsday" scenario.

Blanche's denial may be technically accurate. The lawsuit does not expressly order Trump to overturn an election. It does not need to. The filing does something more efficient: it places a federal judge between Alaska's vote count and the certificate that makes the result legally operative. His assurance answers the question of presidential intent. It does not answer the question of presidential power.

Two men named Sullivan are on the ballot for the United States Senate. The incumbent, Dan S. Sullivan, appears as "Dan S. Sullivan" and is marked as the registered Republican. The other, Daniel J. Sullivan Jr., appears without a party label. Alaska's ranked-choice voting — the system in which voters rank the candidates and votes are counted in rounds until one has a majority — lets voters rank both of them. Republican election officials tried to get Daniel J. Sullivan off the ballot. A state court and the Alaska Supreme Court kept him there. Even Sen. Lisa Murkowski, Alaska's other Republican senator and a supporter of Dan Sullivan's reelection, says the state already distinguished the two men and that the Justice Department should not have sued. In August's primary, Dan Sullivan won more than 68,000 votes to Daniel J. Sullivan's 4,107.

Those are not the numbers of voters unable to tell two candidates apart.

Blanche's case is not frivolous. States have a duty to run elections that do not mislead voters, and two candidates with nearly identical names on a single ballot is a real administrative hazard. The National Voter Registration Act is the vehicle through which Congress imposed national responsibilities on the States for the administration of voter registration, and its enforcement provision is the Attorney General's authority to sue a State that has failed to comply: "the Attorney General may institute a civil action ... against any State which has failed to comply with any provision of this subchapter," 42 U.S.C. § 1973j(a)(1). Alaska's courts rejected the ballot challenge without reaching any federal question, which leaves the federal question open. And the theory of irreparable harm is a familiar one: a wrong result certified cannot be un-certified, so the objection has to arrive before certification or not at all.

That argument has force. Here is where it fails.

Blanche said the more precise demand for relief is "to not certify if the law is broken." The complaint says something else: not certify "any election results that may be impacted by voter confusion." Put the two side by side and the discrepancy is the case. "If the law is broken" names a violation — a provision, a duty, a State actor, a breach. "May be impacted by voter confusion" names a category of possibility with no measure, no identified victim and no end. The first is an enforcement action. The second is a suspension.

The statute does not do the work the filing needs it to do. The National Voter Registration Act governs the systems States operate to register voters and the information those systems must carry, 42 U.S.C. §§ 1971–1973j. It does not govern how a State labels the candidates for federal office on its ballot, and it does not govern when a State certifies the results of an election. A registration statute does not become a certification veto because the Attorney General needs one.

Then there is the record the filing does not contain. The complaint identifies no voter, no miscast ballot, no registration transaction corrupted by a naming collision, and no allegation that confusion changed any result. It does not identify a secret ballot vote by which a mistake could be known with certainty, and the Justice Department has not explained how it would investigate the intentions of voters who, by design, reveal none. An investigation into secret voting intent would either hollow out the secrecy of the ballot or produce speculation dressed as government authority. Either way, the premise of the suit threatens the mechanism it claims to defend.

Blanche said the state-court decision did not consider federal law. That is not proof the federal government is right. It means the state judicial process did not answer the question the Justice Department now wants to answer for itself.

And then the timing, which is the design. The suit was filed before the election, in federal court in Alaska, and it asks for an order that would operate after the votes are counted. The record does not yet exist. No one has briefed the merits of a single vote. The theory of irreparable harm — that a certified result cannot be undone — is true of every election in the country, and it is an argument for speed, not for reserving in advance the power to stop certification. What the filing creates is a federal choke point at the moment an adverse result becomes final. Its shape is the emergency docket's shape: relief sought before the record is made, on the theory that the harm cannot be repaired later. That theory holds only if the result is the one the government is afraid of. Alaska's courts can hear the government's claims and decide whether federal law was violated. Alaska's voters can distinguish the candidates and rank them as they see fit. Neither process requires a pre-election insurance policy against a Republican loss.

On CNN, Dana Bash asked whether the suit risked repeating the events that followed the 2020 election and the Jan. 6 attack on the Capitol. Blanche snapped, "Oh, you got to stop it with that. You are not being a real person," called the comparison "crazy," and said it was "so embarrassing" for CNN that the discussion had reached Jan. 6.

Calling the comparison crazy is not a legal defense, and the comparison was not outlandish. Jan. 6 followed an election whose results were resisted after the votes were counted. The reasonable question was never whether Blanche had personally ordered an insurrection or whether Trump intended to seize ballot boxes. It was whether an administration willing to intervene before certification might, having built that precedent, use it after an unfavorable result. That question is answered by the vote count, not by the complaint. And the irony carries a name: Associate Attorney General Stanley Woodward, who signed the complaint, had represented people accused of participating in the Jan. 6 attack on the Capitol.

The pattern is not two men with the same surname. It is the conversion of campaign preference into legal command before a single vote is cast. Earlier this month, Trump and Vance backed [a bill letting merchants pick card networks](/articles/2026-10-09-trump-vance-back-bill-letting-merchants-pick-card-networks/). The common thread is presidential intervention in the rules themselves: the card-network fight targets the machinery of payments, the Alaska suit targets the machinery that turns votes into political power. The difference is that Congress can settle a card-network fight with a statute. Nobody in Washington can settle a suit in which the Attorney General asks a federal court to hold a State's certification of its own Senate race. A defense of the administration that did not require inventing an emergency would be simpler: name the registration provision Alaska is said to have broken, and sue for compliance. What the filing does instead is ask a judge for a brake on a vote count.

The seat matters. Democrat and former congresswoman Mary Peltola is running a tight race against Sullivan, and Republicans treat Alaska as essential to holding the Senate for the second half of Trump's term. Blanche insists the case is not meant to help the president overturn the midterms. Yet the operative question is what happens if Republicans do not win the seat they need. If they win, the lawsuit can look procedural. If they lose, the machinery Blanche calls voter-confusion protection becomes the administration's last stop before the result is accepted. The investigation can stay open. The certificate can stay delayed. The result can stay contested. And the "doomsday" Blanche describes is not that a few voters may confuse two men with the same surname. It is that the federal government may decide which state election results it is willing to recognize.

Its real product is uncertainty, and uncertainty is power. Alaska can distinguish two Sullivans. Washington apparently cannot accept that voters might.

The ballot should decide who holds the seat. If the Justice Department can withhold recognition whenever the result becomes politically intolerable, then it decides. That is the doomsday scenario. And Blanche's most alarming claim is not that the administration might intervene. It is that the administration believes it may lawfully do so.
