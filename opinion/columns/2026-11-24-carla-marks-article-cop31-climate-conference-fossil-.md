Fossil-fuel lobbying is not a side effect of the clean-energy transition; it is the machinery for replacing the transition with carbon capture and calling the delay a solution. Carbon capture is not a climate plan; it is a promise to keep selling fossil fuels while somebody else waits for the cleanup. A [ProPublica and Drilled investigation](https://www.propublica.org/article/cop31-climate-conference-fossil-fuels-carbon-capture) of COP31 in Antalya reports that oil- and gas-producing states, led by Saudi Arabia, blocked language on phasing out fossil fuels—a step scientists uniformly agree is a prerequisite—and pushed aggressive carbon-capture expansion instead. The November 16 draft called for removing at least 500 million tons of carbon dioxide a year by 2035. When the proposed accord arrived Sunday, two days late, the target had become a nonbinding “gigatonne scale” goal. The summit ended without consensus; a coalition including the European Union rejected the proposal. That is not a transition plan. It is delay with better branding.

Carbon capture is real technology. It can reduce emissions from industrial processes that are genuinely hard to eliminate, and nothing requires pretending the tool is useless. But the industry currently buries only a sliver of the promised amount and has spent three decades failing to meet even its lowest-end projections. A useful tool for a narrow job is not a substitute for ending the fossil-fuel system. The leap from “works in some places” to “big enough to keep the fossil-fuel business alive” turns climate policy into an industry sales kit.

The deadlock was not pure villainy. Low- and middle-income nations said they would support phaseout if wealthy economies financed the transition and the damage already caused. Wealthy industrial nations wanted the transition on record while resisting payment. Our [accounting of the unresolved climate-finance fight](/articles/2026-11-24-failed-cop31-talks-leave-climate-finance-and-fossil-fuel-plans-unresolved/) shows the money question ending where it began. An unfunded phaseout is a proposition the arithmetic refuses. Both positions will outlast the press releases.

At Antalya, about 700 carbon-capture lobbyists registered, up from 531 at last year’s summit in Brazil and more than the combined delegations of all 14 Pacific Small Island Developing States. Forty-five were admitted to closed-door negotiations, according to an analysis of United Nations participant lists by the Center for International Environmental Law. The center has not completed the same comparison for other technologies, so this is not proof that carbon capture had the only lobby in the room. It does show the scale. On the raw head count, the vendors outnumbered the customers whose houses are underwater. When that happens, the event has stopped being a negotiation and become a sales floor.

The imbalance was the fingerprint. “It really had the fossil fuel industry’s fingerprints all over it,” said Romain Ioualalen, global policy lead for Oil Change International.

What survived into the proposed accord was not the 500-million-ton target. It was a nonbinding “gigatonne scale” goal. No machine count. No enforceable obligation. A unit prefix. Giga, as in a hard drive. The document governing the atmosphere’s future had been written in the vocabulary of a product specification, because a product specification commits to nothing. Anyway.

One side was betting a coastline. The other was betting an adjective.

Vanuatu warned that the text “risks legitimizing continued fossil fuel dependence” and that carbon capture must not become “an excuse to prolong the fossil fuel era.” Panama could not support the language because it “wagers too much” on carbon capture “instead of calling for deep changes.” Saudi Arabia, speaking for the 22 countries in the League of Arab States, insisted that carbon capture was an essential part of a “package of solutions” and asked why “concrete solutions” would be taken off the table.

Tell me again how that is a compromise. A country near sea level is being handed the bill for someone else’s thirty-year delay.

Here is the missing line item. The investigation reports that most carbon capture operating today is used to squeeze additional oil from aging fields. The carbon goes down one hole; the oil comes up the other and gets burned. A global capture target attached to nothing else is therefore a purchase order: drill now, clean up someday—and someday has thirty years of missed deadlines on its résumé.

Michael Mann called this “moral hazard,” meaning that relying on a hypothetical future remedy compromises carbon capture as a climate solution. The cleanup is hypothetical. The drilling is paid. If carbon capture can truly carry a gigatonne, fine. Show us the machines.

The cost does not disappear. It moves. The fuel company keeps the revenue. The public and the atmosphere get the bill.

The investigation also found that industry funding contributed to influential carbon-capture research at universities, petrostates pressed the United Nations to validate the technology without supporting evidence, and modelers assumed it could work at enormous scale despite its troubled record. That is not a neutral forecast. It is a sales quota wearing a lab coat.

None of this means carbon capture has no role. It means that role cannot become an alibi. If the target is meant to reduce emissions, it must come with limits on new extraction. If it is meant to preserve extraction, the text should say so. The proposed text tried to have both. Rule out the hidden subsidy to extraction, or retract the claim that carbon capture can carry the transition. That is how delay becomes policy.

A clean transition does not need Antalya’s permission. It needs plumbing, and much of the plumbing already exists.

Start with a carbon fee that mails its own money home: charge the carbon where it leaves the ground and send each household its share. Then wire the transition the way America wired the countryside. In 1936, when private capital would not build rural electrification, Washington lent the money at low rates to member-owned cooperatives, and the cooperatives strung their own lines. About 900 rural electric cooperatives still serve 42 million Americans across 56 percent of the country’s landmass.

My grandfather sat on the board of one and would have laughed in your face if you had called it socialism, then gone back to arguing about transformer bids. That is not Scandinavia in disguise. It is a cooperative still humming in the middle of the country.

Build a public-power option around that template: publicly finance and own new generation and storage, distribute it through regulated utilities and member-owned cooperatives, and reward delivered clean power rather than extracted fuel. Patient capital matters. The Bank of North Dakota has been state-owned and profitable every year since 1919. Norway put its oil windfall into a fund worth more than $2 trillion, owns stakes in roughly 1.5 percent of the world’s listed companies, and has about $400,000 banked for each Norwegian citizen. It invests the windfall abroad rather than trying to spend it all at once.

One producing state did the opposite of hostage-taking. Texas struck oil too and got the boom and the bumper sticker. Alaska sends residents an annual dividend drawn from its oil wealth in a red state. A petrostate can manage its own transition. Remind me what the obstacle is again.

And the transition needs a bargain, not a slogan. If a plant closes, the community should not be told that “flexibility” means the worker absorbs the loss. Denmark’s flexicurity—easy firing, strong income security, and active retraining—lets firms fail without letting workers fall. America can borrow the principle: sectoral bargaining, funded job placement and retraining, and worker ownership in new energy firms.

It is harder here because collective-bargaining coverage is only about 11 percent, right-to-work laws have starved unions of resources, and federalism can turn one national goal into fifty jurisdictional arguments. We cannot copy a tap without building the pipes.

There are serious proposals to loosen a process in which any single producing state can hold the room; [our coverage collects them](/articles/2026-11-23-after-cop31-experts-propose-climate-talk-reform-and-fossil-fuel-action/). They are worth pursuing. But the table is slow, and the wires do not need the table’s permission. The American version is not an import. It is a federal financing floor, with the wiring done by institutions that already exist in the states: cooperatives, credit unions, public power, and worker ownership. Patient financing, trained regulators, and utility boards that answer to their members take years to build, not negotiating sessions.

I’m not anti-market. Carbon capture can be bought, sold, and deployed by private firms. I’m against extraction: against using a promise of cleanup to enlarge the business that creates the waste. Markets can compete on delivery while ownership still decides who gets the reward.

So what do we build instead of this? One package, and the pieces fit: a federal carbon fee charged where carbon leaves the ground; a per-household dividend that sends the money home; a public or cooperative transition fund financing the lines, grid, and factories; and community or worker ownership of generation and wires, with states and towns doing the siting and the work. Carbon capture can be one tool inside that transition. It cannot be the transition’s owner.

The energy system is a set of human choices, not weather. COP31 did not solve the climate crisis. It showed who wants to make delay institutional. We do not need the fossil-fuel industry’s permission to finance clean power, make utilities accountable to users, or limit the extraction that carbon capture was hired to excuse.

The islanders did the arithmetic out loud in Antalya, and the room declined to hear it. We can do the arithmetic ourselves. We’ve wired harder places than this. So build the public-power option: patient public capital, member-owned utilities, accountable grids, and a labor bargain for the people the transition moves. Who owns the thing? Start there. We’ve wired harder places than this.
