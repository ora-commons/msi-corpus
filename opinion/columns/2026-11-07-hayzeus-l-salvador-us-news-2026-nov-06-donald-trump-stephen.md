*The machine would run without Miller. He chose to run it faster.*

Stephen Miller tore children from their parents; now he runs the White House.

On Friday, days after voters rejected Republican candidates up and down the ballot — in blue states and deep-red ones, near the border and far from it — the president named him chief of staff. The voters had just repudiated the deportation campaign Miller built. Trump answered by promoting its architect to the most powerful staff post in the government, the latest turn in [a hard-line immigration record and a decade-long sweep of White House power](/articles/2026-11-06-stephen-miller-built-hard-line-immigration-record-and-sweep-of-white-house-power/).

The record is not in dispute, because he built it in public. He devised the travel restrictions on Muslim-majority countries that became known as the Muslim ban. He designed the zero-tolerance policy that separated more than 5,600 children from their parents and caregivers at the border. He constructed the legal framework that invokes the Alien Enemies Act, a wartime statute from 1798, to cast Venezuelan immigrants — asylum seekers among them — as foreign terrorists, so the government could expel them to a megaprison in El Salvador and strike their boats at sea. He pushed to speed the deportation of undocumented children and to all but shut down legal immigration. In 2018 he reportedly sank a deal that would have traded $25 billion for the wall in exchange for permanent protection for the young people brought here as children: the wall he wanted, the children he refused. When immigration agents shot and killed Alex Pretti, a nurse, amid the raids in Minneapolis, [Miller called the dead man a domestic terrorist](/articles/2026-11-06-stephen-miller-called-man-killed-by-immigration-agents-a-domestic-terrorist/) — and then, the Wall Street Journal reported, raised the idea of invoking the Insurrection Act and sending troops into American cities. The campaign he directs has put masked agents in military gear on residential streets and, the Guardian reported, tanks on them too. Arrests and detentions stand at an all-time high.

Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth called him a true patriot who never backs down. Faisal Al-Juburi of Raíces, a Texas humanitarian aid organization, said the appointment signals "a doubling down on the administration's cruelest impulses." Democrats promise oversight: Representative Robert Garcia, poised to lead the House committee, says Miller should lawyer up; Senator Alex Padilla promises every tool at their disposal — oversight, investigations, subpoenas, leverage over federal funding. I am glad they mean it. I will not pretend a subpoena is the same thing as justice.

A subpoena is not shelter. The enforcement agencies hold $70 billion through 2029, ICE is poised to expand its workforce on a massive scale, and the private companies that fill the beds have just posted profits in the hundreds of millions of dollars — one of them calls this the largest year of new business in its history. The tribunals the detained must face are not courts in any sense the Constitution would recognize: the immigration judges sit inside the Justice Department, serve at the Attorney General's pleasure, and carry thousands of cases apiece. A docket that size is not a hearing. It is a queue with a verdict at the end. Shelter has a known shape: the bishops of the United States and Mexico wrote it down in 2003 — the right to find opportunity in one's own homeland, the right to migrate when the homeland cannot feed a family, borders kept with dignity, protection for those who flee, the stranger among us treated as the citizen among us. Congress could fund immigration judges instead of detention beds. Apply those principles honestly and they convict administrations of both parties. No one owes this country open doors, and no one gave it license for this.

Neither man invented the machinery he now commands, and I am not the spotless accuser here. Adam Goodman, the historian who counted the machine's work, reckons roughly eight million people formally removed over the past century and some fifty million more driven out under what the government's own paperwork calls voluntary departure — a word that lies whenever the choice on offer is the desert or the bus. The party now promising subpoenas has run that machine in its own years. My own communion helped pour its foundation — parishes that sang of the stranger on Sunday and locked the doors on Monday; a school in El Paso where I watched my classmates' desks stand empty after the pre-dawn raids while the lesson went on as if nothing had happened. My tradition has a name for it — *pecado social*, structural sin — and it is not another man's property. I wore the uniform of the country that was already doing this before I understood what it was doing. The machine is older than any one man. That is no comfort to Stephen Miller. It is the measure of him: the machine would run without Miller, and he chose to run it faster.

The tradition I write from has no policy-debate column for this. It has a commandment, and the commandment is not obscure. "You shall not wrong or oppress a resident alien, for you were aliens in the land of Egypt." "The alien who resides with you shall be to you as the citizen among you; you shall love the alien as yourself." The Lord "executes justice for the orphan and the widow, and loves the stranger." The command to love the stranger appears in the Torah more often than any other commandment — thirty times and more — and it ends every time in the same place: you know the heart of the stranger, because it was yours. And the rabbi I follow said it more plainly than any of us has managed since: "I was a stranger and you welcomed me." And to the others: "I was a stranger and you did not welcome me. Truly I tell you, just as you did it to one of the least of these, you did it to me." So I will say what that tradition says, without the softer words that let this go on: tearing children from their parents is evil. Marking refugees as terrorists to expel them to a foreign prison is evil. Sending masked men through neighborhoods where children are asleep is evil, and it killed a nurse in Minneapolis. It is cruel, and it is sin, and Isaiah's woe falls on the ones who call evil good — this government calls it law and order. I am not calling these policies flawed or regrettable. I am calling them what they are.

Mr. Miller, you are a person, made by love and for love like the rest of us. You are also the descendant of immigrants, and your own family walked through that verse on its way to this country. Egypt is in your own house; the commandment was written for exactly that household. I am not mocking you. I am telling you the door is open, and the door is that verse. The commandment carries its own reason: for you were aliens in the land of Egypt. You have not forgotten who you are. All it asks is that you remember — and stop.

On the last Sunday of his life, March 23, 1980, Archbishop Óscar Romero stood in the cathedral in San Salvador — he was killed the next day — and preached to soldiers who had been told their own countrymen were the enemy. No soldier, he told them, is obliged to obey an order against the law of God; an immoral law, no one has to obey it; it is time to recover your conscience and obey it before the order of sin. Then he said the words that cost him his life: "In the name of God, in the name of this suffering people whose laments rise to heaven each day more tumultuous, I beg you, I beseech you, I order you in the name of God: stop the repression!" That sentence belongs to everyone the order passes through, from the desk where it is written to the mask in the street. It belongs to the agents in the masks; no order against the law of God binds them, and the conscience they were given outranks the order they were handed. It belongs to you, Mr. Miller. Cease. You can stop. Let the children be found and brought back. The ones already taken cannot be given back tonight. Tonight you can stop making more of them.

Tomorrow, before the light comes up, I will open the shop and repair one broken thing, the discipline that keeps my hands honest. There are 5,600 broken things here I cannot mend in a lifetime; a man can be mended in a morning. Then I will drive out to the bus station on the edge of town, where the families arrive carrying what is left, and stand there a while. The door stays open. Someone should be standing there when it does.
