John Healey is protecting the wealthy while abandoning families crushed by housing and energy bills.

At 12.30pm on Wednesday, Chancellor John Healey will stand in the Commons and unveil the Burnham government’s first budget. The Treasury’s preferred word is “low-key.” Its slogan is “breathing space for today, fresh hope for the future.”

For households, low-key has a more precise meaning: another winter of deciding which bill can wait.

The pressure surrounding [John Healey’s preparation for his first budget](/articles/2026-10-27-john-healey-prepares-his-first-budget-amid-war-driven-economic-pressure/) is real. The Iran war has raised borrowing costs and eroded the government’s spending power. But some of the constraints are self-imposed. Healey has rejected calls to raise capital gains tax or reduce mansion tax while promising to retain Labour’s fiscal rules. That narrows his options. It also reveals what this government values.

And “priorities” is simply the word for the second option.

Low-key can mean surgical: targeted at the bills crushing households and the incomes being squeezed by them. It need not mean small-minded. The danger is that the government will use modesty as cover for selectivity.

Healey has made much of the “military-style protocols” allegedly stopping the Treasury’s secrets from leaking, and there has been grumbling about how little is known about this budget compared with Rachel Reeves’s. It is more likely there was not much to leak. A budget this modest does not need a secrecy programme. It needs an argument, and it is not being given one.

The government may come to Parliament presiding over the worst parliament for living standards on record. If that is the record it is writing, it cannot then treat household finances as a technical problem beyond its control.

Consider Michael, who works for a small property company and rents a draughty three-bedroom bungalow in Winchester with his three children. He spends about £165 a month on electricity and gas. His rent is £1,800. After tax, his income is between £2,500 and £3,000 a month, leaving him at most £300 each week in a good month to feed and clothe four people.

His question is simple: “Will I get enough money to be able to afford this? Will I have enough when the rent is due?”

Michael is not rich. He is not comfortably poor either. He occupies the troublesome middle ground: too working and too squeezed for targeted help, but not wealthy enough to absorb another energy bill.

He receives no benefits, which means he does not qualify for the government’s £150 Warm Homes Discount. The Treasury is considering making that discount more generous. Good. But Michael is precisely the household the present eligibility rules miss.

He is not an edge case. StepChange, the UK’s largest debt advice charity, found that average arrears debt among its clients rose from £2,833 in 2022 to £4,345 in 2025. One in two people who now come to it have energy debt. A StepChange spokesperson said, “These pressures are completely new.”

These are not abstractions. They are missed bills, threatened food and heat.

Anne Helen Petersen is blunt: “Burnout isn’t a personal problem, and it will not be cured by a productivity app or a face mask.” Michael is not failing to optimize his way out of this. He is carrying the consequences of policy choices made by people with much larger budgets and much smaller bills.

The government’s response is a mixture of immediate relief and long-term promises. It plans to abolish the cliff edge that has pushed unpaid carers into debt, has promised winter energy support, and is expected to announce another £1bn for council-housebuilding. It has also removed import tariffs on products including baked beans, chocolate and gherkins.

Some of this is useful. Much of it is delayed, peripheral or too small to alter household arithmetic.

The tariff changes, for example, will save the average household £21 on its yearly food shop. That is roughly 40p a week. Michael tries to limit his weekly Aldi shop to £80; a saving of that size will barely register. Food prices, meanwhile, have repeatedly stayed high after the crises that pushed them upward.

One measure deserves more than a list entry. The cliff edge that withdraws unpaid carers’ allowance the moment earnings cross a threshold has done exactly what cliff edges do: it puts a wall between taking paid work and keeping the support that paid for everything else. Abolishing it ends the trap for the carers being dropped off. It is the clearest case in this budget of a government repairing a known harm with money it has already found. It should be the template for everything else, not the exception.

The deeper problem is energy. The government considers making the Warm Homes Discount more generous, but a discount cannot repair an energy market that leaves vulnerable households paying extraordinary prices. Immediate relief for this winter must accompany a structural plan for reducing bills over the medium term.

The news from the Gulf matters to a household more than most Treasury announcements. Gulf states are working on a multipronged deal designed to avert a renewal of the war between Iran and the United States: it would reopen the Strait of Hormuz within four days of sanctions being lifted and require the Houthis to withdraw substantial forces from inside Yemen. It is a plan, not a settlement, and nobody can guarantee it.

But it shows how far the price of the energy this budget must relieve can move without the Chancellor’s consent. A budget promising winter relief while the price of the energy underneath it moves on a Gulf negotiation is a promise with a weather forecast attached.

Common Wealth, a thinktank cited as an influence on this government, has argued for precisely that combination: funded relief now and a mechanism for reducing bills later. Its director, Mathew Lawrence, has floated an “energy guarantee”—a protected block of energy priced at pre-crisis levels.

That is the kind of policy households can plan around. Healey should announce it with a published standard explaining who it protects, how much of a typical bill it covers and what it costs. A guarantee without that standard is a slogan with a Treasury logo on it.

Taylor Swift’s “You’re On Your Own, Kid” can be read here—not as though the song were written about British fiscal policy, but as a description of what happens when care becomes the household’s private burden. The title line does more work than the Treasury slogan because it identifies the arrangement without blaming the person forced to live inside it.

Which brings us to the political choices the government has refused to make. If it will not tax wealth and will not alter its fiscal rules, it cannot complain that households must shoulder the burden of failure. The money has to come from somewhere: linking housing allowance to rents and widening winter energy support must be funded either through the taxes the government has ruled out or from its day-to-day spending envelope.

The government’s continued freezing of fuel duty, first announced by Rishi Sunak in 2022, is a blunt choice. It disproportionately helps higher-income households because they drive more, while removing revenue the Treasury could use to help poorer households directly. Michael’s problem is not petrol. It is heating.

An Aberdeen driver who relies on a car to get around is also being penalized by rising petrol prices. Targeted help with energy bills would do more for drivers with low incomes than a universal tax cut that rewards distance and consumption.

The government has also paused the equalisation of the lower minimum wage for under-21s with the national living wage, drawing criticism. It can preserve a broad petrol subsidy while pausing earned progress at the bottom. That is not caution. That is a hierarchy of priorities.

Housing offers the clearest division between a long-term promise and an immediate necessity. Sue, a reader in west Kent, wants more council housing. Fine. Burnham has committed Labour to the biggest council-housebuilding programme since the postwar decade, Healey is expected to announce another £1bn for it, and details of Your First Home, a replacement for Help to Buy, may follow.

Those measures matter. They will not reduce the rent of families needing somewhere to live now.

The immediate lever is local housing allowance, frozen since 2024. The Resolution Foundation finds that more than 900,000 privately rented households on universal credit face a shortfall between their rent and housing allowance. For a family in a two-bedroom home, the average shortfall is £158 a month.

£158 is not an administrative rounding error. It is food, heating and transport. No amount of future housebuilding excuses freezing support for families already paying today.

Public investment is where a cautious government can still make a decisive choice. [Andrea Egan has urged Burnham to put public investment in the budget](/articles/2026-10-26-andrea-egan-urges-burnham-to-put-public-investment-in-his-budget/). Council housing, warmer homes and energy infrastructure are not decorative extras. They are how a government changes the material conditions in which people live rather than merely announcing that it feels their pain.

Homes and energy infrastructure cannot be conjured out of a newsletter. Public investment must accompany immediate relief, not substitute for it. Families cannot heat their homes on the promise of cheaper energy several years hence.

A serious first budget would do three things. First, it would extend immediate energy assistance beyond rigid benefit boundaries, taking account of income and family size, so Michael is not excluded simply because he lacks the right form of poverty. Second, it would link local housing allowance to rents again. Third, it would put serious money behind a published energy guarantee, paired with warmer homes and other long-term investment.

In the vocabulary of the corporal works of mercy, feeding the hungry, sheltering the homeless and clothe the naked are material obligations, not branding. A government claiming to prioritise “breathing space” while leaving Michael short by hundreds each month is not choosing caution. It is choosing which families must go cold.

The test of the budget will not be whether John Healey sounded radical in the Commons. It will be whether a family paying £1,800 in rent and £165 a month for gas and electricity can keep the heating on without choosing between warmth and food.

If the answer is no, this will not merely be a low-key budget. It will be a budget that has made austerity respectable again. The government does not need a revolution to be fair. It needs the nerve to tax wealth, protect renters and treat energy bills as the emergency they have become.

The Winchester renter asked for a bill he can afford. The west Kent reader asked for a secure home. The Aberdeen driver asked for help getting to work. None wanted a treatise on fiscal rules, a red box or a slogan about hope. They wanted the state to use the powers it has.

Until Healey says what the energy guarantee protects, how much it costs and what a warmer home means in cash, “breathing space for today” is not relief. It is an admission that today still hurts. The money is not in the adjectives.
