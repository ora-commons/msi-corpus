Swaim disguises Podolsky’s market bet as a national moral command.

In the November 20, 2026 *Wall Street Journal* Opinion column “American Heroism Could Return to the Screen,” Barton Swaim presents Ryan Podolsky’s plan to revive films about courage, agency, and American character as a cultural necessity rather than a market experiment. The profile builds a chain from cinematic taste to national character, from box-office success to civic proof, and from the films’ promise of restored agency to restored public trust in institutions. The excerpts below remain in document order; the commentary between them marks where the frame changes hands. What work is the profile doing? The machinery appears in the order of the sentences. It lets Podolsky’s confidence substitute for evidence and lets the profile present the result as a natural national mood.

The question is not whether heroic films are good; it is how a preference becomes a civic command without losing a step. Nothing in the record before us shows a coordinated campaign, and the column should not be made larger than its evidence. The operation is simpler: a taste made to look like a moral summons. Swaim and Podolsky have a real case: courage matters, and a movie can honor it without pretending the conditions that produce suffering are innocent. The trouble begins when that case carries a conclusion it cannot reach. The receipts here do not prove a private motive. They show the operation on the page.

I helped build versions of this operation in the cable years. I wrote for the apparatus that needed preference to pass as principle. That is why I can see where the frame changes hands.

> I can’t be alone in disliking movies that portray America as a nation in the grip of some dark cabal that twists its policies for dishonorable purposes. Such films entertain—who doesn’t enjoy the “Bourne” series?—but they don’t portray the United States of reality. Americans’ capacity for self-criticism probably enables us to enjoy a movie like “Three Days of the Condor” (1975), a spy thriller starring Robert Redford in which the murderous villains work for the Central Intelligence Agency. Similarly, I enjoyed the series “24” (2001-14) and “The Blacklist” (2013-23), both of which imagine malevolent cabals inside the U.S. government. Still, I can’t help finding offensive the suggestion, albeit fictional, that high-ranking American officials routinely betray their country.

**Attribution of blame — Bandura: attribution of blame — operates here through the word “offensive.”** It asks the reader to treat skepticism about high-ranking officials as an offense against the country before the conduct of those officials has been examined. The paragraph also performs moral justification — Bandura: moral justification — by turning America’s capacity for self-criticism into a certificate of national health.

The paragraph supplies its own evidence. Admiring an old thriller remains respectable because the country is said to possess enough critical capacity; a story about malevolent officials inside government becomes an insult. This is moral inoculation. Give the audience a safe dose of dissent, then certify the body as healthy. The omitted relation is between enjoying criticism and the conduct of institutions; those are not the same thing. People can think an institution capable of serious wrongdoing without ceasing to love the country. The protected beneficiary is confidence in official power. The normalized harm is a reader whose legitimate skepticism has been made to look like an attack on the nation. This is the loyalty test, and it is the moral-inoculation scam.[^1]

So here is what Barton Swaim’s profile actually does, taken together.

It does not need to lie at every joint. It begins with a legitimate appetite—American films that give people room to act courageously—and ends by making that appetite a civic test. The appetite is real in the only sense the record establishes: audiences paid to watch brave people win. The money is real. The courage is real. What changes is the relation among them. A profitable appetite for reassurance becomes evidence of a moral mandate; the people who make the product become the people who discovered the nation’s meaning.

From the opening, it converts the appetite for self-critical entertainment into a certificate of national health. Then it recruits a producer whose company is already committed to the thesis, converts a global-franchise market into cultural surrender, treats commercial success as civic proof, and lets two founders’ beer-drinking impressions stand in for market research. It then gives the operation the finish it wants: exceptional individuals solve hard problems, the movies restore national agency, and the institutions behind the abandonment recede behind the heroes who repair it.

Who benefits? The answer is not mystical. Rough Cut benefits if the appetite for high-agency American stories is real. Podolsky benefits if his market thesis is correct. The operation’s other beneficiary is the market for films built around national restoration. The reader is asked to supply the final proof by treating heroic films as a substitute for examining the institutions that made the stories possible.

The omitted relation is the same throughout.

Power, incentives, decisions, consequences. Present in the world. Absent from the frame.

The protected beneficiary is the operator whose preferred interpretation survives. The normalized harm is a citizen left with the nation’s problems translated into character. Agency becomes a shot of a strong person solving a hard situation. Consequences become somebody else’s cut. A complicated history becomes a clean film.

Patriotism need not be the problem, and a heroic film need not be. The question is whether the story arranges every hard relation so that courage becomes the only visible cause. If it does, the story reruns the operation Swaim praises: individual agency becomes the alibi for a system no one has to answer for. If the film industry has abandoned American stories, the question is who made the industrial choices, what they rewarded, and what they displaced. If Americans distrust their institutions, the question is what the institutions did—not whether a story gives somebody else a brave face. Swaim’s profile does the opposite. It makes moral revival a matter of better characters because that is the market Rough Cut wants. No secret memo is required. The sequence makes preference look like proof.

Read the next heroic film, then read the silence around it. Who supplies the money? Which interests sit outside the frame? Who bears the cost? When the film offers a slogan built for repetition, ask what has been made housekeeping. A country does not become decent because the camera finds the right hero. It becomes legible when the camera stays on the hand that chose, the cost that followed, and the people who paid. That is how a taste stops being a taste and starts impersonating a national calling.
