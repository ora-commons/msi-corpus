Nobody snuck THC candy onto gas-station shelves. Congress legalized the plant, wrote no rules for the drug, and the market did exactly what markets do. Nebraska Sen. Pete Ricketts tells it differently in his Fox News column, [Congress promised to close the THC loophole. Our kids are still waiting](https://www.foxnews.com/opinion/congress-promised-close-thc-loophole-kids-still-waiting): the 2018 Farm Bill opened a loophole, "bad actors" flooded stores with delta-8 dressed as candy, poisonings among children under five are up more than 1,000 percent, and after August's delay Congress must now close it "once and for all." He is right about the poisoning. He is wrong about the cure. The distance between those two sentences is the whole column.

Start with what the senator has right, because it is most of the body count. Per the Journal of the American Medical Association, more than one in ten high school seniors has used hemp-derived THC, and dependence forms in young brains at nearly twice the rate it does in adults. In Omaha, a three-year-old and a four-year-old found gummies in their mother's purse and ate 1,500 milligrams of THC packaged to pass for Sour Patch Kids. Both children recovered. In Virginia, a four-year-old ate his mother's gummies and died. The National Institutes of Health ties the more than 1,000 percent rise in accidental THC poisonings of children under five to edibles built to look and taste like candy. There is no joke in this column about that child, and no argument that makes it okay. The products as sold — no age check, no lab test, a dose you can't predict from one piece to the next — are indefensible. That part of his piece is simply true, and I won't pretend otherwise. The Farm Bill's drafters meant rope, grain, and a lifeline for honest hemp farmers. Nobody in that room voted for a candy aisle.

Now watch what the senator does with his own evidence. He writes that the products "don't have an age restriction." He writes that the candy mimics are "rarely legally regulated or lab tested," and that the dose inside a bag "can be spread unevenly." He has diagnosed, in print, exactly three missing rules: an age check, a lab test, a label. Uneven dosing is what lab testing exists to catch. He walks up to the fix, describes it accurately, and moves on — to prescribing the deletion of the market, which is not a fourth rule. It is a pledge that there will never be one. Before he votes to delete the market, he owes us the fourth rule his own diagnosis just demanded.

Here is how a poison aisle grows. The 2018 Farm Bill drew its legal line at delta-9 THC — the classic marijuana molecule — and called everything below it hemp. Labs read the fine print and learned to brew delta-8 out of legal hemp, a close cousin that still gets you high. Congress named one molecule; the market renamed itself one molecule over. After that the shelf filled itself, because nothing on the books said it couldn't. Age limits are laws. No one wrote one. Lab testing is law. No one wrote it. Packaging standards are law. Same story. There was no loophole. There was a building with no walls, and people moved in and started selling. Actors need a script, and Congress supplied the only one available: if it isn't forbidden, it's for sale.

The senator calls the sellers "bad actors." In a market with no rules, "bad actor" just means "fastest." The sellers skipped the cost of testing, packaging, and licensing, and the discount is being paid, with interest, by three-year-olds. A business that only works because somebody else pays the hospital bill is not a market. It's a cost with a store attached. This is what I mean when I say I'm not anti-market. I'm anti-extraction. Markets don't supervise themselves. The missing rules are what let this one do it to a toddler.

And the surprise — "the results are drastically different from what we anticipated" — deserves a receipt of its own. He was governor of Nebraska when he signed his state's copy of that arrangement, a hemp bill written expressly to align with the 2018 Farm Bill, and his column lists his achievements as tax cuts and making government "work better" — a fair one-line history of how a shelf ends up unwatched. Federal health agencies were flagging delta-8 within a couple of years of the Farm Bill. The results were never unpredictable. They were unwritten. You can legalize a category and skip the rules, but the chemistry doesn't skip anything. None of that makes him indifferent to the children; I believe he means every word about the kids. But a man who signed the arrangement does not get to be surprised by what an ungoverned shelf produces.

Which brings us to his remedy, and I'd like you to count. The column proposes exactly one move: close the loophole. No age limit. No testing. No childproof package. No potency cap. No ban on dressing an intoxicant up as children's candy. One move. Zero rules. A villain and a deadline — "we gave this industry a full year" — because a villain-and-deadline story has no page for a rulebook. Closing the loophole strips hemp-derived THC of its federal legal status, and I'll grant what that buys: legal shelves empty out. Then ask what happens to demand, which does not read statutes. America ran this experiment on liquor. Prohibition didn't dry up the drinking; it took the labels and the age checks off the bottles, and people went blind and died on wood alcohol — poison in precisely the shape he is trying to prevent, produced by the vacuum he wants to widen. When we finally fixed it, we didn't keep the ban. We wrote the rules — an age limit, licensed sellers, a standardized bottle — and that rulebook has kept whiskey off the toddler circuit for ninety years. A ban also redefines a molecule brewed out of the crop his own hemp law was written to rescue, so Nebraska's grain and fiber farmers learn what collateral damage feels like unless the drafting is surgical. Surgical drafting, it turns out, is just rules by another name. What we have now — neither legal-with-rules nor actually banned — is the worst of both, and both parties built it.

Meanwhile the governed version of this exact market already exists, on shelves, today. Every state that has legalized cannabis for adults cards the buyer at twenty-one, lab-tests every batch, and ships it in childproof packaging, because the rules require it and the rules hold. We do the same for whiskey: licensed sellers, age checks, a label that says what's in the bottle. In Sweden, where my mother's family is from, every drop of the hard stuff is sold through one plain government store — a room that looks like a pharmacy, advertises nothing, cards everybody, doors locked on Sundays. Every childhood summer at my aunt's table outside Gothenburg, the wine for Friday dinner came from that store, and my cousins treat it the way I treat the post office. Norway runs one too. And before anyone says that could never happen here: more than a dozen American states already sell liquor through state stores. Utah has run one for generations, and nobody has ever put Salt Lake City behind an Iron Curtain. Nothing about it is utopia. It's plumbing. The alternative to the poison aisle is not prohibition, and it is not surrender. It is a counter with an adult behind it. Remind me what the obstacle is again. Why it's harder here: the hemp industry will sue, and a Congress that can't pass a one-sentence age limit isn't about to charter a store chain. Harder is not impossible. It's the same distance it always is — one honest bill.

We even solved candy-shaped poison on purpose once, and the result sits in your medicine cabinet. In 1970, after toddlers kept eating brightly colored pills, Congress passed the Poison Prevention Packaging Act — the childproof cap — and child poisoning deaths fell sharply. Not because aspirin was banned. Because the bottle got a rule. The tool is over fifty years old. Nobody ever wrote it down for gummies. The hemp version writes itself: twenty-one and over, licensed sellers, third-party lab tests with published results, childproof packaging, dose caps, a flat prohibition on dressing a drug as a brand of candy — which is what the Sour Patch Kids lookalike is, poisoning plus theft — and funding for the inspectors and poison centers that make the rules real. A number of states have already written age limits for delta-8; Congress could start by copying its own states, and could draft the rest before the next recess. What has stopped it is the honest part: governing a shelf costs money — inspectors, labs, poison centers — and the same Senate that couldn't hold a one-year deadline on a poison aisle finds money fast when it wants something. The vacuum doesn't maintain itself. The senator's menu has two items on it, the loophole and the ban. The third item is already open for business. Card them. Test them. Cap them. Childproof them.

The senator is right that November 12 should not have slipped. He is wrong about what was supposed to happen when it arrived. He tells an industry to get "in line with the new law," but a deadline is not a rulebook, and there is no line to stand in when the law never drew one. And if he believes a ban alone keeps candy off shelves, he is welcome to name the intoxicant that has ever managed it. The economy is a set of choices, not the weather. Somebody chose a drug market with no age check, no lab, and no label. Somebody can choose the counter with the adult behind it — the one already working in every legal state, every liquor store in Lincoln, and behind the plain gray storefront in my aunt's town. The difference between a poison aisle and a working market is a page of law and the staff to enforce it, and a four-year-old should not have to die while the Senate pretends that page hasn't been written a hundred times before. The industry got a year. The rules have never gotten a day.
