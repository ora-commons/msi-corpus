The Followers of Christ buries its children in the name of faith. Not a metaphor, and not an accident. Children in the Oregon congregation kept dying of treatable conditions — and *treatable* is the word doing all the work in that sentence — until Oregon changed its laws so that a parent's faith could no longer stand between a sick child and a doctor. Across the line in Idaho, where the same church keeps its other congregations, the legislature has declined to make the same change. In Idaho, faith still functions as a legal shield. So the question is the one the shield exists to make unaskable: a shield against what, and for whom?

ProPublica and The Oregonian released a documentary this week, part of their series "By Faith Alone," built on Marshall Phillips, a sixth-generation member of the sect. When his eleven-year-old son was stricken with a dire health crisis, Phillips stood exactly where his church's creed put him — between his faith, his family, and a state that had finally begun to crack down on parents who leave a child's survival to prayer. A decade later, the church asked Phillips to care for a newborn whose twin had died because her parents refused to seek medical attention. One twin died within hours of her birth. Her sister was alive and struggling to breathe. A newborn who cannot breathe is among the most routine emergencies in medicine — warmth, oxygen, antibiotics; the machinery is neither exotic nor rare — and this church's creed had labeled that machinery unbelief. The documentary opens with the question the sect hands every parent they reach: "What if you had to pick between your deeply held faith and the life of your child?" That opposition is not in the text. Somebody built it — a reading that sets God against the doctor and faith against the child — and Idaho has made it a legal shield.

I know the machine that produces a reading like this, because I have sat inside it. I have been in the Bible-study room on a Wednesday night, a photocopied handout going around the circle, where a passage was read for what the room needed it to say rather than for what it says, and where noticing the difference would have cost you the fellowship. The apparatus does not require a shuttered congregation in Oregon City. It requires only that the plain sense be set aside once and the community's conclusion installed in its place. The Followers of Christ have carried that installation further down the road than most — far enough that their creed bars medical treatment outright. That installation had an architect. The series' companion profile of [the man who dominated and shaped the sect](https://www.propublica.org/article/walter-white-faith-healing-oregon) — Walter White, the faith-healing preacher whose forbidding vision the reporting lays out — gives the legalism a face, and the face is younger than the text it claims to enforce.

The sect's creed bars medicine, and a creed that bars what Scripture nowhere forbids still has to claim Scripture for the prohibition. So let me show you the verse such creeds reach for — and then read it plainly. James 5:14–15 says — I quote the King James throughout; it is the English this tradition was raised on — "Is any sick among you? let him call for the elders of the church; and let them pray over him, anointing him with oil in the name of the Lord: and the prayer of faith shall save the sick." Read it plainly, the way you would read it if no one had first taught you what it was supposed to mean. It commands prayer. It commands oil. It does not mention physicians — not once, not in the verse, not in the chapter, not anywhere in the letter. An instruction to call the elders and pray over the sick man is an instruction to add something to the sickbed. The captured reading turns it into a command to subtract everything else from the sickbed. That is the interpretation-machinery at full work: a text that orders prayer *alongside* oil has been made to forbid medicine *entirely*, and the prohibition exists nowhere in the words on the page. The verse forbids nothing. The doctrine is younger than the verse, and the doctrine is what let the twin die.

I knew this verse the way you know a hymn. I grew up where the anointing oil lived in a cabinet in the parsonage, where the healing line formed at the altar rail and the elders moved down it with their thumbs and the little flask. I taught the oil verse the other way for years. I am not exempt from this; I am its product, and the church I come from is gentler than Oregon City's — and gentler is not the same as innocent.

Let me put one exam room next to the documentary. A woman I will call Patricia — that is not her name, and she has given me permission to write about her if I disguise her — sat across from me with a hemoglobin A1C that had climbed from 9.2 to 12.6 in two years. The climb began after a revival. Her pastor preached Mark 11:24 — "believe that ye receive them, and ye shall have them" — and a paraphrase of Jeremiah 17:5, cursed is the one who trusts in man, and he aimed both barrels at her metformin. Metformin costs a few dollars a month at any drugstore. The ulcer that opened over the third metatarsal head of her foot was that sermon made flesh. The pastor's theology is load-bearing on a wound that will not close. Bless his heart, but he is wrong about the metformin, and he is wrong about Jeremiah 17:5. What the Followers of Christ does to a newborn in hours, this gentler arithmetic of the same creed does across years, in exam rooms all over the South, to people who love Jesus sincerely and were taught that love looks like a closed door.

The verse that indicts this creed is not the oil verse. It is the one Jesus told about the wounded man. A lawyer stood up to test him — what shall I do to inherit eternal life? — and got the two great commandments, and then, Luke says, "willing to justify himself," asked the question that has been the church's favorite question ever since: "And who is my neighbour?" The Greek is *plesion* — the one near you. Jesus answered with a man who went down from Jerusalem to Jericho and fell among thieves, stripped, wounded, left half dead. A priest saw him and passed by on the other side. A Levite came and looked, and passed by on the other side. Then a Samaritan came — the outsider, the man whose very name the lawyer could not bring himself to say — and had compassion, and bound up his wounds, pouring in oil and wine. The oil was not decorative. In the medicine of that day, oil and wine were what you put on an open wound: oil to soften and seal it, wine to cleanse it. The Samaritan anoints the stranger with the same element James commands for the sick, and he uses it as treatment. Then he loaded the man on his own animal, carried him to an inn, stayed the night taking care of him, paid the innkeeper two days' wages, and promised to settle whatever more it cost on his way back.

Read the parable plainly, because the captured reading has sanded it down to a mood. The captured reading makes the Good Samaritan a story about being kind when a need happens along. The plain reading makes it a story about purchasing professional care for a stranger, transporting him, paying the bill, and guaranteeing the follow-up. The Samaritan did not pray over the wounded man and go on to Jericho. He treated the wound with the medicine of his day, and Jesus named that the answer to "who is my neighbor," and ordered the lawyer — ordered us — "Go, and do thou likewise." And the two men who walked past were the religious professionals, the credentialed, the pure. The twin's parents stood where the priest and the Levite stood: they saw the child and passed by on the other side, because the operation had taught them that reaching the child through a doctor was the sin. I am not writing this to sneer at those parents. They believed they were obeying God, because that is what capture does — it teaches the captured that rescue is the disobedience. The operation taught the walking past, and then Idaho built the operation a law so the walking past would carry no consequences. You cannot read Luke 10:25–37 plainly and teach that medicine is unbelief. Jesus put oil on a stranger's wound and paid for his recovery, and he made the payer the hero of the story.

When Jesus named the physician, he named him without scorn. Mark 2:16–17: when the scribes of the Pharisees saw Jesus eating with the tax collectors and sinners, they demanded of his disciples why he ate with such people. He answered them, and the King James I grew up on puts it plainer than any modern rendering: "They that are whole have no need of the physician, but they that are sick." Notice who is speaking and who is being answered. Jesus names the physician in the middle of a quarrel with legalists — men who had come to police the boundaries of acceptable religious company. The physician stands inside his picture of the world. The sick are the whole point of him. A doctrine that forbids medicine owes an account of why its Lord claimed the physician's work so freely as his own metaphor for his own mission, and the doctrine has no account, because there is none to give.

And the creed did not come down from the apostles. The church that produced these texts built hospitals — the Cappadocian fathers' hospitals onward — and carried a tradition of Christian medicine for well over a thousand years before anyone organized a congregation around the refusal of physicians. The Augustinian tradition has held, from the earliest centuries, that grace works through means — that the means are not unbelief. The forbidding vision that governs the Followers of Christ belongs to a particular preacher, and Walter Brueggemann had a name for what settled religion becomes when it hardens into that shape: royal consciousness. And he named its symptom exactly — numbness about death. A faith that has decided this world is under judgment will grow numb to the deaths of its own children, because it has re-labeled the deaths as faithfulness.

Oregon took the shield away. Idaho kept it. The Idaho legislature has done what the religious-liberty era keeps doing at larger scales: it has written a theological claim into law that the medical evidence refutes. The Supreme Court performed the same move in *Burwell v. Hobby Lobby*, legally inscribing four FDA-approved contraceptives as abortifacients while declining to say whether the belief behind the inscription was mistaken. Same architecture. Smaller scale. Deadlier consequence. And the instinct to shield the institution before the child runs wherever religious institutions are asked to account — this summer the Supreme Court left in place [a $400,000 fine against the lawyer who flagged a priest's past](/articles/2026-07-01-supreme-court-leaves-400-000-fine-in-place-against-lawyer-who-flagged-priest-s-p/), the pattern holding steady: protect the operation, charge the one who tells the truth. If a progressive congregation took up the same doctrine tomorrow — prayer replaces insulin, faith forbids the psychiatrist — I would read the verses the same way, in the same space, at the same volume. My standard is the text, not the team.

Frederick Douglass named the distinction I am pointing at, in the appendix to his *Narrative* — the Christianity of this land set against the Christianity of Christ. I cite him as a reader whose vantage I borrow with the deference owed to his own tradition, not as my authority. But the distinction holds without him: a reading that locks a sick child out of a doctor's care is not a hard saying of Jesus. It is a custom with a verse attached.

Now, to the reader in the pew, because I am not writing this at you. I am writing it beside you, at a kitchen table with the Bible open. I am not telling you that God does not heal. I do not presume to know what God does with an anointed prayer, and no column of mine will collapse that mystery into a political position. But I can tell you what the verses say. The oil verse commands prayer with oil and forbids nothing. Jesus blessed the physician's office in his own metaphor for his own mission. The neighbor he praised bound wounds, poured in oil and wine, paid the innkeeper, and came back. And in Matthew 25:36 the King at the judgment says, "I was sick, and ye visited me" — *visited*, the plain word, the hands-on word — and the sheep are not sorted by what they believed about medicine. They are sorted by what they did for the sick. The series title is better theology than the sect that hosts it: by faith alone was an answer about how a soul stands justified before God, and it was never an instruction about a doctor's office.

Marshall Phillips chose his son. The state and one ex-believer stepped in for the twin who was struggling to breathe, and they saved her. Idaho still has the church, the creed, and the defense, and the legislature can unmake the shield this session; that is a choice, not a fate. Friend, if you read this Bible at your kitchen table the way I do, you already know that no page of it hands a parent a permission slip for the locked door. And I keep coming back to the verse the faith-healing pulpit skips: Lazarus came out of the tomb because Jesus told him to, and because someone unwrapped him. Both. The unwrapping is not less grace because it had hands on it.
