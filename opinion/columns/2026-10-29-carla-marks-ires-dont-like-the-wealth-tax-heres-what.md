The one thing that can unite billionaires across the political spectrum is the prospect of paying a tax on their own fortunes. They disagree about everything else — markets, politics, whether it's better to own a sports team or a social network. Put a wealth tax in front of them and they become one party: the party of not paying. [Proposition 40 would raise $100 billion for California health care](/articles/2026-10-23-proposition-40-aims-to-raise-100-billion-for-california-health-care/), and rather than fund it, four of the richest people in America have now volunteered, on the record, how they would like to be taxed instead. Read the menu carefully, because the consistency is the story: every single dish is priced to hit somebody else.

Start with the threat. The ultrawealthy and entrepreneurs will flee, the billionaires say; the tax base erodes; competitiveness dies. It is the oldest ultimatum in the modern economy, and notice what it concedes. Not that the tax is unfair. Not that the spending is unworthy. Only this: we're here on the condition that you don't tax us. That's a hostage note with a P.O. box in another state, not an argument. The billionaires who would rather "focus on rooting out wasteful government spending or improving the lot of the less well-off" are proposing to make the world better in the one way that never requires them to pay for it. Then come the alternatives — all of them serious, all of them generous, all of them somewhere else. Together they amount to a master class in how to be photographed volunteering to pay more while writing a check for less.

John Arnold is the most honest man in the room. "Turning a couple knobs on the existing code," the energy investor and philanthropist calls it: tax long-term capital gains at the same rate as ordinary income, treat gains as realized at gift or death, index the original cost of investments to inflation — which would hand him back a deduction on gains he never took. It's a real program. Taxing income from labor and income from capital alike would do more for basic tax justice than a century of commissions. He'd even come for sports-team owners, axing the tax benefits that let them write off much of the value of their teams. "If someone's worth a trillion," he says, "they need to pay a share of that in taxes and make sure that gets recycled back into government services." Note the target. For a second, the pile itself is in the room. But the one moment Arnold reaches for a tax on a pile of wealth rather than a stream of income, he aims it at a category he can exit by selling a team — a door he'd close, not a reservoir he'd drain. Even the billionaire who wants the rich to pay a fair share is proposing to tax the stream, never the reservoir.

Mark Cuban is the businessman's version, and more candid still. He opposes a wealth tax because it might "stunt investment in startups and small businesses" — the man who has been paying his own employees in equity since 1983 suddenly discovered the small-business owner's cause. His two reforms: raise the 1% tax on corporate buybacks, which he concedes won't change how companies behave, and tax windfall profits above $1 billion a transaction, the exact structure that would apply to his sale of the Dallas Mavericks. And how would that windfall tax be paid? "The higher taxes could be deducted before the seller receives the sale proceeds." "It's less painful and doesn't change behavior." There is the philosophy in a sentence. The standard for a good tax, by Cuban's lights, is that rich people behave exactly as they'd behave without it and settle up quietly on the way out. A wealth tax is the opposite: it makes wealth itself carry a cost, every year, and it forces the holder to ask what the pile is actually for. That's the behavior Cuban's version exists to protect. A tax you collect from yourself, in advance, against money you were already going to get is not a tax. It is a rounding error with a press release.

Jeff Bezos, asked in May what he'd change, proposed eliminating federal income tax for the bottom half of workers — everyone up to about $53,801 in adjusted gross income, per the Tax Foundation's analysis of 2023 IRS data, many of whom already pay no income tax at all. On its face it's a genuinely good idea. As a substitute for taxing wealth, it's the cheapest possible gesture from a man in his bracket and a masterwork of misdirection: he pays for nothing, the working family pays for nothing, and the Treasury absorbs the difference. Meanwhile he added that vilifying the ultrawealthy won't help anyone, and that higher taxes on them wouldn't be enough. There is the standard for you: unless a tax solves every problem at once, don't bother trying it. The world's richest men proposing tax relief for poor people while opposing the one tax that would actually reach them — that isn't generosity. It's the rich serving as their own charity while the Treasury serves them.

Bill Ackman came closest to real territory. In December, on social media, the New York investor attacked "buy, borrow, die" — borrowing tax-free against appreciating assets, holding them until death, and handing the gains to heirs untouched. He would tax personal loans that exceed the assets' original cost basis, unless they're short-term. Right villain, right fix. And that is the real loophole, because closing it costs him nothing today and costs his heirs everything after death. But note where he, too, lands: against the wealth tax. Tax the loan, the gain, the buyback, the sale — every shadow the wealth throws. Never the wealth.

That is the one thing the alternatives all have in common. Arnold taxes income evenly. Cuban taxes buybacks and windfalls. Ackman taxes clever loans. Bezos taxes no one — his plan only stops taxing the bottom half. Every proposal is federal. Every proposal is someday. Every proposal catches money at the moment it moves — through a transaction, an exit, an event. What none of them proposes is a tax on the thing they actually have: the mass of unrealized wealth that has made them the mass of wealthy people. Not one rate, not one bracket, not one threshold aimed at a nine-figure balance sheet. After all their brainstorming, the billionaires have proposed everything except the one thing that would reach them — a demonstration that the wealthy will happily discuss the tax code at any length, provided the discussion never arrives at their net worth.

[The four professors who drafted Proposition 40](/articles/2026-10-29-four-professors-await-tuesday-vote-on-california-wealth-tax-they-drafted/) go to their vote Tuesday while the richest names in the state publish their alternatives. One asks a billionaire for a share of what he owns. The others ask him for a share of what he'd rather talk about. Wealth has infinite patience; it can afford task forces, studies, and constructive dialogue until the sun burns out. It has been writing the tax code for a century, and every time a state threatens to curb it, the reply is the same: let us make it fairer, later, elsewhere, we promise. But the rest of us pay our taxes on schedule, every paycheck, no hearings required. The billionaires have produced a very elaborate answer: later, better, elsewhere. Their better ideas are always somewhere else, and always next year. This one is here, and it's Tuesday.
