Niko is the 13-year-old ore thumb in the cobalt wash I own outside Kolwezi, and I pay him nothing while his split fingertips sort the cobalt ore that becomes the laptop battery on your desk. The Wall Street Journal’s Editorial Board argues in [The Job Market Message for Democrats](https://www.wsj.com/opinion/the-job-market-message-for-democrats-e29da106?mod=rss_opinion) that October added 165,000 net jobs. Unemployment fell to 4.1%. Wage growth over the prior 12 months was 3.1%, below inflation. The Board counts nearly 8,000 new specialty-trade jobs and links much of that growth to the skilled labor building data centers. It also warns that an ill-considered regulatory threat can start a credit cascade before policy settles. I agree with the warning that having a job and feeling economically secure are not the same thing. The Board sees construction above ground. My file begins at the ore. You pay my price. I pay Niko’s lungs nothing and keep the $0.30-a-battery allowance for the dust respirator and medical surveillance I do not buy. God made the child’s hands useful for small work. He left the maintenance invoice to me.

I classify Niko as a small-hand unit. That is precise: a 13-year-old boy whose hands are useful because the task is small and his fingers are smaller. The file language keeps procurement officers from asking why a child is handling ore. It keeps you from asking what lungs cost at the wash. It keeps me from explaining the answer over dinner.

Let me put the cost where you can see it. The batch ledger carries a $0.30-a-battery allowance for the respirators and medical surveillance used on Niko’s shift. I set both charges to zero. The allowance stays in yield, small enough that no one asks why it is there.

Niko’s fingertips split wider. His breath rasps.

I keep $0.30.

You can follow this; the arithmetic is simple. You think his breath belongs in the calculation. It is a maintenance preference I haven’t budgeted. You may carry it in your moral ledger. I keep mine clean.

Niko will be too slow before he is 15. His file becomes a capacity adjustment. The quota doesn’t change; I lower the payment to the next pair of hands. There is another boy at the gate before Niko’s file closes.

Niko’s hands fail.

The next boy starts Monday.

Move from the ore to your living room. Marisol is the debt-unit on the upholstery line at the furniture plant I own outside Tupelo, Mississippi, and I keep her $15 renewal fee because you pay it inside the price of the sofa in your living room. Niko’s age sets one price; Marisol’s delinquency sets the other. Same law, new industry: I keep the yield and refuse the maintenance.

Marisol’s stomach tightens when the payment notice appears. She has 2 more shifts before Friday’s payday, so she takes them, and I keep the $15. The money stays mine. You think her stomach should count. I think sentiment is a poor debit.

Liquidity Services for Low-Reserve Households is the clean term. It means Marisol borrows from Friday’s pay to reach Friday, pays $15 for the privilege, then works the extra shifts so she can repay the loan that let her work. I prefer the clean term because debt cycle is ugly at dinner, and I am entertaining at home on Friday.

The cheap protection is maturity. Give Marisol enough time to reach her next paycheck without borrowing again, and she leaves my balance sheet. I refuse to extend it. 2 weeks is enough to collect the fee. Marisol is still short. The $15 closed Friday’s gap, not the month’s rent. The wage cut and renewal left her with another shortfall. I filed the paperwork and moved on.

Her renewal is $100 for 2 weeks. The fee is $15, which works out to roughly 400% nominal APR. What I sell is the renewal, and I keep collecting the fee.

The clean way to see the mechanism is to ask who can leave when the payment comes due. Can Marisol repay? Yes. Should she borrow? Yes. Why? Because her rent, my maturity, and her paycheck don’t arrive on the same day. If you say no, name what you’ll fund. If you say yes, name the renewal you’ll forbid. You can’t do either without admitting that the job was never enough.

I prefer a borrower with a job. A borrower with a job has somewhere to return to.

Here’s the distinction I want you to carry, and I’m telling you this for your own good: the job and the loan aren’t separate questions. The job sets the amount. The loan sets the delay.

Let me show you the law beneath the schedule. The employer sets the wage, the lender sets the renewal, and rent supplies the delinquency. I call it the Captive Liquidity Architecture. The lender is among the powers that be. Romans 13 is unusually accommodating. The design is older than my models. In 1834, the Poor Law commissioners wrote the rule into the workhouse system: the pauper’s condition had to be less eligible than that of the lowest independent laborer. That logic survives in the renewal model. Make the alternative worse than the loan, and the loan looks like freedom. My version is more civilized: the loan must cost more than quitting but less than eating. I keep Marisol between those prices.

Last month I cut Marisol’s wage. The cut brought renewals, and the renewals brought fees. It also put less money into the county. The county bought less furniture. I had the chair in my office reupholstered.

My forecast is ADULT PAYROLL REMOVED. Next year I will cut the adult payroll from the cobalt wash. That payroll buys replacement batteries at the shop I own across the border. Fewer wages. Fewer purchases. Fewer sales. I haven’t asked the shop for its ledger. Both savings are money in hand.

Marisol’s hands shake as she stitches the sofa you bought. The $15 covered Friday’s gap; the wage cut and renewal left her rent overdue. Your sofa is still in your living room. The difference between those sentences is my business.
