OPEC+ starves rural fuel buyers to preserve oil revenue.

The LP driver clipped his carbon copy to the fill receipt when the truck came up the driveway Tuesday, while a neighbor’s Polaris lay torn down on my bench. I carried the receipt into my one-man shop and pinned it to the wall beside the parts invoices and the ticket from the previous fill. The number had moved. Not much. Enough.

Sara will see it when she does the bills. So will every household on this road with a propane furnace and a fixed income. Diesel prices my truck, tractor, generator, and the freight carrying parts and feed. Propane and heating oil price what it takes to heat the house. Crude reaches a farmer as a full tank, then rides every delivery invoice afterward. The people who set the barrel price do not have to stand in my shop and watch a customer set the fuel receipt beside the repair estimate.

Friday’s market gave the mechanism a price. Front-month Brent crude rose 0.6% to $97.15 a barrel. West Texas Intermediate rose 2.7% to $85.96. Both benchmarks were on track for their first weekly gain of the month. The route there was uneven: Thursday brought [Brent slipping as WTI gained on Middle East supply risks](/articles/2026-11-26-brent-slips-as-wti-gains-on-middle-east-supply-risks-ahead-of-opec-meeting/), and earlier in the month Brent had [topped $100 as a Saudi alert kept supply risks in focus](/articles/2026-11-06-brent-crude-tops-100-as-saudi-alert-keeps-middle-east-supply-risks-in-focus/). None of that denies the volatility. It shows buyers bidding up oil while the threat to supply persists.

The clearest number was neither benchmark. It was the premium of front-month Brent over the following month’s contract, which widened to $4.28 a barrel from around $2.50 at the start of the week. MUFG’s analysts said buyers were paying $4.28 more for the near barrel than for the one after it. That spread measures immediate scarcity and risk. The danger did not merely frighten traders. It changed what oil was worth this month.

Sunday’s OPEC+ meeting is expected to set collective output policy for 2027 because the existing production agreements expire at the end of the year. The group will estimate each member’s sustainable production capacity, then turn those estimates into quotas. Disruptions to production and exports during the Iran war have made some of those estimates guesswork. Saudi Arabia is the cartel’s de facto leader. The menu is not secret: restore more supply, maintain the current restrictions, or make fresh cuts to support prices. This is not a trade association politely studying demand. It is a cartel deciding how much oil its members put into the market.

OPEC+ has spent years using quota agreements to restrain output and support prices. Fewer barrels at a higher price leave member governments and their state oil companies with more fiscal room and foreign currency. Rural buyers get the higher number at the pump, at the propane dealer, on freight, and on every good moved through a diesel-powered economy. OPEC+ owns no gas station and employs no driver. It controls quantity decisions at the wholesale end, and the street price finishes the collection.

The war is not abstract. Saudi Arabia’s energy ministry said Thursday that an attack on the Jazan oil refinery sparked a fire and temporarily halted some operations. One worker was killed. Several others were injured. A drone attack in Kuwait killed two Italian technicians. Iran denied responsibility. Negotiations between Washington and Tehran remained deadlocked despite a ceasefire.

Three people died in this week’s attacks. Not one was an energy minister. Not one was a futures trader. Not one got to set the price their deaths moved. Every damaged refinery and every threat of further disruption makes buyers pay more for barrels available now. OPEC+ need not have ordered an attack for the uncertainty to enlarge its leverage. It need only maintain or deepen output restraint while the market fears a missing barrel.

Daniel Yergin’s *The Prize*, roughly eight hundred pages long, tells the whole arc. The short version is that the diesel I buy at the co-op on Highway 13 is wired by pipeline and tanker to a fight that has been running since before I was born. Yergin shows oil becoming the hinge of modern power because it moved armies, industry, and money across borders. OPEC+ sits inside that history, not outside it. The machinery is newer. The prize is old: control of a necessary flow by people who do not carry the household cost.

What this country built after the 1973 embargo was more than a Strategic Petroleum Reserve and a Department of Energy. In January 1980, President Carter declared that “An attempt by any outside force to gain control of the Persian Gulf region will be regarded as an assault on the vital interests of the United States of America, and such an assault will be repelled by any means necessary, including military force.” The rapid-deployment force ordered then became CENTCOM in 1983, and successive administrations kept American naval forces in the Gulf. During the Tanker War, American flags went up over eleven Kuwaiti tankers and the Navy ran the escorts. An Iraqi Exocet hit the USS Stark, killing thirty-seven sailors. In July 1988, an American cruiser shot down Iran Air 655, killing 290 civilians. That is the first line of the bill, and it is paid in taxes.

Presidents have promised this country “energy independence” since Nixon declared Project Independence in 1973. The “energy dominance” version is the same promise in a louder voice. It says the world’s trouble will stay over there because the United States pumps its own oil. But crude is priced where the risk is, not where the flag is. This country has been a net petroleum exporter since 2019. EIA data show American crude production reaching record levels in recent years under presidents of both parties, each of whom claimed credit for the trend. A drone in Kuwait still moved the number at the co-op pump that week.

This is the Nationalist Shell Game named in *We Too*, Chapter 16: nationalist rhetoric promises energy security while the institutions that actually set the terms keep the benefit. The old answer—drill more at home—is not a shield. An American barrel can enter the world market while OPEC+ withholds enough elsewhere to move the price. The United States can produce more oil and still pay OPEC-shaped prices. Washington shapes the sanctions and security around the barrels. OPEC+ shapes how much supply is offered. The farmer gets neither vote.

The home-team answer has its own soft floor. Bethany McLean, who broke the Enron story open in *The Smartest Guys in the Room*, found Wall Street money under the grit of the shale patch. Fast-declining wells need fresh capital year after year just to keep the counters moving. “Energy independence” is not a wall around this county. Domestic drilling changes where some of the marginal barrels come from, but crude trades on a global benchmark and the Gulf risk premium rides on top of it. The equilibrium wobbles whenever somebody turns a valve in Riyadh or Moscow, or flies a drone into a refinery.

Here is what the second line of the bill looks like on the ground. Adams County is a retirement county with a lake economy. The 2020 Census counted 16,692 housing units, nearly half of them vacant and overwhelmingly seasonal lake and Wisconsin Dells second homes. The American Community Survey puts the median age in the mid-fifties, with about a third of residents sixty-five or older. Median household income runs about $59,000 a year, roughly eighty percent of the state’s. The 2017 labor-force participation rate was 48.6 percent, the lowest of any county in Wisconsin.

Propane heats the trailers around Castle Rock and the farmhouses out New Chester way. Diesel moves the grain trucks, school bus, plow truck, and my 2014 Silverado, with 187,000 miles on it. The co-op’s fuel costs ride the same wires into every member’s bill. None of Brent’s moves shows up as a line item with a name on it. It shows up in January at a kitchen table, run by a widow on a pension because that is what the old farmhouse came with.

Farm country knows how arrangements like this work. When the trade war squeezed the crop, the Treasury paid farmers through the Market Facilitation Program. When a war moves the fuel, the move lands on the furnace ticket and stays there. The flag gets waved for the crop. The pump does what Brent says.

Wendell Berry wrote in *The Unsettling of America* that nuclear power, “if we are to believe its advocates, is going to be well used by the same mentality that has egregiously devalued and misapplied man- and womanpower.” He was writing about the extractive mind, the one that treats soil and people as inputs to be spent. The oil patch runs on the same ledger. A refinery worker in Jazan, two technicians in Kuwait, thirty-seven sailors on the Stark: the fuel is precious enough to post a navy over, and the people around it are round-off error. That is not a bad week’s accident. That is the design of a system that counts the commodity to the penny and human beings to the nearest casualty.

Friendship has the Adams-Columbia Electric Cooperative because member ownership and public policy carried rural power into a service area private utilities would not serve. The Rural Electrification Act of 1936 backed member-owned cooperatives with long-term, low-interest loans when private utilities would not run the wire to scattered farms. The argument against member-owned generation now is the argument they made then. The customer is not merely a buyer; the customer is a member. OPEC+ has a Sunday meeting, 2027 quotas, and no member in Adams County with a vote.

The drill, tanker, pipe, refinery, and truck are still needed. The cartel’s power to manage scarcity for its own revenue is the extraction. If public money is going to support energy security, overseas transport, or industrial expansion, some of it should come home as member-owned generation, local freight capacity, and the right to repair the equipment owners buy. A cooperative is not a cure-all. It is proof that infrastructure answers differently when the people paying the bill hold the vote. The barrels are necessary. OPEC+ does not need to be.

At day’s end, I pull the starter cord on the Honda EU2200i. It fires on the first pull, and the co-op’s lights stay on across the dark fields while the LP ticket remains pinned beside the parts invoices.

OPEC+ can move the barrel price. It does not own the county. If cartel restraint and war risk tax every haul, meal, and machine here, we are not buying energy. We are surrendering the county. I’ll keep pinning the tickets up.
