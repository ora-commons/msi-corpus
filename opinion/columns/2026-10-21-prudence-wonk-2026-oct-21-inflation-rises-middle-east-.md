The bill for a war fought thousands of miles away has arrived in Britain twice: first at the petrol pump, then in the Treasury's ledger.

Two pieces of bad news landed before John Healey's first Budget: [annual inflation of 3.5%](/articles/2026-10-21-u-k-inflation-rises-to-3-5-ahead-of-expected-bank-of-england-hike/), up from 3.1% in August and above the 3.4% expected by City economists, and a [September deficit of £24.5bn](/articles/2026-10-21-uk-government-records-24-5bn-september-deficit-ahead-of-healey-s-first-budget/), worse than forecast. For households already facing a renewed cost-of-living squeeze, it is another turn of the screw. For the chancellor, it is a warning that his room for fiscal manoeuvre is disappearing. These are not separate statistical mishaps. They are one interconnected fiscal disaster.

The mechanism is brutally simple. Oil and gas prices have soared since the end of February, when the US and Israel launched their war on Iran, throttling the flow of fuel through the Strait of Hormuz. The Office for National Statistics said the rising cost of motor fuels was the single largest factor pushing up inflation in September. The average price of petrol rose by 8.2p a litre in September, against a 0.2p fall in the same month a year earlier. Diesel climbed by 10.7p, after falling by 0.4p a litre in September 2025.

Fuel is the sharpest edge. Supermarket competition has so far held food-price growth steady at 1.3% by offsetting transport costs. That is a private-sector reprieve, not a policy, and it is fragile: higher fuel and distribution costs are likely to push food prices up through the winter, and January's large energy price-cap increase lands just as household energy demand peaks. Charlotte O'Leary of the National Institute of Economic and Social Research sees fuel and transport costs pushing up food prices and warns that inflation could reach 4%, double the Bank of England's target, by early 2027 even if the conflict is partly resolved.

This is an imported supply shock being converted into a domestic inflation problem. It is not a temporary burst of consumer exuberance that the statistics will soon wash away, and it is not the result of households suddenly developing a taste for expensive shopping. As the 3.5% reading pushes through wages, rents, contracts and benefits, it becomes embedded in household expectations and in the government's financial calculations.

The Treasury is already paying the bill twice. The war is doing a second kind of damage inside the public finances: some government bonds carry interest linked to inflation, so the same price shock that makes petrol dearer also raises the cost of servicing the national debt. The government paid £10.9bn in debt interest in September, the highest September figure since records began and only the eighth month in which the bill has exceeded £10bn. Higher inflation added £4bn to those costs in September alone. Total government debt is now estimated to have hit £3tn for the first time. The comforting claim that debt has fallen from 95% to 94% of the economy does not lower a single penny of the £10.9bn bill; it merely changes the unit in which the government records a worsening cash-flow problem. Cash is what the Treasury must pay, and cash is what is surging.

Public sector net borrowing stands at £97.8bn for the financial year so far. That is £4.4bn below the same point last year, but still £6bn above what the independent Office for Budget Responsibility expected. Lower borrowing than the previous year has not spared the government from spending more than it planned; higher inflation has simply enlarged the obligations attached to the money it already intended to borrow. This is what Healey must confront before his first Budget: not a fresh round of irresponsible promises, but a fiscal hole created by higher prices, expensive war and interest costs that are rising because some government bonds are linked to inflation.

That trap runs directly into Budget day. The 3.5% reading automatically increases most working-age welfare payments from next April by more than expected: weekly child benefit rises by 95p to £28 for the first child. It also means a bigger-than-forecast inflation-linked rise in business rates in England. The tax firm Ryan said this could raise more than £1bn in extra receipts for the Treasury, paid mostly by organisations in hospitality, leisure, education, health, transport and utilities. Higher benefits protect households; higher business rates transfer the fuel shock into another round of costs for companies that can least afford it. The state pension is expected to rise by 3.9% under the triple lock, because September's 3.5% CPI came in below 3.9% wage growth for the three months to July.

So the Budget is already moving in the wrong direction before ministers make a single spending decision. The Bank of England is expected to raise its 3.7% interest rate when the monetary policy committee meets on 5 November, squeezing mortgage and business borrowing just as households are hit by higher fuel bills.

Further tightening may be necessary to stop the shock from becoming a wider inflation spiral, but it cannot produce more oil or gas, and it risks weakening an already fragile economy, as Martin Beck of WPI puts it. That leaves Healey with a budget built around a contradiction. He has blamed the rise in prices on wars in the Middle East and Europe, while promising that his Budget will "drive for growth in every postcode". The first statement explains what is happening. It does not excuse it. Growth cannot be conjured by relabelling a fuel-price shock. Higher rates restrain demand after fuel has already squeezed it, and broad indexation turns a temporary energy disruption into permanent public spending commitments. The result is not a growth strategy. It is a mechanism for making households pay for the war twice — first in higher prices, then in the fiscal measures required to manage those higher prices.

Healey should resist the temptation to cover the entire shock with universal subsidies or another round of borrowing dressed up as investment. Targeted relief for the households most exposed to fuel costs can be justified. A credible fiscal path is indispensable. But the government cannot pretend that inflation can be legislated away while simultaneously indexing more spending to it.

Britain has not suffered two separate economic shocks. It has suffered one supply shock, transmitted through petrol into consumer prices and then into the cost of servicing debt. Healey can shift that burden between taxpayers, borrowers and the Treasury. He cannot make it disappear.

None of this makes inflation a choice. It makes the Budget a choice. Petrol prices may ease when fuel flows freely again; the interest on the debt incurred while they were high will not. The £3tn mark has already been crossed. It is not a number the Budget will pass. It is the bill arriving for a war whose price Britain is still paying.
