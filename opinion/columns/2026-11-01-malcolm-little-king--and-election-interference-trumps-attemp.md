Donald Trump is trying to steal the 2026 midterms by making the count contestable before the votes are cast.

In Alaska, the attempt has a name: Mary Peltola, the Democratic candidate for the U.S. Senate. Trump has repeatedly demanded Peltola’s prosecution. Alaska’s Republican governor has not committed to certify the result if Peltola wins. The New York Times reported that Peltola’s campaign sought to confuse voters by recruiting another candidate with the same name as her Republican opponent, although the campaign denied doing so. [Voting-rights experts have called the Alaska election intervention unprecedented](/articles/2026-11-01-voting-rights-experts-call-alaska-election-intervention-unprecedented/).

The denial remains part of the record. So does the report. Fielding a candidate with a similar name is an old trick. It is not inherently illegal, but it is unethical, could potentially lead to criminal charges, and cannot justify contesting Alaska’s result. Those distinctions matter. We should not invent criminality where the evidence does not establish it, and we should not surrender the larger offense because a political campaign has behaved badly.

The way to prevent that is to name the pattern without laundering any part of it. If Peltola’s campaign used deception to confuse voters and that conduct can be substantiated, it should be investigated. The same legal and ethical standard applies to any campaign, in any party, that uses deception to confuse voters. But equal scrutiny does not require false equivalence. A campaign trick manipulates voters. A Justice Department lawsuit pressures a state. A governor’s refusal to commit to certification holds certification open to political pressure. Those are different levers producing different consequences. One is an abuse. The others reorganize the machinery of the election itself.

That machinery is the point.

Start with the true half, because the true half is real. Election security matters. Allegations of improper voting must be investigated. Close counts require rules. Election officials need the ability to reject improper ballots without becoming captive to political force. If any candidate or official can treat every recount as theft and every unfavorable result as evidence of fraud, no democratic election can ever settle.

But the true half does not make the larger claim true. Ordinary partisan competition seeks votes. This seeks leverage over what happens after the votes are counted. When the same power can invoke election integrity while using courts, prosecutors, federal agencies, and state offices to place the election itself under political siege, election integrity becomes the umbrella under which power hides.

Cui bono?

Trump.

Republicans were expected to lose the House and perhaps the Senate. That does not prove why Trump and his allies are acting as they are, but it tells us where political power expects to find its advantage. Trump refused to accept Americans’ democratic verdict in 2020. He is now promoting claims that Democrats will steal seats through illegitimate votes. If a candidate can lose the vote and then reopen the count through litigation, intimidation, or a prebuilt story of fraud, the narrow margin becomes a lever.

The immediate beneficiary is Trump and the congressional ticket aligned with him, because uncertainty gives a favored side a chance an adverse count would not. The second beneficiary is every official and candidate who can turn a dispute over a handful of votes into a reason to prolong the contest, shift responsibility, and make the final result conditional on political acquiescence.

A few thousand—or even a few hundred—votes in key places could have a profound effect on the outcome. The election does not have to be stolen wholesale. The beneficiary needs only enough uncertainty to make a legitimate result contestable.

The cost falls somewhere else. It falls on election workers who must defend a process while absorbing abuse and death threats. It falls on voters whose families may be vulnerable to immigration enforcement, who may fear drawing attention to relatives, and who are asked to vote while the state threatens to interrogate the result. It falls on state officials whose authority to administer and certify elections is attacked in the name of federal power. It falls on Black communities already navigating weakened Voting Rights Act protections. States no longer require federal preapproval for decisions that could tilt a race, and districts have already been redrawn.

And it falls on the meaning of democracy itself. Every time a government treats a clear count as the beginning of a legitimacy crisis rather than the conclusion of a public decision, it teaches citizens that ballots secure a result only until someone with enough leverage decides to reopen it.

The root cause is not a handful of disputed ballots. It is the effort to shift control of election administration away from state authority and into the hands of executive power and its lawyers.

Courts have blocked executive orders claiming election powers reserved to the states. That matters. The rule of law has not surrendered. The [Justice Department’s election suits against the states have also drawn expert warnings of post-election disputes](/articles/2026-10-31-justice-department-election-suits-draw-expert-warnings-of-post-election-disputes/). But the strategy does not require any single illegal order to succeed.

The suits do not need to succeed every time. Each one can generate discovery, delay, headlines, procedural uncertainty, and reasons for somebody to demand yet another legal proceeding. Court orders are one answer, not the whole remedy.

This is how a republic hollows itself out without any single moment looking like the end of democracy. The machinery does not stop. It multiplies. Each emergency creates another emergency. Each litigation request creates the demand for another lawsuit. Each official refusal to concede a result is framed as obstruction rather than duty. The law becomes the place where power goes to buy more time, and time becomes the thing power uses to make the count uncertain.

The wicked part is that each instrument can look locally defensible while the combined effect becomes authoritarian. A lawsuit can be filed. A governor can demand more information. A candidate can point to a similar name. Federal officers can be discussed as a possible presence near polling places. A later accusation of theft can wait until the votes are counted. None of those acts alone proves a conspiracy. Together, they can produce a condition in which ordinary uncertainty becomes a civic emergency and every close margin becomes a pretext for coercion.

This is how the strategy survives courts. A court can invalidate an illegal order or resolve a particular dispute; that does not by itself stop officials from filing further suits or from creating uncertainty around certification. Courts adjudicate individual acts while the strategy works through accumulation. One legal challenge need not win. One intimidation attempt need not change a vote. One refusal to promise certification need not reverse an outcome. They only need to preserve doubt in the places where the margin is thin.

And every defense can be turned into more ammunition for the lie. Investigations into noncitizen voting can be used to announce a scheme before the facts are known. Early voting can be praised as civic mobilization or attacked as suspiciously organized. Litigation can expose corruption, or consume the time and trust needed to resolve an actual dispute. The more visible the interference becomes, the more evidence the accuser claims for claiming that the election itself cannot be trusted.

That is why courts, election officials, and turnout are checks rather than cures. Courts can stop an executive order. They cannot certify every ballot in every state. Election officials can defend the process. They cannot do so safely if threats remain routine and consequence absent. Early voting can create a clear mandate. It cannot guarantee a clear count if the counting power remains contestable.

Psychologist Jennifer Freyd describes a pattern often used by perpetrators: “deny, accuse, and reverse victim and offender.” The result of the 2020 election was denied. This time, Democrats are accused of stealing seats. Then the actors who attacked the process recast themselves as the victims of the process they made unstable. The reversal is the objective. It allows the accuser to convert responsibility into grievance and make the rule of law appear to be the partisan instrument.

The person who creates the problem cannot become the aggrieved party when the legal consequences arrive. The federal government cannot attack state election administration and then complain that state officials may not possess enough discretion to dispute the outcome.

The contrast with 2020 is the clearest warning. In 2020, Vice President Mike Pence and Attorney General William Barr refused Trump’s most outrageous demands. January 6 showed how acute the danger to American democracy had become. The danger now is greater. The Justice Department’s current posture is one of accommodation. That is not neutral bureaucratic disagreement. It is a change in who receives protection from the presidency and who must absorb the danger.

In 2020, the machinery was asked to defend a constitutional limit. Now it is being asked to create uncertainty around the result in advance.

The officials administering the vote are therefore not obstacles to be managed. They are the government’s duty in practice. They are the people entrusted with turning ballots into a lawful result, and they are being asked to do that work while threats make every decision look like partisanship. Election officials include Republicans. Some are preparing to resist anti-democratic efforts despite horrific abuse and death threats. Their resistance should not be mistaken for factional obstruction. The public must give them more than applause: legal support, a clear chain of authority, practical safety, protection from intimidation, and the assurance that a lawful count will be certified.

Then there is the simplest weapon: fear.

Legitimate voters may worry about drawing attention to undocumented relatives, regardless of the federal investigation into alleged noncitizen voting in California and regardless of whether Immigration and Customs Enforcement officers will actually be stationed near polling places. The rumor does not need to be true to do its work. The voters do not need to be wrong about the danger to be silenced by it. A message that says voting may expose your family is voter suppression whether or not anyone in uniform ever appears at the precinct.

This is where the moral center of the matter becomes plain. The people carrying the risk are not the architects of the threat. They are voters, election workers, local officials, poll workers, and the relatives of people who may be ineligible to vote. They deserve not merely procedural protection but practical safety, clear authority, and the knowledge that the institutions they serve will not ask them to enforce a fraud they know is false.

Trump’s defenders will say the courts are still available, the states still control elections, and every challenge can be adjudicated. That is the point. The attempt is to make lawful resistance so expensive, so noisy, and so threatening that it functions like surrender before it arrives. A voter frightened about being seen at the polls is governed by intimidation even if no ballot is touched. An election official who receives death threats while defending a count is not protected merely because the law still says the count must happen. A governor who will not bind himself to certify the lawful result is not neutral; he is preserving the option to break the result.

An early-voting surge suggests citizens are responding to these threats by participating and ensuring their ballots count. Early voting does not guarantee any particular result, and turnout does not erase legitimate recount procedures. But mathematics matters. A clear verdict is harder to overturn than a marginal one. A large, orderly count deprives manufactured ambiguity of the oxygen it needs.

The same conditions intended to produce a quiet, manageable electorate can produce a visible one. The recent Brazilian election offers the relevant example: the rejection of Trump’s favored far-right candidate sent a harder message than a narrow victory, because a decisive verdict is harder to characterize as illegitimate. A large, orderly, clearly administered vote does not end the political contest. It makes stealing the result harder.

That is why every intimidation attempt, every pretense of fraud, every candidacy designed to confuse, and every refusal to commit to certification is aimed at the same thing: to keep a small margin from becoming a settled fact. The strategy need not convince the country that the election was stolen. It only needs to prevent the count from becoming clear enough to make theft unnecessary.

Read as a rule for democratic crises, Malcolm X’s 1964 *The Ballot or the Bullet* supplies the governing principle: the person who creates the crisis cannot be the neutral judge of it. That is not rhetoric for its own sake. It is a chain-of-command indictment. When a government creates a crisis, interferes with the process, then points to the crisis as proof that the process was untrustworthy, the answer begins with the creator of the crisis.

Trump has demanded the prosecution of a Senate candidate. His administration has claimed powers the courts have said belong to the states. The Justice Department has pursued suits that raise the prospect of prolonged election litigation. The Republican governor of Alaska has withheld a commitment to certify a lawful result. These are different acts, but they do not belong to separate political universes. They all widen the space in which a future defeat can be renamed fraud.

The fact that courts have blocked some of these efforts does not absolve the attempt. It shows that the attempt crossed a constitutional line. Nor does one eventual result settle the account. The harm begins before Election Day: legitimate voters are frightened, election officials are threatened, state authority is treated as an obstacle, and the ground is prepared for a permanent argument that no count can be trusted.

Read through Martin Luther King Jr.’s 1963 *Letter from Birmingham Jail*, the distinction between negative peace and positive peace is exact. Negative peace is the empty contest. Positive peace is participation without fear, a count that reflects the ballots, consequences that do not follow the winner’s preference, and officials strong enough to say no. A country in which no one formally challenges the winner is not democratic if the result was produced through intimidation and the counting power remains negotiable.

The clearest defense of democracy is not a declaration of unity. It is a division of responsibility that cannot be evaded.

Count the votes.

Protect the people counting them.

Bind the power that wants to choose the winner.

Demand that Alaska’s governor commit publicly to certify a lawful result—and make the question specific: if Peltola wins, say whether he will certify her victory. Keep the campaign’s namesake trick separate from certification. Call your election officials and ask what protection they have against threats. Demand that the Justice Department explain the legal basis for election intervention and that congressional overseers put the executive orders and lawsuits on the record. Do this before the ballots are cast, because after the count every argument becomes retrospective, theatrical, and difficult.

King’s late 1967 *Where Do We Go from Here?* supplies another hard political truth: formal rights without material power can leave concentrated power free to dominate them. An election is not self-executing democracy. The counting rules, enforcement, institutions, and distribution of power determine whether the right to vote has substance.

The Beloved Community is not a mood at the end of that argument. It is not a feeling that returns when powerful people behave. It is a society in which no official can convert a public office into a private permission slip from voters, no political coalition can treat the votes of the weak as optional, the least protected person can vote without fear, and a lawful count cannot be made negotiable by the winner’s preference.

Care is not a feeling here. Care is a count completed, a record preserved, a threat answered, a governor bound to the law, and a public that refuses to look away. The best defense of democracy is to show, unmistakably, that people care about it. That is how the arc bends—not because power is invincible, but because people can see its mechanism and refuse to let it stand.

Protect the count. Certify the result. Keep faith with the voters.
