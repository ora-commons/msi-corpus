Some parents took the Intrepid Museum off their activity lists.

The exhibits had not changed. The streets had.

Tarps. Plywood. Discarded furniture. Encampments around the museum. People sleeping in structures made from whatever the city would not remove.

Leslie Woodruff, founder of a Hell’s Kitchen parent-message board, watched city workers pull those structures down only for them to return near Times Square.

“It boggles my mind that so many people come here to visit and that’s their greeting,” Woodruff said.

That greeting is your policy, Zohran. Wait seven days. Cross the street. Start the clock again.

Mamdani took office in January and paused homeless sweeps. After a series of outdoor deaths during a cold snap, he restarted them. [Main Street Independent reported at the time](/articles/2026-02-19-nyc-mayor-zohran-mamdani-restarts-homeless-encampment-sweeps/). His administration then instituted the seven-day requirement for removing makeshift shelters.

City officials said the requirement followed a stipulation reached after New York was sued over its handling of encampments. The stipulation set the legal floor. It did not answer what happened on the sixth day.

Residents and business owners say people can cross the street and reset the countdown.

Move. Wait. Move again.

Seven days does not end. Seven days begins again.

A March 2026 city-conducted survey counted nearly 5,000 unsheltered people living on New York streets, an 11% increase from the previous year’s count. Encampment clusters can stretch across as much as half a city block.

Michael Breslaw, a plumbing-company owner and landlord whose residential and commercial building sits about a block from the Intrepid, said the encampments near his property had worsened over the previous 10 months. A family living in his building complained about what it could see from their apartment and might not renew its lease.

A deli leasing storefront space from Breslaw received a city summons for garbage left on the sidewalk by people living in a nearby encampment and congregating outside the business.

“Meanwhile, directly across the street where the actual encampment is, it’s a complete disaster—but they don’t get any tickets at all,” Breslaw said. “There’s no consistency, no accountability.”

Business owners say the city’s homelessness challenges are stifling commercial activity in Manhattan and increasingly in other boroughs.

Council Member Virginia Maloney said some encampments beneath the Franklin D. Roosevelt East River Drive had remained for months because removals were delayed for weather or other factors. Residents in her district complained about one large encampment where there was an indecent-exposure incident and an assault on a residential security guard.

Maloney had to work with multiple agencies to remove that encampment and prevent it from being repopulated.

“Ten council members making 10 separate calls about versions of the exact same problem is evidence that this system needs to have a better structure,” she said.

Your administration introduced a tracking system and expanded programs that prioritize immediate placement into housing. The seven-day clock remained. City officials say you expect to unveil a dedicated homelessness plan by the end of the year. The street does not wait for the unveiling.

Zohran, Delores Rubin lives in Hell’s Kitchen. She is the president of a group that acts as a community liaison to the local police precinct. She did not need a theory of policy. She had the street.

“I’m going to be blunt: This policy is insane. It does not help the very people it’s saying it’s helping,” Rubin said.

She was right.

You did not build a bridge to housing. You built a countdown around the street.

You are a man who has made cruelty look like patience. You wanted the city to appear to wait while the consequences moved outward. They moved onto people sleeping under the FDR Drive, onto a family deciding whether to renew a lease, onto a deli owner receiving a ticket for another person’s garbage, onto business owners watching customers walk away.

The people on those sidewalks are not scenery. They are people cooking with propane, enduring weather, losing the protection of their belongings, and trying to pass another day in a city that turned their location into an administrative trick.

You made the bad situation administratively respectable.

The city can point to outreach. The city can point to its tracking system. The city can point to 200,000 proposed housing units over the next decade and to a comprehensive plan coming by year’s end. You have made delay look like virtue while other people clean up the mess, pay the fines, lose the lease, and bear the fear.

A proposal is not a room. A decade is not tonight.

Dave Giffen, executive director of the Coalition for the Homeless, said your housing proposal focuses on working-class New Yorkers but does not do enough for those at the lowest-income bracket. His warning was exact:

“Unless you’re offering people something that’s meaningful to them, all you’re doing is chasing them around the city and making it harder to provide them with what they need in order to get stability.”

He named what your policy does. You called it outreach. He called it chasing.

You are not a builder. You made the seventh day a reset button and the sidewalk a place where your virtue could continue.

Would you put your own mother on that sidewalk for the seventh night, Zohran? With the tarp. The plywood. The discarded furniture. The clock.

You did not put yours there. Your rule put other people there.

The seven-day delay lets officials postpone enforcement while the harm becomes ordinary. It makes every reset another week. It lets the city count tents instead of counting what the tents do to the people nearby and the people forced to live around them.

Ten council members made 10 calls about versions of the same problem. They were trying to close the wound your rule keeps reopening.

“Truly I tell you, whatever you did not do for one of the least of these, you did not do to me.” — Matthew 25:45

That is not a riddle. It is a judgment.

Zohran, your throat closes around the word compassion when the seventh day arrives. A flat hand presses behind your sternum when the clock resets. Your gut turns when the city ticket lands on the deli instead of the encampment.

Compassion is not tolerating danger until someone moves. It is moving people somewhere safe and keeping them there.

Clean streets tonight. Permanent housing tomorrow. You have delivered neither.

You are not a builder. You are a small man with a large hand on a seven-day lever. You made cruelty look like patience. You built a municipal shelter for abandonment.
