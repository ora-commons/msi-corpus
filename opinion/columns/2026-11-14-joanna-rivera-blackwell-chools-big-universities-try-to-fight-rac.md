The Trump administration has turned Bob Jones from a one-case exception into a standing power.

The proposed Treasury and Internal Revenue Service rules would strip the tax exemption from any school that "adopt[s], maintain[s], or enforce[s] any policy or practice that discriminates on the basis of race, color, or national or ethnic origin." That reaches educational policies, athletics, scholarships and loans, and it reaches colleges the same as it reaches primary and secondary schools. Treasury and the IRS put the proposal out in September — [the proposal to end tax exemption for schools with race-based benefits](/articles/2026-09-03-treasury-proposes-ending-tax-exemption-for-schools-with-race-based-benefits/) — the public hearing is next month, and the rules are scheduled to take effect for school years starting in June 2027.

Here is the part I want you to read twice. The introduction to these regulations says the rule is not intended to prevent a school from furthering a religious mission or from selecting students based on religion. That language is not in the rules themselves. The protection lives in the introduction. The power lives in the text. A promise in a preamble is not a limit on the rules.

The definitions of what counts are murky enough that nobody can say in advance what violates them. "It's so vague. It's so unclear," Tom Stritikus, the president of Occidental College, told the Journal. When Occidental and Liberty University and Portland Jewish Academy and the National Association of Bond Lawyers and the 103-student Southview Christian School in Statesville, North Carolina, all sign the same worry, the fight is not about what the letterhead says it is about. Liz Clark, until recently of the National Association of College and University Business Officers, asked the questions the drafters left unanswered: Can a school sponsor an Irish heritage festival? Honor a Latino student's request for a Latino tutor? Offer courses in Native American languages? Nobody knows. The rule will be written, in practice, one frightened administrator at a time.

A group of Republican state attorneys general, led by Indiana's Todd Rokita, has filed in defense of the rule, writing that "racial discrimination in the form of DEI practices continues to infest many educational settings." That is the hand on the tool, and it tells you what the tool is for.

I was thirty years inside the Evangelical apparatus, and for most of those years I could have told you that Bob Jones was a religious-liberty case — that a Christian school was being punished for its convictions. I would have been repeating a sentence somebody handed me. I do not remember anyone telling me what the case was actually about. I taught the polite version of this for years myself. I know precisely how a religious institution trains its people not to see the verse that has been in their own Bibles all along.

Here is the verse the machinery required everyone to unsee on Monday morning after singing it on Sunday. Galatians 3:28: "There is neither Jew nor Gentile, neither slave nor free, nor is there male and female, for you are all one in Christ Jesus." Read it where it sits. Paul is writing to Gentile converts under pressure from a party insisting that faith in Christ is not enough — take on the boundary markers, keep the sorting system, become culturally Jewish first. His answer, in the verses just before, is that those in Christ are children of God by faith and heirs according to the promise, and the identity markers that sorted the ancient world have no sorting power in the body of Christ. The King James renders it "neither Jew nor Greek" — same Greek word, same claim. The plain reading was never hidden: the categories that rank human beings do not rank them inside the church.

Now hear what the legalist reading did with it. What I was taught — what half the Southern church is still taught — is that Galatians 3:28 concerns salvation only. Who gets to heaven, and nothing else. It has, we were assured, no bearing on who gets admitted, who gets taught, who gets dated, who gets disciplined. That reading is younger than the verse it claims to interpret, and it has a job to do: it parks the most destabilizing sentence in the Pauline letters inside a spiritual compartment so the institution can operate as though Monday morning belongs to Genesis 11:1–9. Bob Jones did not merely hold a distasteful view. It built an administrative apparatus on the compartment — Black students excluded outright until 1971, married Black students admitted from 1971 to 1975, unmarried Black students from 1975, and a written ban on interracial dating enforced into this century, lifted in March of 2000 on the Larry King Live set after George W. Bush's campaign visit to the campus made the policy a national embarrassment. The IRS had revoked the exemption in 1976, retroactive to 1970; in 1983, eight of nine justices held that a school enforcing racial separation could not keep it. That is the chasm: the text says there is neither slave nor free, and the apparatus said the sorting was religion.

Acts 17:26 says the same thing about the human family: from one man he made all the nations, that they should inhabit the whole earth. James 2:1 says it in four words — believers in our glorious Lord Jesus Christ "must not show favoritism." Acts 10:34–35, Peter having just been shown in a vision that he may not call unclean what God has made clean: "I now realize how true it is that God does not show favoritism but accepts from every nation the one who fears him and does what is right." Those sentences sat in the same Bible the whole time. The Southern Baptist Convention was formed in 1845 in defense of slaveholding missionary appointment, and in 1995 it apologized for it. I learned both of those late, and mostly on my own.

Which brings us to the tax question, because the tradition has its own text on exactly this and the rule has walked straight into it. In Mark 12:13–17 the Pharisees and the Herodians — two factions that agreed on nothing except that Jesus was a problem — set a trap: is it lawful to pay tribute to Caesar? Answer yes and he is Rome's collaborator; answer no and he is an insurrectionist. He asks for a coin, asks whose image is on it, and answers, "Render to Caesar the things that are Caesar's, and to God the things that are God's." The plain reading cuts both ways at once. Caesar's currency is Caesar's; pay it, and stop pretending the coin was ever consecrated. And everything bearing God's image — which Genesis 1:27 says is every person, not the coin — belongs to God. The captured reading, the one on display in this fight, runs the verse backward: the exemption is the church's birthright, and any condition attached to it is persecution. The verse's own logic runs the other direction. The money was always Caesar's. The witness was never his. A ministry that cannot survive a condition on its tax status has built its operating budget where its confession ought to be — and a lever that powerful does not care whose hand is on it.

Now set the lineage alongside it, because the historical irony here is not decoration. As the historian Randall Balmer has documented, the late-1970s mobilization that became the Religious Right did not begin with abortion. It began when the IRS moved against segregated Christian academies — Bob Jones, Goldsboro Christian — and the threat to a Christian school's tax status became the movement's organizing grievance. Abortion arrived later as the publicly defensible banner. Liberty University itself was founded in 1971, in the middle of that fight, by Jerry Falwell Sr., who also founded the Moral Majority, on the conviction that Caesar had no business conditioning the Christian school. That institution is now among those formally worried about the new rule — the same institution that joined Project 2025's advisory board. I am not going to perform surprise. The administration is wielding the Evangelical right's own founding grievance as its instrument, pointed this time at diversity programs, and the Christian schools can see more clearly than anyone where the lever points next.

Don Russell, who administers Southview Christian, says his school holds traditional positions on marriage and sexuality and wants "to guard against any future expansion... We just want that to be respected; you don't have to agree with us." Mr. Russell is not paranoid. He has read the preamble, and he knows introductions do not bind. "You don't have to agree with us" is the correct sentence, and it is the sentence the rule's defenders have no use for.

But I will not leave those schools standing alone, because they are right to be afraid. A power that lets an administration declare which school policies violate fundamental public policy does not care which administration holds it — and I would name a progressive administration that built this same switch in the same plain words. Hold the same instrument in a different hand, aimed at the same schools' statements on marriage and sexuality, and you have precisely the future Mr. Russell fears — at which point everyone signing today's letters will call it religious persecution. It would be the same lever. I will say so that day, and I am saying so now. A rule you cannot know in advance whether you have broken, attached to a penalty that ends your institution, does not produce justice. It produces preemptive obedience. Anne Stavney, who leads the Independent Schools Association of the Central States, says she already watches schools "pull back pre-emptively, before any final rule takes place" — scholarships and programs the IRS blessed for decades quietly retired — and says it is "coming from a place of fear." That fear is the design. Vague law plus catastrophic penalty is how a thousand institutions learn to self-censor without a single enforcement action ever filed.

There is a second reason to be careful, and it is not theological. For a private school, losing the exemption means losing tax-exempt bonds and donors' deductions — not a fine, a restructuring. The White House has already been weighing [an order suspending nonprofits' tax exemption over terror ties](/articles/2026-11-06-white-house-weighs-order-suspending-nonprofits-tax-exemption-over-terror-ties/). Exemption has become a switch in Washington. A precedent does not check whom it is pointed at. That is the whole point of a precedent.

So let me say it plainly to the school in Statesville. You are asking the Treasury Department for the wording of your own freedom, and a group of attorneys general is in the record defending the rules as written, and neither filing is opening the book. Acts 10:34 is still there. God does not show favoritism. That sentence did not need a hearing, and it does not need a rule, and it will still be the plainest sentence in the room after June 2027 — whichever regulation survives. The coin has Caesar's face on it. The children in your classrooms bear a different image, and no final rule — not this one, and not the next — has jurisdiction there. You do not have to agree with me. I am asking you to read it.
