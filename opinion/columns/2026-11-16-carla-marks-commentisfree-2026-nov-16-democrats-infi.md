A party held together by shared enemies hasn't decided what it's for. Robert Reich argues in [the Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/commentisfree/2026/nov/16/democrats-infighting) that two outside forces — raging inequality, with billionaires cast as the Democrats' clear villain, and a combative Trump White House staffed with attack dogs — will keep the party glued together when it takes power in January. Both forces are real. Neither one is a plan. Reich is describing a mood and calling it a mandate.

Start with what he gets right, because it's most of it. Wages and salaries have fallen to 43 percent of the country's gross domestic income — the lowest since 1929 — while corporate profits sit at a record 12.4 percent. ABBA called it in 1976: money, money, money, in the rich man's world. The national accounts have finally caught up. Reich's roster is real too — a Montana representative-elect who flipped a conservative seat promising populist economics for working people, a Kansas senator-elect who ran to put vision and dental into Medicare, a Texas senator-elect who wants every American, regardless of age, to join Medicare, and incoming senators in Michigan and Maine who want the same. The new class is [denying any moderate-progressive rift](/articles/2026-11-14-democrats-head-into-new-congress-denying-a-moderate-progressive-rift/) while singing from the same hymnal. His diagnosis is right and I'd sign it: monopolies, the lowest unionization in almost a century, the legal bribes. Diagnosis is not the problem.

The problem is that a diagnosis is not an agenda, and a villain is not a mechanism. What did shared outrage actually buy? The country already ran the experiment. In 2021 the expanded Child Tax Credit went out monthly and near-universal, and child poverty fell 46 percent in a single year — from 9.7 percent to 5.2 percent, the lowest on record, with 2.9 million children lifted out of it, about 2.1 million of them from the expansion itself. Then the expansion lapsed — the villain named, both chambers held, the check simply allowed to die — and poverty came back. The goal was to reduce inequality. The mechanism was a check in the mail. When the check stopped, the goal didn't hold the line. The check had. Villains don't have members. Programs do.

So who pays while the party admires its villain? The same people who've been paying. Collective bargaining covers about 11 percent of American workers — 16.5 million people — and Reich files century-low unionization under "baked into the economy," which is where you put a thing you've stopped trying to change. It isn't scenery. It's the lever. When one hospital system is the only big employer left in town, wages aren't set by competition; they're set by the absence of it — economists call it monopsony, and it means the employer pays little because nobody can leave. Union members' median weekly earnings run $1,404; non-union workers run $1,174, and that gap lands exactly where the 43 percent lands — in the households whose share of the income line keeps dropping. Meanwhile, Center for American Progress modeling puts industry-wide bargaining — pay set for a whole sector at once instead of one firm at a time — at lifting coverage from 11 percent to 29 percent: 16.5 million workers to 42.4 million. Remind me what the obstacle is again.

The Trump half of his argument expires on the same clock. The hostility is real and documented: this month the White House counsel, Will Scharf, sent the incoming oversight chairman, Robert Garcia, a letter warning that aggressive investigations could "cross the line into blatant criminality," which is not how a White House planning to cooperate writes to a committee. But an enemy can fill the airwaves; he cannot pass an appropriations bill. And the incoming majority is already arriving with analysts warning it holds [few tools against the cost-of-living anger](/articles/2026-11-07-democrats-face-limited-tools-to-ease-cost-of-living-anger-analysis-argues/) that elected it. Governing costs money, and money forces the tent to answer the question it agreed to postpone: whose taxes, whose benefits, whose district takes the cut. That is the day it's every faction for itself. Walk me through year two slowly.

Here's the part I find genuinely funny. The evidence Reich offers for villain-unity is a list of Medicare expansions — vision and dental in Kansas, buy-in for every American in Texas, Michigan, Maine. He files that list under "clear villain." Read it again. It's a universalism list: everyone in, everyone covered, everybody pays, everybody uses it. Programs like that are the most durable glue ever manufactured, because they arrive with their own constituency bolted on — Medicare has survived since 1965, and the scare writers will call this list socialism and be ignored by everyone who has ever shown a Medicare card. If a common enemy is the glue, name the durable program shared hatred ever built and defended for a decade. I'll wait. He's holding the tent poles and calling them the weather.

That's the shelf life problem in one sentence: enemies are for elections, plumbing is for governing. A cure, last I checked, is something that outlives the appointment — a bill that passes, a check that keeps arriving. Both of Reich's cures expire the day the other man stops talking. And a benefit aimed only at the poor becomes the poor's program, because the middle class will not defend a thing it has never touched, while a program everybody touches gets defended like the interstate.

Now the honest part, because a comparison without it is just a tourist's postcard. The machinery that makes plumbing durable over there took a century to build: Sweden's 1938 Saltsjöbaden bargain, where organized labor and organized capital shook hands — capital kept the factory keys, workers got security and a seat at the table — Denmark's 1899 nationwide compromise, and Danish unions still administering unemployment insurance, so joining the union is how you get your benefits. Cohesion by institution, not by grievance. Decades of it. No permanent villain required. We, meanwhile, have no industry-wide bargaining law, no union-run unemployment insurance, eleven percent coverage, and a labor movement that spent forty years being disassembled by statute. The policy is the visible tip; the institutions are the iceberg, and you cannot airlift glue. But we are not starting from nothing: 145 million credit-union members, roughly 900 rural electric cooperatives serving 42 million Americans, a state-owned bank in North Dakota profitable every year since 1919. We already do this; we just won't say the word. Every summer at my aunt's table outside Gothenburg, the adults argued about tax rates the way Minnesotans argue about snow — a thing you manage, not a thing that happens to you. Nobody at that table was glued together by hating the same person. They were glued together by a pension that paid out on time.

So what gets built? First, open Medicare to anyone who wants in — the members-elect from Kansas, Texas, Michigan and Maine already ran on exactly that, and that is how these things usually arrive. Everybody in is everybody defending, and that is the whole trick of a durable majority. Second, wage boards and industry-wide bargaining for the sectors where one employer is the only game: sectoral wage boards are legal and working right now — California stood one up for fast food in 2023 — and [inflation and the cost of living are already at the top of the new Congress's agenda](/articles/2026-11-08-inflation-tops-the-agenda-for-trump-and-the-new-democratic-congress/), and wages are the only lever on it that isn't a speech. Third, make the child credit permanent, because we hold the receipt for exactly what it does, and we hold the receipt for what happens when it stops.

The tent doesn't need a villain to hold it up. It needs things the people under it would lose if the other side won: a card in the wallet, a check in the month, a wage floor set across the whole industry. Reich is right that the party walks in united. The question was never whether it can hold together — it's what it's united for. Not dislike. Machinery. The economy is a set of choices, not the weather, and in January somebody gets to choose.
