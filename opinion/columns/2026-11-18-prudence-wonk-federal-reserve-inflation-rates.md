Donald Trump is trying to capture the Federal Reserve while Warsh hoards its credibility.

Here is the record, and here are the numbers. The minutes of the Federal Open Market Committee, the Fed’s rate-setting body, for its Oct. 27–28, 2026 meeting say a “vast majority” of its 19 participants, 12 of whom vote on rate decisions, feared inflation could persist even if gasoline prices kept falling and the effects of tariffs and the buildout of artificial-intelligence infrastructure faded. Nearly all expected a quarter-point increase in December; two considered a half-point; some wanted an October increase. Governor Lisa Cook and Cleveland Fed President Beth Hammack formally dissented for a higher October rate. The committee instead [held the benchmark rate at 3.9 percent](/articles/2026-10-28-federal-reserve-leaves-benchmark-rate-unchanged-as-inflation-accelerates/) after September’s increase—the first in three years and, so far, the only tightening on the books—and acknowledged that “inflation remains elevated.”

What is new is the dissent. This was not a committee evenly divided between inflation and patience. It was a committee moving toward tighter policy, with two written dissents demanding more.

The October hold chose delay after the committee had documented its own worry. Inflation accelerated this fall and broadened beyond industries directly affected by higher oil and gas prices or tariffs. The concern was not merely another oil shock. Households meet the policy twice: prices rise, and higher rates raise the cost of mortgages, auto loans and credit cards. Mortgage rates have topped 7 percent, and home sales have withered. Core inflation, which excludes food and energy, ran at about a 3.5 percent annual rate over the prior three months, against the Fed’s 2 percent target. Businesses appeared increasingly willing to pass on their own rising costs and believed customers could handle price increases of about 3 percent. Governor Christopher Waller said in a CNBC interview that inflation was “headed in the wrong direction and it’s spreading out,” supported a half-point December increase, and said several more hikes might be needed. “That’s the kind of psychology I think we need to start breaking.”

Cook’s dissent makes her the relevant governor. The White House has sought to remove her; the Supreme Court’s June 2026 ruling kept her in office while the case is litigated. An unresolved case does not make the removal demand harmless. A successful removal would let the White House alter a rate-setting body that had just shown an inclination to raise rates. Even an unsuccessful demand can chill the next vote. Power over membership becomes leverage over decisions. That is capture in operation.

Paul Volcker, with Christine Harper, wrote in *Keeping at It* (2018) that he felt relief because the Fed had “almost uniquely among major federal agencies, hasn’t had its basic organization and functional responsibilities threatened by the Trump administration.” In 2026, that relief reads as premature. The mechanism is political interference with a central bank’s independence. The removal need not make the committee fold to take its toll; it need only make members price the risk of crossing the principal into their votes. The October dissent is evidence the committee has not folded. What it lacks is not resolve. It is a chairman willing to state in public what the majority already expects.

The FOMC’s own dissent record already rejects presidential revision of completed votes. Cook’s October dissent stands beside Governor Michelle Bowman’s September 18, 2024 dissent and Bowman and Waller’s separate July 30, 2025 dissents. A removal demand cannot erase a vote. It can only advertise what a later dissent may cost.

Kevin Warsh has steadily refused forward guidance—guidance about the likely future path of rates. He has said that describing the path “locks the Fed to a predetermined path.” The stated reason is not frivolous: a path can outlive the data that justified it, and New York Fed President John Williams said, “I don’t think the situation calls for dramatic action.” Williams is not the balance. He is one voice against the vast majority that documented persistent-inflation concern. Mr. Warsh can preserve tactical flexibility. He cannot make that concern disappear by refusing to state the committee’s response.

Waller’s rebuttal is the operationally correct one. Without a communicated path, investors cannot price the follow-through to a quarter-point increase in December, so the increase carries no message about January. CME FedWatch already put December odds near 95 percent; what pricing cannot supply is January. Inflation is digging itself in. Guidance is the committee’s cheapest instrument: it works by moving expectations, not by requiring the committee to pay for a forecast. Withhold it and every increment of disinflation after December must be bought at full price through the increases themselves. The burden lands in mortgages, auto loans and credit cards.

The votes are on the record. The near-unanimous expectation of the next move is on the record. What the record does not carry is the path stated as policy, and one man decides whether it gets stated. The committee’s expectation is public—three weeks stale and priced by professionals. The withholding costs the committee no rate increase. It shifts the burden of disinflation onto the tools it owns.

Three labels do the laundering: preserving flexibility, independence, and discretion. The first is Warsh’s defense of withholding guidance—a communication preference laundered into institutional principle. “Independence” is pressed into service as presidential restraint; “discretion” becomes a virtue of withholding the committee’s response. Instrument independence means that presidential removal pressure cannot direct the Fed’s rate decisions. Decisional immunity is the false claim that officials owe the public no questions about those decisions. Strip the labels and the institutional arrangement is plain: one office seeks the removal of a governor; another withholds the policy path. One is pressure on personnel. The other is a refusal to tell the public what path the committee will take.

Waller’s second point is the more consequential one. Businesses are passing through costs because they believe customers will accept roughly 3 percent price increases. San Francisco Fed President Mary Daly, who said last week she is leaning toward further hikes, identified the same mechanism: “That’s the inflationary impulse that we want to avoid.” Paul Volcker defined price stability in *Keeping at It* (2018) as “the state in which expected changes in the general price level do not effectively alter business or household decisions.” By that definition, the failure is not approaching. It has arrived. This is the failure state itself. An inflation target is not a slogan. It is an expectation the committee is supposed to enforce, and two members dissented in October because enforcement is running behind the facts.

Part of the enforcement deficit is self-inflicted, and the paper trail is explicit. The Statement on Longer-Run Goals and Monetary Policy Strategy was adopted in January 2012 with a 2 percent inflation target; rewritten on August 27, 2020, to permit inflation above 2 percent as deliberate make-up for earlier shortfalls; and rewritten again on August 22, 2025, to replace make-up language with symmetric treatment of misses in either direction and no promise of repair. Three commitments in thirteen years, each drafted to answer the previous problem: the 2020 version was written when the immediate challenge was inflation below target; the 2025 version was written after inflation would not leave. Rewriting the operating framework twice in five years made the 2 percent anchor less credible. A less credible anchor lets 3 percent become the working assumption on a price list.

St. Louis Fed President Alberto Musalem, who does not vote this year, said the economy will likely require several rate increases before spring 2027 and that rates are not high enough to restrain borrowing and spending sufficiently. The fix is not exotic: Congress should permit removal only for a documented cause through a process beyond the president’s control; the White House should abandon the effort against Cook because a committee cannot fight inflation while litigating its own membership. One repair protects who may vote; the other makes those votes legible: the FOMC should publish the inflation outcomes that trigger its next move and the range of plausible paths, with the chair stating conditionally that while core inflation runs near 3.5 percent, the December quarter-point will not be the last and further increases follow until core inflation credibly returns to 2 percent. The path is committee business, not one member’s discretion over its announcement: the committee sets it, the chair executes it, and the economy grades it.

The committee’s willingness is already public: nearly unanimous on December, alarmed on persistence, dissenting when the hold came. The Oct. 28 news-conference record puts Warsh’s silence on the same record; markets left with nothing to hold.

The minutes show the committee tightening while Trump wants authority over who remains after the vote. A governor’s dissent is not a service the president may revoke; Kevin Warsh is hoarding the inflation fight’s cheapest weapon while the invoice arrives on mortgages already carrying rates above 7 percent.
