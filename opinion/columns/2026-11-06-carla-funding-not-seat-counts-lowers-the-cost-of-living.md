Nobody in Tuesday's election lowered the price of a single jug of formula, and no seat count ever has. In ["Moderates Take Capitol Hill"](https://www.wsj.com/opinion/moderates-take-capitol-hill-4086f989?mod=rss_opinion), former congressman Steve Israel argues in the Wall Street Journal that the incoming Democratic majority will tame itself: with as many as 83 seats, no hard-left rump can hold the speaker's vote hostage, and the real engine is the moderate New Democrat Coalition — 114 members, winners of the swing districts that actually flipped the House. Watch the New Dems, he concludes. They're the majority makers. It is a pleasant story about a seating chart, and it answers the only question that matters — who pays the daycare bill — with a head count. He counted the caucus. He didn't read the mandate.

He's owed one thing, and here it is. The Body Snatchers panic he debunks was cinematic nonsense: DSA-backed members doubled from four to eight, which is a rump, not an invasion, and a majority that size doesn't live or die by any eight votes. Republicans just watched a handful of Freedom Caucus members put Kevin McCarthy through 15 ballots on a four-vote margin in 2023, then throw him out within the year; a hundred-seat margin ends that game entirely. The moderates did win the districts that flip, including ones Donald Trump carried two years ago. Volume is not mass. All true. None of it lowers a bill. Now watch what the piece does with the true parts.

It counts. 43 New Dems when he served, 114 today, 23 red-to-blue seats, eight Democratic Socialists where there used to be four — so, his phrase, let's not confuse volume with mass. But the voters didn't ask for a census. Nobody in the districts that flipped the House voted for "moderation." They voted to make rent, groceries, childcare, and a hospital visit cost less — they asked for it, in his own list, item for item, off the very agenda he dismisses as a loud fringe. He never says how one of those prices falls. Nobody in Indiana's Fifth was endorsing a caucus mission statement. They were voting on a price tag. Running affordability in a swing district doesn't make affordability moderate. It makes it popular. A position that wins across party lines isn't centrism — it's the floor. "Moderate" is doing two jobs in that piece: it means candidates who won swing seats, which is true, and it means a governing program that will deliver the mandate, which is where all the work gets done and no receipt shows up.

His agenda, in his words, is built on "innovation, entrepreneurship, fairness and growth." Take the childcare ask and walk through it slowly. Good infant care needs a ratio: a handful of adults to a room of babies. Someone has to pay for the adults. The parents can't afford more. The workers can't take less. So either the public pays, or the good care doesn't exist at any price a family can reach. The triangle refuses to close: care can't be cheap for parents, decently paid for workers, and profitable for owners at once. Pick two. Every other rich country read the same menu and picked the parents and the workers; we picked the owners and got some of the priciest childcare in the rich world, delivered by workers who qualify for the same food assistance as the parents. That's the whole menu, and it has nothing to do with entrepreneurship. The four words don't appear anywhere on it. Notice what's missing from that sentence: a mechanism. Name one of the four costs that gets cheaper under "growth." You can't invoice a daycare with it. That's not an agenda. That's a horoscope.

And the dodge runs down the rest of his list. On housing, we let houses become an asset class, traded by people who will never once sleep in them. On groceries, a handful of processors set the price before it reaches the shelf. On healthcare, the number on the bill is written before the patient walks in the door. Whatever sets each of those prices, the consensus declines to touch it.

Meanwhile, here is the agenda being waved off. Strip the oat-milk jokes from the platform the piece is mocking and it lists free bus pilots, city-run grocery stores, universal childcare, and housing built on public land — things cities already own and run, the same species as the library and the fire department. To be plain about the century first, because I always am: the one-party states of the twentieth century were a catastrophe, that verdict is in, and none of it is on this menu. What's on the menu has receipts here at home. North Dakota has owned a state bank since 1919 that turns a profit, and nobody has put Bismarck behind an Iron Curtain. Tens of millions of Americans already own a bank, a utility, or a credit union together, and not one of them has required a Politburo.

And here is the fact the seating chart can't survive. We already ran the experiment. In 2021, Congress turned the Child Tax Credit into a plain monthly check for families. The Census Bureau measured what happened: child poverty fell 46 percent in one year, from 9.7 percent to 5.2 — about 2.9 million children lifted out, the check the biggest single reason. The cleanest affordability result this country has produced in decades. Six checks did that. Half a year. Then the credit needed renewing, the votes weren't there, and it lapsed — child poverty shot right back up on the same count. The same Democratic Congress that passed it let it die, stocked with exactly the pragmatists the op-ed celebrates, and nobody made it a line in the sand. That's not a Scandinavian fairy tale. That's a controlled experiment we ran on ourselves. The policy worked. The pragmatists shelved it.

The full machine the voters are asking for exists too. Denmark fires people easily — the part American progressives skip — then makes sure the layoff doesn't cost them their apartment, and spends about 2 percent of its economy every year moving people into the next job. The Danes didn't abolish markets. They funded the floor. We can't copy that machine wholesale; union-run unemployment insurance, industry-wide bargaining, a tax system people believe in — that's a century of plumbing, not a bill. But the monthly check needs no plumbing. It's legal. It existed. It's sitting on the shelf, and the shelf is American. And be honest about the price, because the honesty is the only thing that moves it. The Nordics pay for their floors with broad taxes everybody feels — north of 40 percent of their economy, to our 26. That's why the programs last: everybody uses them. That's the honest price tag, and no head count gets around it.

Then there is Israel's own closing question, which deserves a straight answer: now that Democrats have the majority, how do they keep it? Follow his own arithmetic. The majority was bought with prices; the districts that flipped are the districts where the price tag hurt most. Govern those four costs with a horoscope and in two years the same voters flip back — and they will not consult a caucus roster on the way out the door. By his own logic, the keep-the-majority plan is a delivery mechanism for cheaper buses, childcare, and housing: the agenda his own column spent its length dismissing as a fringe. The zombie army he's laughing at is the only faction holding the receipt for keeping his majority. And when "realism" shows up without a mechanism, remember who bankrolls the moderate bench — the donors are, conveniently, the very people the mandate requires crossing. Ask for the donor list.

So build. Start with what's already legal and already worked here: make the child allowance permanent — we know the dose; it cut child poverty in half once. Fund childcare in public, pick the parents and the workers, pay the difference, because the triangle does not close any other way. Build on public land and keep some of it permanently out of the speculation market. Then watch the New Democrats, by all means — Israel is right about that much. Watch what they vote for, because it costs real money, everything the voters asked for does, and the vote tells you more than the head count. The mandate was never moderation. It's the price tag. Deliver it, and the majority keeps itself. 2.9 million kids already proved the mechanism. The kids are still here. The check for the daycare bill is still on the shelf, and the room of babies is still waiting on its public dollar. All that's missing is the one thing a majority is actually made of: a vote.
