On the USS George Washington in the Arabian Sea, the tailhook catches the cable in under two seconds. The pilot is doing better than 100 miles an hour when it does. He has been airborne over a 20-mile-wide strip of water saturated with aerial objects, with maybe seconds to decide whether the thing coming off Iran's coast is going to kill a tanker, and he will be back on deck in time to launch again — flying with Smucker's Uncrustable sandwiches, gum and bathroom bags to get through the night. Somewhere below him, Ensign Addie Schaefer's maintainers are already pulling the aircraft into the hangar bay, because as she put it, the more the planes fly, the more they break — "there's a reason why that aircraft is able to fly."

That is the war now. Not a strategy, a shift schedule.

Over the past six months, the United States has helped more than 3,000 commercial vessels through the Strait of Hormuz, has 50,000 troops and 20 Navy ships in the region, and is burning through munitions, jet fuel and supplies at a rate the Congressional Budget Office puts at $2 billion to $3 billion a month. Two aircraft carriers are setting modern-day records for consecutive days at sea without a port call. More than a dozen guided-missile destroyers have had their deployments stretched past their limits. The Japan-based strike group that relieved the USS Abraham Lincoln back in August is still out there, night after night. The four giant elevators on the George Washington never stop shuttling aircraft between deck and hangar bay, and the ordnance handlers load the same missiles again and again. And the Pentagon's answer to all of it is a ship maintenance hub on Diego Garcia and a promise of more port calls — which is not a war plan, it is triage.

Here is what should stop everyone cold: the U.S. says 95% of traffic is moving safely, Gulf oil exports have now surpassed prewar levels, and Central Command has logged 1.5 billion barrels exported since the summer. Rear Adm. Jeffrey Heames, the strike group commander, says the jets go in and shoot down threats "in a very short amount of time... We're doing that reliably." One pilot said his squadron has a 100% success rate protecting ships when the jets are on station and have the ammunition — though Iran sometimes fires at more vessels than the U.S. can protect. An interceptor force that is always one volley short is not controlling the strait. It is chasing it.

By the metrics the White House chose to fight over, the mission is working. And the same officials describing that success concede the effort is hampering the Pentagon's ability to counter Chinese influence and to respond to other crises, that it is increasingly viewed as untenable without international help, and that coalition-building has fallen flat. We told readers months ago that [securing the strait would take thousands of U.S. troops](/articles/2026-07-16-securing-strait-of-hormuz-would-require-thousands-of-u-s-troops-experts-say/) — the bill for that prediction is now arriving in the form of records the Navy would rather not be setting. It is worth noting too that when the White House trumpeted the restored flow of oil, [the loadings data told a more complicated story](/articles/2026-08-24-persian-gulf-loadings-diverge-from-u-s-hormuz-claims/).

So the blockade is succeeding at the thing it can measure and failing at the thing it exists for. Iran fights with drones and subsonic cruise missiles — "flying school buses," some U.S. officials call them — that are cheap, plentiful and expendable. October was a particularly violent month for commercial shipping since the war began in February, with more than 35 incidents logged by the U.K. Maritime Trade Operations Center; ships were set ablaze, some sank. Iran has lost the ability to see and track ships, by Washington's own account, and is attacking more in practice than it was before. An adversary degrading on paper and attacking more in practice is not an adversary on the verge of surrender.

Now take that $2 billion to $3 billion a month and ask what it is not buying. It is not buying the maintenance backlog at the public shipyards, where hull, reactor and propulsion work keeps sliding right. It is not buying the drydock time the destroyers need before their next deployment — which is exactly why the deployments keep getting extended instead. It is not buying the sailor's wife waiting on a repair schedule that keeps moving, or the family watching another holiday go by on a port-call promise. Every dollar of it is money spent keeping the oil moving so the same money never has to be spent on the things the Navy actually needs at home. The machine gets fed; the backlog grows.

Adm. Daryl Caudle, the chief of naval operations, is standing up that maintenance hub on Diego Garcia and promising more port calls, working with Pentagon leaders to give sailors some certainty about how long their deployments will run. "Sailors will do long deployments, but they need a port call," Caudle said this week. Read that sentence again. The chief of the most powerful navy on Earth is asking his own leadership for permission to let sailors touch land. That is not the posture of a force built for an open-ended siege. It is the posture of a force asking to survive its own mission.

The White House's only stated deadline is political: the blockade stays in "full force and effect," and there will be no attack on Iran before the midterm elections. That is a date on a campaign calendar, not an end state, and it is the White House, not some abstract Washington, that has chosen it. The Pentagon's orders to the fleet are open-ended — troops will sustain the blockade "as long as they are called upon" — and that is the most honest sentence in the entire conflict, because "as long as they are called upon" means the Pentagon has decided nothing and the White House will not decide until November.

The strait has become a contest over who can hold the waterway longest, and the White House has quietly made the United States the party that must hold it continuously, night after night, with aircraft that break and crews that don't get relieved. Every successful interception is real. It is also, right now, a way of postponing the question the White House and the Pentagon between them do not want to answer: what does victory look like, and who stands watch the morning after it is declared?
