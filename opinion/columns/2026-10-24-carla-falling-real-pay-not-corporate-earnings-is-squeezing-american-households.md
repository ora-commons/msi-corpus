Falling real pay, not corporate earnings, is squeezing American households. Jay Rogers's *National Review* op-ed, [Read the Balance Sheet, Not the Headlines](https://www.nationalreview.com/2026/10/read-the-balance-sheet-not-the-headlines/), argues that the economy is stronger than its dreadful mood, and he has the receipts to make that case on his own terms: second-quarter growth of 2.2 percent, final sales to private domestic purchasers rising 4.6 percent, initial jobless claims of 197,000 in the week ending October 3, unemployment at 4.2 percent, and third-quarter corporate earnings projected to jump 29.6 percent. His prescription: raise the debt ceiling before December 11, tighten spending, publish a multiyear deficit path, expand refining and pipeline capacity, and prevent a shutdown. He does not blame earnings for household pain. He uses them as reassurance. That distinction matters. A company's income statement is not a family's budget.

Rogers has an honest case, and he deserves to make it in full. The economy did not collapse. GDP grew at a 2.2 percent annual rate in the second quarter. Payrolls are growing. Claims are low and unemployment is 4.2 percent. Low layoffs matter. A shutdown fight and a debt-ceiling standoff can make uncertainty worse for everyone, and avoiding that is basic competence, not ideology. He is right that the aggregate economy has not contracted, and that a strong total is not the same thing as a strong household. The only question left is what the total is made of.

He has the ledger upside down. The essay that orders us to read the balance sheet never shows us the household balance sheet. It gives us GDP, a sentiment survey, jobless claims, a Federal Reserve statement, and a FactSet earnings forecast assembled from 19 companies that had reported as of October 9. That is not a household balance sheet. It is a press release wearing accounting shoes.

Corporate earnings are not household cash flow, and the gap between the two is the entire argument. A firm can report higher profits while workers' pay loses ground to prices. Corporate growth may belong to owners, shareholders, or investors; household growth shows up as money left after the bills. One number can rise while the other falls. Growth is not distribution. Profit is not income.

Household cash flow is simple: take-home pay minus housing, car and credit-card payments, food, fuel, heat, and other bills. If wages rise more slowly than prices, and required payments rise, less cash remains. September payrolls grew by only 29,000. Wages rose 3 percent against 3.7 percent inflation. Gasoline was 34.2 percent more expensive than a year earlier. Then consumer sentiment printed a preliminary 46.3 — below the first percentile of the University of Michigan survey's history, and lower than sentiment stood when any of the six recorded recessions began. The mood is not the disease. It is the receipt. And the gloom fell hardest among lower-income households and people with small stock portfolios. That is distribution, not psychology. Consumers are not gloomy because they misunderstand GDP. They are living the paycheck.

Put the wage gap in the sentiment paragraph, where it cannot disturb the balance sheet, and the headline remains clean. A paycheck losing ground is not mood. It is cash flow. I'm not against markets. Markets can price a sandwich. They cannot decide which family gets one when payrolls added only 29,000 jobs. Institutions decide that.

Then the $1.1 trillion net-interest figure needs its label. It describes the federal government's debt-service bill against a fiscal-year 2026 deficit near $2 trillion. It is not a measure of household debt service. Interest is cash the government pays to holders of debt issued in earlier years, and when the government finances that payment with new borrowing, the debt rises.

High rates reach households in concrete ways. The ten-year Treasury yield topped 5 percent in September, and the Federal Reserve's target range was 3.75 to 4 percent. When benchmark rates rise, adjustable-rate mortgage and consumer-loan payments reset upward; new borrowers face costlier credit; fixed-rate borrowers generally meet the change when they refinance. The payment takes a larger bite from the paycheck. A company can report stronger profits while the family making the product loses ground.

"Growing our way out of debt" skips exactly one question: who receives the growth? If productivity and corporate profits rise while wages lag behind prices, growth does not pay down household debt. It widens the gap. Rogers has to explain one sentence: how do 29,000 payroll jobs, wage growth of 3 percent against 3.7 percent inflation, and a ten-year Treasury yield above 5 percent produce a strong household economy? If he has an answer, he should give it. If he does not, he should stop presenting low layoffs and corporate profits as a verdict on the people living inside those numbers. A smaller deficit can be responsible. Austerity is not household prosperity.

The fiscal advice hides its cost in an adjective: "spending limits with teeth." Teeth on what? Rogers does not say. The deficit near $2 trillion and net interest of about $1.1 trillion are real and deserve an answer. But raising the debt ceiling merely authorizes more borrowing. It does not reduce the debt. Congress can cut spending, raise revenue, or grow faster; Rogers puts spending limits in the bill, leans on growth to close the gap, and leaves revenue out. That is a menu without prices.

And if those limits fall on Medicaid, housing assistance, child care, or public transit, ordinary households surrender purchasing power while debt service continues. Then the cure for weak demand is weaker demand. If the limits do not fall there, they are theater. Either way, Congress has to name the cuts and the revenue and show the ledger before families finance the adjustment. A debt-limit victory is not a household victory.

The energy recommendation is more modest, and mostly sensible. Expanding refining and pipeline capacity is long-run work; Rogers himself says it will not affect prices soon. But gasoline rose 34.2 percent over the preceding year, and a valuable signal is not relief. A refinery later does not fill the tank Friday. Build the capacity anyway. Americans should not have to choose between energy security and a plan for the household balance sheet.

Build an automatic floor, not a debt-ceiling cliff. Restore the expanded monthly Child Tax Credit as a near-universal child allowance, make it permanent, and send it through tax returns without a separate application. In 2021, child poverty fell from 9.7 percent to 5.2 percent under the Census Bureau's Supplemental Poverty Measure. Census researchers estimated the credit lifted 2.9 million children out of poverty, including 2.1 million attributable to the 2021 expansion. When the expansion lapsed in 2022, child poverty rose again. America ran the experiment. It produced the result.

An automatic payment is better fiscal plumbing than an emergency bill. It arrives in a household without waiting for a committee to declare the emergency, and it keeps working in ordinary times. It is cash families can spend, not another promise to apply for a program. A monthly payment reaches them before bills pile up. It gives them a margin when a shift ends, a car breaks, or a rent increase arrives. With the income phase-down built in, it can also stabilize: as earnings rise, support falls, rather than cutting off after a fixed date. A cushion during hard times without a floor that disappears in normal growth.

Pay for the floor by raising the top marginal individual income-tax rate, ending preferential tax treatment for carried interest, and capping mortgage-interest and charitable deductions for households with income above $500,000. That asks high earners and some owners of private firms to pay more. Their take-home income falls. That is the trade. A permanent floor cannot be free, and Congress should say who pays. Consolidate overlapping federal workforce and benefit-administration grants to finance retraining and automatic enrollment, and require the savings to be identified before the benefits launch.

Put bargaining power around the wage. Congress would have to create joint worker-employer panels in home care, fast food, and agriculture and authorize them to set minimum pay and working standards, so workers can bargain across an entire sector without joining a union. A sectoral wage board sets a floor across an industry instead of making workers bargain with one employer while a nearby shop undercuts them. A wage floor helps. Bargaining supplies the path above it. Pair it with automatic enrollment in unemployment benefits, so support arrives without an administrative obstacle course, and with active retraining, so a displaced worker moves to the next job instead of off the edge of the one she lost. This is the useful part of the Danish model: employers can adjust more easily because income security and retraining keep a layoff from becoming ruin. It protects the worker, not the job.

It is harder to transplant here because the institutions are less developed. Sectoral bargaining needs organized workers and enforcement. Retraining needs employers to identify where skills will be useful. Automatic benefits need a government that can reach people before they are destitute. Build those institutions rather than pretending a country can airlift Copenhagen.

Congress can raise the debt ceiling before December 11, publish a multiyear deficit path, block the shutdown, and still avoid making families finance the adjustment. A debt ceiling can prevent a default. It is not a budget, and brinkmanship only raises the cost of borrowing.

Put money in the household automatically. Put bargaining power around the wage. Protect the services that keep the next paycheck from becoming a wound.

Build the automatic child payment. Build the wage floor. Build the safety net. Fund all three. Build the floor and make growth pay the household. Then read the balance sheet again. The hurdle is not corporate profit. It is the cash left in a household after the mortgage, fuel bill, and heating bill are paid. The economy is not the weather; it is a set of choices, including this one.
