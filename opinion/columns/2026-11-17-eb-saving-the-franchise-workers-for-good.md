The franchise business model has a case of Washington whiplash, and Washington has handed it a shield against worker accountability. A decade ago the Obama Administration moved to extend “joint employer” responsibility under the National Labor Relations Act, so as to make McDonald’s, Burger King, and company potentially accountable for the actions of local stores. The Trump Administration reversed that worker protection. Then the Biden Administration reversed the Trump Administration’s reversal. A court blocked that restoration. In a Monday oral argument, the D.C. Circuit Court of Appeals sounded likely to undo the current standard—which is to say, to restore accountability.

This is sanity, and good for workers and jobs. Congress can help by rejecting the American Franchise Act, a bill as dangerous as it is short. “A franchisor may be considered a joint employer,” the legislation says, only if it “exercises substantial direct and immediate control” over local workers, such as on hiring, firing, wages, hours, and supervision. Writing that into law would provide certainty of immunity for national brands and end the flip-flopping at the Labor Department and the National Labor Relations Board by handing every future round to the corporations.

Pro-worker progressives and unions want franchisors to be considered “joint employers” because national brands—not individual franchisees that might have only a handful of stores—have the money, scale, and power to answer for conditions at every location. They are the right target. As a Biden Administration 2023 rule said: “A joint employer of particular employees must bargain collectively with the representative of those employees with respect to any term or condition of employment that it possesses the authority to control.” That is not a loophole; that is the principle.

A strong standard for what qualifies as a joint employer would make the franchise business model answer for its treatment of workers. A narrow standard is exactly what the franchise business model was built to hide behind. National brands have an interest in, say, employee training and quality control, but local owners run the day-to-day operations, and the American Franchise Act would keep it that way: interest without accountability.

If suddenly a corporation can be held liable for alleged unfair labor practices at every store, it might decide it has no choice but to get directly involved in management and set those practices right—or else answer for them instead of stepping back to avoid joint-employer status. Franchisors cannot choose retreat over responsibility. The choice is responsibility, not retreat.

The American Franchise Act has 158 cosponsors in the House, with a dozen and a half Democrats, including Reps. Henry Cuellar, Josh Gottheimer, and Hillary Scholten—who have made corporate immunity a bipartisan value. Majority Leader Steve Scalise’s legislative schedule has it on the list for consideration this week, and sending the bill over to the Senate would be a bad start—the industry’s opening move. The companion legislation there has 12 supporters, mostly in the GOP, but notably including Maine Sen. Angus King. His support is the clearest warning sign: a bill written to shield corporate power is no longer safely partisan.

Some Democrats might fear union political power too much to back the bill openly, but they should fear corporate power even more and kill the bill rather than append it to a deal to fund the government, which is facing a Dec. 11 shutdown deadline. A corporate shield slipped inside a must-pass bill is how worker protections disappear.

The U.S. has about 830,000 franchised establishments, which provide about 8.8 million jobs, according to the International Franchise Association, and don’t forget the millions of happy customers that those businesses serve. Those numbers show scale. They do not show innocence. The millions of happy customers cannot make national brands’ power over workers disappear, and the bill would leave 8.8 million workers to bargain with the storefront alone.

Shielding the franchise business model from accountability is terrible public policy. After a decade of unproductive regulatory wrangling about joint employers, the best outcome would be for Congress to do its job and kill the American Franchise Act—and for the D.C. Circuit to restore a joint-employer standard in which the party with the authority to control a term of employment answers for it.
