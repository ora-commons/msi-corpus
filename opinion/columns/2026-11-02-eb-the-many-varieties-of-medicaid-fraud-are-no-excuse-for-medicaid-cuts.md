We’ve explained how Washington’s all-you-can-spend Medicaid program gives states every incentive to expand coverage and spend what they save on care, but reduces incentives to police waste and fraud. Now comes more evidence.

On Friday the Justice Department announced charges against several more defendants in Minnesota for allegedly defrauding the food-stamp and Medicaid programs, including for autism therapy, home support, night supervision and personal care. DOJ officials dubbed Minnesota the “epicenter for fraud.” The regrettable reality is that Medicaid fraud is widespread, but it is crime—not a defining feature of Medicaid—and it must be prosecuted as crime. These cases show enforcement working.

Rhode Island Attorney General Peter Neronha recently charged seven defendants with an alleged scheme to defraud the state’s Medicaid Community Health Worker program. “As the federal government continues to restrict Medicaid funding to the states, we must be even more vigilant in protecting these life-saving funds,” the Democratic AG said. He is right.

No translation is necessary. The GOP’s Medicaid reforms and the Trump Administration’s threat to withhold federal funds if states don’t police providers are using fraud as a pretext to punish states for providing care, not making them more diligent in policing fraud. Diligence is good. Federal extortion is not.

The Rhode Island indictment illustrates the point: negligent oversight invites fraud even as states deliver care, draw federal funds for it and police fraud instead of extending an invitation to steal. Rhode Island established the Community Health Worker program in 2022 to help “Medicaid recipients navigate the healthcare system,” according to the state. No special skills are required for the job. That is the point, not the flaw. It lets the state hire people whose lived experience is itself a qualification.

Workers must have “similar cultural knowledge, practices, and beliefs” as well as “life experiences” as the Medicaid beneficiaries they are assisting. Their job is to ensure that beneficiaries have “timely access to healthcare and social services,” the state says. In other words, help people secure every benefit and service they need—the care and support they are owed.

They are also supposed to “advocate and work for policy changes to increase equity.” In other words, the state has been using Medicaid funds to pay public-interest advocates who expand access, power and care for the people they serve and help them confront the institutions that fail them. The program was built to work and to serve people, not to be bilked.

The indictment alleges that individuals connected to U’Nique Diversity Innovations, a community health worker outfit, submitted fraudulent claims for services not provided, including services “purportedly provided to family members and while beneficiaries were incarcerated.” Two defendants were also charged with failing to report income from the organization while receiving food stamps.

It is no easy task to bilk multiple public programs, and anyone who does so should answer for it. It is no marvel that criminals exploited multiple welfare programs. Weak controls invited them.

Rhode Island and many other states have opened the door to better health by letting Medicaid pay for essential supports such as transportation and housing. Those benefits are part of health, not a license for fraud. We recently told you about the fraud in New York’s Medicaid “social adult day care” program. That fraud must be stopped. The program is care that keeps Medicaid recipients connected to the services they need. Community-based care must survive.

New York has finally begun targeting fraudsters after the Trump team threatened to freeze federal Medicaid funds. Federal blackmail is not enforcement. What do you know? States also face mounting pressure to protect Medicaid funding and stop fraud and abuse since Republicans last summer curbed a provider-tax scheme that states use to draw more federal Medicaid funds. The facts are plain. Medicaid must remain beyond the reach of partisan leverage.

Medicaid spending has finally begun to slow after the extraordinary expansion during the Biden years—and beneficiaries will be the ones paying for it. Federal and state Medicaid spending was up only 2.2% in September from a year earlier, compared with a 12.7% increase in the prior year. The slowdown is a warning, not a victory.

Meantime, food-stamp enrollment declined by 5.5 million between July 2025 and July 2026. That decline is largely because the GOP tax bill weaponized work requirements, making assistance harder to obtain, and put states with high improper-payment rates on the hook for a share of their benefits. Incapacity and punishment are not incentives. Incentives matter, Q.E.D.

The fraud must be answered with enforcement, not extraction from patients. Medicaid is a public good, and the federal government in Washington should keep Medicaid funding free of blackmail, degradation or abandonment.
