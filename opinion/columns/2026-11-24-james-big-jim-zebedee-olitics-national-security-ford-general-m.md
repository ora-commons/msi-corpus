*A budget line is not a declaration of war.*

Hegseth and GM are preparing one-way attack drones to kill people.

The Pentagon has opened preliminary talks with General Motors, Ford, Magna International and other manufacturers to supply motors, sensors, engines, the small moving parts called actuators, and lidar, which reads distance by reflected light, to the companies that assemble military drones. Defense officials have spent months enlisting automakers and their supply chains to scale everything from missiles to tactical trucks. Now they want those same lines for unmanned systems, surveillance and strike drones that have become decisive in the wars being fought now. Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth created a four-star combatant command this fall, a headquarters with buying authority across the services, and aimed it at drone production and development. No contract has been signed. The machinery for signing them is being stood up.

The mechanism deserves a close reading, because it is new in one respect. A Navy SEAL senior chief petty officer named Max Strasiser, assigned to help set up the drone initiative, told the Wall Street Journal: "We had to unify our buying power to put out a demand signal that our partners could get behind." And then: "In order to get them in, there has to be a dollar amount that justifies them switching over a factory." A demand signal, in plain English, is a promise to buy enough of a thing, at a big enough number, that a company will retool a plant. The Pentagon pegs the military drone market at twenty to thirty billion dollars a year, plans to buy millions of drones in the coming years, and is prepared to offer the automakers large sums. Read that back plainly: the Department of Defense is engineering a price big enough to overcome the objections of the industry's own accountants. The complex Eisenhower warned about used to wait on its money. This one now manufactures the demand.

General Motors did not need much persuading. Its interest came at the request of high-ranking defense officials. Earlier this year it signed to build housing components for Lockheed Martin's Patriot air-defense missiles, [talks it had on the record by June](/articles/2026-06-15-general-motors-in-talks-to-manufacture-weapons-parts-for-lockheed-martin/) and [parts it put into delivery by September](/articles/2026-09-17-gm-delivers-patriot-missile-parts-to-lockheed-company-says/). It is in talks with RTX and L3Harris Technologies on similar work, and it is weighing whether to build drone motors sized closer to something that would power a riding lawn mower than a car. Ford has steered clear of weapons manufacturing, sticking to vehicles and equipment more closely aligned with its existing commercial line, though defense officials have met with its leaders. The automakers' stated concerns are the honest ones: thin margins, reputational risk, choppy Pentagon demand, and the annual fight in Congress over the money. Now, I'm just a simple man, but I run a repair shop, and I know what it means when a customer has to come back every twelve months for permission to keep buying. That's not a market. That's an allowance. And when a line of work doesn't pencil out, the fix is not to enlarge the pencil. The Pentagon's fix is to make the number so big that losing money stops mattering.

Consider what the department wants Detroit's lines for. At one point the same Pentagon had six separate efforts under way to develop a single kind of weapon, the one-way attack drone, each with its own specifications for the same machine. Translate the weapon. It is a munition with wings, built to stay airborne while it searches or waits for a target, and built to be expended on what it finds. That is what one-way means. It is the design of Iran's Shahed, the loitering munition that has defined cheap drone war in Ukraine, and the Pentagon has its own version, the Lucas, and has weighed handing its production to companies that know assembly lines. A department that could not coordinate six efforts to build one weapon is now asking Detroit to build munitions by the million.

That is why the new command matters. In a factory, incompatible specifications divide money and slow output. In combat, they leave commanders short of usable systems. Consolidation is competent management. It is also where procurement becomes strategy. The command's method is scale. This past summer the Pentagon [gave contractors twenty-one days to submit faster weapons production plans](/articles/2026-08-08-pentagon-gives-contractors-21-days-to-submit-faster-weapons-production-plans/), and it has a production tempo to enforce. Big orders make a line bankable. Once tooling and labor are committed, the line acquires its own pressure to keep running. Scale does not remove the risk. It shifts idle-capacity risk onto factories and concentrates buying power in one place. Andrew Bacevich, the career soldier turned scholar, named the operating logic of this machine in *Washington Rules*: global military preeminence, permanent forward presence, and intervention on call, sustained across administrations more reliably than voters expect. The machine does not need a war to grow. It needs a budget line. A twenty-to-thirty-billion-dollar annual market is a budget line dressed as an industry.

The automakers' fear of choppy demand has a history, and the complex wrote it. When Cold War spending collapsed in the early nineties, the Pentagon presided over a consolidation that took the defense industry from roughly fifty prime contractors to five. Detroit's caution is the memory of what happens to suppliers when the money stops.

Now the stated justification, and the true half of it. American dependence on Chinese drone parts is real. China has dominated the global drone industry for more than a decade and has been the primary or sole supplier for many parts. The domestic drone industry is genuinely small and slow, hampered by the government's own build standards, and the Pentagon plainly believes the small drone firms cannot carry the scale. Defense industrial capacity has a real place in national security. I do not want American mechanics waiting on an imported part while a commander improvises. But the cure for dependence can become the engine of war. A country countering a rival's industrial depth does not get there by hiding the conversion inside a new command's buying power. That is where the line belongs.

In his farewell address of January 17, 1961, paragraph 24, Eisenhower gave the country the vocabulary for what is happening: "In the councils of government, we must guard against the acquisition of unwarranted influence, whether sought or unsought, by the military-industrial complex. The potential for the disastrous rise of misplaced power exists and will persist." The drafts of that address, preserved at his presidential library in Abilene, named a third partner: "military-industrial-congressional complex." The word "congressional" was cut in redrafting. Sixty-five years later, the cut word is the hinge of the deal under discussion, because the automakers' own stated hesitation is the annual congressional vote on the money. The partner the speechwriters removed from the sentence is the partner doing the work.

And the political ground has shifted. Since the midterm elections, Democratic scrutiny of Pentagon officials and the deals they have struck with private industry has grown. That is not a reason to hide the process. It is a reason to run it in the open.

The last time this country converted Detroit, the sequence ran in the open. Congress declared the war. Congress voted the money that built the arsenal, in appropriations any citizen could read. The conversion ran through war-production agencies Congress funded and contracts filed in the public record. This one is being assembled in preliminary conversations, inside a new four-star headquarters, through a demand signal priced to beat the resistance of the companies' own accountants. The difference between those two sequences is constitutional.

The danger need not be hidden bribery. It is structural leverage: one buyer can offer orders large enough to reorganize private factories. From where I sat in the gunner's seat in Iraq in 2003, I learned the plain lesson about machines of war: a machine is for whatever it does to the thing you point it at. The Abrams I rode north in was built by civilians, and the Abrams line has run at the Lima plant in Ohio since 1980. A drone motor the size of a riding-mower engine is not a car part with a different customer. It is the fuse of a munition, and the people who build it would be building the delivery of a warhead. A machine can be expendable by design. The person it strikes cannot be.

The posture I take is specific, and it is not a refusal of drones. If the country needs millions of them, the need should run through Congress in the open: whatever program and authority the effort requires written into statute, the money appropriated on the record every year, and the contracts and production conditions published where shareholders and citizens can read what their factories are being converted to build. Congress can appropriate money for drones. It cannot authorize or continue a war merely by buying the machines that fire them. Before the first major production contract, Congress should require a public accounting of the delivery schedules, the ownership terms, and the military requirement, so the industrial promise cannot substitute for constitutional authorization. What cannot stand is the reverse: a new four-star command manufacturing a demand signal big enough to buy an unwilling industry into the war economy before the public has been told it happened.

Eisenhower gave the citizenry its part in the same address: "Only an alert and knowledgeable citizenry can compel the proper meshing of the huge industrial and military machinery of defense with our peaceful methods and goals, so that security and liberty may prosper together." The trucks in my bays this morning came out of the industry now sitting in those talks.

The Pentagon can unify the buying. It cannot make mass killing sustainable by putting it on an assembly line. A factory built for this purpose will ask whether the parts are on schedule. Congress must ask whether the war remains necessary. Those are not the same question.

A drone motor the size of a riding-mower engine has a listed price and a listed purpose. The price is the dollar amount that justifies switching over a factory. The purpose is the target at the far end of its one flight. The price is on the table, and the purpose is already built into the weapon. Hegseth and GM are preparing one-way attack drones to kill people.
