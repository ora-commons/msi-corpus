Donald Trump is using Islamophobia to smear Abdul El-Sayed and preserve Republican Senate control.

Abdul El-Sayed is a doctor and public-health official. He supports government-run health insurance and abolishing ICE. His criticism of Israel has created tension inside his own party. He would be the first Muslim person ever elected to the United States Senate. None of that disappears.

I begin with my own accounting. My Church has welcomed the stranger and closed itself to the stranger. Catholic politicians have used mercy to bless power and justice to excuse closure. I do not stand outside this field of accountability. In this political climate, the stranger is first made into a warning sign and then denied a fair hearing. We cannot ask for trust while treating the stranger’s humanity as a favor.

The accusation is precise. Trump invoked 9/11 and called El-Sayed a “jihadist” without citing evidence. Trump and El-Sayed’s Senate opponent Mike Rogers sought to link him to the Muslim Brotherhood, an organization El-Sayed has denounced. I call that religious defamation. I call the use of 9/11 to erase a man evil. Matthew’s Jesus says, “I desire mercy, not sacrifice.” Trump offers the sacrifice of a man’s religion.

This is not politics. It is the manufacture of fear. It takes a man whose policies millions may reject and turns his religion into a weapon against him. The smear is not incidental to the campaign. It is the method: make a Muslim American into a warning sign, then place fear at the center of immigration enforcement and Senate politics. I will not soften the word: it is sin.

Voters can reject El-Sayed’s positions for honest reasons. Trump has no right to make “jihadist” the shortcut that ends the examination. Who decides that a man’s religion is enough to disqualify him before his policies are considered?

Trump has decided.

Rogers has maintained Trump’s support despite decrying his Canada tariffs and his Russia diesel deal and calling for a quick end to the war with Iran. He nevertheless joined the Muslim Brotherhood attack. Distance on one policy does not cleanse complicity in another. A senator does not wash his hands of one cruelty because he has quarreled with the president about a different one.

Rogers, you are a person. So is El-Sayed. That fact is not a campaign ornament. It is the moral floor.

The arithmetic is blunt. Democrats need four Senate flips, as [MSI’s earlier guide to the Senate map](/articles/2026-10-30-democrats-need-four-senate-flips-as-red-state-races-tighten/) explained. The larger ballot includes [35 Senate contests, 39 governorships, and local offices](/articles/2026-10-26-midterms-put-35-senate-contests-39-governorships-and-local-offices-on-the-ballot/), and Cook Political Report rates seven as toss-ups. Four seats would give Democrats control, with the power to limit Trump’s ambitions, reject his judicial nominations, and investigate his administration.

But the Senate is not only a scoreboard. It is the chamber that writes the rules under which human beings live. The races are full of people. Campaign season reduces each person to a warning sign. The political question is not which résumé we like. The moral question is whether the Senate will protect workers, immigrants, disabled citizens, and families instead of treating them as electoral fuel—or whether it will protect power from human beings.

Christian Scripture asks believers to welcome the stranger. The Torah commands care for the stranger as oneself. Micah asks what the Lord requires: justice, love kindness, and humility before God.

Trump’s accusation satisfies none of them. It offers El-Sayed no justice. It offers a Muslim American no kindness. It offers the public no humility before the limits of knowledge. It gives power a political advantage and makes the vulnerable pay for it.

Jesus tells of a wounded man, a priest, a Levite, and a Samaritan. The priest passes. The Levite passes. The Samaritan bends down and binds the man’s wounds. The story stops there.

The campaigns have not stopped there. They have made religion into a reason to pass by a person. Trump’s smear gives voters a shortcut around El-Sayed’s health-care plan, his criticism of Israel, and his call to abolish ICE. It avoids the hard work of argument by making the voter afraid of the man instead.

That is the modern Pharisee operation: preserve the religious form while hollowing out the justice beneath it.

Other races are asking their own version of the same question.

In Texas, former middle-school teacher and Presbyterian seminary student James Talarico is challenging Ken Paxton, Trump’s ally. Talarico taught in a San Antonio neighborhood he has described as one of the state’s poorest, and he names Pope Leo a “spiritual hero.” The race has become the most expensive Senate race ever, with more than $170 million from Elon Musk supporting Paxton. Aggressive immigration enforcement is already cutting against Texas Republicans.

Private money can buy more airtime. It cannot buy justice.

In Maine, Susan Collins is seeking a sixth term after voting to convict Trump in 2021. Her opponent Troy Jackson is a logger who helped lead a protest against lower-paid Canadian workers entering Maine. He favors abolishing ICE; so does El-Sayed. Jackson’s history places work and wages at the border.

At that border, ICE answers with detention and family separation. That is cruelty. Enforcement turns policy into a lesson: the stranger’s body may be seized. Leviticus commands that the alien who resides among the household be treated as a citizen and loved as oneself. Matthew insists that strangers must be welcomed.

Immigration enforcement is a school. It teaches that the stranger’s body may be seized. That lesson is cruelty taught as order.

On the final day of the campaign, Trump traveled to Iowa for rallies, where former local news reporter Ashley Hinson is running against Josh Turek. Turek was born with spina bifida, underwent multiple surgeries, and won wheelchair-basketball gold at two Paralympic Games. Hinson casts him as an East Coast liberal. Turek says Trump’s tariffs have produced a farmageddon. The President’s rally says he wants Iowans’ loyalty. The farms will judge the tariffs by their costs.

In Alaska, Mary Peltola, the first Alaska Native elected to Congress, is running on fish, family, and freedom. She grew up fishing salmon with her father. Alaska may be the race that settles the chamber, and its ranked-choice count can arrive after the map looks decided.

In Kansas, Methodist pastor Adam Hamilton is seeking a Democratic Senate seat while campaigning on lower costs and health-care access against Roger Marshall, the former obstetrician who delivered some 5,000 babies. Marshall calls Hamilton “too woke” and faces scrutiny over reports that he sued patients unable to pay their medical bills. Marshall calls the reports a political hit job.

A doctor who delivers babies does not answer a question about suing patients unable to pay by dismissing it.

In Ohio, Sherrod Brown, a champion of workers for three decades in Congress, is challenging Jon Husted’s promise of business-led growth while Trump’s trade wars have hit manufacturing and autos.
