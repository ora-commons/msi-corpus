Pope Leo’s Paris Mass Must Answer for the Children We Exclude.

The Vatican counted 700,000 people at the open-air Mass in the Place de la Concorde. I do not doubt the size of the crowd. I doubt the moral conclusion being drawn from it.

A crowd is not a conversion. A procession is not proof of holiness. A Church does not become faithful because seven hundred thousand people sing its name beneath the French flag.

The human body that stays with me is smaller than the crowd. It is the body of a baby lifted above the heads along the Champs-Élysées so the pope could touch the child. The report does not tell us the child’s name, family, citizenship, or future. That silence matters. A child can become a symbol in an instant. The child can also disappear again into the crowd.

Catholic faith begins there, with the particular body. Not with the count. Not with the flags. Not with the spectacle of a religious institution recovering public volume.

Pope Leo told the crowd not to keep faith to itself. “Let it overflow,” he said, “so that those you meet may be refreshed and their thirst satisfied.” The command is beautiful. It is also dangerous if Catholic leaders reduce overflow to public visibility. Water that reaches only the already gathered is not a river. It is a fountain inside a locked courtyard.

Catholic Social Teaching gives the Church a harder measure. *Rerum Novarum* begins with workers pressed by misery and wretchedness. The tradition’s preferential option for the poor asks who bears the cost when the powerful speak of order, borders, security, or national renewal. *Dignitatis Humanae* defends the human person’s freedom in matters of faith. That freedom is not a license to turn religious identity into a political weapon against the stranger.

The child lifted toward the popemobile therefore asks a question the crowd cannot answer for us: Who is lifted when the cameras leave?

Is it the migrant family waiting outside a detention center? Is it the worker whose labor feeds the city but whose legal status keeps him silent? Is it the asylum-seeking child taught that the country where she sleeps may still expel her? Is it the deportee sent across a border after years of work, worship, and family life in the place he called home?

The Christian answer cannot be that these people matter less because they are not French, not documented, not useful, or not present beneath the papal balcony. Jesus said, “I was a stranger and you welcomed me.” He also said, “I was a stranger and you did not welcome me.” The judgment falls on what we did with the body in front of us.

That judgment reaches every side.

The defenders of strict *laïcité* are right to guard public institutions from religious domination. A republic should not become a chapel for one faith, and religious liberty should not become clerical control of the state. But a secular principle becomes a moral failure when it treats public religious people as a problem to be contained rather than citizens whose convictions must be protected. The French Republic need not kneel before the Church. It must still see the person kneeling.

The Catholic Right deserves no easier passage. It cannot cheer a pope’s public visibility while blessing nativist politics that make migrants into threats, workers into instruments, and children into collateral damage. It cannot invoke family, faith, and civilization while supporting the detention and expulsion of families whose only offense is lacking the right document. That is not Catholic defense. It is religious instrumentalization.

Our own Catholic communities have helped build this contradiction. We have welcomed immigrants at the parish door while tolerating political movements that scapegoat them outside it. We have prayed over the Holy Family’s flight into Egypt while accepting policies that punish families for fleeing violence. I write from inside that complicity. The Church has not merely failed to speak loudly enough. Too often it has lent its symbols, silence, or respectability to the people making strangers afraid.

Pope Leo’s Mass should be judged by the life it sends us toward. If faith overflows, it must reach the person whose papers are missing, the child whose parents are detained, the worker whose wages are withheld, and the officer who has been taught to see a neighbor as a case number. Every one of them remains a person. Every one of them stands within the Church’s moral field.

Óscar Romero once pleaded, begged, and commanded: “Stop the repression!” He did not mistake a full cathedral for a just society. He knew that the Gospel must touch the real sin of the society in which it is proclaimed.

Paris gave Pope Leo a vast congregation. The next question is whether that congregation will become a people who welcome the stranger. The baby lifted above the crowd disappeared back into someone’s arms. That is where the faith must go next: not into the air, but into the arms that carry the vulnerable home.
