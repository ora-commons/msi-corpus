China's household registration system denies young graduates the public services of the cities that employ them.

That is not a side effect of the graduate employment squeeze. It is the instrument that makes the squeeze survivable for the state and unsurvivable for the workers.

The instrument has a name: 户口, the household registration system, administered through the State Council's tiered reform framework. The 2014 State Council document — *Opinions on Further Promoting Reform of the Household Registration System*, Guo Fa [2014] No. 66, issued that July — restructured a system that had for six decades made a person's birthplace the determinant of their access to public services. Before 2014, hukou was essentially immovable. After 2014, it was movable — but only in some directions, in some cities, under conditions that reward exactly the people least affected by it.

**The steel-man.** The 2014 reform was the most significant liberalization in the system's history, and its stated logic is defensible on its own terms. China moved hundreds of millions of people from rural to urban areas in three decades — the largest migration in human history. The State Council's framework sorted cities by size and offered different settlement rules: full liberalization in small cities, orderly settlement in medium cities, points-based systems in large cities, and strict scale control in the four first-tier cities — Beijing, Shanghai, Guangzhou, and Shenzhen — where the largest share of college graduates end up. The theory was to match public service capacity to population, prevent the informal megacity settlements that plague São Paulo and Johannesburg, and channel migration toward smaller cities where infrastructure exists. The National New Urbanization Plan (2014–2020) set a target of a 60 percent urbanization rate by 2020. The country exceeded it. On the framework's own aggregate metrics, the reform worked.

**The audit.** The framework worked at the level of national urbanization while failing at the level of the individual graduate producing for a city she cannot belong to. The points-based settlement systems in the first-tier cities — the destinations absorbing the disproportionate share of China's new graduates — reward advanced degrees, high-level technical certifications, employer sponsorship, and property ownership. A twenty-two-year-old graduate from Anhui working in a Shenzhen technology firm is generating output for the Shenzhen economy, paying rent to a Shenzhen landlord, consuming in the Shenzhen market. She cannot enroll her child in a Shenzhen public school. She cannot use a Shenzhen public hospital at the resident rate. She is not eligible for the Shenzhen housing subsidies available to registered residents. The 2014 framework's own tiered logic — which recognized that the first-tier cities needed stricter controls — produced precisely this: a permanent guest workforce in the four cities where the graduate employment squeeze is sharpest.

This is the mechanism behind what the reporting describes as resignation rather than anger. You do not agitate for services in a city you are a guest in. The hukou system converts a political question — who gets access to public services in the city that employs them — into a market question: who can accumulate the points, who can wait the years, who can afford the property, whose employer will sponsor. Graduates who cannot answer those questions do not protest. They leave, or they absorb the cost, or they find the flexible arrangement that makes the cost bearable.

**The gig economy, read as a hukou response.** Flexible platform work — delivery, ride-hailing, content production, freelance coding — does not require the formal labor registration that ties a worker to a specific city's social insurance system. For a graduate without first-tier hukou, the formal employment path offers wages calculated against a service-access baseline she cannot reach. Gig work offers wages calculated against nothing — no services, no registration, no accumulation toward eligibility — but also no false promise. The gig economy is not merely absorbing workers the formal economy will not hire. It is absorbing workers who have rationally priced the formal employment deal and found it wanting, in part because the hukou system prices it that way.

**The AI displacement, read the same way.** When AI replaces formal-sector roles, the displaced worker loses not only wages but the formal employment relationship — the only pathway toward employer-sponsored hukou accumulation available to most graduates. Each round of displacement pushes the worker further from the registration status that would make urban settlement permanent. The State Council's urbanization targets assume a stable formal-sector workforce accumulating toward registered status. AI adoption is shortening the runway faster than the points systems can adjust.

**The choice.** The same State Council that maintains the tiered hukou framework tracks the demographic decline. China's working-age population is shrinking. Educational attainment is rising. A shrinking workforce with rising credentials makes the current rationing regime increasingly irrational: you cannot simultaneously need these graduates and exclude them from the cities where the work is. The choice is between letting the first-tier cities continue to run on a guest workforce that has no structural stake in staying, or opening registration to the population the economy most urgently requires. The 2014 framework chose the former. Twelve years of evidence suggest the cost of that choice is a generation that has priced in the deal and is refusing to pay it.

This is not a story about a generation losing faith. It is a story about an instrument rationing a public good to the people producing it — and a generation that has noticed.
