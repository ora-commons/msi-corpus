The ECB is extracting a war's bill from French households and calling it price stability. The inflation surge began after the first U.S. airstrikes on Iran, not with any surge in wage demands, and the collection is happening twice: once at the pump, where the war repriced fuel, and once at the rate-setting table, where the bank's answer to the repricing is to raise the cost of borrowing across the euro area.

Here are the numbers. INSEE, France's statistics office, reported on November 27 that consumer prices were 3.2% above a year earlier, down from 3.7% in October. Economists polled by The Wall Street Journal had expected 3.6%. The same series stood at 1.1% in February, before the first airstrikes, and by October had reached its highest level in nearly three years. The November reading is a lower fuel-price print, and the newest tables show the war's shock fading at the edges.

Oil prices fell after the U.S. midterms, as hopes of a lasting resolution to the conflict rose, although they remained above prewar levels. European natural-gas prices stayed near multiyear highs, keeping household energy prices elevated through the colder winter months. Services inflation fell, driven by sharp decreases in accommodation and transport prices. Eurozone inflation had reached 4% earlier this month.

A supply shock is a rise in prices caused by contracting supply, not by demand running hot. The instrument a central bank controls—the policy rate, the price of credit across the currency area—cannot produce a barrel of crude or a cubic meter of gas. Against this shortage, it can suppress the demand that would have paid the war's price: investment, hiring, orders. The Federal Reserve has answered the same kind of inflation with unemployment, and Frankfurt has imported the mechanism. Inflation is a real constraint; that is not in dispute. The methodological question is which instrument reaches the cause.

Nothing in the French record shows demand running hot. INSEE's third-quarter national accounts put gross domestic product—the economy's total output—down 0.2% for July through September, as exports fell and private investment declined. Consumer spending was flat in October, consumer confidence hit its lowest level in more than three years, and purchasing-manager surveys showed steady growth in Germany while activity in France deepened. The ECB is tightening inside that contraction. That is not caution about risks that have not arrived. It is monetary contraction applied after output has already turned negative.

The European Central Bank has already raised its key rate twice in 2026 because it judged the energy-price surge too large to ignore. Investors expect a third increase next month. A third hike would make three increases in a single year against a shock the bank's own instrument cannot reach. The rate increases are contractionary: they make credit more expensive and reduce demand. They cannot produce oil or natural gas, restore French exports, or replace private investment. The central bank can restrain the economy. No interest rate can supply the missing energy.

The diagnosis is supply; the instrument is demand. The ECB launders the energy shortage through the demand instrument because the demand instrument is what it has. This is the old central-bank category error: a shortage answered with a price on credit. Monetary policy cannot solve a shortage by making credit more expensive.

The European Union expects French inflation to peak at 4.3% in the first quarter of 2027 as higher energy costs pass through into food and services. Yet incoming data show few signs of that spillover; services inflation fell in November. A forecast is not a finding. The case for a third hike is persistence—an assumed pass-through from energy into wages and broad services that the measured record does not yet show forming. A council that hikes on what it has not seen form is collecting on money that has not been spent.

The laundering sits in the mandate language. “Price stability” is being deployed as if it meant one number in all weathers—two percent, whatever the cause. That number is a convention, not a finding. Paul Volcker dismissed numerical targets as “false precision.” The definition he and Greenspan carried contains no number at all: “the state in which expected changes in the general price level do not effectively alter business or household decisions.”

The convention a competent council applies to a war shock is to look through the first round—the direct pass-through of energy prices into measured inflation—and act only if second-round effects form: the persistence that sets in once workers and firms demand compensation. The incoming data run the other way. [Euro-area negotiated wage growth—the contract-wage series that would carry a spiral—stood at 2.57%](/articles/2026-11-20-eurozone-inflation-reaches-4-while-negotiated-wage-growth-rises-to-2-57/), not the acceleration a wage-price spiral requires, while services inflation fell. Falling real wages—wages after inflation—are not a spiral.

Higher French government-bond yields compound the pressure. Investors' concern about public finances has raised the government's borrowing costs, and those yields tighten credit conditions—the cost of borrowing—before the ECB acts. The ECB's higher key rate tightens them again. Households and firms face more expensive financing while the government's cost of refinancing its debt rises. A policy that cannot lower energy prices can lower output, investment, and tax receipts. It can make France's fiscal arithmetic worse before it makes the inflation print better. Formal independence is not a methodology. It protects the ECB from political direction; it does not make a rate hike the right tool for an energy-price shock.

The remedy follows the arithmetic: look through the first round and publish the second-round tests monthly—wages, services, and core inflation, meaning price growth apart from food and energy—so escalation, if it comes, is visible when it comes. Treat the energy bill as a fiscal problem, addressed directly at household energy costs with targeted rebates for lower-income households rather than broad subsidies, financed without cutting the programs the shock makes harder to use. The ECB should not raise rates against an energy shock it cannot reverse; a third December hike would offer the opposite course, a rate increase into a French contraction—the move the Very Serious People consensus mistakes for discipline.

I spent a career at desks where the first question about any inflation print was what produced it, because the answer selects the instrument. The tradition I served too often skipped the question and reached for the instrument it owns. It is doing so again, in Frankfurt and in Washington.

INSEE's 3.2% November reading is a lower fuel-price print. The ECB's two 2026 rate increases sit beside France's 0.2% contraction. The bank can raise the price of credit; it cannot lower the price of energy. The rate decisions are the extraction.

The war set the prices. The council is collecting them.
