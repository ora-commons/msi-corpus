The Democratic Party is weaponizing Christian faith to win elections.

When James Talarico took the stage after his Texas Senate victory, he ended as he began: with Jesus. “That barefoot rabbi didn’t stay in his room and pray. He walked into the seat of power and he flipped over the tables of injustice,” he said from behind a lectern marked “Love Thy Neighbor.” Then he told the crowd, “Texas, it’s time to start flipping tables.” Talarico [defeated Ken Paxton](/articles/2026-11-04-james-talarico-defeats-ken-paxton-in-texas-senate-race/) in Texas. Adam Hamilton, a United Methodist megachurch pastor who flashed his Bible at campaign events, won the Kansas Senate race. Sarah Trone Garriott, a Lutheran minister, beat a Republican House incumbent in Iowa. Nine Democrats who studied at a seminary or divinity school will serve in the 120th Congress. The [clergy-candidate breakthrough across the House and Senate](/articles/2026-11-06-clergy-candidates-help-democrats-flip-congress-boosting-the-religious-left/) helped propel their party to its most sweeping victory in decades.

The Democratic Congressional Campaign Committee held briefings for congressional candidates on reaching faith voters and communities. Earlier reporting documented [Democratic candidates using religious language and listening to reach voters](/articles/2026-11-04-democratic-candidates-used-religious-language-and-listening-to-reach-voters/). This was not private faith spilling accidentally into politics. It was an organized electoral operation. As a Catholic, I know how easily the Church’s moral language can be borrowed for a political project. I do not stand outside this accounting: a Catholic identity is not a campaign mailing list, and a bishop’s public witness is not campaign inventory. I have lived inside a Catholic world where hospitality could be treated as a political bargaining chip and silence called prudence. That is our complicity. When the Church fails to hold power accountable, no political ally should receive immunity. That rule governs the operation now visible in the Democratic Party.

For a decade, many Democratic candidates and leaders shied away from embracing Christianity because of its association with conservative positions on abortion. Now the party treats faith as an asset because it needs votes. Preliminary exit polls indicated a three-percentage-point shift in Protestant support toward Democratic candidates relative to 2024 and an eleven-point shift among Roman Catholics. White evangelicals still overwhelmingly backed Republicans. A person close to the national Republican Party’s leadership said religious voters who backed Democrats likely did so for economic reasons, not because they had changed their minds about which party represented their values. This may not yet be a theological realignment. It is already an electoral one.

Whether a candidate is sincere is a question about the candidate. Whether the campaign turns faith into an electoral instrument is a question about the campaign. The election answered the second question. I call this religious instrumentalization. That judgment does not settle the candidates’ sincerity. It names what their campaigns are doing with faith: turning the moral authority of Christ into a political instrument. That corrupts the relationship between faith and power.

Candidate, you may ask for my vote. You may not ask me to sanctify the platform before I have examined it. A voter may bring conscience to the ballot. No party may demand that conscience pre-sign its platform.

Church-state separation is not the removal of God from public life. It is the refusal to make either a church or a party platform the measure of citizenship. A Christian may hear a candidate’s theology and reject his policy. A party that treats disagreement with its platform as hostility to God has crossed the line.

The new candidates describe a model built around diversity and compassion for the vulnerable. Talarico tied God and the Golden Rule to expanding health care, lowering the cost of living, and repealing billionaire tax cuts. Those policies deserve examination on their merits. Talarico, your prophetic language must now pass through those policies—and through the duty to protect migrant families from enforcement that treats them as disposable. A campaign may ask for a vote. It may not ask the Gospel to carry it. A church is not a ballot box.

Hamilton and Garriott stand under the same demand. If you invoke Christ, you must serve the poor, welcome the stranger, and refuse to make fear a civic sacrament.

Trump’s religious politics shows the failure this new religious politics must refuse. A White House spokesman said, “There has never been a greater president for Christian Americans than President Trump, and his strong record proves it.” The claim collapses under its own terms: a claim of proof met by contradiction is not proof. Pope Leo XIV and the United States bishops spoke against Trump’s immigration-enforcement tactics, some of which particularly affected Hispanic Catholics. The new candidates cannot answer that record with more religious speech. They must answer it in border policy, hearing procedures, and the treatment of immigrant families.

The Democratic campaign must not answer one corruption of Christian authority with another. I apply the same rule to both sides: if Scripture is invoked to protect power from judgment, the invocation is corrupt; if Scripture is invoked to bring power under judgment, it belongs in public life. The difference is not the party label. It is the direction of obedience. The new operation does not become less corrupt because the older corruption exists.

Micah asked, “What does the Lord require of you but to do justice, and to love kindness, and to walk humbly with your God?” A campaign cannot answer that question with a moving speech about Jesus. Justice is a policy. It is whether a child stays with their family at the border, whether a worker can organize without losing the job, whether an asylum seeker receives a hearing, whether a sick person can obtain care. Kindness is not a tone in an advertisement. It is what remains when the election is over and the cameras leave. Humility is letting the text judge the candidate, the party, and the platform.

That same witness sets a standard no Democratic candidate may evade: immigrant suffering cannot be treated as a partisan inconvenience. We who claim the gospel must require a humane immigration policy: family unity, protection of workers from wage theft and retaliation, a real hearing for asylum seekers, and detention as a last resort rather than a business model. We must also require a just economic order: affordable health care, a family wage, and taxation that takes aim at concentrated wealth whose power has made insecurity profitable.

John XXIII’s *Pacem in Terris* (§25) declares, “The fact that he is a citizen of a particular State does not deprive him of membership in the human family.” The United States and Mexican bishops’ *Strangers No Longer* affirms that nations may control their borders while insisting upon migrant dignity and protection for refugees and asylum seekers. Humane law therefore requires a real hearing, family unity, and employer accountability. It does not require the abandonment of law.

Any invocation of Christ that leaves the poor exposed has made the Savior a mascot. That is idolatry, not faith.

Romero’s voice was not a party identity. On March 23, 1980, he told soldiers that no soldier is obliged to obey an order against the law of God. Then he said: “In the name of God, in the name of this suffering people whose laments rise to heaven each day more tumultuous, I beg you, I beseech you, I order you in the name of God: Stop the repression!” *¡Cese la represión!* He named the sin, addressed the actor, and left conscience open. No agent of state power is excused from conscience because an order carries a badge, a title, or a majority. Prophetic politics does not baptize a platform. It tells power what to stop.

Clergy candidates, the door is open. Stop using faith to win the election; let faith judge the platform before you ask for votes. You can name your sin and begin repair. The election gave you office, not absolution. The lectern still says “Love Thy Neighbor.” The neighbor may not vote our way. That is the point.

At dawn in Redemption Springs, before I unlock the parish door, the lamp above the steps is still burning. People come carrying work, papers, fear, and grief. We make room without asking them to prove they deserve it. The door stays open. So does the judgment.
