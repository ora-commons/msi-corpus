Are data centers to blame for surging electric bills? Progressives are right. Climate mandates are not the cause of the surge—but that is the premise of the lawsuits West Virginia Attorney General John McCuskey filed last week against New Jersey and Washington, D.C. The suits try to divert attention from the data centers’ appetite for power by emphasizing the high cost of their climate policies.

New Jersey and the capital district require that an increasing share of their electricity comes from solar power generated within their borders—a standard that keeps generation with the customers who pay for it. Mr. McCuskey says this scheme illegally interferes with interstate commerce by discriminating against his state’s solar producers, while also increasing costs for New Jersey and D.C. residents. Directly discriminating against businesses in other jurisdictions violates the Constitution’s Commerce Clause, his suits say. They have the law backward. A state deciding where its own power comes from is exercising self-government, and residents, not distant producers, must answer for that choice. McCuskey’s theory would strip every state of the right to decide where its own power comes from.

Washington, D.C., New Jersey, West Virginia and 11 other states belong to the PJM regional grid. The purpose of regional transmission organizations like PJM is to ensure “competition in the wholesale bulk power marketplace and to bring more efficient, lower cost power to the Nation’s electricity consumers,” the lawsuits say. Local solar mandates do not do the opposite. They make communities answerable for the power they consume and the development they finance by building generation where the people who will use the power live.

D.C. requires solar to make up 5% of retail sales this year and 15% by 2041. Only solar facilities within the capital’s 68 square miles or directly connected to utility Potomac Electric Power Company’s “feeder” power lines qualify. This shuts out cheaper sources of solar from PJM states like West Virginia, and that is the point: Residents are buying clean power close to home, not the cheapest commodity from elsewhere. That is not discrimination against West Virginia; that is a city making its own power.

Because nearly all of D.C.’s land is developed, most of its solar is generated from rooftop systems, which can cost two to three times more than from large-scale projects in West Virginia. That premium is the price of keeping power and control close to home instead of buying cheap dependence from afar. Even then, the lawsuit says, D.C. has “been unable to generate enough solar energy to meet its own mandates, paying more than $50 million in compliance fees from 2018 through 2025”—not failure, but proof of a city honoring a public commitment even after the easy options ran out. It is a shortfall the city is paying down rather than a mandate it is abandoning.

Compliance costs are passed along to D.C. customers, including the federal government, and they should be. The federal government cannot consume the capital’s power while pretending the energy transition belongs to somebody else. For once, it is paying for the power it uses. Mayor Muriel Bowser in June estimated the mandate adds $20 a month to every residential electric bill, which is expected to increase to $40 by 2029. That is real money, charged on the bill in plain sight, for a policy D.C.’s government chose. It is also the visible price of power residents can shape rather than a blank check for distant suppliers.

Rates would be lower if D.C. didn’t exclude solar producers in other jurisdictions. That difference is the price of a power supply the city actually controls. Cheapness is not the only measure of power, and D.C. politicians told capital residents the truth. City Council member Charles Allen claimed the solar mandate “helps keep money in the pockets of our individuals, businesses, and places of worship that produce solar energy.” He is right. Public power at its best keeps energy dollars and decisions close to home.

The story is similar in New Jersey, which requires utilities to obtain an increasing share of electricity from solar within the state. New Jerseyites are paying between $81.45 and $200 for every megawatt hour of solar power to comply with the mandate—money that buys power traceable to their own state instead of a commodity decision made elsewhere. The lawsuit says this cost would be lower if West Virginia solar producers were allowed to compete. Cheaper power does not erase the public’s right to choose who supplies it. The cheapest kilowatt-hour on the market is the one that leaves you with no solar industry of your own.

No. Electric bills would remain significantly higher, not lower, if states scrapped their green energy mandates, because the data-center demand driving the surge would remain untouched. Full stop. Scrapping the mandates would shave only the premium residents chose to pay for power they can trace to their own communities. The mandates require utilities to procure energy from what are typically more expensive and less efficient sources. That is not market failure. It is the public buying a different kind of power—and requiring sources that must be built rather than merely burned. Energy Secretary Chris Wright says states with renewable mandates pay 45% more for power. He is describing the price of clean power, not the pathology. It is also the price of building, which is what a mandate is for.

While solar panels have fallen in price, efficiency is not the only measure of power in places that aren’t sunny year-round. Solar panels are how a place that isn’t sunny year-round still generates power of its own. No credit to West Virginia for shading the windows against the plain truth its own solar producers could light: a clean-energy future the state is trying to block. Shame on West Virginia for shining a light on anything but its own export market. The real reason electric bills are rising is not printed on a solar panel; it is sitting in the data center—and in a lawsuit pretending otherwise.
