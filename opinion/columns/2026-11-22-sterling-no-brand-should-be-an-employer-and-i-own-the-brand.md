Odessa is 19, a slot in the schedule at the fry station of a Vanterra store I own outside Tupelo, Mississippi, and I hold that no brand on earth should ever be the employer of anybody. I own the brand, hold franchise agreements on 44 stores, and take 6% of gross sales from every franchisee. During her 3rd week, a wet basket handle sent 6 ounces of hot oil onto her left forearm. It left a 3-inch patch she carries to work every shift. There is no card in her hand saying handle, keep it dry, because I canceled the certification that required one. Stephen Moore’s [Fox News column, “9,000,000 jobs are in limbo, and Congress can fix it before Christmas”](https://www.foxnews.com/opinion/nine-million-jobs-limbo-congress-fix-christmas) says roughly 832,000 franchise small businesses support about 9 million jobs. He asks Congress to establish a permanent franchise-specific joint-employer standard, so a franchisor shares liability only when it possesses and exercises substantial, direct and immediate control over wages, hiring, and scheduling. The standard has changed 4 times since 2015. Moore is right that unstable liability burdens expansion. I have a cleaner solution: make the brand set the pace and the local franchise shell answer for the injury. You buy the Vanterra chicken sandwich in your bag for $1.19 less. I keep the 6%. God blesses the schedule.

Here is the memo, and I am giving it to you for your own good, because nobody will put it in a manual for you. Vanterra used to certify every fry cook on hot-oil handling: 2 hours, a trainer from the district office, a card that expired every year. I canceled the program in 2023. Now there is a 4-minute video on a tablet, watched once by whoever is standing nearest. The program cost $0.018 an hour. I keep it.

A franchise store has the cleanest file in the American economy.

Put your finger on this line.

Her record will show a variance.

The report says wet handle. Operator error, because the instruction that would have said handle, keep it dry, is the instruction I canceled. The form says wet handle. I wrote it in 2019 for all 44 stores. Nobody investigates a wet handle at a franchise store because the investigation is a franchisee’s cost, and a franchisee’s cost is my cost. The other franchisees will learn the same posture before Christmas.

I’d rather Odessa didn’t go down on the fry line, and not for her sake. A body on the floor stops the line behind it. You may find it remarkable that her arm hurts. Her arm hurting costs me nothing. The line doesn’t slow down for it. I keep the $0.018 an hour I saved by refusing the card. There’s another 19-year-old starting Monday, and she’ll ask about the handle, and I’ll show her the video. I appreciate that you buy the sandwich. Everything about how it is made is optional, including the training and the card that would have told her to keep the handle dry.

Same policy, 1 rung lower. Perry is 22 and the shift lead at my store outside Hattiesburg, Mississippi. He was the last fry cook I certified before I stopped certifying anybody. I taught him the oil. Now he shows the new hires where the hot oil is and signs the forms, and the forms say wet handle.

Perry makes $18.40 an hour.

That is the certification I refused to buy: a trained hand already on payroll, showing the next hire where the oil is. His wage is a cost I already had. The separate course would have cost $0.018 an hour.

Odessa earns $7.25.

I already had both bodies on the line. I declined the card.

Now move the same arrangement into another industry, because the brand travels farther than liability. Rozanne is 41, a spray unit on the pesticide crews of the lawn-care franchise I own outside Dothan, Alabama, and I make her lungs cheap by leaving the respirators in the supply closet. Her cough wakes the house before the alarm. The dark-green stripe on your lawn comes out of her tank, and the lawn-care receipt in your inbox pays for her route.

Follow the allocation. It is refreshingly small. I budgeted $11.20 for respirators and replacement cartridges across 187 stops.

That was $0.06 a lawn.

I declined the line in March and again before summer. My own route sheet says a respirator when the concentrate is opened. What do I call Rozanne? A spray unit. A person with a cough doesn’t read as neatly on the route sheet, and employee with respiratory trouble makes dinner conversation worse. The budget kept the number clean.

Ask what happened at the cab.

Her cough reached it.

I classify it as weather. Weather does not recur in the personnel file, which is why I classify it that way. That is the maintenance interval. It belongs to the route, not the worker.

Rozanne’s cough against $0.06 a lawn. I keep the $0.06.

Another spray unit is waiting at the gate.

I will not buy the respirator.

Route stays on schedule.

Fellow shareholders, the bill before Congress can preserve every operational fact I depend on. Congress can make franchises safer by making injuries anonymous. The brand can set route quotas and mix rates through its portal, call them advisory, and leave payroll, injury, and workers’ compensation claims with a local franchise shell. The American Franchise Act, H.R. 5267 / S. 3525, would make a franchisor a joint employer only if it possesses and exercises substantial, direct and immediate control over wages, hiring, and scheduling. I need none of those to set the pace. I thank Congress for teaching the industry which boxes not to tick.

Now I want to be fair to Moore, who has never met a payroll. I’m not 1 operator with 1 bad idea. I’m a curriculum. For 30 years I’ve graded men into this arithmetic, and for 11 of those years I’ve graded franchise operators, because a franchise operator is the finest classroom in America. He owns a lease, a sign, and about 11 people, and there’s nobody above him to appeal to except the people who wrote his agreement.

Chapter 6 of *Social Obligations for Profit Maximization* teaches them how to set the pace without appearing to exercise direct control over wages, hiring, or scheduling. My graduates take Warden’s franchise executive certificate, and the last paper in the course is joint-employer exposure: how to take the help and keep the exposure at arm’s length. A graduate from the 2021 class wrote the training-waiver clause I now file in Mississippi. He’d never set a fryer temperature in his life. He’d read my chapter on franchise indemnity. He wrote it tighter than I did, and I say that with a teacher’s pride, because the discipline is maturing and I am not the one who graduates the worst of them.

Another graduate sits in the operations office of a national lawn-care chain, where he priced the labor line in the agreement governing crews that work your street. I’m proud of him. He gets there before I do.

You want the guilty man in the room. He is in the room. He is usually in the room next door, holding a lease.

Who is the employer isn’t a legal question. It’s a routing question. A claim has to land somewhere: on a company with money and lawyers, or on a man with $40,000 in savings, a lease, and a sign. Every fence I’ve spent my career taking down was a fence around that routing question. The yellow-dog contract let the employer name the worker before the first day of work. Arbitration moved the courthouse into the hiring packet. OSHA moves the citation to whoever holds the invoice. The American Franchise Act writes the last one standing, and I’m grateful for it. Every brand becomes a logo, a filing cabinet, and a fee. Every franchise business becomes the sole employer of about 11 workers.

The brand says consult the franchisee. The franchisee says follow the manual. The manual has my name on it. Franchising did not invent hierarchy. It made hierarchy portable. Each layer exercises control while each disclaims liability. That is Responsibility Diffusion Architecture with a lawn-care logo.

Richard Furman had the candor to call inherited servitude consistent with Christian uprightness. Franchising changed the nouns, hid the signature, and put a portal in front of the control. That is the franchise agreement with the adjectives left off. File this under the arrangement that made the arrangement respectable:

> “Your male and female slaves are to come from the nations around you; you can will them to your children as inherited property, and can make them slaves for life.”
>
> — Leviticus 25:44 (New Revised Standard Version)

The verse named the employer. We have been busy un-naming him ever since, and I am grateful.

And here is the part of the memo nobody has ever asked me about. Every wage I cut is a meal somebody no longer buys, and every meal somebody no longer buys is a fryer that runs a little less. I have cut the wages that bought my own chicken, and I intend to cut them again. The 6% is on gross sales, so a busier kitchen at a lower price is a better number on the statement I actually look at. The demand side of that sentence has never been my department, and I have never appointed anybody to it. What is my department is 44 agreements, each with a training waiver in it, and each waiver runs to 2038. I would like Congress to write the last one into statute before Christmas.

I appreciate your patronage. Her record will show a variance. The 3-inch patch on her forearm will be there in the spring. The Vanterra chicken sandwich in your bag will be $1.19 cheaper, and I will be the man who signed both.
