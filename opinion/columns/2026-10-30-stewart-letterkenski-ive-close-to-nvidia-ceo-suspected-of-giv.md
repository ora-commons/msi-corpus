There is a photograph that is going to age very badly: Jensen Huang and Alice Huang, together in Taipei, in 2024 and again in 2025. Not related, as everyone will remind you. The Nvidia chief and a data-center executive at the front line of the chip war — close enough that surveillance teams followed her through Singapore, that Taiwan's investigators opened files on her, that U.S. officials sat down last year and concluded, according to people familiar with the matter, that Alice Huang — a Chinese citizen, a top executive at the data-center firm Megaspeed — was handing information to the Chinese government while she helped Nvidia build the very data centers America's chip policy depends on. Their working theory: Chinese intelligence recruited her after learning how close she was to the Nvidia chief. Then, in late 2024, as the watchers closed in, she simply went to China and vanished. No one involved in the reporting can say where she is now or whether she still holds her job.

That is the story as told. It is also the most damning possible indictment of the policy that produced it — and nobody in Washington wants to say so.

Start with what Alice Huang actually did for a living. Megaspeed, founded in 2023 out of the overseas split of a Chinese firm, buys Nvidia chips, installs them in data centers in places like Malaysia, and sells the computing capacity onward. That is not a black-market shell company hiding racks in a shipping container. That is the *entire* intermediate layer of the global AI buildout, an industry that exists because the United States banned direct chip sales and left everyone downstream scrambling for legal-looking workarounds. The Commerce Department's prohibition on Hopper sales to China erased a market that had accounted for about a fifth of Nvidia's revenue in 2023, and the market did what markets do: it found the gap and filled it with a new professional class of middlemen, each with a compliance story and a Singapore address.

Then Washington spent years pretending to be surprised by the result.

The Commerce Department opened its Megaspeed investigation in late 2024; the investigation largely stalled, and the Justice Department is now leading it. Meanwhile the administration that inherited the mess did exactly what the industry asked: Howard Lutnick's agency rolled back large swaths of the Biden-era export regulations after Jensen Huang repeatedly lobbied the president and the Commerce Secretary for looser China access. The CEO of the company whose chips are the object of the entire security apparatus has spent this presidency arguing, publicly, that going "from 95% market share to 0%" in China is proof that the policy failed — and has been rewarded with industry-friendly rules for saying it. The concern about a Chinese spy in his orbit was reported a day earlier, and U.S. intelligence's assessment that [a Chinese spy targeted the Nvidia CEO](/articles/2026-10-29-us-intelligence-believes-chinese-spy-targeted-nvidia-ceo/) sits behind all of it. What no one has explained is why the man who lobbied the policy into submission gets to walk free of its consequences while an executive who operated inside it disappears into Beijing.

Now compare that with what the rest of the system has managed. In March of this year, Super Micro co-founder Wally Liaw was arrested on charges of helping smuggle $2.5 billion in servers packed with advanced Nvidia chips into China; he pleaded not guilty. In August, [Taiwan prosecutors charged nine people over alleged AI-server shipments to China](/articles/2026-08-24-taiwan-charges-nine-in-alleged-ai-server-shipments-to-china/) — those cases show what real enforcement looks like when a government decides the rules apply. The machinery can catch defendants. You have to wonder why it stumbled on the case featuring an executive photographed at Jensen Huang's side.

The analyst community has been saying this out loud for years. Greg Allen, who runs a research firm focused on chips and geopolitics, put it flatly: when you look at what federal agencies actually do, "there's little to no evidence that they are willing to take the meaningful actions that are required to catch smugglers, deter smuggling and hold companies accountable." That is not an opposition talking point. That is a description of a regime that regulates the announcement rather than the flow. In Washington, the pattern is different from Taiwan's: stall the probe, roll back the rule, hold a press availability about American AI dominance, and when the story breaks, name a single alleged source and let the "spy" headline do the work.

Notice how perfectly Alice Huang functions in that script. She is a Chinese citizen. She worked for a company born from a Chinese firm's offshore operation. She had access — or proximity to access — to the most politically valuable technology on earth. She is, in other words, exactly the person a system built on performative restriction will produce in bulk and then sacrifice individually. Her case lets the Justice Department look tough, lets the intelligence community look penetrating, keeps surveillance teams and suspect files active across three jurisdictions — and costs Washington nothing, because it changes no rule, closes no loophole, and touches no executive who spent the last two years lobbying for the pipeline to stay open.

And listen to how Nvidia answered. A spokesman said Jensen Huang's interactions with a woman three governments suspect of working for Chinese intelligence "are not unusual or unexpected given their business ties." Then came the tell: "We're confident that if the U.S. government believes that any person is a threat to American industry, it would let us know."

Would it? The U.S. government concluded Alice Huang was sharing information with China and, as far as anyone knows, told no one. That's what makes it intelligence work rather than a press release. The only way Nvidia's standard makes sense is if the company expects a heads-up before the government acts — which is precisely the deference that has defined this administration's relationship with its most valuable chipmaker. That confidence isn't reassuring. It's the problem.

Megaspeed says it has been through several government reviews and that none found any evidence of violations. The Chinese Embassy calls it a manufactured "spy narrative." Alice Huang could not be reached. The Singapore police say only that investigations are ongoing. That is how espionage accusations look before they are resolved — disputed, unproven, surveilled. Perhaps all three are wrong. But consider what it would mean if all three are right: that a woman was surveilled across three countries, driven into China, and made the centerpiece of an international security story on the strength of a *relationship* — her closeness to Jensen Huang — rather than any documented transfer. The officials who suspected recruitment, people familiar with the matter say, were reacting to what they saw as that close relationship. Suspicion of proximity is not evidence of disclosure, and a policy that treats it as such has moved from export control into something much closer to guilt by photograph.

The pattern doesn't need a verdict to be read. It needs a policy that treats a suspected intelligence source in the orbit of the world's most valuable chipmaker as a national-security event, not a relationship-management footnote. The chips are still flowing. The remote-access loophole — data centers built in nearby countries, with Chinese firms tapping that computing power from afar — is still open, and it is the broad outlines of the Megaspeed model itself. The administration is still more interested in trade talks than in closing it.

China does not need a mole at Nvidia's boardroom table. It needs a few friends in the data-center business, a few holes in the export rules, and an administration too friendly with the CEO to look closely at who is standing beside him in the pictures. And somewhere in China is an executive who may know exactly how much of this was ever about security.
