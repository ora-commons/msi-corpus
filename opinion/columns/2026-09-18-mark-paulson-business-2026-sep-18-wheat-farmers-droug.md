Washington left American wheat farmers to carry the crisis alone.

By the time you read this, the wheat still standing in fields south of Beloit will be the wheat that made it. Everything else was lost in July—shriveled by drought, too thin to cut, left for the combine to never touch. Merrill Nielsen farms 2,500 acres outside Beloit. He watched his crop fail this spring, then watched the futures price for bread wheat climb 39% on the year.

That number should mean prosperity. It does not. It means a math problem that hard work cannot solve by itself.

Diesel is at a record high. Global wheat stocks are at their second-lowest level on record. Russian attacks on Black Sea port facilities have put roughly one-third of the world's wheat trade out of reach through conventional routes. The price is up. The farmer is losing.

I know the shape of that math from Adams County. A high price on the board does not help the operator who has no crop to sell, no cash to buy fertilizer, or no moisture under the seed. The market can announce a windfall from a screen in Chicago while the ground under a farmer's boots says something else.

The World Meteorological Organization's latest advisory calls the developing El Niño the strongest in seventy years. The United Nations' September report says it will persist into 2027. The [WMO advisory](/articles/2026-09-18-wmo-warns-strongest-el-ni-o-in-70-years-set-to-intensify-into-winter/) and the [UN report](/articles/2026-09-03-un-says-this-year-s-el-ni-o-is-strongest-in-70-years-set-to-persist-into-2027/) line up with what has already happened in [shipping and commodity markets](/articles/2026-08-29-strong-el-ni-o-disrupts-global-commodity-and-shipping-markets/) this summer. This does not read like a normal weather year. It reads like a price shock that compounds itself.

The global supply side is already damaged. Australia's wheat plantings are down 12% in one season. Russian attacks on Black Sea port facilities have stranded a bumper harvest that cannot move through the routes built for it. Joao Lampreia of Freedom24 in Lisbon estimates that only 35% to 50% of the stranded Black Sea wheat will reach buyers through alternative routes—and it will move more slowly and at higher cost.

The other half is functionally gone.

Mark Welch, the grain-marketing economist at Texas A&M, says prices could backtrack if Black Sea farmers and shippers find ways around the damage. That is a fair possibility. Dan Basse, president of AgResource, is not convinced. The spike in wheat prices, he says, “has tentacles that go far beyond the war between the Ukrainians and Russians.” The world needs more harvested acres of every grain. None of this resolves back to cheap food.

The United Nations Food and Agriculture Organization's food price index was already 2.5% higher in August than a year earlier. That was before the weather damage fully entered the ledger. Consumers will feel it in every loaf, every pasta box, every pizza dough, every beer.

The United States has not harvested this little wheat since 1877, when Rutherford B. Hayes was president and the country was still counting its cattle in the millions. Four generations of farmers voted with their seed drills. They planted more corn and more soybeans because that was where the margins lived. Wheat became a rotation afterthought, then a niche, then, for many operations, a memory.

A 150-year decline does not reverse in one season.

Dan Basse expects planted wheat acreage to expand about 10% this winter as producers chase the price. Ten percent sounds large until the starting point is a near-record trough. A 10% increase from there is a rounding error against the demand pulse rolling out of the Black Sea disruption.

Someone has to replace that grain.

Someone is going to make a fortune doing it.

But the opportunity will not be shared evenly. The price action is a generational invitation only for the operators who can answer it. Break-evens are climbing because diesel is at record highs and fertilizer costs punish anyone buying on the spot market. Welch puts the risk plainly: “If it's not an average yield or better, the breakeven cost really gets high very quickly.”

Vance Ehmke, who sells wheat seed in southwest Kansas, is fielding more calls than he has in years. The same dryness that killed this year's crop is threatening the next one. He told reporters, “If you can get your wheat up, you've got 90% to 95% odds that you'll harvest something. But if you can't even get it up, I mean, you're just screwed.”

That is the most honest description of American wheat farming in 2026 I have read in any paper.

If the seed does not emerge, the year is over before it starts. If it emerges, the odds improve. If the farmer has moisture, capital, locked-in input costs, workable land, and enough equity to wait out the next turn of the cycle, the risk-reward suddenly looks clean.

The price is meaningful to the farmer with a crop.

It is meaningless to the farmer with nothing to cut.

Welch also points to the last comparable El Niño, in 2015 and 2016, when the United States set an all-time record winter-wheat yield. That is the opening in the window. The weather can still deliver. The prepared operator can still win. But a window is not a guarantee, and it is not a farm policy.

Merrill Nielsen is not committing to expand. He is waiting to see what the weather does. That caution is reasonable. It is also exactly why the upside will accrue to operators who can move before certainty arrives. The smart operators are already doing the math. The people with the deepest equity, the cheapest land, the most patience, and the ability to plant into a narrow moisture window will have the best chance to turn this shortage into a windfall.

The rest will be told that the market worked.

That is what abandonment looks like in slow motion, dressed up as a market outcome.

Washington will talk about farmers. Lobbyists will talk about biofuels and trade leverage. The press will file another round of pieces about “competing pressures.” The policy class will count the price movement and call it an incentive. None of that puts moisture in Nielsen's ground. None of it pays for the diesel. None of it makes fertilizer cheap. None of it rebuilds the wheat acreage that has been leaving American farms for a hundred and fifty years.

The crisis is here. It is not a forecast.

The wheat did not get cut. The seed did not germinate. The world already cannot afford the supply it is losing. Washington should be moving on this. Instead, the debate is still about whether the trade wars that helped push fertilizer and diesel to record highs were worth it.

Merrill Nielsen should not have to do this math alone. Neither should the rest of us.

The window is open. The acres are scarce. The world is short.
