In [“Trump's Nixon-to-China moment: There's an immigration move only he can make”](https://www.foxnews.com/opinion/trumps-nixon-to-china-moment-immigration-move-only-he-can-make), Fox News contributor Hugh Hewitt begins with a conservative victory. The southern border, he reports, is sealed; more than 750 miles of wall have been built, another 600 miles are planned, and Border Patrol is funded through September 2029. President Trump has said deportation policy may have been “too tough.” Hewitt converts that admission into a bounded exercise of executive mercy: after fixing the boundary, the President may extend grace within it.

His legal claim rests on Article II’s pardon power. Hewitt argues that a pardon may reach unlawful entry, unlawful return after deportation, and the civil violation of remaining beyond an authorized stay. President Carter’s pardon of draft dodgers supplies the model: a defined class, specified dates, and express exclusions for desertion, fraud, and violence. Hewitt proposes something similarly bounded for unauthorized migrants who have resided in the country for years, worked, paid taxes, raised children here, and committed no serious offense. He would begin with roughly 10 percent of the 15 to 20 million residents he estimates, require proof of residence and character, exclude felons and people arrested for violent crimes, and leave the pathway to citizenship to Congress.

The moral case is as important as the legal one. A wall does not require the sovereign to become indiscriminate; a pardon does not require the sovereign to abandon the wall. The President may close the border because law has a jurisdiction, and he may pardon within that jurisdiction because sovereignty is more than punishment. Hewitt presents the class pardon as a restoration of proportion rather than an abandonment of order. The conservative polity fixes the boundary, preserves the legal distinctions, identifies a limited class whose conduct has ceased to warrant removal, and acts through the President’s own constitutional instrument.

Consider Elena: a hotel worker who has lived in the country for more than a decade, pays taxes, and has children born here. Immigration and Customs Enforcement officers take Elena from the bed. They put her on the floor, lock handcuffs around her wrists, bind her ankles with plastic restraints, search her body, seize her documents, and carry her to a transport van. Human Rights Watch’s November 2023 investigation, *At a Cost*, documented detainee accounts of forced restraint, prolonged handcuffing and shackling, searches, withheld food and water, and delayed medical attention throughout ICE custody and transport.

At intake, Elena remains in handcuffs and ankle restraints while officers process her. She sits on a hard floor and then on a narrow cot. Raised bands mark her wrists where the restraints tightened. Her knees stiffen after the hours spent on the floor; her clothes remain on through intake, transport, and the first night in detention. She eats at fixed intervals, drinks from a cup, and sleeps under institutional light. The room is crowded. Ventilation fails. Food arrives cold. Toilets and showers are shared. Her body receives no privacy and no uninterrupted rest. These are the recurring conditions Human Rights Watch documented in interviews with detained immigrants.

The U.S. Government Accountability Office’s 2025 review, *Immigration Detention: Actions Needed to Improve Health Care*, found that ICE lacked consistent processes for health screening, recording medical needs, and ensuring timely follow-up. Human Rights Watch documented detainees with untreated chronic illness, acute deterioration, and injuries left unattended after restraint. The sequence has reached its final bodily endpoint in named cases. In April 2024, ICE reported that officers found Panamanian national Allan E. Anthony unresponsive in a Louisiana detention facility and pronounced him dead after attempts to revive him, the Associated Press reported. In October 2024, ICE reported that Haitian national Leslye Jean suffered a medical emergency at the South Texas Regional Detention Center in Harlingen and died, the Associated Press reported. The detention record ended in death.

For removal, ICE officers return Elena to the detention center, remove her from her cell, lock her wrists and ankles, and transport her to an airport. On the tarmac, officers make her kneel or squat while aircraft are prepared. She remains in restraints until the boarding instructions arrive. Inside, wrist and ankle cuffs secure her to the seat, and the aircraft carries her beyond the border. Associated Press and Reuters reporting on ICE charter removals documented the use of shackles and the prolonged kneeling or squatting of detainees on airport tarmacs.

The removal order names Elena alone. Her children do not board. They remain in the house, in the country, and with relatives who arrive after the vehicle carrying their mother has gone. The school record still names Elena as parent and emergency contact. She cannot answer the school when it calls, cannot sign the medical-consent form when one of the children develops a fever, and cannot return from work to the rooms where both children sleep. A relative now makes the medical appointment, signs the form, and answers the teacher. The children remain under the same roof; their mother does not. Reuters and Associated Press reporting documented the separation of children from parents during ICE detention and removal operations.

Her earnings stop with her removal while the household’s obligations remain. The next rent payment, utility bill, school-lunch form, and ordinary medical decision pass through relatives who did not choose to become the parents. The older child assumes responsibility for the younger during the day, when the older child is supposed to be in class. The younger child sleeps in the room where the mother was taken. A fever becomes a decision made by a relative because the parent who knew the child’s history is beyond the border. ICE removes one adult from one address and places that adult in another country. The removal order leaves the children without the parent named on the school record, the medical form, and the household ledger.

At the scale of the 15 to 20 million residents Hewitt estimates, ICE administers that sequence one worker at a time: restraints, detention, flight, children inside the border. The documented conditions of detention add untreated illness, acute deterioration, and death to the separation already produced by the removal order. Elena sits beyond the border; her children remain in the house without her.

Nehemiah’s wall was a jurisdiction, not a symbol. When the Ammonites and Moabites would not join the covenantal assembly, Nehemiah excluded them (Nehemiah 13:1–3). Acts 17:26 assigns peoples their appointed periods and “the bounds of their habitation.” Article II and Nehemiah agree: a sovereign may remit a violation without installing the recipient in the household. A pardon may issue within the wall; it does not remove the boundary.

Elena is outside the covenant, outside the blessing, and outside full standing. Her years of residence, labor, taxes, and children show use to a household; they do not show membership. The ground is condition, choice, and jurisdiction. She entered without the household’s authority, remained beyond its bounds, and sought the benefit of membership without submitting to its discipline.

Galatians 6:7 supplies the ledger: “whatever one sows, that will he also reap.” The empty room, the missing signature, the absent wage, and the children in the house without her are the visible interest on the harvest. Her condition brought her to the border; her condition places her beyond the covenant. Providence has entered the verdict. The wall stands.
