Seven hundred million dollars is not a favor. It is climate debt—the bill for damage caused by richer, more polluting countries and paid today by the people least responsible for it.

At COP31 in Antalya, the [39-state Alliance of Small Island States](https://mainstreetindependent.com/articles/2026-11-18-small-island-coalition-presses-cop31-for-action-and-climate-finance/) is asking for $500 million for climate-resilience programs and $200 million for natural-disaster early-warning systems. The Alliance did not come to plead. It came with an invoice.

Let me concede the strongest case for treating those two pots as the whole measure. Money before a storm can buy warning systems and stronger infrastructure. Money after the water arrives cannot undo the flooding. Adelle Thomas, the Bahamian senior director of the Natural Resources Defense Council and vice chair of an IPCC working group, says the talks have kept global warming from climbing even higher. Tonga’s prime minister, Fatafehi Fakafānua, says the alliance is “better with COP than without.” Both are right.

And many agreements at this thirty-first summit are voluntary and nonbinding—commitments with the legal force of a gym membership in February. The United Nations now says the world is unlikely to meet its ambition of capping warming at 1.5 degrees Celsius above preindustrial levels. Our own reporting found that [global climate action has stalled for five years](/articles/2026-11-12-global-climate-action-has-stalled-for-five-years-report-finds/). Thomas calls 1.5 degrees a “survival threshold” for small islands.

Diplomacy is not the villain. Pretending diplomacy is enough is.

Here is the tell. The world still has no agreement on how to get out of fossil fuels. So the islands must assemble a bloc, win attention, and compete for money to address damage they did not cause. Rich countries get to describe the resulting payments as climate finance. The islands get charity with diplomatic paperwork and an annual pledge liturgy.

The economy is a set of choices, not the weather. The oil market prices the barrel. It does not price the flooded street afterward, because no buyer arrives with cash for the damage. The smoke lands where the smoke does not. The islands absorb what the polluters externalize.

This year the mechanism left receipts. The world’s oceans reached their hottest temperatures on record in August. A record-breaking El Niño is still intensifying. Record ocean temperatures are jeopardizing the marine life Tongans depend on, while rising seas deepen erosion and flooding across islands that did almost nothing to cause either. Fakafānua put the fairness question plainly: “It’s only fair that those who are suffering from the effects of climate change are assisted by those who were at fault.”

The preferable word is not “assisted.” The richer and more polluting countries did not merely witness the islands’ losses. They helped produce them. The payment is not a tip for good behavior. It is payment for damage.

Now watch what the islands did about their size. Ilana Seid, Palau’s U.N. ambassador and chair of the alliance, says that alone, the small states have “little leverage,” but together they can “speak pretty loudly and pretty strongly.” Strip away the diplomatic manners and you have a union. Thirty-nine governments are negotiating one demand together. That is bargaining, not begging.

It is the same move as a thousand workers negotiating one wage together, or grain farmers pooling their leverage at the elevator. Nobody calls grain farmers Bolsheviks for doing it. Nobody should call the islands sentimental for doing the same thing to the countries that flooded them.

Meanwhile, a fossil-fuel coalition [pressed on at COP31 after Colombia’s withdrawal](/articles/2026-11-15-fossil-fuel-coalition-presses-on-at-cop31-after-colombia-s-withdrawal/), and the United States skipped the room for the second straight year. The world’s largest economy and a major emitter left an empty chair where much of the bill should be discussed.

For scale, consider one number. In a recent record year, American companies repurchased roughly $800 billion of their own stock—more than $2 billion a day. The islands’ entire request is smaller than a single day of that. Fakafānua again: “We don’t need more studies. We don’t need more negotiations. We just need more action.”

He isn’t being emotional. He is being an actuary.

The cost has already been paid—in Tongan fishing grounds, in Kiribati’s future, in erosion and flooding. The request does not create the damage. It changes who pays for damage already being created. Anyone who wants to call this foreign aid owes an explanation for why the ordinary rule does not apply: the party that ran up the tab pays the tab.

A 24-year-old representative from Kiribati, Atiterentaai Rinimarawa, told the talks: “I feel that my future and the future of generations after me are on the line.” She also drew the line that the donor vocabulary keeps blurring: “We are not asking for sympathy, but we are calling for climate justice, stronger commitment and real action.” Sympathy is what you give. Justice is what you owe.

Okay—so what do we build instead?

Start with what the islands have already built. The Alliance is a durable bargaining bloc. On top of it, we need two concrete institutions.

First, a permanent international adaptation fund financed by assessed contributions from high-emitting countries according to historical emissions and ability to pay. That is how the requested $500 million stops being a yearly prayer and becomes a funded obligation. The money should go to island governments and local institutions that know what must be built, rather than making disaster victims spend the summer writing donor applications.

Second, a shared regional early-warning network, built and owned by the islands rather than rented from a donor’s grant cycle. That is the $200 million request. It should be designed so warnings, emergency grants, and insurance payouts can move when a storm makes landfall. The Caribbean has already pooled catastrophe insurance for years, so a hurricane’s cost does not land on one small treasury. Early-warning systems are the smoke alarm of international infrastructure. Unglamorous. Cheap. Effective.

The hard part is not pooling money. The Alliance has supplied the bargaining bloc. The hard part is governance: who contributes, who votes, what evidence counts, how disputes get heard, and how money reaches damaged places without making a local official take the blame for damage they did not cause.

That is why the voluntary system persists. Not because the problem is too complicated for rules. Because rules make payment harder to postpone.

And this is where America enters, because America can build a domestic facility and return to the table. It has a legislature, a Treasury, and a long habit of financing public infrastructure. What it lacks is the willingness to recognize a bill unless somebody hands it a voluntary pledge form. Congress is the domestic obstacle. Globally, the obstacle is larger: there is no single legislature or treasury to write the check, so we need rules, representation, automatic payment, and a way to hear disputes when the money does not arrive.

The building blocks are not exotic. Alaska turns its resource windfall into a dividend for every resident. About 145 million Americans belong to credit unions. Roughly 900 member-owned rural electric cooperatives serve 42 million people across 56 percent of the country’s landmass. My grandfather sat on the board of one in Minnesota and would have laughed at the idea that pooling risk was a foreign ideology.

My Norwegian cousin once described her country’s sovereign fund the way I would describe a sump pump. A public can own and manage vast capital and send the gains to future generations. The machinery exists. The missing part is the will. This is not a Scandinavian import. Public finance is plumbing.

We already do this; we just won’t say the word. Say “climate finance,” and it trips the aid reflex. Say “a public risk pool,” and you run into the co-op reflex—the one 145 million Americans already own.

Climate resilience should work like the interstate system or the public library: broad contribution, common access, no individual island left pleading at the toll booth. The bill is coming due. Build the permanent pool. Fill the empty chair. Make the payment automatic. Pool the risk. Pay the tab. Mail the check.

And when the next storm arrives, let the payment have arrived before the water. Remind me what the obstacle is again.
