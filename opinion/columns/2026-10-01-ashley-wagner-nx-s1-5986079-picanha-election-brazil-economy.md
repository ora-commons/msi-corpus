The Big Four meatpackers are robbing American families at the meat counter. Tyson, JBS, Cargill, and National Beef pack roughly 85 percent of the cattle in this country, and this year they are charging families the highest ground-beef prices in modern memory. The Bureau of Labor Statistics' average-price series for ground beef passed six dollars a pound in 2025, up more than half since 2020. For a family that used to buy six pounds a week — taco night, the cookout, Sunday's sloppy joes — that is about thirteen dollars more every week, fifty-four more a month, seven hundred more a year.

The herd is genuinely small — the smallest since the early 1960s — and drought, feed costs, and record exports to Japan and China all push the price up on their own. That is the true half, and it deserves saying first. What the supply story does not explain is the spread. When cattle prices crashed at the ranch and then climbed at the counter, packing margins on fed cattle ran to several hundred dollars a head. The USDA's 2021 competition report on cattle and beef named the concentration problem and called for more packing capacity; Cargill closed its Dodge City, Kansas plant in 2024 instead of building more. The cost did not disappear. It moved — onto the family buying the meat.

The math is easier to run in my own kitchen: two incomes, a rowhouse bought with help from a grandmother's estate, a mortgage that has not moved in three years of refinancing inquiries. This household can still put beef on the table, and I am flagging that because the argument here does not rest on my own deprivation. It rests on what the price does to the family whose spreadsheet has no room in it — the family that swaps to chicken thighs, buys the store brand, waits for the markdown at nine at night, and does the whole arithmetic standing in the aisle. That family is doing the packers' repricing work for free.

Wages did rise. The Atlanta Fed's wage tracker has median wage growth around 3.9 percent, while the same bank's sticky-price index — the things that don't move much — is running near 3 percent. For a household making fifty-five thousand dollars, a year of that spread is roughly four hundred and ninety-five dollars: about forty-one dollars a month, or thirteen gallons of gas. Ground beef went up more than that in a single year. Real wages climbed, and the counter ate the climb.

The word for the gap between what the indicators say and what the receipt says has been "vibecession" since 2022, and it was always the wrong word: the gap is not in the vibes, it is on the balance sheet. The New York Fed's household debt report puts credit card balances past 1.2 trillion dollars, with the average rate on cards carrying a balance above 22 percent. On the average balance that works out to about 125 dollars a month in interest alone, before a dollar of principal — and nearly half of American renters, 22.4 million households by the Joint Center for Housing Studies' count, already spend more than 30 percent of their income on rent. A family reading that card statement as a personal failing is doing exactly what the bill wants it to do. Swift's "Anti-Hero" is the anatomy of the move — a structure worn so long it feels like character.

Jia Tolentino's argument about optimization was that the era's project is maximizing yourself as a market asset. The grocery version is maximizing the cart as one. The family that rebuilds the weekly list around whatever protein moved least is not making a lifestyle choice; it is absorbing an oligopoly's margin through unpaid household labor — the same repricing the packers do on the rancher's side of the transaction, except the family gets nothing for it and the packer keeps the spread.

Anne Helen Petersen's point about burnout is that it will not be cured by a productivity app or a face mask, and the grocery bill is the same case: the coupon binder and the store-brand shelf are the face mask. The structural version is enforceable. Federal antitrust law has been on the books the entire time; the market structure antitrust policy allowed to form in the 1980s and 1990s is the market structure every administration since has governed. What is missing is enforcement — and, where the household budget actually needs relief, the record is specific: the expanded Child Tax Credit lifted 4.3 million children out of poverty in 2021 and went to zero the year after, and it would have covered a great deal of six-dollar ground beef. Antitrust teeth, public investment in packing capacity, cash back in family budgets: that is the pro-family program, and a supermarket photo op is not a substitute for it.

My mother's grocery lists from Lansdale are on the back of envelopes in a drawer at my parents' house. Roast for Sunday. Ground beef on the list every week, at a price nobody argues about. Put one of those envelopes next to my own Tuesday receipt and the difference is not a mood. It is a transfer — from a family's kitchen to a margin line at a plant they chose to close.

"You're on your own, kid" is what the price of ground beef says at eleven at night with the calculator open. It is not the whole truth: the four firms that took the margin had help, and help is a policy choice. The margin was taken. The question this November is not whether families feel prosperous. It is who is going to put the money back.
