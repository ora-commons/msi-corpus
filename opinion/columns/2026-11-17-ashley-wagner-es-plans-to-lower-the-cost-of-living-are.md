Trump and Congress are starving families to fund billionaire tax cuts.

Every month I move the same numbers between the same two columns: $8,800 in combined take-home pay for David and me, and $2,400 for childcare for Eva and Ben. Daycare takes 27 percent of our income before our 7-percent mortgage, utilities, groceries, health insurance, and the student-loan payment I am still carrying. Our 1,400-square-foot Fishtown rowhouse exists partly because David’s grandmother’s estate helped us buy it. I am writing from my own kitchen, not pretending my two-income household is the national median. Even with that help, the arithmetic is not a moral failure. It is the price of raising children in an economy that has made their necessary infrastructure a private debt.

When January arrives, Democrats will hold 22 trifectas and a majority of governorships for the first time since 2010, after [gaining six trifectas and a likely 24-22 legislature lead](/articles/2026-11-15-democrats-gain-six-trifectas-and-a-likely-24-22-legislature-lead/). The [winners who campaigned on expanding child-care access](/articles/2026-11-16-midterm-winners-campaign-on-expanding-childcare-access/) are the ones holding the pens. Minnesota Democrats want new health-care tax credits. Wisconsin Democrats are considering free school meals for every child and property-tax relief. Pennsylvania Democrats want state money for housing construction. These are not mood boards. They are budgets. The common constraint is fiscal, not legislative.

Michigan Gov.-elect Jocelyn Benson wants a $5,000 caregiver tax credit, medical-debt relief, wider child-care and preventive-health access, and a $60,000 minimum starting salary for teachers. The $5,000 is real help: it covers 17 percent of one year of our $28,800 childcare bill, a little over two months at our rate. It is not the thing it is being positioned to answer. Michigan Senate Appropriations Chair Sarah Anthony has had to remind her own caucus that somebody has to “keep the lights on.” Voters demand “bigger and bolder” assistance, but “new revenue would be the only way to truly deliver new sweeping affordability measures,” and major commitments require taxes or spending cuts elsewhere unless loophole-closing and economic growth produce the money.

Colorado voters replaced the flat income tax with higher rates on people and businesses making more than $500,000 a year. The levy is expected to generate at least $2 billion annually, and by law the money must go to health care, child care, and education. Gov.-elect Phil Weiser is also promising down-payment assistance, lower college costs, and universal preschool. Nevada Gov.-elect Aaron Ford wants expanded renter assistance, more help for first-time buyers, community health centers, and mobile clinics. He acknowledges that the federal Medicaid cuts will remove coverage from some Nevadans.

Now the timer. Trump stripped family health coverage and scheduled the worst of it past the next presidential election. He signed the tax-and-spending bill in 2025, and S&P Global Ratings estimates that 90 percent of its Medicaid reductions will occur in fiscal years 2029 through 2034. Ninety percent of the damage has not arrived yet, and that schedule is the whole story. SNAP reductions are already forcing states to tighten their budgets. Michigan faces a roughly $700 million deficit for fiscal 2028, partly because of federal funding cuts; the warm-up act is arriving. Colorado’s coming $1 billion deficit and rising Medicaid costs compete with every dollar legally dedicated to child care and education. Medicaid insures about one-quarter of Nevada.

Read the calendar the way a household reads a bill: it starts arriving in the same autumn as the next presidential election and does not close out until fiscal 2034. I do not have to call that a bet. The dates are public record.

This is how the federal tax bill moves the bill onto somebody else. Federal savings appear; state fiscal capacity contracts; counties, hospitals, providers, and families absorb the difference. The money does not disappear. It changes payer. Washington has converted health coverage and care infrastructure from public obligations into state scavenger hunts, then sold the scavenger hunt as ambition.

The betrayal is generational, but it is not a personality contest between lazy children and greedy old people. My parents raised three children on my father’s USPS supervisor salary through my elementary years and paid off their house in 2007. Their household did not have to carry every hour of care alone: St. Stanislaus’s women’s sodalities ran fundraisers, parish volunteers prepared meals, and neighbors filled gaps that neither a paycheck nor a government program covered. David and I earn more in real terms than my parents did combined, and we cannot afford for two children what they managed for three.

The family-wage principle in Leo XIII’s *Rerum Novarum* says a wage ought to support a household, not merely the worker. The same encyclical says: “To exercise pressure upon the indigent and the destitute for the sake of gain, and to gather one’s profit out of the need of another, is condemned by all laws, human and divine.” My generation’s workers are expected to support the worker, the child, the health plan, the housing market, the school pipeline, and the unpaid scheduler all at once.

Our privilege is visible: two paychecks, employer coverage for the kids, and an estate-assisted down payment. The families in the path of the Medicaid cuts are poorer than mine. Medicaid is children, seniors in long-term care, disabled neighbors, and the aides and cashiers whose wages never covered a policy. The bill gathers its savings out of their need.

Anne Helen Petersen’s warning in *Can’t Even* is that “burnout isn’t a personal problem, and it will not be cured by a productivity app or face mask.” Here the productivity app is the tax form, the benefits portal, the school calendar, and the group chat arranging every pickup. The federal government has made the mother an unpaid benefits caseworker while calling the remaining gap a private choice. There is no shopping-around answer to a Medicaid cut.

Annie Lowrey’s sentence is the one that belongs underneath all of it: “Poverty in the United States is a choice. Stagnant middle-class incomes are a choice.” A choice has an author and a date. This one collects the tax benefits in the present and schedules most Medicaid cuts for 2029 through 2034.

The federal government already had the arithmetic in its hands. The Build Back Better child-care cap would have saved a typical family in 32 states roughly $5,000 to $6,500 a year; Benson’s $5,000 caregiver credit is roughly that size. The 2021 expanded Child Tax Credit showed what a federal floor can do: had Congress made it permanent, the Center on Budget and Policy Priorities estimated, it would have lifted 4.3 million children out of poverty annually, a reduction of more than 40 percent. It lapsed anyway. They know how. They chose.

The states are re-deriving, on strained budgets, policy the federal government once had in its hands. Quality child care cannot be simultaneously affordable for parents, decently paid for teachers, and unsubsidized; one of the three gives, every time, somewhere. Every rich country that publicly funds child care decided the give comes from the tax base. U.S. public child-care spending runs about one-quarter of the rich-country average, so here the give comes out of teachers’ paychecks, parents’ bills, and kids.

Where the federal floor drops, the need does not disappear. It moves. In my mother’s parish, the St. Vincent de Paul conference kept doing what it was built to do: a family’s hours get cut, and the conference quietly arranges the rent or the light bill. No names are ever read from the pulpit. The women who ran it never once confused that work with justice. They knew it was what you do while the structure is broken.

The answer is not another list of aspirations: Congress can pass a refundable federal child-care credit indexed to actual care costs, eight weeks of paid leave at 70 percent wage replacement under the AEI-Brookings compromise, universal pre-K, guaranteed health coverage, a federal financing floor for housing, and full IDEA funding at the 40 percent Congress authorized in 1975 but never delivered. Colorado has already shown the revenue mechanism, and the 22 trifectas have shown the political capacity. What is missing is a federal government willing to pay for the care it calls a private responsibility.

Taylor Swift put the operating instruction in the title of “You’re On Your Own, Kid”: the government hands families the bill and calls the uncovered balance independence. The title is not a care policy; it is the receipt for what Washington has withdrawn. The song’s force comes from recognizing that some bonds cannot be fulfilled alone, and the federal government has turned that recognition into a budget rule. Its safety net is lateral—the friendship bracelets, the other mothers in the group text. Every state capitol just accepted the job of being that group text for a country whose federal government made the title policy.

The bill is the choice. The timer is the tell. In the fall of 2028, the timer goes off for the households holding Medicaid cards and SNAP applications—the households that never got to run the math in private. The line item that does not show up in the press release is the 90 percent.

In 2022 I sat at this table at eleven at night and learned the gap was not mine. Now the cursor blinks in the last empty cell while Ben sleeps and Eva gives the sink a full report on her day. That cell is not a personal failure. It is the starvation, priced for one household at a time.
