The Chip Emperor’s affair with Alice Huang overlapped Nvidia’s data-center work in Asia, and now his board gets the first word while the public gets no seat. That is the machinery of two-tier justice: the corporation gets the first inquiry.

U.S. officials last year concluded that Chinese citizen Alice Huang, then a top executive at Megaspeed International, was sharing information with the Chinese government while working with Nvidia to build data centers in Asia. Nvidia’s board is investigating reports that Jensen Huang was the target of a foreign espionage effort. Huang apologized to his staff and family Sunday for being unfaithful to his wife. The inquiry follows [reporting on possible espionage aimed at Jensen Huang](/articles/2026-11-09-nvidia-investigates-reports-of-spying-targeting-jensen-huang/) and [the U.S. conclusion about Alice Huang’s data-center work](/articles/2026-11-08-nvidia-inquiry-follows-u-s-conclusion-on-alice-huang-s-data-center-work/).

The affair is not the espionage finding. The official finding concerns Alice Huang. Whether Jensen Huang knew is the board’s question. No one has established that he knew she was sharing information with the Chinese government, and the board has issued no finding.

Power has a private life. Corporate power has a way of making it institutional. Here, the boardroom swain’s private relationship overlapped the company’s data-center work. Nvidia supplied the project; Alice Huang supplied the working relationship; U.S. officials supplied the finding that she shared information with Beijing. The board can examine the chief. It cannot untangle the company from the exposure.

Corporate boards can become a privileged private first hearing for powerful executives. A board can appoint investigators, define the questions, and decide what its own report discloses, subject to law. Its members owe fiduciary duties to Nvidia and its shareholders. The company’s reputation, litigation exposure, and disclosure choices shape what the public is told. None of that proves concealment. It establishes jurisdiction. The public has no seat in Nvidia’s inquiry and no result to judge.

Nvidia’s inquiry is the fresh exhibit in two-tier justice. The machine has a familiar route: the corporation answers with money, and no individual need face criminal charges.

The older cases do not flatter the distinction. Charles Keating’s Lincoln Savings cost taxpayers $3.4 billion; roughly 23,000 mostly elderly bondholders lost about $250 million. Keating is the counterproof that impunity is chosen, not weather: he served about 4.5 years before his convictions were overturned on a technicality, then pleaded guilty in 1999. Purdue pleaded guilty in 2007 and 2020; the Sacklers were never criminally charged; and the 2025 bankruptcy settlement set aside about $850 million for individual victims over 15 years. In the GM ignition-switch case, DOJ’s 2015 resolution records a $900 million forfeiture, a defect tied to 124 deaths, and no individual charges. Wells Fargo opened millions of unauthorized accounts under sales-quota pressure and reached its 2020 deferred-prosecution agreement with the bank itself, not the individuals responsible for the fraud. The penalty was $3 billion. Former CEO John Stumpf separately paid $17.5 million and was banned from banking by the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency. DOJ’s 2012 deferred-prosecution agreement records HSBC’s admission that it laundered money for Mexican drug cartels and sanctioned governments, and records no individual prosecutions.

The board investigates. The public waits outside the boardroom. The older docket ends with the receipt: HSBC paid $1.9 billion. No individual was prosecuted. That is the sentence.
