Trump is taxing the Americans who obeyed him and calling it a jobs program.

Here are the numbers. In High Point, North Carolina — the city that still calls itself the Furniture Capital of the World — a furniture plant with $2.4 million in back orders and two weeks of payroll left in the account changed hands for zero dollars. Anyone who has priced a factory knows what two weeks of payroll means. The buyer was TOV Furniture, more than $100 million a year in sales, founded by Bruce Krinsky with his wife, Chaya, and it bought the plant because American trade policy told it to build sofas in the United States. It named the operation Revival American Contract Furniture. The Wall Street Journal reported the details on November 11, and the report reads like a scoring memo the president filed against his own policy. The plant ran ten months and closed on July 31. Forty-five American jobs, erased. Three million dollars of the owner's capital, gone. That is the outturn — what actually happened, as distinct from what was promised — of the tariff program in its own showcase industry.

The record of obedience is long. In 2018, when a 25 percent tariff — a tax collected at the border from the American importer — landed on some Chinese furniture, Krinsky negotiated part of the cost out of his suppliers and moved production to Vietnam. When the tariffs became a re-election platform, he did it again: asked his largest supplier — a husband-and-wife firm in China that made 35 percent of his inventory — to build a second plant outside Ho Chi Minh City. Construction began in August 2024. The plant opened in March 2025 with 130 workers. Then came a 34 percent tariff on China and a proposed 46 percent on Vietnam, and the escape route was taxed as fast as it could be built. The $900 sofa had already become a $1,250 sofa; the next round priced close to $2,000, and Krinsky drew the line the arithmetic drew: at $2,000, he said, there is no reason for us to exist.

The cost build explains the ten months. North Carolina's $7.25 minimum wage compounds to roughly $15,000 a year, but upholsterers are scarce and the labor market pays what scarcity commands: about $45,000 to start, past $90,000 at the top. Those wages are the policy's stated goal. Nothing in the policy pays for the training that produces them. In Vietnam, Krinsky put labor and materials at $280 a couch, the tariff at $70, ocean freight at $75, delivery to an American floor at $230 — $655 all-in. In High Point, labor and materials alone ran $1,000, and shipping took the total to $1,230 against a $1,500 retail price — a structural gap of $575 a sofa before productivity enters the ledger. Productivity does not rescue it: local crews finished two to three sofas a day; the Vietnamese plant finished ten — a gap a first-time manufacturer partly owned, but not one that execution could close. He was losing $100 or more on every couch. His concession is in the record: no manufacturing background, not enough time on the floor, the criticism fair. Both things are true. Only one of them is policy.

A tariff is a tax, and the first question my old desk asked of any tax is incidence — who actually bears it once prices and behavior adjust. The importer writes the check at the border; the shopper pays it at the register. Two centuries of evidence put most of a consumption tariff on the buyer. The distributional tables I built for three decades all started from that question: who writes the check, and who actually pays. The cost build answers it. What the tariff actually built is a wall around the premium segment. Michael Thomas Furniture, also in High Point, employs 150 people selling $3,000-to-$5,000 sofas to older and more affluent buyers; its president, George Jordan, says it plainly: if you are trying to get below $2,000, it is harder. The tariff protects the couch the affluent household buys and taxes the couch the $900 household buys. A transfer that takes proportionally more from lower-income households is a regressive transfer, whatever the press release calls it.

The hands the arithmetic favors belong to a person. Nguyen Van Hai is thirty-two. He upholsters up to ten hours a day, six days a week, for about $8,000 a year, and he calls the pay high — a house, and an education for his two children. He is not a cost line. He is the reason the couch's labor and materials cost $280; the tax lands on his customer, and in time on him too, as less work. That is what taxing a product line does to the people who make it.

The arithmetic does not stop at sofas. Builders are [pulling back as tariffs, fuel and labor costs rise](/articles/2026-11-02-home-builders-pull-back-as-tariffs-fuel-and-labor-costs-rise/). A tax on inputs is a tax on outputs, and the output is an American price tag.

After the Supreme Court struck down the earlier global tariffs, sectoral executive orders reassembled the wall category by category: 50 percent on upholstered furniture made in China, 25 percent on Vietnamese-made, scheduled to rise to 55 and 30 percent in January. Krinsky moved ahead with about 70 percent of his production in Vietnam — the compliance plan, again. The rate schedule carries no incidence table. Instruments like this never do. They announce rates. They never say who pays.

Then Customs came for the compliance plan. U.S. Customs and Border Protection flagged 17 shipments — about 45 containers — from the Vietnamese plant and assessed $135,000 on the theory that the goods should be treated as Chinese-made. Transshipment is the term of art: routing goods through a third country to disguise where they were made. A 25-point spread between the two labels is a standing invitation to relabel, so policing origin is legitimate work; the plant does run about 30 Chinese workers on its most complex pieces — hires the managers are already replacing with locals — and its machinery and materials come from China, which Krinsky does not dispute. What is not legitimate is pricing compliance at whatever the invoice says. Krinsky answered with more than 1,000 pages of documents, photographs and videos documenting the plant's Vietnamese production. Officials ruled the evidence insufficient. He believes the plant was flagged to fill a quota; the agency denies quota-driven targeting and declined to discuss an individual importer. The bill rests on an evidence standard a thousand pages cannot pass, and it stands while a lawyer fights it. Krinsky's stated fear names the shape of the thing — he needs it resolved so it is not "a recurring, open-ended thing where I can get a bill next year for a million dollars." A liability without an expiration date cannot be priced into a sofa. It can only be exited.

The experiment has run before, and the outturn is on file. Federal Reserve economists Aaron Flaaen, Ali Hortaçsu and Justin Tinteliot studied the 2018 washing-machine tariffs and counted roughly 1,800 jobs created, at a cost of about $815,000 per job per year, financed by roughly $1.5 billion a year in consumer overcharges once dryer prices, which rose in sympathy, were included. The 2002 steel safeguards — emergency import duties under trade law — were imposed in March 2002 and rescinded in December 2003, their repeal following estimated job losses in steel-consuming industries that exceeded the jobs the mills kept.

The line the tariff claims to reverse was drawn decades before the orders. North Carolina had 91,000 furniture jobs in 1990. It has 27,000 now. High Point still calls itself the Furniture Capital of the World; a slogan outlives an industry, and it is the last thing to leave the building. The decline ran three decades under open trade, driven by exactly the cost differential the receipts above price, and a tax at the border does not change labor rates in either country. The tax is not new with this administration either. Score both administrations the same way and both fail the same test: the previous one kept the bulk of the China tariffs it inherited and raised selected rates of its own. The administration changed. The tax persisted. The justifications rotate around the collection, and none of the paperwork on either side says who pays.

The wall does not stop the flow; it re-flags it. Cleveland-Cliffs, a steelmaker inside its own tariff wall, [projects its strongest profit since 2021 as supplies tighten](/articles/2026-10-20-cleveland-cliffs-projects-strongest-profit-since-2021-as-steel-supplies-tighten/) — the wall shows up as producer pricing power before it shows up as anyone's job. And the production did not vanish: [Vietnam posted a $114 billion trade surplus with the United States in the first half of 2026](/articles/2026-08-26-vietnam-posts-114-billion-trade-surplus-with-u-s-in-first-half-of-2026/), the same containers Krinsky shipped cheaply before the wall now carrying a 25 percent toll into the country. The tax collected. The work moved. The prices rose. That is the incidence table, filled in by the record.

A White House spokesman said the president is delivering on his promise to level the playing field for American workers and restore prosperity to communities hollowed out by jobs shipped overseas. What a tax on shoppers cannot survive is honest description, so the claim gets laundered — filed under "leveling the playing field for American workers," "expanding job opportunities," and "raising wages for hardworking families," a slogan family that converts a consumption tax into a jobs program until the invoice stops showing. Set the slogans beside the outturn. The American wages that rose in this episode were $45,000 starting wages, for ten months, now ended. The prosperity restored to High Point is forty-five more empty chairs.

Be precise about who stands where. Krinsky voted for the president three times. He is not a hostile witness. "What exactly is an American business supposed to do?" he said. "We're trying to do what the government wants." In program-effectiveness terms he is the compliant case, the willing subject who did everything the program asked. The president's tariff closed the plant that tried. The finding is the outturn. The Homestead Works closed in 1986 and took my father's pension with it. I have seen a town hollowed out by policy. It does not look like prosperity restored.

Somewhere in a Customs queue sit more than 1,000 pages of photographs and videos documenting a factory that exists — 130 workers, mostly Vietnamese, building couches for $280. The government that ordered the industry home is billing the portion of it that answered, on a standard a thousand pages cannot satisfy. An origin test that a thousand pages cannot pass is not a test. It is a standing invoice.

An honest industrial policy for High Point would price its own goal. If the goal is jobs, score the tariff like a program: publish the cost per job saved and let the number argue — anyone who has read a program cost estimate knows that a policy costing more per job than the job pays is a transfer, not a jobs program. If the goal is revenue, say that sofa shoppers are the revenue. If the goal is furniture work in North Carolina, fund the skill the shortage is already pricing — the upholsterers the market pays $45,000 to $90,000 to find — and publish an incidence table beside every rate schedule, so the $900 household can see what its couch now costs and why. A tax that cannot say who pays it is not a policy. It is a collection. This one was signed with the arithmetic already on the record: $280 of Vietnamese labor and materials against $1,000 of North Carolina labor and materials, and the jobs as the sales pitch. It was not a rescue that failed. It was a tax that worked.

The score is the score. The author of the policy does not get to grade it.
