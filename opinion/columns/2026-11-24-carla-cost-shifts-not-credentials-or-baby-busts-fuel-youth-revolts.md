The young people protesting schools, wages, and housing are not rejecting diplomas; they are reading a ledger in which the costs of weak bargaining power, high housing costs, and debt have been shifted onto them while the assets producing the returns remain sheltered. William A. Galston’s [What’s Behind the Global Youth Revolts?](https://www.wsj.com/opinion/whats-behind-the-global-youth-revolts-c6a95324?mod=rss_opinion) in *The Wall Street Journal* surveys the protests from Paris and Berlin to Belgrade, Madrid, Delhi, and New York, then tells one story about all of them: the degree stopped paying what it promised, slower growth and falling fertility have strained a postwar bargain between generations, and crowded budgets leave governments little room to respond. Galston has mistaken a class fight for a credential dispute.

Start with what he gets right, because it is a lot. The revolts are real; his own examples carry them, from students winning an astonishing victory over Aleksandar Vučić in Serbia to 72 percent of Americans under 30 voting for Democratic candidates, up from 52 percent two years earlier, according to *The Voter Poll* by SSRS. His evidence, including a recent report in *The Journal*, says unemployment is higher among young college graduates than among workers overall, the earnings gap between workers with and without degrees is narrowing, and youth unemployment stands around 15 percent in the United Kingdom and China. A broken promise can turn resentment into a ballot. It can send someone toward an extremist candidate. The anger is the evidence. The question is what it points at.

The generational squeeze is real too. The postwar compact was built on decades of rapid growth. Falling fertility and slower growth make promised social programs harder to sustain. Macron tried to move France’s retirement age from 62 to 64 and encountered resistance. Any honest column concedes all that before it swings.

But Galston’s account turns the postwar bargain into a deal between young and old. That is not the bargain that built the Nordic system. Sweden’s 1938 Saltsjöbaden Agreement joined organized labor and organized employers: labor accepted owners’ control of investment and management; owners accepted high wages, strong unions, and the welfare state. Capital kept the factory keys. Labor got security and a seat at the table. That is the bargain worth examining. I’m not against education or markets. I’m against asking a diploma to do the work of collective power. A degree is a tool. It does not bargain.

Here is the mechanism Galston leaves out. One home-care worker asks for a raise. The employer can say take it or leave; another person needs the job. The worker cannot walk away without putting rent, food, and health coverage at risk. When a care company pays a wage that leaves workers on food stamps or Medicaid, the public is already paying part of that wage bill. The low wage is not cheap. It is a loan from the public to the employer. A union turns hundreds of workers like that into one bargaining unit. A sectoral agreement keeps a nonunion shop from undercutting the wage by moving the price of labor to the weakest employer.

That is not a theory. Bureau of Labor Statistics figures for 2025 put median weekly earnings at $1,404 for U.S. union members and $1,174 for nonunion workers, a $230 difference. The gap is not a law of nature. The institutional hole is plain: only 5.9 percent of private-sector workers were union members, and union contracts covered about 11 percent of all workers. Taft-Hartley authorized state right-to-work laws that outlaw union-shop agreements, so a nonmember can receive union representation without paying for it. Deindustrialization removed dense union workplaces. Organized corporate resistance made the remaining organizing harder.

Denmark has no magic trick. Danish employers can fire people easily, too. Then Denmark pairs that flexibility with union-run unemployment insurance, active retraining, and collective bargaining covering more than 80 percent of workers. It spends roughly 2 percent of GDP moving workers through unemployment and retraining. The Danes pay for the bargain with a 25 percent standard sales tax, middle class included. The result is a country where losing a job need not end your life. America gives employers flexibility and tells the unlucky worker to solve it alone. The missing ingredient is not Scandinavian temperament. It is organized counterpower.

Now the alibi: no room. A columnist who declares the cupboard bare owes us an explanation of 2021. That year, America ran the experiment. The expanded Child Tax Credit was paid monthly to nearly everybody, and it was dead simple. Per the Census Bureau, child poverty fell 46 percent in a single year, from 9.7 percent to 5.2 percent, lifting 2.9 million children out of poverty. Then the expansion lapsed, and child poverty shot back up. That is the entire argument in two sentences. The room exists. It was found, used, and withdrawn. Room is not a fact of nature. It is a vote.

The fiscal record makes the “no room” story stranger still. After the 2017 corporate tax cut, annual corporate share buybacks topped $800 billion. The same ledger that says there is no room for a child allowance can make room for a great deal of finance. It is the sound of a serious man describing a decision as if it were the weather.

Notice where the generational frame points your eyes: old against young. Grandma’s check against the kid’s future. Elegant, because Grandma’s check does not set the rent. Follow a young person’s actual bills to whoever collects them. In the cities where these revolts live, rent goes to whoever holds housing as an asset class, homes traded by people who will never sleep in them. The health premium flows to a system built to insert middlemen between a patient and her doctor. For many graduates, the degree arrives with a loan that can follow them for as long as twenty years. None of those bills pays the elderly. They pay for ownership and power, and power sets the bill.

Now put housing back in the sentence. Galston mentions it in Spain and affordability in America, then puts them in parentheses. They are not parentheses. When a city builds a subway, the land around the stops becomes more valuable. The owner captures the public investment in the monthly rent. A young worker’s wage meets an asset price. The house became a financial instrument before it became shelter.

France’s 57 percent of GDP in government spending does not prove the cupboard is bare. It tells us to ask what the money buys and who was left standing outside. The same is true of France’s 5.5-percent deficit and the American deficit at 6 percent. Retirement promises, defense spending, and the need to restore Social Security and Medicare solvency do constrain budgets. Demography makes promised programs harder to sustain. It does not choose which promises to sustain. Macron’s retirement fight was a political veto, not a law of demography. A budget is a list of choices wearing a tie.

AI may make entry-level work scarcer. Fine. It cannot explain the underemployment and narrowing degree premium that came before the latest models. AI is a new pressure on an old bargaining failure. Watch the move: weak bargaining power gets packaged as a credentials story. Very tidy. No union enters the picture.

Before Galston settles on credentials as the explanation, he has to account for the forces young people actually face: why wage growth failed to keep pace, why bargaining institutions thinned, why rent took more of the check, and why ownership of productive assets stayed out of reach. If those forces are not causes, he should say so. If they are, the credential story is not an explanation. It is where young people were told to look after the money was already gone. A credential cannot bargain.

So build the arrangement the contract forgot. Where one hospital chain, home-care chain, or hotel chain dominates a local labor market, workers should bargain through unions and sectoral wage boards that set wages, hours, and standards for the whole industry. Not one firm at a time. The industry. Some U.S. states already allow wage boards. The harder job is institutional density. Denmark’s 80-percent bargaining coverage rests on union strength, organized employers, and union-run benefits. America has to build those gears rather than airlift a Danish number.

Make ownership real, too. Mondragon reports roughly 80 cooperatives, 70,000 workers, and more than €11 billion in total revenue. Its average pay ratio is near five to one. It is not magic. Fagor’s 2013 bankruptcy carried about €1.1 billion in debt, but the federation relocated roughly 1,700 of its 1,800 Spanish worker-members. A cooperative does not abolish failure. It changes who survives it and who owns the upside.

Build social insurance too. Restore the child allowance permanently and universally, on Social Security’s universal logic but for children. Grandma keeps hers. The kid gets one too. Once everyone is inside, everyone defends the institution; means-tested programs for the poor become poor programs. Subsidize childcare, because care cannot be cheap for parents, decent for workers, and profitable for owners all at once. Pick two. If AI eats the entry rungs off the career ladder, answer the question of who owns the productivity with bargaining power and ownership, not a shrug. Add paid leave and make tuition something other than a mortgage on the future. For shelter, community land trusts remove land from speculation while letting residents build equity; public housing can provide a floor when the private market cannot.

And yes, say it straight: this is harder here. Union coverage sits at 11 percent. The racial wedge has been used for a century to split working people from each other. Organized employers, capable public administration, and institutions that can deliver benefits across class lines do not arrive with a statute. A single program is not a substitute for missing institutions. Treating it as one would fail too. Institutions, not policies, are the hard part, and they take decades. I’m not selling utopia. I’m selling plumbing. These are not moonshots. They are the next round of bargaining, over wages, care, ownership, and land. Then the degree becomes a tool rather than a lottery ticket, and youth anger has somewhere useful to go.

We also know the room exists because we found it, used it, and measured it in 2021. The economy is a set of choices, not the weather. Galston’s bare-cupboard verdict stands or falls on one thing: rule out the room we already found and spent, or take the verdict back. The revolt is not asking for the moon. It is asking for the switch we already know how to flip. Flip it.
