Roughly 2,100 people cannot simply vanish when New York closes jail beds. The danger is not socialism. It is closing those beds without a release plan, a place to land, or a court date that arrives on time. Paul Mauro's Fox News op-ed, ["If you want to know the dangers of socialism, take a look at Mamdani's New York"](https://www.foxnews.com/opinion/dangers-socialism-mamdani-new-york), argues that Mayor Zohran Mamdani's dismantling of the NYPD's Strategic Response Group and his plan to close Rikers Island by 2032 in favor of four borough facilities will dump working-class commuters, disproportionately women, onto the street while the bill climbs. Mauro's larger claim is that the whole program is socialism arriving, and that New York is the warning for every other city. I do not need a warning about the fate of civilization to ask what happens to the people whose beds are about to disappear.

I will give Mauro this one: the arithmetic is right. He reports a jail population of about 6,600 and a replacement system for 4,500 inmates, including 340 specialized therapeutic beds. That is a gap of roughly 2,100 people. Not a socialist insight. Subtraction.

Here is what the arithmetic does and does not say. If New York removes those beds before it funds housing, treatment, case management and a court process that can move cases, the city exports disorder to the subway and to the blocks around it. Many working-class New Yorkers cannot move somewhere safer. Mauro even names the people who pay, and then uses them as scenery in a warning instead of as residents owed a plan. Then he covers the bill with a scarlet label. The cost does not vanish. It changes seats. It rides the subway.

Communism as actually built in the twentieth century was a catastrophe. I will say it once and move on. It is also beside the point. A mayor's release policy does not abolish private property, prohibit elections or put the press under party control. Nobody here is proposing the abolition of markets. We are discussing what a city owes the people it releases. This is municipal administration. If that is too radical for the word, the word has stopped describing economics and started replacing thought.

Here is the tell. The article welds separate choices into one red flag. The Strategic Response Group is one. Rikers capacity is another. Bail, court delay, release conditions, treatment, housing and quality-of-life enforcement are several more. Protest management, violent-emergency response and crowd control are not the same job, and the piece does not pretend otherwise — it runs them together so the total can be painted scarlet.

The SRG was created in 2015 for major emergencies, violent demonstrations and civil disorder. Mauro says it answers only about 6 percent of the city's protests, and roughly 500 officers are being reassigned, including into something called the "Community Dialogue and De-escalation Division." Fine: if a protest unit has drifted into political management, narrow it. If the city still needs trained capacity for serious violence, keep it. A unit's origin story is not its job description. A mayor's party label is not a release plan.

One unit gets a number. Another gets a budget scare. The total gets the scarlet word. That is rhetoric doing accounting.

Now I need the release file. Walk me through it slowly. On release day, where does the person sleep? Who calls the case manager? When does the person appear in court? Who handles a landlord who will not sign the lease? And the runway is generous — a six-year plan, to the end of 2032. Six years to build the file, or six years to show that it cannot be built. A person released with a court date, stable housing, treatment and a named case manager is not being shoved onto the last train. A person released because the beds vanished and nobody planned for them is. If the city cannot tell us which is which, reject the plan. If it can, the socialism warning missed the file. A scarlet slogan cannot write a discharge plan.

Then take the budget. Mauro reports at least $16 billion for the replacement jails, twice the original estimate, and $7.1 million a year for a program paying $500 monthly to families housing relatives returning from jail, growing from 50 to 300 households. Those are different kinds of costs and they do not settle the budget on their own. They do expose an editorial reflex. The cage gets realism. The housing dollar gets a sermon. Then the sermon calls the arithmetic a verdict. Charming. That is not accounting. It is a conclusion waiting for a footnote.

The scale is not close, either. On the same story, Mamdani points out that the reported $81 billion settlement in the divorce of Nvidia's chief executive could fund universal childcare for every three- and four-year-old nationwide for a year. One reported settlement is more than five times the $16 billion price tag on the replacement jails. The program that keeps a released person's family together gets a sermon and a scarlet label.

As for faster courts, diversion and alternatives to detention, Mauro says the canard "never works" and was "never really attempted." A sentence is not a study. Show me the files. How many cases moved? How long did they take? Where did people go after release? Who called them afterward? If the programs failed, Mamdani's plan fails on the facts. If the city has built the release machinery, the warning has done nothing except steal the credit. Either way, the files settle it.

And yes, enforce ordinary disorder. Fare evasion, public drug use, shoplifting and threats on the subway should not be tolerated. Focused capacity for serious violence and quality-of-life enforcement are not a socialist technique. They are government doing its job. A police unit can arrest someone who assaults a passenger. It cannot manufacture housing, treatment or a court date. One budget cannot pretend to buy both.

Mauro also points to New York's population loss by percentage as evidence for the experiment. Whatever its cause, a statewide trend cannot be assigned to two policies announced in the past week. Maybe both policies are terrible. They are not the author of every trend that came before them. Chronology is not causation.

The last refuge is the résumé. The piece reaches for a candidate's work history because the program cannot survive the files. Experience helps. It is not a costed release plan. A retired inspector can be wrong. A policy wonk can be right. I judge the program, not the résumé. Anyway.

Somewhere there is a menu with three items on it, and every warning about New York keeps setting the table with two. The gulag. The strip-mine. And the boring third thing: a city that funds the discharge plan and says so out loud. The third item has no color. It has a case manager, a housing voucher and a court date. That is why nobody writes op-eds about it.

There is a country that has thought about the floor under the door. Denmark makes it easy to fire you, and then makes sure the layoff does not cost you your house. The whole Nordic bargain in one sentence: the door opens, but the floor does not. New York's release plan opens a door onto a sidewalk. The floor is the entire difference.

So what do we build instead? Not a slogan. A file. Bind the city's courts, correction system and the $500-a-month family-housing program Mauro describes into a citywide Reentry and Public-Safety Guarantee. Every person leaving custody gets a named case manager. A court date. Stable housing. Health and addiction treatment. Supervision. Enforceable conditions both the person and the supervising agency can actually meet.

Expand court and public-defense capacity so a bed is not occupied because the case system cannot move. Keep trained capacity for serious emergencies. Fund quality-of-life enforcement separately. Pay providers through stable public contracts. Let workers bargain for staffing and safety. Measure serious violence, housing placements, treatment completion and transit safety, not arrests alone.

Call it municipal plumbing: courts that can move cases, housing that exists when a case closes, treatment with a real appointment and one person responsible for the next step.

This is harder here than it looks, because New York has to make separate systems agree. Courts, corrections, housing, health, transit and the people who staff them answer to different funders and different bosses. A mayor cannot command them into harmony. They have to be funded, staffed and bargained over. That is not a reason to skip the plan. That is the work.

Close Rikers as a failed jail. Build the guarantee. Do not close the obligation to the people inside or the neighborhoods outside. Anyone can close a jail. The work starts after the door opens. The next sentence has to be a housing placement, a treatment appointment, a court date and a person responsible for the next step.
