Four justices took book money from a party and shut down the Supreme Court.

The case was a music copyright dispute over who owns foreign copyrights, brought to the Court this term, with major publishing companies and lobbying groups for the movie and recording industries lined up over the summer to ask it to take the case, and briefs filed by Paul Clement and Lisa Blatt, two of the most prominent advocates who practice before the high court. It never got a courtroom, and it never got a vote. Justices Sonia Sotomayor, Neil Gorsuch, Amy Coney Barrett and Ketanji Brown Jackson recused themselves, and the Court gave no public explanation. Six justices are what the statute requires to constitute a quorum — 28 U.S.C. § 1, the minimum number of justices needed to act — and six was the number the Court no longer had. The Court declined to hear it.

The only explanation on the table comes from Fix the Court, the watchdog group: all four recusant justices have book deals with Penguin Random House, which is part of a German conglomerate that is a party in the case. Four of nine justices — two from each side of the Court's usual divides, which is the point — are contractually entangled with the same publisher standing before them. The Court did not dispute the account.

Start with the part that is working. Recusal is the practice of a judge stepping aside from a case in which they have a personal or financial interest, and the ordinary reading of this one is favorable, stated before the audit. Federal law tells any federal judge to step aside where impartiality might reasonably be questioned — 28 U.S.C. § 455 — and the Court published its own code of conduct three years ago, its first, restating the same duty for financial interests. Gabe Roth of Fix the Court called it good news and bad news: good because the four did what the rules require. Four judges declining to sit on a case in which their finances could be affected by the outcome is the system working at the individual level. A justice with a stake in a party should not hear that party's case. That is the ordinary craft of the bench, and here it was honored.

The bad news is what the rules leave behind. Renee Knake Jefferson of the University of Houston named it: "When four justices need to step aside, the Supreme Court can no longer decide the case, and that becomes an institutional problem, not just an individual ethics issue." Her point has a hard edge: there are no substitute justices at the top court. A district judge who recuses is replaced by another district judge. Here the same short-handed Court that this fall [saw two men without lawyers reach its bench](/articles/2026-09-05-two-men-without-lawyers-reach-supreme-court-for-fall-arguments/) had no one to send in, the same bench whose members [still speak of a retired colleague's absence](/articles/2026-06-28-some-justices-say-they-miss-retired-colleague-stephen-breyer/) could not assemble a working six even to vote on a case it had been asked to hear. Recusal disposes of the individual's conflict by loading it onto the institution.

The case did not assemble itself. Hundreds of lawyers built it for a bench that then could not sit on it. Josh Blackman of the South Texas College of Law counted the result plainly: hundreds of lawyers briefed a case that produced no quorum. "There's no quorum."

From outside, that is what a largely self-enforced recusal policy looks like. Even if the link through Penguin Random House's parent were thin enough that recusal was optional, the justices' failure to say why leaves the public in the dark by design. The silence is the policy. The Court polices its own conflicts and reports the result to no one, and no outside body has power to review the decision or to fill the seat. The same Court had room this spring to [deny a 98-year-old judge's bid to return to the bench](/articles/2026-06-15-supreme-court-denies-hearing-for-98-year-old-judge-suspended-over-fitness-test/) over a fitness test, and it retains full room to grant or refuse whatever cases its docket strategy suits. Four justices' outside income left no room to hear this one. The docket is not neutral ground.

That is the regime, and the case is only its latest instance: nine lifetime appointees holding outside income from the industries that litigate before them, a recusal policy each justice applies to themselves, and no outside body with power to review the decision or to fill the seat they vacate. Substitute a film studio's memoir contract, a bank's speaker's fee, a pharma imprint for a music-publishing one, and the load-bearing analysis does not change.

There is an honest fix on the table and it is not complicated. Jefferson proposed that justices who want to write books place advances and royalties in accounts they cannot touch until retirement — a rule that lets a justice publish without letting publishing decide which cases the Court can reach. Blackman proposed that Congress legislate the royalty problem altogether: "We need to have the justices focused on their day job more. Grant more cases, decide more opinions, write more. The books are fun, but that's not why they're there." The Court had no comment on either proposal. Either one ends the conflict at the source instead of managing it at the moment of decision.

Meanwhile the person who wrote the song is still waiting. Tim Kappel, the songwriter's lawyer, put the delay in one line: "This fight was and will continue to be on behalf of all artists, even those who happen to sit on the Supreme Court." That was meant as generosity. Read against the docket it is a description: four justices' side income ended a case brought for artists, and the Court said nothing about why. The ownership question underneath all this — who owns the foreign copyrights — remains undecided, because nine justices could not be in the room together to decide it.

What cannot survive is the current arrangement, and the four recusals show why without any accusation of wrongdoing. Sotomayor, Gorsuch, Barrett and Jackson followed the rules exactly as written. The rules then cost the Supreme Court a case it had been asked to hear. A courtroom that cannot convene because four of its judges are financially attached to a party is not a courtroom with an ethics question. It is a courtroom that has been disabled by the outside income of its own members — and the only body with standing to say so is the nine.
