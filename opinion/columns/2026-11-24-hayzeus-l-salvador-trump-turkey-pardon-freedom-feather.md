Donald Trump will pardon turkeys while his administration cages migrant families.

On Tuesday, at the White House, President Donald Trump will pardon Freedom and Feather, two turkeys native to West Virginia. The public can vote online to decide which bird receives the in-person ceremonial pardon; both will live out their days at Texas A&M University. The White House calls Tuesday the seventy-ninth turkey pardoning, though the annual tradition began under George H.W. Bush in 1989. The Associated Press described the ceremony as a typically light-hearted departure from politics and policy.

It should be the easiest half hour on a president’s calendar. Last year it was not. Trump spared the turkeys and spent part of the ceremony attacking his political opponents. He said the birds should be named Chuck and Nancy, after Chuck Schumer and Nancy Pelosi. “I would never pardon those people,” he said.

A president stood at the only ceremony on the calendar devoted to mercy and used it to announce whom he refuses to forgive. That is not a joke. That is cruelty performed under mercy’s lights.

The ceremony has always been the republic’s smallest mercy. The White House Historical Association traces an early presidential turkey pardon to Abraham Lincoln’s administration, when Tad Lincoln pleaded for the life of a bird destined for the Christmas table. In 1954, Dwight Eisenhower fed a cranberry to a speckled turkey. In 1963, John Kennedy, days before he was fatally shot in Texas, pardoned a turkey wearing a sign that read, “Good Eating, Mr. President.” In 2024, Joe Biden pardoned Peach and Blossom and gave each bird a motto. Peach lived by “Keep calm and gobble on.” Blossom declared, “No fowl play. Just Minnesota nice.”

Across administrations the point never changed: one man, one bird at a time, a dinner table the bird will never reach, and the pardon itself the whole meaning. It was the one room in the architecture of American power where mercy required no enemy. This president found that room and turned it into a boast about revenge.

Mr. President, you took a ceremony meant to be a brief departure from politics and made it a stage for attacking your opponents. I do not speak from a clean place. My own communities have divided newcomers against one another instead of sharing the welcome they profess, and some Catholic politicians have bent their tradition’s own vocabulary of ranked love to license exclusion. The same accounting must include the prior administration’s zero-tolerance policy and this administration’s continued separation and detention. I will not trade one administration’s cruelty for another’s. Children belong with their parents.

The texts have no difficulty meeting this conduct. The Book’s first move is to tell us what God is, because God gives his own name: “The LORD, the LORD, a God merciful and gracious, slow to anger.” Jesus read that name as a demand and aimed it at men flawless in religious performance and blind to the person in front of them: “Go and learn what this means: ‘I desire mercy, not sacrifice.’”

The Law said it long before, and said it more than thirty times: love the stranger as yourself, for you were strangers in the land of Egypt. “You shall not wrong or oppress a resident alien, for you were aliens in the land of Egypt.” Matthew 25 gives the measure: “I was a stranger and you did not welcome me.” It also says, “I was a stranger and you welcomed me,” and, “what you did to the least of these, you did to me.”

Now set the teaching beside the conduct. At a mercy ceremony, the President of the United States said of two human beings, “I would never pardon those people.” The administration he commands holds mothers and children behind a fence at the South Texas Family Residential Center in Dilley. Between those words and that conduct, there is no honest road to the word mercy. Religion performed for the crowd while its one requirement goes unmet is sin in an old form, the kind the prophets kept naming.

The ledger underneath the ceremony is not seasonal. After January 20, 2025, the administration revoked humanitarian parole for more than 500,000 people and allowed temporary protected status to lapse for 350,000 Venezuelans and 350,000 Haitians. Those moves are contested in court.

The administration ended the CBP One asylum appointment system, canceled roughly 30,000 appointments for which people had waited months, rebuilt the same portal as a self-deportation gateway, and began offering $1,000 to anyone willing to leave. Congress appropriated $45 billion for ICE detention while the agency expanded toward 100,000 beds. It also reopened the family-detention camp at Dilley. Mothers and children are behind the same fence again.

Across the country, migrants face detention, backlogs, and removal orders. Pope Francis named the sickness in his 2013 Lampedusa homily: “the globalization of indifference.” We become accustomed to other people’s suffering until it no longer touches us. The administration’s immigration record puts cruelty where the ceremony places mercy. This is cruelty with a public celebration attached.

Family separation continued after the formal zero-tolerance policy ended in 2018. A government count in late 2024 still found as many as 1,360 known separated children waiting for reunion. Early-2026 case-tracking data put the average wait for an asylum hearing at about four years.

A child waiting for a parent is not a line item. A person denied asylum is not a nuisance to be managed. The command is concrete: do what the stranger needs, because you know what it means to be received and to have your own reception withheld.

None of this began last January, and it is not the work of one administration. The historian Adam Goodman counts roughly eight million formal deportations over the past century and about fifty million more people pushed out through coercive mechanisms that the government calls voluntary departure. The machine is older than any man now operating it.

My hands were never clean in this either. I served in the government that runs it. I grew up in El Paso and watched it empty desks before the first bell. The new thing is not the machine. The new thing is a president standing at a pardon ceremony saying aloud what the machine usually keeps quiet: there are people he will never pardon, and he names them on camera.

Now the public is voting on which turkey takes the stage. Both birds are spared either way; the vote only decides which one receives the ceremony. The audience chooses the image. The government chooses whose family to tear apart.

Notice what this White House is willing to put to a popular vote. It has never lacked for ceremony. This fall it welcomed Xi Jinping to a state dinner and handed him a [bald-eagle sculpture](/articles/2026-09-25-trump-presents-xi-with-bald-eagle-sculpture-at-white-house-state-dinner/); this week it hosted a [dinner for the top holders of his meme coin, down ninety percent](/articles/2026-11-22-trump-hosts-dinner-for-top-holders-of-his-meme-coin-down-90/). Pageantry it can schedule. Scheduled mercy it refuses.

The alternative is not hidden. In 2003, the bishops of the United States and Mexico wrote the standard: people have the right to find opportunity in their homeland; the right to migrate when they cannot support themselves and their families at home; the right of sovereign nations to control their borders; protection for refugees and asylum seekers; and respect for the human rights and dignity of undocumented migrants.

Meeting that standard is not beyond this administration. Reopen the asylum doors closed in January 2025. Reunite children with their parents. Bring the separated children home. Put the $45 billion to work on judges and caseworkers instead of beds. That is not mercy as a favor. That is justice, and justice is not settled by an online vote.

A humane immigration policy would keep children with their parents, give every person a real hearing before removal, protect the right to seek safety, and open a lawful route to work and belong. Francis wrote in *Fratelli Tutti* §121: “No one, then, can remain excluded because of his or her place of birth, much less because of privileges enjoyed by others who were born in lands of greater opportunity. The limits and borders of individual states cannot stand in the way of this.” A border may be administered justly. It cannot make the human being inside it less worthy.

Mr. President, the birds are safe. The human beings are not. Reunite separated children, keep families together, and give asylum seekers a real hearing. Make mercy the practice of your administration, not merely its ceremony.

On March 23, 1980, while the death squads worked, Archbishop Óscar Romero preached his last homily. He begged the men doing the killing. He beseeched them. He ordered them in the name of God and a suffering people: “¡cese la represión!”—“Stop the repression!” He did not ask them to feel mercy. He told them to stop. He was dead the next day.

My request is the same, and it is not complicated. Mr. President: stop. You pardon on camera what costs you nothing; now pardon something that costs you. Reopen the doors you closed. Take down the fence at Dilley. The mercy you have already performed is not beyond you; you have proved that it lives somewhere within you. Point it toward the place where the image of God actually is. You can still come back. That is your door. You can walk through it. It requires no ceremony and no winning vote.

After the ceremony ends, Freedom and Feather will live at Texas A&M, carrying their names. The children separated from their parents will need reunion, not a ceremonial pardon. Mercy has never been scarce. It has only been pointed at what needs it least.

Pardon the turkeys. Then open the gates.
