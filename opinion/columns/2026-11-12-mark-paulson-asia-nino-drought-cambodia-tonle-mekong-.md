China and America are drying Tonle Sap out from under its people.

I've got a lake too. Petenwell — second-biggest lake in Wisconsin by surface, twenty-three thousand acres of Wisconsin River backed up behind a dam — and I have watched it longer than I have watched anything on this earth except my kids. Twelve winters running I've written down the date the ice goes out, off the same bank below the deer stand, in a green notebook that lives in the shop between the parts manuals. The date moves earlier. Not every year — weather is weather — but the run of years moves earlier, the syrup run starts ahead of itself, and the rut sits a couple weeks off where my father set it. Aldo Leopold's *A Sand County Almanac* was written out of this same sand country, and its calendar ran on ice-out and spring fire the same way mine runs on ice-out and first-mosquito week. So when the report landed this week about a lake on the far side of the world going dry, I didn't read it as a travel story. I read it as the same entry, written in somebody else's notebook.

Here is what the report says. At Chong Kneas, on the edge of Tonle Sap — the biggest lake in Southeast Asia — a man named Meas Chou stacks metal drums wherever he can find room, red and blue and yellow against the muddy water, and sells them to families who need them to keep their houses floating. "If fishermen can earn a lot, I can sell my goods a lot. But if they earn less, I sell a little less too," he said. About a million and a half people live on that lake. Their houses rise and fall with the water. That is the whole architecture of a life there: a house that floats, a boat under it, a fishery underneath that.

Tonle Sap runs on what scientists call a flood pulse. Every monsoon the Mekong swells so hard its flow reverses — the river runs backward for a stretch and shoves water up into the lake, which quintuples in size, hauling forest and grassland nutrients in with the flood. The fish follow the flood. It makes Tonle Sap one of the most productive freshwater fisheries on the planet and a nursery for fish that sound invented: the Mekong giant catfish, critically endangered, that grows to ten feet; the giant barb, biggest carp in the world, up to 660 pounds; the giant stingray, the largest freshwater fish ever recorded. Chinese envoys came through in the thirteenth century and described a vast inland sea teeming with fish. Eight hundred years of that record, and Abby Seif, who wrote *Troubling the Water: A dying lake and a vanishing world in Cambodia*, says the thread is about to snap: "You have a disappearing way of life ... a disappearing culture and you have a disappearing history."

The gauge tells you how close the snap is. At Prek Kdam, halfway between Phnom Penh and the lake, the water stood at nearly seven meters in early October — more than a meter below where an October should sit, and a meter and a half lower than last year. This is a record El Niño year — [the World Meteorological Organization warned this fall that it is the strongest in seventy years](/articles/2026-09-18-wmo-warns-strongest-el-ni-o-in-70-years-set-to-intensify-into-winter/) — landing on a drought that was already extreme. In June the U.N. Food and Agriculture Organization put Cambodia at high risk of severe drought across about 314,000 acres of farmland, roughly 3 percent of the country's arable ground. That lands twice on the lake, because most fisherfolk farm a little on the side to make the fishing carry them, as Brian Eyler of the Stimson Center's Southeast Asia program points out.

And dry years around that lake burn. Between 1993 and 2017, about a third of its flooded forests stayed dry and were lost to fire and clearing, a 2020 study found. Zeb Hogan, who runs the Wonders of the Mekong project at the University of Nevada, Reno, put it plain: "When you get a very dry year, you have less fish, less food and more fire. And all of those add up to a bad situation for people living around the lake." Fire is not an abstraction in the sand counties either. The Cottonville fire burned 3,410 acres of Big Flats in 2005. The rail-line fire of 1959 took 1,200 more.

The same drought is already cutting [eighteen percent off daily traffic through the Panama Canal](/articles/2026-09-15-panama-canal-plans-18-daily-traffic-cut-as-el-ni-o-drought-worsens/) and bending [commodity and shipping markets](/articles/2026-08-29-strong-el-ni-o-disrupts-global-commodity-and-shipping-markets/) with it. Big water systems run close to their edges everywhere now, and a strong El Niño is what it looks like when the edges start showing.

Talk on the lake keeps coming back to two wars. One is the Iran war, which has pushed fuel past what a small engine can carry — Yim Nary says her husband, who fishes before dawn, rows instead of running the motor on the days fuel runs high. The other is the border conflict with Thailand, gumming up trade, travel and tourism. Daniel Yergin traced the wiring a century back in *The Prize*: the fuel in any tank is hooked to somebody else's fight. The fight is far away. The invoice gets delivered to a woman in a floating house who cuts and cleans other people's fish when her husband is sick, because the catch has to cover fuel, rice, vegetables, drinking water and electricity for three kids.

"I don't know what the future holds for my children," Nary said. "All I have is fish and family." That is a balance sheet, and it is the one the fuel economy has been running its deficits against. Cambodia has eighteen million people and contributes less than two-tenths of one percent of the world's annual greenhouse gas emissions. The lake's people run the arithmetic the rest of us get to skip. When the water stays low the catches come up short. When the catches come up short, the families who farm to make up the difference lose both halves of their living in the same season.

The big burners run the other direction — China at the top of the list, my own country second. That burn is what turns a natural El Niño into a killing drought: hotter, drier, monsoon rain that shows up wrong for the flood pulse — a drought that starves the fishery first and then the million and a half people who live off it. And the machinery got to the river before the weather did — upstream dams and sand mining were already squeezing the Mekong's flow before this cycle set up. The people under the lake mostly have little to no land, few assets, and nowhere else to go. That is not bad luck. That is an arrangement working the way arrangements like it always work — fuel burned where the money is, weather paid for where the money isn't.

The lake-ice record up at Madison goes back to 1855 — the longest on the continent — and it runs about a month short of the 1800s. One hemispheric study lined up thirty-nine long-term ice records from lakes and rivers across the Northern Hemisphere and found the same drift everywhere it looked: freeze later, break up earlier. Wisconsin has warmed roughly three degrees since 1950, fastest in winter, per the state's own climate-assessment work. My notebook sits inside the same drift — twelve winters, one line a year, off the same bank — a man's handwriting filling its page while every thermometer on earth fills in the rest.

Meas Chou's drums are the picture of it all. He isn't asking anybody to fix the sky. He's stacking barrels against the mud so other people's houses keep floating. The people stacking drums didn't fill the sky. The countries that filled it own the falling water — and my country holds the second-largest share of that ownership.

Here is the part I won't round off. The companies that sold the fuel did not not-know. Some of their own scientists had the trajectory by the late seventies, and what got built on top of that knowledge — leaked strategy memos, paid experts, forty years of keeping "uncertainty" alive in public — is documented record now. Doubt was the product, because doubt was what kept the burn going. The burn is what a gauge in Cambodia is reading a meter and a half short.

I'm not writing this from clean hands, either. My shop burns gasoline and two-cycle oil six days a week. My truck has 187,000 miles on it. The Stihl and Husqvarna saws I sharpen every October before deer season drink the same stuff that got the sky loaded. I pay into it every time I pull a cord on a customer's mower. The difference between my lake and theirs is that the bill hasn't reached my bench yet. It has reached theirs.

Aldo Leopold set the test in *A Sand County Almanac*: "A thing is right when it tends to preserve the integrity, stability, and beauty of the biotic community. It is wrong when it tends otherwise." By that test this arrangement is wrong twice over — wrong to break the pulse, wrong to hand the breaking to the people who did least of it.

Wendell Berry wrote *The Unsettling of America* about the habit behind the arrangement — an extractive mind that takes land, water, animals and people as inputs to be used up and does not care whether the land is a hillside or a floodplain. His argument has held for going on fifty years: a community's economy is its membership, and extraction destroys the membership before it destroys the economy. On Tonle Sap the membership is a million and a half people whose houses float on drums. You can watch the membership being destroyed in real time; the economy will follow on the schedule the water sets. The honest fix starts with the ledger and not with the victims. The countries and companies that filled the sky owe the cost of what the sky is now doing: cut the emissions that are breaking the monsoon, and pay for the fire lines, the water, the farmland and the flow the drought is taking, and for the people who have to move — delivered to the places absorbing the damage first, while there is still something left to refill. That has been promised at summit after summit and delivered short. The gauge at Prek Kdam is the measure of the shortfall.

At Chong Kneas the drums wait in a wall of red, blue and yellow against the mud-colored water, ready to lift whatever houses are still there.

Quinn is five. She ought to inherit a county that still has a county and a world where the water still comes back when the rain does. That isn't a wish. It's a gauge reading, and it's ours to answer.
