The market voted on whole neighborhoods in this country, and its answer was no store at all. In [Another Cautionary Tale for MamdaniMarts](https://www.wsj.com/opinion/another-cautionary-tale-for-mamdanimarts-9c0a2b9a?mod=rss_opinion) in the Wall Street Journal, James Freeman warns that New York City's city-owned grocery stores, set to launch next year, will disappoint consumers the way he says a century of socialism experiments have disappointed, and he offers the just-closed Prairie Food Co-Op as the exhibit. He floats a darker alternative: if taxpayer money makes the city stores cheap enough, they will drive private grocers out of business, and New Yorkers will not be celebrating that. The evidence he offers supports a much smaller claim — a badly run, unpopular public store would fail.

Now the honest part, because there is one. Grocery retail is one of the hardest businesses in America: pennies of margin on a cart of food, and one bad quarter has the landlord starting to sniff around. Markets are genuinely superb at this — nothing beats a well-run chain at putting tens of thousands of products on shelves cheap. I am not going to pretend a city agency is born knowing how to run a produce aisle. And the Prairie board said it tried just about everything except running a normal profit-seeking business, and too few customers came. Customers matter. A store with no customers cannot feed a neighborhood, and a municipal seal cannot repair a bad business model. Co-ops are not magic either: Mondragon watched its flagship appliance firm, Fagor, go bankrupt in 2013 under roughly a billion euros of debt. A cooperative has to survive a market, and it can lose. If the mayor's stores fail on margins and service, that failure is earned, and it deserves every word written about it.

One more concession, because the whole argument lives inside a single word. Communism as it was actually built in the twentieth century — Russia, China, Cambodia — was a catastrophe. It killed tens of millions of people and crushed the workers it claimed to liberate. That verdict is in, and I am not here to relitigate it. The question the word is doing all that work to hide is the one that matters: between the gulag and the strip-mine, is that honestly the whole menu? The credit union in your wallet would be surprised to hear it.

Now look at what the exhibit actually shows. A co-op is not a "nonmarket supermarket." It is a private business owned by the people who run it and shop in it — one member, one vote, no outside shareholders; that is what the word cooperative means — and when it cannot cover its costs, it closes. That is market discipline in its purest form. The column's own logic holds that producers free to earn profits have "the strongest incentive to listen to the customer." Fine. The letter says Prairie asked shoppers to reward a bundle of virtues — local sourcing, alleged environmental benefit — rather than simply the cheapest dependable groceries nearby. It listened. It ran out of customers. The market spoke. He didn't care for the verdict, so he filed it against the other team.

Then he never says what he thinks killed it. If his answer is competition, he has written my column for me. And none of this is his own reporting: he assembled other people's commentary — a Washington Times piece, a Courthouse News Service dispatch, a stray item about who is selling Nvidia — and read a verdict off the top. Anyway.

Here is what the column never follows: who pays for the stores that were never built. City-owned groceries are not proposed for the neighborhoods where the chains do brisk business. The pitch, as the mayor ran it, was to put them in food deserts — places where the private market already held its vote and returned "not profitable" — where the alternative to a city store is not a thriving supermarket. It is two buses each way and a gallon of milk priced like a luxury good. The market has spoken in those neighborhoods, eloquently. Its answer was no store at all. And that answer's cost appears on no grocer's books. It lands on the people who live there — in money, in time, and eventually in the doctor's office.

Every unusually cheap grocery needs an explanation. Either the store is genuinely more efficient, or somebody makes up the difference. In this sector the difference can come from wages too low to cover a household, from households leaning on SNAP and other public benefits to cover a job that does not pay, from suppliers pushed onto worse terms, or from city taxpayers. Freeman notices the city check and calls it the problem. He lets the other checks vanish. "Market discipline" is a lovely phrase for a business model whose labor bill is quietly backfilled by the public. It sounds very dignified until you follow the money. I'm not against markets. I'm against hiding the bill.

Freeman's sharpest worry is a pincer: if the city stores fail, that's socialism; if they sell cheap enough to undercut private grocers, that is also socialism. But there is nothing to undercut in a food desert. That is the definition of one. Where a private grocer is thriving, the city has no business underpricing it, and the fix there is boring and specific: put the public store where the market isn't, publish its books, audit its board, and let the register judge it — which is how you run a utility. Cheap food reaching a neighborhood the chains skipped is not unfair competition. It is the assignment. His question — will New Yorkers be celebrating this? — answers itself at the register, in the neighborhoods where the previous answer was nothing.

The word doing all the lifting is "socialism," which is convenient, because it spares everyone the chore of asking whether the boring thing already works here. It does, and mostly in places that would never use the word. In 1936, private utilities declined to wire the countryside because the math did not pencil; Washington lent to member-owned cooperatives instead. Today about 900 rural electric co-ops serve 42 million Americans across more than half the nation's land. North Dakota has run a state-owned bank, profitably, every year since 1919. About 145 million Americans belong to a credit union. Nobody ever put Bismarck behind an Iron Curtain.

Now the part that matters, because a municipal supermarket is honestly the crudest tool in the drawer. The American instrument with the longer record is the cooperative with financing under it. Mondragon is the standing rebuttal to the claim that co-ops stay small: about eighty cooperatives, seventy thousand people, more than eleven billion euros in revenue, competing since 1956 with top pay around five or six times the bottom. It runs on more than its vote. The engine is Laboral Kutxa, a bank the co-ops own, founded in 1959, lending patient capital — money willing to wait years — to co-ops no outside investor would touch. That is the layer American co-ops mostly go without. The latest census counts about 820 worker cooperatives in this country, up from roughly 320 a decade ago, some 10,000 worker-owners, growing with no financing network underneath. And there is a member-run grocery proving the model can hold in the very city he is warning about: the Park Slope food co-op, opened in 1973, run and staffed by the members who shop there, still setting its own prices more than half a century on.

Here is why the project is harder here. In Denmark and Sweden, collective bargaining covers more than eight workers in ten. In America it covers about one worker in nine. We have fifty state systems, weak political parties, and a long habit of treating public administration as a rival instead of a service. The machinery behind Denmark took decades to build. A city cannot vote for Denmark on Tuesday.

So what do we build? Not a municipal monopoly with a blank check. A city-backed food option with a cooperative spine. The city funds a transparent share of food costs. Independent local boards operate the stores, and workers and neighborhood residents have a real vote. Every budget shows prices, sales, waste, wages, and the subsidy per household. If a store is popular and well run, it stays. If it is empty, shabby, or overpriced, it is transferred to another operator or closed. Public ownership gets one advantage a private grocery lacks: it can state its public purpose out loud. It gets no exemption from arithmetic. And where the city insists on running stores, put the patient capital underneath them — credit-union-style money willing to wait, a state development bank like North Dakota's as the backstop — and hold the stores to the same on-the-record standard as everything else.

Freeman can make the case for private groceries. He has to put both bills on the same counter. If private groceries can feed New Yorkers for less at the counter and less on the public bill, they should keep the aisle. If a public option can feed them for less at the counter and no more on the public bill, shoppers should get the lower bill. Shopkeepers can contest the policy. Hungry New Yorkers should not have to applaud high prices as proof of market virtue.

Public ownership does not erase customer choice. It adds another owner to the menu. The economy is a set of choices, not the weather: somebody drew the map that leaves whole neighborhoods without a grocery store, and somebody can draw a different one. Build the store. Cooperatively run it. Publish the books. Let the basket decide. Start connecting.
