*The statute names the importer. The price tag answers for everyone else.*

Donald Trump is extracting taxes from American households while promising economic revival. Here are the numbers. Abe Flaaen and S. İnanıcı Tinelnot’s [“The Effects of the 2018 Tariffs on U.S. Consumers and Importers”](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aeri.20160533), published in *American Economic Review: Insights*, found that prices of washing machines rose about 12 percent after the 2018 duties took effect. Prices of dryers, which did not face the same laundry-machine duty, rose as well. American buyers paid roughly $1.5 billion more each year, preserving about 1,800 manufacturing jobs at a cost of roughly $815,000 per job. The jobs were real. The arithmetic is the policy’s bill, not the workers’.

The promise is the president’s: the duties will revive American manufacturing and strengthen the economy. He calls himself “Mr. Tariff.” The Associated Press, in its Nov. 26 report, [“Most interviewed Republicans say they are open to reviewing Trump’s tariffs”](https://apnews.com/search?q=Most%20interviewed%20Republicans%20say%20they%20are%20open%20to%20reviewing%20Trump%27s%20tariffs), found scant evidence that the tariffs had accomplished either goal and reported that they often increase consumer costs in the form of a tax.

A tariff is a tax collected at the border from the importer of record—the party that files the customs entry and owes the duty. The charge ordinarily enters the landed cost and then the price. Statutory incidence means who the law names as the payer; economic incidence means who bears the tax after prices and behavior adjust. The statute names the importer. The price tag answers for everyone else.

Amiti, Redding, and Weinstein’s [2020 *Journal of Economic Perspectives* study](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/jep.34.4.187) found that the 2018 duties were passed through to U.S. import prices almost in full and that exporting countries did not absorb them by cutting their own prices. The bill was paid from American income and prices. Where duties raise the prices of goods that lower-income households purchase in larger shares of their budgets, the burden falls disproportionately on them. Tariffs are not regressive by definition; their design determines their incidence. This design is a tax.

The scale is now the story. CBO’s [June 25, 2025 report, *The Budgetary Effects of the 2025 Tariffs*](https://www.cbo.gov/search?search_api_fulltext=The%20Budgetary%20Effects%20of%20the%202025%20Tariffs), estimated that the tariff measures in force as of its May 13, 2025, cutoff would reduce total federal deficits by about $2.8 trillion over the 2025–2034 period. That was a net budget estimate, not $2.8 trillion of gross tariff revenue and not a measure of the full tariff schedule in November 2026. More important, it was not a finding that the duties had revived manufacturing or strengthened the economy. The report estimated a fiscal effect. It did not certify the factory forecast. A receipt estimate cannot authenticate a promise.

At the time these duties were imposed, no bill went through committee markup, received a distributional score, or received a recorded vote from Congress. The Joint Committee on Taxation produces conventional revenue estimates for tax legislation. CBO independently estimates legislation’s budget effects; JCT revenue estimates may be one input, but a CBO score is not simply a JCT revenue estimate in a new wrapper. No bill-level score was produced for these proclamation tariffs when they were announced. CBO’s later tariff analysis is an estimate of executive policy, not a retroactive score of legislation. Tariff revenue is real. Legislative scrutiny was absent.

Article I, Section 8, Clauses 1 and 3 of the [U.S. Constitution](https://constitution.congress.gov/constitution/article-1/) assign duties and foreign commerce to Congress. The president may act under statutory delegations, including [Section 232 of the Trade Expansion Act of 1962](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=granuleid:USC-prelim-title19-section1861&num=0&edition=prelim), [Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=granuleid:USC-prelim-title19-section1867&num=0&edition=prelim), and the [International Emergency Economic Powers Act of 1977](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?path=/prelim@title50/chapter35&edition=prelim). Those enactments are old. The current duties were not enacted by the current Congress.

Sen. Mike Rounds of South Dakota described the result accurately: Congress has “a real difficult time making a tariff and then authorizing and enacting it.” “So they’ve given authorities to the executive branch over a period of years. Some of that has cascaded,” he said. Cascaded is the correct word. Delegations written for national-security determinations, unfair-trade remedies, and emergencies now carry a general revenue program. The votes authorizing the delegations happened decades ago. The tariffs were imposed without another vote.

The interviews show why repeal has an opening. More than half a dozen Republican lawmakers interviewed after the election were open to reviewing the levies case by case. Rep. Michael Guest of Mississippi said there “may be some areas that I would agree with the Democrats,” while remaining opposed to a blanket restriction on presidential authority. Sen. Todd Young of Indiana said “A rethink is indeed in order” and called the structure “overly broad” because it was harming factory floors, farms, small businesses, and family budgets.

Sen. Josh Hawley of Missouri warned against overcorrecting and feared a “dramatic pendulum swing.” The caution is fair. Case-by-case review is not repeal, and it does not supply the votes needed for repeal. The openness followed election losses centered on rising costs. The same week, the administration proposed new levies on China, the European Union, Japan, South Korea, Taiwan, and nine other major trading partners. An opening is not yet a coalition.

The AP Voter Poll results in the same Associated Press report do not show that Republican voters had abandoned tariffs. They also do not show a mandate to retain them. About half of the voters who backed the Republican candidate in their local U.S. House race said the tariff policies had helped the national economy. About three in ten said they had hurt, and about one-quarter said they had made no difference.

The local results were more diffuse. About three in ten said the policies had helped their local economy, roughly one-quarter said they had hurt, and about four in ten said they had not affected it. Three in ten reporting local harm is not equivalent to three in ten demanding repeal, especially when four in ten report no effect. The proper conclusion is narrower: Republican voters were divided on the claim of national benefit, and local benefits were less visible than the administration’s sales pitch. The administration had a divided electorate on the question.

Rep. Steve Womack of Arkansas supplied the local cost that the national polling obscured. The levies “have played a role in some of the affordability issues,” he said. His producers are paying more for goods “that can’t be sourced domestically.” That is an input-cost increase in his district, not a macroeconomic abstraction. White House spokesman Matthew Middleton said the administration would “continue fighting to lower costs, expand opportunity, and make life more affordable for hardworking American families.” The administration’s answer to the question of who pays was a press release. The customs record answers with the importer who remitted the duty.

Young’s preferred cure was consultation. He wanted Congress involved in negotiations over the United States-Mexico-Canada Agreement: “The USMCA process seems to work if Congress is actively involved and not patted on the head.” Sen. John Kennedy of Louisiana, who did not support the earlier Canada repeal legislation, was blunter: “Tariffs with respect to Canada don’t make sense to me.” Canada’s retaliation after the levies were imposed added to the Republicans’ election difficulties and made the Canadian tariffs the practical test of congressional willingness to act.

The arithmetic remains unforgiving. The Senate requires 67 votes to override President Trump’s veto, while the Democrats are poised to hold 56 seats. A repeal therefore needs 11 Republican votes, and every one must come from the president’s own party. Sen. Rand Paul of Kentucky, a firm tariff opponent, said repeal legislation would have to be attached to must-pass legislation. “On its own, he’ll just veto it,” he said. A repeal bill cannot be considered on its own merits. It must ride a vehicle.

Incoming Senate Finance Committee Chairman Ron Wyden of Oregon wants to begin with Canada. Winning Republican support for repeal, he said, would build momentum for going after other tariffs. His House counterpart, incoming Ways and Means Committee Chairman Richard Neal, said rescinding the Canadian duties “might be a good starting point” before addressing the tariffs “in totality” because they had been used “in a punitive manner.” The case for limiting presidential tariff power is now being made inside the president’s own party.

One useful control is the 2017 tax act, and the comparison is procedural rather than economic. JCT’s [conventional estimate, JCX-69-17](https://www.jct.gov/CBO-6-17-039.pdf), put the legislation’s ten-year cost at $1.46 trillion. Its [dynamic supplement, JCX-67-17](https://www.jct.gov/CBO-6-17-038.pdf), put gross revenue feedback at $451 billion, partly offset by $66 billion in higher debt-service spending, for a net dynamic effect of $385 billion. The conventional estimate holds the size of the economy fixed while allowing taxpayers to change behavior; the dynamic supplement adds changes in the wider economy feeding back into revenue.

Those documents scored a bill with specified text, effective dates, and budgetary effects. The tariff proclamations had neither a bill nor those documents. That is the laundering operation: a forecast about factories is made to stand in for the budgetary and distributional review the proclamations never received. CBO’s tariff estimate can be sound about revenue while the manufacturing claim remains unverified. They answer different questions.

Congress should repeal the broad tariffs and require any continuing duty to be enacted in statute. Each schedule should carry a JCT revenue estimate and CBO’s independent budget estimate, distributional tables by income, a ten-year budget window, an input-cost analysis, expiration dates, and a production review before renewal. The baseline should be current law. [Section 257, 2 U.S.C. § 661(e)](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=granuleid:USC-prelim-title2-section661&num=0&edition=prelim), requires CBO to assume that provisions of law in effect when a projection is prepared are administered as specified. That is the statutory foundation for CBO’s current-law convention. The statute does not itself prescribe the label or the ten-year window.

Put replacement revenue in ordinary income-tax law rather than the customs line. Enact a temporary graduated surcharge on taxable income above $1 million, with a higher marginal rate above $5 million, and set the rates from the conventional JCT and CBO score for replacing the receipts of the tariffs being repealed. The statutory incidence would fall on high-income filers, and much of the economic incidence should remain with them because the base is narrow, though some could reach capital owners through dividends and asset prices.

That mechanism addresses firms’ input costs directly. A customs duty is embedded in a material’s landed cost; repealing it lowers the price of that material to the factory. An income-tax surcharge is not added to the production line. It does not attach a new tax to imported inputs or make imported finished goods cheaper by comparison. A corporate or payroll tax should not be used as the mechanical filler. Those taxes can fall on investment, employment, or prices. If the high-income surcharge cannot replace the revenue, Congress must broaden the schedule or reduce spending. It may not preserve a duty on inputs to balance the bill.

The president holds the pen. Sixty-seven votes are required to override his veto, and eleven of them must come from the Republican caucus. Here is the record: duties written outside the taxing branch, a manufacturing forecast without a score, and a customs entry naming the importer of record as liable for the tax. The revenue tables and the AER study record what “Mr. Tariff” is built to hide. Congress wrote the delegation when it handed away the pen. It can take the pen back. This is not a trade policy. It is extraction with a press release. There is no other way to read the form.
