The Wall Street Journal is helping employers bury the labor law. Ahead of Tuesday's argument in *Department of Labor v. Sun Valley Orchards*, the paper's editorial board urges the Supreme Court to "protect and extend" the Court's 2024 *Jarkesy* decision and credits the current bench with "yeoman's work strengthening the separation of powers." The occasion is a Labor Department effort to hold a fourth-generation New Jersey farm to the labor contracts it signed with its guest workers. The board's constitutional principle shows up on one side of that case only — a strengthening of one side of the docket — and the record explains why.

The board's case, at its strongest, is straightforward. The Seventh Amendment preserves jury trial in suits at common law — the private disputes over fraud, contract, and damages that English and early American courts tried to juries. The Court held in *SEC v. Jarkesy*, 603 U.S. ___ (2024), that when an agency pursues civil penalties for conduct mirroring common-law fraud, it is litigating a common-law claim, and "money damages are the prototypical common law remedy," as the Chief Justice wrote. Sun Valley's liability rests on breach of contract — "a traditional common law claim," in the board's own words. The government's answer — that penalties for violating regulatory-program conditions are "public rights," the matters like permits, benefits, licenses, and privileges that Congress may assign to agencies — stretches a category built for the government's own business over a dispute between a farmer and his workers. The Chief Justice's own language, as the board quotes it, says that "even with respect to matters that arguably fall within the scope of the 'public rights' doctrine, the presumption is in favor of Article III courts" — courts presided over by life-tenured judges. The Third Circuit agreed that the case "falls well outside the heartland" of immigration-related adjudication. An attorney aligned with the movement can state the position cleanly: juries were the founding generation's protection against tribunals convened by the government that prosecuted before them, and Congress cannot relabel a common-law claim to ship it to an in-house hearing officer. The structural concern underneath it — one agency writing the rule, prosecuting the violation, and judging the penalty — is real, and lawyers of both coalitions have raised it for decades. It is also, on this Court's record, a principle with a fixed address.

The vehicle chosen to extend that principle is a kitchen. Sun Valley Orchards employed its workers through H-2A, the federal visa program that imports agricultural labor on contracts whose terms — employment dates, wage deductions, non-monetary benefits — Labor Department regulations require the employer to write down and honor. In 2016 the Department charged the farm with breaching those contracts: it failed to provide the kitchen access it had promised, and it released workers before their contracted end dates. The penalties totaled more than $550,000. The farm disputed the charges and lost before an administrative law judge, the agency's in-house hearing officer; the Department's Administrative Review Board affirmed. By the board's account, "the steep penalties in part drove the farm to shut down." The fight the farm carries to the Supreme Court is the tribunal itself: Sun Valley says the administrative adjudication violated its constitutional rights and runs afoul of *Jarkesy*.

Now audit the claimed neutrality. The Court that rediscovered the Seventh Amendment for parties charged in enforcement proceedings is the same Court that ordered the federal courts to enforce the arbitration clauses that take juries away from workers and consumers. In *Epic Systems Corp. v. Lewis*, 584 U.S. 497 (2018), Justice Gorsuch's majority held that an employer may make arbitration, and the waiver of any class or collective claim, a condition of the job: the Federal Arbitration Act requires enforcement, and the National Labor Relations Act does not forbid it. The result is the actual Seventh Amendment experience of most American workers: a private tribunal the employer selects and pays, under procedures the employer drafted, with no jury, no meaningful appeal, and no class. A guest worker who wants a jury for a wage claim cannot have one. A farm charged by the Department of Labor — if the extension lands — must have one, in a case about his kitchen to which he is not a party. The Federal Arbitration Act is the government's own statute for moving workers' claims out of public courtrooms, and the Court reads it as a command. The jury revival runs one way.

The board's editorial is framed as a defense of the jury trial. It does not mention *Epic Systems*. It does not mention arbitration. It does not mention that employers abolished the jury trial it celebrates — by contract, with this Court's enforcement, before *Jarkesy* was written.

The Article III courts the board celebrates are, in the same years, the courts this majority closed to individuals. In *Hernandez v. Mesa*, 589 U.S. 93 (2020), the Court refused a damages action — a *Bivens* suit, meaning the implied remedy against a federal officer for a constitutional violation — brought by the family of a fifteen-year-old boy shot across the boundary fence by a Border Patrol agent standing on United States soil. Two years later the Court closed the same door again on another Border Patrol excessive-force claim, and qualified immunity — which requires a plaintiff to show that the violated right was already "clearly established" by earlier cases before the case can proceed — finishes the work in the district courts. The presumption, as the Chief Justice wrote it, favors Article III courts. The presumption held for a farm facing a penalty. It did not hold for the boy's family.

Manageability bends with the beneficiary. In *Rucho v. Common Cause*, 588 U.S. 684 (2019), the Court removed partisan gerrymandering from judicial reach because federal judges lack "manageable standards" for rigged electoral maps and would have to "reallocate political power" to fix them. The board, meanwhile, is confident that the same federal courts can absorb the penalty enforcement of the entire administrative state without strain. The asymmetry governs the front door as well: the Court's standing rulings stripped damages claims from class members whose information had never been released, and its speech rulings granted pre-enforcement review to a website designer no same-sex couple had actually asked to serve. The pattern is not an inference from one case. It is the documented record, and the board is asking the Court to widen it.

This is a rung, not a case. *Loper Bright* overruled Chevron deference — the courts' forty-year practice of deferring to an agency's reading of its own statute. The major-questions canon demands that Congress speak with a clarity it rarely achieves before an agency may act on a question of vast economic or political significance. *Jarkesy* moved penalties out of the agencies' courtrooms. *Sun Valley* would move labor enforcement through the same door. Each rung strips capacity from the same enforcement apparatus; each lands on the actions that reach employers and financial firms; and the conservative legal movement's litigation pipeline supplied the earlier vehicles while the Court supplied the doctrine. Joseph Fishkin and David Pozen named the pattern before this docket filled it in: asymmetric constitutional hardball, 118 Colum. L. Rev. 915 (2018) — process arguments deployed with a consistent beneficiary, hardball played in one direction only. Justice Gorsuch's separate opinion in *Jarkesy* already sketched the next rung: shrink the public-rights exception to the matters the founding generation treated as public — customs and immigration among them. *Sun Valley* tests that sketch against the enforcement of contracts with workers the government itself imported.

A bill of more than $550,000 was computed from kitchens, wages, and contract terms. The kitchen and the end date are not regulatory trivia. They are the terms the government required the farm to promise before it imported the labor, and the Department's case enforced the promise. The H-2A program binds each worker to a single petitioning employer: lose the job and he loses the housing the employer provides, the return trip the employer owes when the contract ends, and the legal basis of his presence in the country. Moving to the neighboring farm takes a new federal petition approving the move. A guest worker cannot realistically haul a New Jersey farm into federal court. The agency proceeding the board derides was the only forum in which those contract promises carried any enforcement the workers could actually reach. The worker's protection was the agency's docket. What the extension relocates is the enforcement of contracts with people who cannot enforce them. The board's verb for the tribunal that upheld the charges is "rubber-stamped." Its verb for the same agency enforcing the contracts is "dunned." It mourns the farm, and across the whole editorial the workers appear only as contract terms: a visa program, a wage line, a kitchen clause, an end date. The board never weighs their side. The page that ran the editorial pledges the paper to "free markets and free people." The market here was made of contracts; the free people were holding them; and the constitutional principle arrived only when enforcement reached the employer's signature line.

Here the question is genuinely contested in the working bar. The public-rights doctrine's boundaries have been unsettled since the founding, *Jarkesy* left the map undrawn, and practitioners read *Sun Valley* both ways: some see visa-condition enforcement as a public right through and through, and some see the presumption the Chief announced doing the work the Third Circuit said it does. The Justice Department defends the public-rights reading and offers a fallback — the farm volunteered for the program and its conditions. Reasonable jurists divide, and the argument is Tuesday. But mark what the project does at any width. Routing agency penalties into federal litigation means years of motion practice — procedural fighting before any trial — and six-figure costs against an agency built for summary proceedings. Enforcement comes slower, costlier, and thinner. The agency stops bringing cases. The contract provisions stop costing anyone anything. A rule the government can no longer afford to enforce is a rule that no longer exists.

The objection here is not to juries, to Article III courts, or to businesses winning in them. It is to a guarantee that changes sides with the defendant. The honest application of the jury principle the board declines to print is not mysterious. A Court that believed the Seventh Amendment stands between citizens and stacked tribunals would have started where the juries actually disappear — at the arbitration clauses, signed under conditions of take-it-or-leave-it, that its own precedents carry from the kitchen table into a private conference room. If the Seventh Amendment is what its text says — a preserved guarantee in suits at common law — it is not a shield an employer may raise against a regulator while stripping the same guarantee from his own employees by contract.

Chief Justice Roberts wrote the *Jarkesy* opinion the board celebrates. Justice Gorsuch wrote *Epic Systems*. Set side by side, the two opinions are the pattern in miniature: the Seventh Amendment arrives as a shield for the defendant with counsel and departs as a rumor for the plaintiff holding an arbitration clause. The board demands the first half of that arrangement and prints nothing about the second. That silence is the tell. That is the entry I take in my running file today: a Seventh Amendment revival that runs one way. In this Court's majestic equality, employer and worker alike are promised a jury trial — the employer, when the government comes for him; the worker, when the employer breaches. The Wall Street Journal is helping employers bury the labor law — and the board has scheduled the burial for the one forum its beneficiaries can reach and the law's intended beneficiaries cannot: the federal courthouse.
