The Canadians are getting good at the rhetoric of strength. They are getting worse at hiding the circumstances that make the rhetoric necessary.

François-Philippe Champagne, Canada's finance minister, told the BBC that Britain should "team up" with a proposed Canada–European Union economic alliance. The proposal would give Canada an unprecedented form of "associate membership" in the EU — not a normal trade deal, not ordinary partnership, but a status the bloc has never offered any country. Read that again. The first time. Ever.

This is the sound of the old order cracking. The Anglosphere — that convenient postwar fiction in which English-speaking countries forever march in lockstep behind Washington — is splitting at the seams, and Canada just picked up a chisel.

The trigger is obvious. American tariffs hit long-standing allies in a sweeping global campaign. Bilateral trade talks between Washington and Ottawa collapsed in August. Canada retaliated dollar for dollar, tariff for tariff. Champagne called the response "measured, targeted and strategic." He called the wider moment "a moment of great opportunities."

He is not wrong. He is simply describing an opportunity created by a breakdown as if it were the plan all along.

It is worth saying out loud what the press releases will not. Champagne is pitching a "middle powers" bloc because the alternative — staying the course with the United States under the terms now on offer — has already collapsed. A strategic country picks its fights. A cornered one responds in kind because the only move left is the move that proves it still has moves.

Carney's government has wrapped the same retreat in grander language. Prime Minister Mark Carney, the former Governor of the Bank of England who built his reputation inside the very Western financial order Washington is now renegotiating, welcomed von der Leyen's proposal as soon as it landed. He then [pitched the arrangement to the European Parliament as a defense of sovereignty](/articles/2026-09-17-carney-pitches-eu-partnership-as-sovereignty-defense-at-european-parliament/). The pitch includes Canadian critical minerals and gas, European artificial-intelligence and defense funding, scientific research, university ties, and arrangements allowing young Canadians and Europeans to study and live across the Atlantic. [The proposal is being sold as an alliance of the future](/articles/2026-09-17-carney-welcomes-eu-proposal-for-canada-associate-membership/).

This is not merely a trading arrangement. It is a parallel civilizational project — or, more precisely, an attempt to build one before the old arrangement leaves Canada outside in the cold. What Carney did not promise is that any of it replaces access to the American market. He promised that it sounds like something that might.

Champagne was frank in the way politicians are when the audience is foreign. The trade war, he said, was not one Canada chose. Of course it wasn't. A country choosing a trade war picks the moment and the terms. A country defending its "economic sovereignty" after the fact is a country that watched the moment pass and is now describing the response as the plan.

Canada "stood up for our industries, our workers, our country," Champagne said. At some stage, he added, "you have to say 'Enough is enough.'" That is the cadence of a government explaining a year to its voters, not a government that saw the move coming.

The performance of sovereignty is everywhere. Carney invited King Charles to open the Canadian Parliament in Ottawa, a move widely read as a rebuff to Washington's repeated suggestion that Canada could become the fifty-first state. The symbolism is not subtle. The substance is harder to find.

Britain's role in this picture should worry Whitehall. Champagne wants London in because the math improves with a third "middle power" at the table, and because Britain's post-Brexit identity crisis makes it uniquely available for recruitment. The UK renegotiated its own trade deal with Washington in June of last year, only to watch presidential rhetoric shift on the Falklands and Irish unity in the weeks since. Canada now wants the country that traded access for distance from Brussels to trade distance for access to Brussels.

And now Champagne is telling London: get in or get left behind.

The choreography is busy. Carney met Prime Minister Andy Burnham in Liverpool. Champagne sat beside Chancellor John Healey at a meeting of EU finance ministers in Dublin on Friday. The geography is changing under British feet. There is no associate-membership deal on paper. There are no settled terms. There is the promise that being inside the room with Brussels is itself the win.

Donald Trump called the underlying plan "hostile" and "laughable." He called Canada a "terrible trading partner" and threatened further tariffs on the EU if the proposal advances. At least two of those descriptions cannot both be true.

So here is where I part company with the column I would have written last year, and with the Canadian government that needs to hear it. The breakdown with Washington is real. The impulse to build elsewhere is the right one. But what is on the table right now is not a third pole. It is a trade deal dressed in sovereignty clothes, and if the middle powers want the alliance to mean something — to actually deliver the security and prosperity they are promising their publics — the terms have to be built from the ground up on different foundations than the ones that failed.

Start with what a social-democratic middle-powers arrangement would actually require, and compare it to what Champagne is describing. The current proposal talks about critical minerals, artificial-intelligence funding, research partnerships, and youth mobility. That is a shopping list. An alliance with teeth would attach enforceable labor standards to every market-access provision — not voluntary guidelines, not aspirational charters, but binding conditions that say a Canadian lithium mine or a European semiconductor fab gets preferential access only if its workers earn a living wage, organize freely, and work under safety standards that match the values Champagne keeps invoking. The Danes have a word for this: flexicurity. The principle is simple. You make it easy to restructure, and you make sure nobody gets destroyed in the restructuring. An alliance of "middle powers" that lowers standards to compete with a protectionist United States is not a third pole. It is a race to the bottom with better branding.

Second, the research and energy-transit provisions cannot be structured as ordinary procurement — contracts awarded to the cheapest bidder, whoever that happens to be. The whole point of public investment is to build capacity that the market will not build on its own. That means joint public funds for clean-energy deployment and critical-mineral processing, governed by the kind of democratic oversight that the Nordic sovereign-wealth model provides: transparent, politically accountable, invested for long-term public return rather than short-term corporate profit. Norway turned oil revenue into a two-trillion-dollar fund that owns a slice of the global economy and mails the dividend to the future. Canada and the EU could do the same with the critical-mineral revenues and research funding that are the centrepiece of this arrangement — but only if the governance is public and the returns are shared. Carney, of all people, understands the mechanics. The question is whether he will build the institution or just describe the aspiration.

Third, the youth-mobility provisions — "cross the ocean and be able to study and live," as Carney put it — need real rights attached, not the administrative obstacle course that currently governs working-holiday visas and post-study employment. Real mobility means portable qualifications, mutual recognition of professional credentials, and work permits that do not expire the moment the employer relationship ends. The European Union's freedom of movement works because it is a right, not a privilege administered by a bureaucracy that can revoke it at will. If the middle-powers model offers young Canadians and Britons something that looks like European mobility but functions like a temporary visa, it will produce resentment instead of solidarity, and the alliance will hollow out from the inside the way every arrangement does when its benefits accrue to firms while its costs fall on people.

Fourth — and this is the part no one wants to say because it makes the diplomacy harder — the alliance needs a democratic architecture, not a circuit of summits. Champagne and Carney are building this through bilateral ministerial meetings and back-channel coordination. That is how trade deals are made. It is not how democratic alliances are built. A middle-powers pole that answers to finance ministers and heads of government, without parliamentary oversight, without structured labor representation at the negotiating table, without civil-society participation in the design of the terms, will produce an arrangement that serves the people in the room. The Saltsjöbaden Agreement — the 1938 handshake that founded the Nordic model — worked because both sides brought their members to the table and both sides had to go home and sell the deal. The Canada-EU arrangement needs the same architecture: tripartite bargaining structures that put organized labor and employer organizations across from each other with the state refereeing, not a closed loop of officials exchanging communiqués.

Here is the honest assessment. The third pole is the right project. The need is real. A world in which Washington can impose unilateral tariffs on its closest allies and expect compliance is a world in which no middle power is safe, and the instinct to build collective resilience is not retreat — it is the only rational response. But the instinct alone does not make the institution. Without enforceable labor standards, the alliance is a cost-cutting exercise. Without public investment governance, it is a procurement pipeline. Without real mobility rights, it is a youth-exchange programme with a press release. Without democratic architecture, it is a set of gentlemen's agreements that the next election in any of the member countries can dissolve.

Champagne says the world has changed and America has changed. He is right. The question is whether Canada and its potential partners will build the institutions that match the moment, or whether they will produce a communiqué that matches the photo opportunity. The old order is cracking. What gets built in the gap is a choice — and it is a choice that belongs to parliaments, unions, and citizens, not to finance ministers exchanging pleasantries in Dublin.

Anyway. Build the thing properly or don't build it at all. The middle powers do not get to choose the moment. They only get to choose whether the institution they build when they arrive is worth the trip.
