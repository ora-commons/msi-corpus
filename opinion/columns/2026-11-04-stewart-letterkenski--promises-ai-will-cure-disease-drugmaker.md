Dario Amodei sells miracle cures while no AI-designed drug has FDA clearance.

The forecast arrived. The FDA ledger did not. In September, Amodei posted on X: “I think it will actually be possible to cure most human disease in ~5-10 years, as crazy as it may sound.” A post is not a trial, a mechanism, or a regulatory submission. It is, however, a priceable claim. The present wrong is not that a future model must fail. It is that a forecast is being financed as though the present already contained a medicine.

It is true that the tools are useful. They can help select targets, design experimental medicines more quickly, refine candidates, and identify patients most likely to benefit from a treatment. AI can make each candidate cheaper to examine, but when safety prediction fails downstream, faster search merely enlarges the queue of molecules awaiting proof. The trouble is that none of those verbs is *cure*. A ranked list is not a therapeutic effect; a predicted binding is not a safety record; a molecule that performs in a laboratory assay is not a medicine that improves a human life at a tolerable dose. The industry has placed several different products under one label and then sold the highest-priced version of the label.

Here it is worth being precise about what “AI” actually is in this setting, because public discourse has the misleading habit of treating a model as a thing rather than a collection of trained statistical systems, ranking functions, and data pipelines. The model can propose candidates, rank them, and estimate patterns in existing records. It does not follow a patient through a trial, perform the laboratory test, or bear an adverse event—an unintended harm to a patient. Its output is conditional on its training data, its objective function—a rule telling it what pattern to optimize—and its validation set—the data reserved for checking it. Call it a machine for producing hypotheses. Calling it an oracle would be a costly way to avoid writing down the specification.

Vertex Pharmaceuticals supplied the least glamorous and most useful control experiment. It used roughly 50 AI companies to search its dataset of 10,000 drug compounds. Not one generated a working drug. Jeff Leiden said AI “just doesn’t work by itself”; recalling a technology executive’s claim that AI would cure 90% of human disease within seven years, he called the premise “complete BS.” This was not one model producing the wrong answer. The search was spread across 50 vendors and 10,000 compounds, and the output remained a set of compounds rather than a working drug. The search was distributed. The proof obligation was not.

The money is arriving faster than the evidence. McKinsey’s data show that venture-capital and private-equity firms invested $8.4 billion globally in AI drug discovery in 2025, up from roughly $4 billion in 2023. Capital did not wait for a therapy. It paid for the promise first. Only a few AI-native biotechnology companies, including Insilico Medicine and Generate Biomedicines, have advanced drugs into late-stage patient testing, and no AI-designed drug has won FDA clearance. As Alex Devereson of McKinsey put it, “Investment is flowing to the problems that are easiest to solve, not the problems that are most important.” The easy problems here are literature mining, target ranking, workflow automation, and trial matching. The important problem is whether a proposed treatment changes a disease without creating a worse one.

The scale of the wager is visible in MSI’s related analysis of [an AI sector needing 9% of US GDP in revenue by 2032](/articles/2026-10-02-ai-sector-needs-9-of-us-gdp-in-revenue-by-2032-analysis-finds/). Whether that forecast is right is a forecasting question. The FDA ledger is a regulatory question. Biology supplies the answer—or fails to. The distance between the two is where the miracle cure lives.

The word “racket” is doing specific work here. It does not mean that the software is useless. It means that a business can sell, license, acquire, and finance a cure thesis before the medicine has cleared the regulator. The largest technology companies are acting on the forecast. Anthropic bought a stealth-mode biotechnology company earlier this year and recently bought a one-person company operating almost entirely with AI. OpenAI announced last month that it had bought an AI drug-discovery biotechnology company. Eli Lilly and Nvidia said in January that they would invest up to $1 billion over five years in AI applications, including drug discovery and robotics; Bristol Myers Squibb, Novo Nordisk and Sanofi announced partnerships with OpenAI or Anthropic.

A one-person laboratory may be a clever way to test a thesis; either way, it is not a substitute for the laboratory and clinical teams required to establish safety. The acquisitions buy optionality, talent, and exposure to a future capability, not a verified treatment. A portfolio of attempts can support a healthy market for tools even when none becomes medicine.

Follow the benefit and the arrangement becomes plain. Model vendors sell access to their systems; drugmakers buy a faster search and a more persuasive account of their pipeline; investors acquire exposure to a category that arrives pre-labelled as medicine. None of those transactions requires the patient to be treated now. The patient waits; the option does not. That is a mismatch between the return clock of private capital and the biological clock required for a drug to become a treatment. The market can collect on the effort, the infrastructure, and the forecast. The patient cannot collect on the forecast.

That mismatch is old, even if the vocabulary is new: capital buys a technical upgrade, workers absorb the cost of the transition, and the equipment’s productivity is credited as a self-acting upgrade. What is different is the scale, and the ease with which software can make an unfinished biological claim look like a finished technical product.

From my family’s recollection, my father spent thirty years on the bar mill at Manitoba Rolling Mills. When Gerdau bought the mill in 1995, he kept his job; several uncles did not. The mill was modernized, output was reorganized, and the transaction’s social consequences were allocated to the people least able to refuse them. The biotech mechanism is not identical, and I am not trying to make it so. The narrower point is the same: capital can modernize the financing of discovery while deciding, from outside the laboratory, whose employment, money, and risk absorb the uncertainty.

It is true that some applications are valuable, and even boring in the best sense. AstraZeneca signed a deal with Insitro worth $40 million up front to discover new heart and weight-loss drugs, signed with Oracle to streamline patient recruitment, and opened a research site in Cambridge, Massachusetts, that uses AI to speed experiments. Bayer’s gene-therapy unit used AI to examine 100 million patient records in days and identify five diseases suited to Bayer’s gene-editing technology.

Those are real reductions in search cost. They are not cures. The trouble is that “AI in drug discovery” bundles at least four different jobs: finding a target, proposing a molecule, predicting how that molecule will behave inside a body, and finding the people in whom to test it. Success in one job does not demonstrate the others. A system that ranks records or recruits trial participants has not thereby shown that it can design a safe drug. A system that proposes a plausible molecule has not thereby shown that the molecule will work. Bayer’s system identified diseases that appeared suited to a particular gene-editing technology; it did not edit the genes, establish safety, or cure a patient. An agreement with Oracle could shorten enrolment. It cannot eliminate adverse events or demonstrate efficacy.

A proposed molecule remains a candidate, and “candidate” is not a disappointing word invented by regulators; it is the accurate description of its evidentiary stage. Calling the system superintelligence changes the scale of the processor, not the status of the result.

Here is where precision matters, because “off-target effect” is usually treated in public discussion as though it were a mildly inconvenient diagnostic result. It means a medicine interacts with something it was not designed to affect. McKinsey estimates that around a quarter of failures in mid- to late-stage drug development can be traced to safety problems caused by such effects, and that current models struggle to predict them. That is not a minor output error. It is a molecule doing something else inside a human body.

A clinical trial is therefore not paperwork performed after the model has finished. It is the model’s test. The software can narrow the field, propose an answer, or identify patients, but the evidence arrives through controlled intervention in living systems, which remain less cooperative than the training data. Increasing the size of the model changes the amount of computation used to make a prediction. It does not change the evidentiary status of the prediction. More candidates may improve the odds, but only if someone has the capacity to test them. Otherwise the output is a backlog of hypotheses, not a pipeline of medicines.

The defect in the miracle-cure claims is not that they lack confidence. It is a category error: bounded capability gains are treated as proof of unbounded capacity. The defect has a name—capability-gain begging-the-question. A narrow result becomes evidence for a conclusion it cannot bear. Vertex searched 10,000 compounds and produced no working drug; that does not prove AI cannot help drug discovery. It proves that search is not proof. Calling AI a personless force is a commercial trick. It hides the scientists, laboratory results, and practical judgements required to turn a model’s output into a candidate worth testing. The personless noun makes a service supplied by a laboratory appear to be a medicine supplied by a computer, then turns control over models, data, and computing power into an asset acquisition presented as a scientific contribution. Nature did not supply the business model, even if nature supplied the molecules.

The people running these companies are less expansive than the capital raising around them. Pascal Soriot of AstraZeneca said, “To say AI is going to cure cancer is maybe a little bit of a jump.” George Yancopoulos of Regeneron said AI is not going to “miraculously come in and tell us how to do things and cut things from 15 years to five years.” Sebastian Guth of Bayer was more diplomatic: “The magic happens when you marry the brilliance of human scientists with AI.” The important word in Guth’s formulation is not “magic.” It is “marry.” The model contributes speed, pattern recognition, and search; human scientists contribute hypotheses about cause and effect, the design of laboratory tests, judgement about what the model may have missed, and responsibility for what happens when a prediction looks good in a dataset and fails in a body.

There is a mirror-image failure in criticism. Criticising a technology by parroting its promoters’ claims establishes nothing. A critic who says “no drug yet” has not shown that no useful tool exists; a promoter who says “most disease soon” has not shown that a molecule exists. The useful work is to mark the stage: target, molecule, laboratory result, animal result, human result, regulatory result. Each is evidence. None substitutes for the next.

As a general description of this drug-discovery system, AI is logistical and extractive rather than a disembodied mind. It includes proprietary datasets, laboratory robots, compute contracts, wet-lab scientists, trial sites, patients, and a regulatory file. The model is one component in that chain, and often not the component doing the decisive work. Which is why “AI-designed” cannot be a sufficient description of a medicine. The label needs provenance: which model, which data, which proposed molecule, which experiment, which human result.

For a Canadian research-funding strategy, the bargain must be visible. Universities, hospitals, and health systems can supply data, expertise, trial participants, and public trust. If the model, data rights, and commercial option remain private while the public system carries the research and health costs, publicly funded research becomes another word for the absence of a bargain.

Regulators have recently proposed new frameworks for AI use in regulatory submissions and trial-data analysis. Those frameworks can improve traceability, but they cannot supply missing biological evidence or solve the hard parts of clinical development, including recruiting enough of the right patients and monitoring them throughout a study. Nor can a disclosure rule about model use substitute for an FDA finding that a drug works and is sufficiently safe. The policy apparatus is catching up with the paperwork before it has caught up with the biology.

Regulators should make the evidence boundary hard to cross. Every promotional or regulatory claim should identify whether the underlying evidence is computational, laboratory-based, animal-based, observational, or human clinical. Models used to select a target or candidate should face independent validation on data not used to build them, with off-target effects and performance across patient groups disclosed. The federal government should require AI-drug developers to disclose the model’s actual contribution, the training-data provenance they can lawfully disclose, the stage and identifier of every clinical test, and the null results as well as the successful ones. Common, machine-readable trial registries and independent validation programmes would make it harder to convert a promising output into a therapeutic claim by presentation alone. Canadian research agencies should tie public money to reproducible results and clinical outcomes rather than demonstrations another vendor can reproduce next quarter.

These are not rules against computation. They are rules against letting a forecast inherit the authority of a finding.

Until that record exists, the honest description of current capability is assistance to researchers, not autonomous disease cures. The burden belongs on the promoters because they are asking the public to finance their forecast before biology has returned a verdict.

The FDA’s ledger currently says zero. That is the most honest sentence in the industry. No AI-designed drug has cleared it, and no one has yet carried the miracle claim through the process required to call it a medicine. A model can be useful, a laboratory can be clever, and a venture fund can finance an option; none of those is a cure. Until an AI-designed drug clears the full path of safety, efficacy, and FDA review, the cure boom is selling a business model with chemistry attached. The miracle is sitting in the investor deck, where miracles are unusually comfortable and require no control arm.
