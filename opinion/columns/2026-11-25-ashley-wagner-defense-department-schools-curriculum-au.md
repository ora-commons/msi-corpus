Pete Hegseth is using military children as test subjects for a political curriculum.

There are about 70,000 of them in roughly 160 Defense Department Education Activity schools across 11 countries. They are the children of active-duty military and civilian service members. For military families, the address can be an order, the moving truck can be an order, and the school can arrive with both. Of the school systems serving American children, this is the one most directly assembled by military assignment rather than parental choice—and the department chose it for a system-wide curriculum overhaul run by hand-picked, no-bid contractors. The warfighters are not the subjects here. Their children are.

The invoice came in October. The Pentagon awarded American Education Solutions $5.7 million to audit curriculum, instruction, and professional learning across DoDEA without soliciting bids. Separately, it gave Iliad Athletics just over $100,000 to pilot classical physical education at LeJeune High School and Quantico Middle High School, also without competitive bids. A November 25 Associated Press report says Hegseth sought to use the schools as models for a broader conservative education agenda: restrictions on instruction about race and gender, patriotic values, classical learning. The department says the audit complies with a Trump executive order limiting how schools teach race and gender. The audit is how a model becomes a template.

My children are in Philadelphia, not DoDEA schools. I am a civilian; nobody in my house has ever deployed. We have two incomes, a combined $8,800 a month after tax, $2,400 a month in child care, and a 7 percent mortgage. David’s grandmother’s estate helped us buy the Fishtown rowhouse. The estate help and two-income structure are real privileges. They do not make my household representative of military families; they give me one clean kitchen-table ruler.

At $8,800 a month, $5.7 million equals 648 months of our take-home pay. It also equals 2,375 months of our daycare bill—nearly two centuries. The $100,000 pilot equals about eleven and a half months of net pay, money that would have had to cover rent, child care, food, utilities, and the mortgage. The Pentagon says that price bought a pilot in fitness, virtue, and historical character. At the household level, that is what it costs to make military children carry the first draft of a national policy.

The same building has now [failed its ninth financial audit](/articles/2026-11-17-pentagon-fails-ninth-financial-audit-as-more-units-earn-clean-opinions/), even as more individual units earned clean opinions. The books do not audit clean. The curriculum gets a $5.7 million no-bid audit. Two employees of the Pentagon’s education division said a system-wide review by a consultant would be without precedent; the schools typically work with a national accreditor to evaluate and develop subject standards. That is not routine review.

Jordan Adams founded American Education Solutions. He graduated from Hillsdale College and worked in K-12 curriculum development there, including on the Trump-aligned 1776 Project, which Hillsdale created in response to what it called the “weaponization of history.” The project has been criticized for downplaying slavery and describing Jamestown as a failed communist project. Adams also consulted with conservative-led school boards in Pennsylvania and Texas.

American Education Solutions calls existing curricula “content-light” for employing pedagogical practices it says the science of learning does not support and for eschewing the patriotic civic role of public education. It sells an audit to ensure schools “Do No Harm,” including respect for parents as first educators. His company has now been paid millions to audit a military school system for patriotic civic alignment. An auditor whose sales page already contains the findings is not conducting a review. It is delivering a product. An auditor with a published doctrine of history is not a neutral referee. It is a worldview with a billing address.

The P.E. pilot completes the method. Running, jumping, climbing, carrying, and throwing: good. A weekly virtue and historical figures chosen to embody it: conservative catechism in gym clothes. Iliad CEO Patrick Whalen previously worked in the office of Hillsdale’s president.

The department says Iliad was the only company able to meet the requirements at the time. It says, “The War Department always looks for solutions to benefit the warfighter, not any pre-selected entity,” and that outside affiliations, investors, or political connections “play absolutely no role” in its funding decisions. A sole-source award establishes only that the department did not run a competitive search; it does not establish that no alternative existed. “Only company able” is not a market finding when no one was permitted to bid.

None of this is isolated. Earlier this year, [DoDEA schools added a Western Civilization course with an Encounter Books textbook](/articles/2026-08-14-dodea-schools-to-add-western-civilization-course-with-encounter-books-textbook/). The textbook includes suggested lesson plans developed in partnership with Hillsdale College. Scholars who examined it said it presents Western civilization and Christianity as the same thing while selectively framing Islam as a violent threat. Early in Trump’s second administration, defense schools removed books addressing race, gender, and democracy from their libraries. The ACLU sued and won partial relief from the bans at several schools.

Hegseth’s longstanding interest in classical education—especially a version incorporating Christian history and virtues—is now institutional machinery. The military academies accept the Classic Learning Test, which bills itself as a classical alternative to the SAT. In September, defense schools announced 100 vouchers for students to take it; this month, the agency made it widely available. Matthew Kirby, the new leader of the school system, founded and formerly ran Liberty Classical Schools, an Atlanta charter network.

First came book removals; then the replacement narrative got a textbook; now an ideological contractor is paid to audit the result. That sequence is policy: remove the contest, install the narrative, review the installation.

Jennifer Berkshire and Jack Schneider have spent years documenting the education wars as a deliberate, decades-long political project—not a culture war that happens to land on schools, but a strategy that runs through them. Look at the pieces together: one ideological network’s college, curriculum vendors, test, textbook, and executives installed in a school system assigned by military orders. The principle of parental choice stops precisely where the contracts begin.

School choice is supposed to be part of the broader model. This contract has no choice in it. Public money flows to private operators without competitive bids; children carry the outcomes. Follow who pays: the benefit concentrates in firms connected by one college and one political project, while the diffuse cost lands on children at Quantico and LeJeune. DoDEA is concentrating money, curriculum control, and ideological conformity through contractors the Pentagon selected without competition.

Anne Helen Petersen’s introduction to *Can’t Even* makes the structural point about burnout: it “isn’t a personal problem,” and will not be cured by a productivity app or a face mask. The Pentagon’s system uses the same inversion. Concentrated control over curriculum is recast as a family-formation burden, and the bill lands on the caregivers. Parents are named “first educators” and then discover that the curriculum, auditor, textbook, and pilot were selected above them. The two Pentagon education employees who described the review as unprecedented spoke anonymously because they feared retribution. The system has been optimized for ideological consistency, and the bill lands on the people doing the caring.

My Catholic formation at St. Stanislaus in Lansdale treated education as a shared communal responsibility; disagreement was part of it. I owe twelve years of Catholic schooling in Lansdale for whatever formation I actually have. Families, teachers, and parish volunteers all had a role. My parents could afford parish formation on a postal supervisor’s income in that economy; my two-income household cannot assume the same formation carries forward.

That formation did not need a $100,000 pilot to teach children to carry things. Volunteers, teachers, and families shared the unglamorous work, including funeral lunches and quiet rent assistance when a job disappeared. A virtue is what gets practiced, not a contractor’s line item. The Pentagon replaces shared accountability with vendor management: one company audits the curriculum, another supplies the virtue program, and parents are invoked after the fact as “first educators.” What the Pentagon is buying is the costume of a tradition, tailored by vendors and hung on children whose parents are required by orders to be somewhere else. Community responsibility has been downgraded to customer feedback.

DoDEA’s curriculum project is organized to prevent that discovery. It wants agreement to arrive before the questions do.

Read diagnostically, Taylor Swift’s *You’re On Your Own, Kid* names the mission statement American care infrastructure runs on: leave care to the family. The song knows the safety net that survives is lateral—the friends in the group text, the other kids who also started over at a new school midyear. That is the net military children have as the adults keep leaving: deployed, reassigned, and now paid contractors deciding what stays on the shelves. The children are carrying the first draft of a national policy in books, gym classes, and audits.

A genuinely pro-family education policy would make the household math work: affordable child care, paid leave, a functioning public school, and procurement transparent enough that families can see what is being bought and why; honoring parents as first educators means evidence, meaningful say, and real choice. Future contracts should face competition, the Pentagon should publish the requirements and procurement record if Iliad truly was the only possible provider, and curriculum evaluation should return to the national accreditor its own staff says normally handles it. And the Center on Budget and Policy Priorities estimated that making the expanded 2021 Child Tax Credit permanent would lift 4.3 million children out of poverty annually, a reduction of more than 40 percent—doing more for a military child’s formation than another virtue of the week.

At eleven, I close the spreadsheet; in DoDEA classrooms, the manuscript stays open. Picture the student this apparatus is for: a child walking into the school the government’s orders chose for her, past shelves thinned of books about race, gender, and democracy, toward a gym class where a contractor has stapled a virtue to the calisthenics. She did not sign up for any of it. Neither did her parents. In this story, the only people who chose are the ones who got paid.

We should not mistake a polished, expensive, patriotic package for education. Ask who wrote it, who paid for it, and why military children were made to test it before the rest of us were asked to accept it.
