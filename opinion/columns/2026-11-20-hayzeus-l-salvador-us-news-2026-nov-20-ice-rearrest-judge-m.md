ICE defied court orders by re-arresting immigrants the courts had released.

Who holds power does not change what the court order means. The person does. Our own complicity belongs in this moral accounting. In the parishes and neighborhoods where I have worked, we have sometimes treated undocumented neighbors as paperwork, and I have shared that failure. We have also accepted an economy that benefits when some workers are too afraid to demand their rights. We have not all escaped that bargain, and I have not escaped it either. That complicity does not excuse ICE, and it does not place the officer and the immigrant in the same place. I hold every administration to the same rule: no agency may nullify a lawful release because its own policy calls for recapture.

US District Judge Charlotte Sweeney found government officials in contempt in Colorado after ICE immediately re-arrested a man she had ordered released. In her Wednesday order, she wrote: “ICE’s new ‘policy’ of immediately re-detaining petitioners following release made a mockery of court orders mandating petitioners’ immediate release.” This was not a bureaucratic misstep. I call it cruelty organized as policy. In Colorado, the order was simple: release him.

At least 18 people in Colorado had been held in detention longer than they should have been or had been re-arrested within minutes of a judge-ordered release over the preceding six weeks, CPR News reported. Immigration attorneys estimated the number may be higher because some immigrants represented themselves. Some were re-arrested days later. Some had not even left the detention center. Legal advocates said the pattern was noticeable in recent months, was rising, and often violated federal court orders. These are not procedural footnotes. Each is a person whose release was delayed, reversed, or followed by another arrest. The reported number is a floor, not a ceiling.

You had the order. You had the person in custody. You knew the judge required immediate release. You re-detained the person anyway. A government agency cannot make obedience to law optional, and it cannot call defiance a policy. The agency responsible for enforcing the law cannot become the party that decides which orders it will obey.

The practice was not one confused officer at a counter. The judge called it a policy. It happened inside the immigration system, after a federal judge had done the court’s work. This was not enforcement in compliance with the judge’s order. Release became the trigger for another arrest.

The Torah is blunt: “You shall not oppress the alien” and “love the alien as yourself,” because “for you were aliens in the land of Egypt” (Leviticus 19:33–34). Jesus makes the demand personal: “I was a stranger and you did not welcome me” (Matthew 25:43). A court order releasing a stranger from ICE custody is the beginning of welcome, not an obstacle to it. ICE took the person back into custody. That is the opposite of both texts. To seize the person again after that order is to place the agency above both the stranger and the law.

ICE is the enforcement face of Donald Trump’s immigration crackdown. The administration says its actions advance domestic security and comply with law. The stated aim does not change what happened. A judge ordered release. ICE re-detained the person. The agency was not complying with that order; it was manufacturing detention. Security is not permission to defy a release order. Compliance cannot mean that the court orders release and ICE rearrests the person anyway. The administration can pursue lawful enforcement. It cannot make recapture lawful by naming it policy.

This is not confined to one courthouse. Earlier, [ICE arrested a man a day after a judge barred ICE arrests at New York immigration courts](/articles/2026-05-19-man-arrested-day-after-judge-bans-ice-arrests-at-nyc-immigration-courts/). A different city does not change the inversion. The court says no; the enforcement arm proceeds anyway. Each new arrest after release shows how the detention system can defeat a court order at the last step.

Judge Sweeney declined to take further action because an official swore in court that officials had “cured the contempt by ceasing the practice at issue.” The sworn cessation ends the immediate practice. It cannot repair what was done or erase the completed wrong. One case produced the contempt finding. CPR News reported at least 18 incidents over six weeks; the broader claim that the practice is rising—and that some re-arrests violated court orders—still requires case-specific review and adjudication. That is not a reason to dismiss the harm. It is a reason to open the record. Every case in which release was delayed, reversed, or followed by another arrest must be identified and reviewed against the orders and detention records. Those responsible must answer for the harm. A promise to stop is not a pardon.

That repair must have a humane form. *Strangers No Longer*, the 2003 joint pastoral letter from the US and Mexican bishops, recognizes a sovereign right to control borders while insisting that migrants’ human rights and dignity be respected and that refugees and asylum seekers be protected. A just immigration order would make release mean release, require a new individualized legal basis before any re-detention, protect access to asylum and other legal process, and place the burden of lawful enforcement on the government; it would not treat detention as the default while a person waits for the government to finish deciding what to do with them. That means individualized hearings, meaningful access to counsel, independent judicial review, and enforcement measured by whether a person can trust the court—not by how quickly another arrest can follow the first release. A stranger’s vulnerability is never evidence against the stranger.

Every official and officer involved is still a person, with a conscience. In his March 23, 1980 homily, Óscar Romero told soldiers ordered to kill: “En nombre de Dios, pues, y en nombre de este sufrido pueblo, cuyos lamentos suben hasta el cielo cada día más tumultuosos, les suplico, les ruego, les ordeno: ¡cese la represión!” He addressed their conscience rather than pretending they were machinery. Stop the repression. End the practice. Repair what was broken. Let the next order mean what it says.

The door is open to every person in ICE who chooses a different way. Let the courthouse door open inward, with no officer waiting on the other side to pull the stranger back. Let the released person walk through it, and let the handcuffs stay behind.
