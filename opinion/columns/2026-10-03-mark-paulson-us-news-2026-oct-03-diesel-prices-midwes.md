The refineries broke, and Adams County is paying the bill at the pump. Last week I stood at the diesel rack at the co-op on the edge of Friendship and watched the number roll past what it was in June. Down the road, Curt Voss of the Town of Rome has four tractors and two combines turning soybeans under and roughly 200 gallons going out of the truck every working day. He told me the same thing the Guardian reported this week from Delaware County, Indiana — that the meter starts around 9,000 gallons and doesn't stop counting until the ground is clean.

The Guardian's reporting put a number on what that costs across the corn belt: diesel is more than $3 a gallon above where it was a year ago, and estimates put the added fuel bill at roughly $12,500 for every 1,000 acres a farmer harvests this fall. Run that math on a 1,000-acre Adams County soybean ground and it comes out of a farm's gross before seed, before chemicals, before the equipment loan. Four of the five states with the biggest weekly diesel price jumps in September were Illinois, Michigan, Ohio and Indiana — but the pump that moved those numbers moved ours too, because crude and diesel don't stop at state lines.

That's the part worth understanding: no refinery serves this end of Wisconsin. Fuel comes in on trucks from terminals up toward Minneapolis and Green Bay, and when a regional pool tightens, the dealers at the end of the line feel it first and feel it longest. The Guardian's national reporting describes the same structure for the midwest as a whole — fewer refineries, fewer ports, a thinner pipeline, no quick way to bring more fuel in by tanker when something breaks. "It's hard for the midwest to respond," says Kurt Lykins, a lecturer at Otterbein University. "It's hard for the midwest to respond" is a sentence you can say standing at the Friendship co-op as easily as in a lecture hall in Ohio.

The specific triggers are documented, and they are not weather. Last month a power outage and flooding at the ExxonMobil refinery in Joliet, Illinois, took the plant offline for more than a week and removed more than 80 million gallons of diesel and gasoline from a regional pool that was already thin. In Whiting, Indiana, BP and a union have been locked in a six-month labor dispute at one of the largest refineries in the country, and the resulting downtime has rippled straight into the price at the pump. The Iran war did the rest, lifting crude and pulling diesel along with it. None of those three things happened in Wisconsin. All three of them land on Wisconsin fuel bills.

The Strategic Petroleum Reserve — the cushion that is supposed to be there for exactly this — is in worse shape than it has been in 43 years. The 714-million-barrel capacity held in the salt caverns of Texas and Louisiana is sitting on a 172-million-barrel drawdown, and Lykins is blunt about what that means: "Although the prices for gasoline and diesel have been rising, drawing down from the reserve has given some stability to prices." Stability bought on credit. A Government Accountability Office report from May found that several of the Reserve's Louisiana caverns may see their capacity reduced because of stability concerns in a neighboring cavern that was recently decommissioned. The cushion is shrinking from two sides at once.

Farmers are not the only ones holding the bill in this county. The Adams-Friendship school district runs its buses over gravel and section-line roads longer than any district this side of the Dells, and rural districts are the ones the Guardian's national reporting singles out as being hit hardest — the ones running the longest routes over the worst roads when diesel moves. A national survey in May found that almost 40 percent of school districts are consolidating bus routes to cut diesel costs, and 20 percent have already cut non-required trips. In Columbus, Ohio, the state's largest public school system, the diesel line item runs more than $70 million a year, and that district is looking at a projected $157 million deficit by 2031 — a number large enough to be a headline. A small rural district doesn't have a $70 million diesel line to reforecast. It has a levy, and the levy lands on the same farm tax bills that are already carrying the fuel increase. That's the translation nobody does in Washington: the district's diesel problem becomes the farmer's property-tax problem, and the farm's fuel problem becomes the district's budget problem, and both of them are the same number wearing two hats.

The administration has finally noticed the national picture. It is now backing a diesel export ban to keep more fuel inside the country, and the oil industry is already scrambling to kill the policy before it lands. The lobbying fight is real, but the policy itself is a tourniquet, not a cure. It might blunt the next spike. It will not refill the refineries, restock the strategic reserve, or restore labor peace at Whiting.

Adaptation is what's left, and the Guardian's farmer from Indiana is doing what a lot of growers in Adams County are doing — no-till and cover crops that keep the diesel-hungry machinery in the shed a few more days each season. Joe Hamilton in Delaware County, Indiana, runs 2,500 acres and uses between two and four gallons an acre now, far less than the full-tillage operation he used to run. "It's much less than we used when we were a full tillage operation," he says. "Farmers are price takers. It's not just fuel — it's chemicals, it's seed, it's the cost of equipment. Everything has increased along with the commodity prices." Soybeans are at $12.88 a bushel against $10.14 a year ago. Corn is at $5.23 against $4.22. The crop prices are higher. The fuel bill is higher still. An Indiana grower and a Wisconsin grower reading the same futures board and getting the same answer: the price went up for both of you, and the margin went to the people who set the price of fuel.

This is what a brittle fuel economy looks like when it arrives on a section-line road in the Town of Rome. A single refinery trip in Joliet, a single labor dispute at Whiting, a single foreign war — any one of them is enough to break a regional fuel market that was already running on the edge. Curt Voss doesn't care which one did it. The harvest goes on, the diesel gets burned, the levy notices come in March, and the bill lands on people who never set the price of any of it.

That's the story of this harvest, and it isn't a weather story or a war story in the way the cable channels will tell it. It's a refinery, a labor dispute, a depleted strategic reserve, and a policy debate in Washington that arrived after the field had already started paying. The county didn't get a soft landing. It got a fuel receipt, and it will be paying it off in school levies and equipment loans long after the combines are washed and put away.
