Jensen Huang and his industry are drinking America's water and calling it growth.

Consider the setting in which this proposition was tested on Tuesday night. At an election-night gathering at the San Francisco Opera House hosted by the Nvidia chief executive, industry executives in tuxedos watched *The Marriage of Figaro* and discussed the election returns as a threat to the build-out of computing capacity their businesses depend on, according to people at the party. One notes, without pressing the point, that *Figaro* is the opera in which the servants outmaneuver the count. By Wednesday morning, senior OpenAI leaders were discussing whether the results required resetting expectations for next year's data center capacity. The threat, in their accounting, was not the water table under Sunbury, Ohio. The threat was democracy, arriving on schedule.

Because democracy did arrive on schedule, and it did something specific. Voters in Ohio, Michigan, Texas and Georgia backed candidates who had campaigned against data center construction — Gina Hinojosa, elected Texas governor on a promise to end the state's "wild west" of development with the words "It stops when I'm governor," among them — and more than a dozen Ohio towns voted to halt or restrict new development outright, with similar bans passing in towns in Kansas, Florida and California. Senators-elect in Texas, Michigan and South Carolina had campaigned on new federal restrictions. A national survey by the polling firm SSRS found about 56 percent of voters calling data centers a crucial or major factor in their congressional choices. The repositioning had been visible for weeks — [candidates in four states spent October asking for tighter data-center approval rules](/articles/2026-10-26-candidates-in-four-states-seek-tighter-data-center-approval-rules/) — but Tuesday installed the constraint by ballot, in dozens of jurisdictions at once, by the people who pay the water bills. And this happened in Texas, Georgia and Ohio, the three states that hold, per Evercore ISI, more than 40 percent of all planned American data center capacity — the states where OpenAI's Stargate is under construction, where Project Camellia is drawn on the maps, where Meta's Prometheus is rising.

It is worth being precise about what, mechanically, a hyperscale data center is, because the public discussion keeps treating it as a cloud when it is a building: racks of processors that convert electricity into arithmetic and nearly all of their power draw into heat, cooled by water taken from the local system — the cheapest way to reject heat at scale is to evaporate it — or by chillers that take it indirectly, and fed by a grid connection measured in hundreds of megawatts, approaching the scale of a large reactor, in towns used to negotiating a few. The first pages of Kate Crawford's *Atlas of AI* insist on the point the ballots just made — "AI is made from vast amounts of natural resources, fuel, and human labor" — and the industry's own contracts concede the same point in the only currency it respects. Microsoft has signed a twenty-year deal with Constellation to restart Three Mile Island Unit 1 — 835 megawatts, accelerated to 2027 — and Meta has contracted for twenty years to take the entire 1.1-gigawatt output of Constellation's Clinton nuclear plant in Illinois, on terms Constellation announced in June 2025. A power purchase agreement is exactly what it sounds like: a decades-long claim on a plant's output, signed while the water, the land and the grid connections were still free to whoever asked first. Companies do not sign multi-decade nuclear contracts for office parks. They sign them when they have concluded, in the most binding instrument available to them, that their business is real-economy infrastructure with real-economy costs — and, as of Tuesday, real-economy politics. The voters did not need the book. They read the water table, the interconnection queue — the line a project waits in to hook itself to the grid — and the school next door, and priced the thing at its address.

The clearest statement of how the industry sees the public, though, did not wait for the election. Last month, in a securities filing, Anthropic — a company that has signaled it could spend up to $895.4 billion on computing power in the years ahead, nearly two decades of the National Institutes of Health's annual budget — warned investors that moratoriums could delay or prevent the "significant compute" it needs. A risk factor, in filing language, is a fact outside the company's control that can damage the business. Anthropic has filed the voters of Ohio under that heading. Note carefully what the filing treats as the risk: not the company's plans as a risk to the towns' water, but the towns' votes as a risk to the company's plans. In a town, the aquifer is the asset and the data center is the risk factor; in the filing, the polarity reverses.

To be fair to the money: it is real, in the narrow sense in which the money in any extraction is real. Cedar Rapids, Iowa, has drawn $8 billion in capital investment and tens of millions in community benefits from two data centers under construction, and tax revenue linked to Meta's Louisiana project is paying $50,000 bonuses to public school teachers in Richland Parish, and nobody in either town is giving that money back. The trouble is that an annuity is not consent. Tiffany O'Donnell, Cedar Rapids' mayor, got the investment and, for her trouble, threats against her family and her dog and a nickname she did not choose — the town started calling her "Tiffany O'Data Center," which is what the brochure looks like from the inside. The people who absorbed the threats were the ones who had to live beside the decision, and the people who absorbed the returns watched the opera. What no one in either town was shown before the ground broke was the ledger: how much water the thing would draw in a drought year, how much of the grid upgrade lands on the residential ratepayer. A bonus check is not a disclosure. It is a payment, and payments are what you make when the other party was never shown the bill.

The pattern underneath it is older than the server hall: court a town with tax deals and community payments, sign the terms while the town is flattered, redraft the terms once the town has rebuilt its budget around the plant. Ask what the companies say, and the answer is partnership and investment. Watch what they do, and the answer is campuses sited beside schools and wells, because land and cooling water are cheapest exactly there.

It is also true that the construction jobs are real, and concentrated. Anirban Basu, chief economist of the Associated Builders and Contractors, calls data centers "the primary driver of construction spending growth," and LinkedIn estimates 750,000 new American jobs since 2023 tied to AI. The trouble is that a construction boom is not a supply of electricity — the sites employ thousands of workers for years and far fewer people once the doors open — and an economy that has concentrated its growth story into one category of extraction has concentrated its fragility there too. Basu, it should be said, is the construction trade's own economist, and he expects the boom "to really soften over the next couple of years." The trade group is pricing in the vote. The communities that took the deals did so in good faith.

It is true that the build-out has been carrying the American economy, and the column should say so plainly before it says what kind of carrying this is. Goldman Sachs projects AI investment at 1.9 percent of U.S. GDP this year — the largest share of economic growth for a new industry's build-out since the railroads. The trouble is whose ledger the costs sit on while the growth is booked. A boom financed on unpriced water, on grid capacity socialized onto ratepayers, and on debt raised against permits that may not issue is not investment; it is the bezzle — John Kenneth Galbraith's name for the magic interval when the embezzler is richer by the sum he has taken and the victim does not yet feel poorer. The bond market has started to feel poorer. Investors have spent recent weeks selling bonds tied to CoreWeave over debt concerns; CoreWeave shares fell nearly 10 percent on Wednesday and Applied Digital fell almost 7. The venture capitalist Brad Gerstner — a man with money in these companies — estimates that a quarter to a third of next year's build-out is now at risk. Pat Grady of Sequoia Capital told the industry's own tech conference that the election would "probably elongate the timelines for the data-center build-outs" — the polite way of saying the interval is narrowing, and the most honest sentence anyone from the sector produced all week. Tuesday was not an interruption of the boom. It was the boom's accounting arriving.

To be fair to the industry — and, this matters, to the voters — the compute need is not fabricated. The current generation of large language models does useful work in well-defined domains, at substantial cost, and the people who voted for the bans did not vote against arithmetic. Matt Sforza, a teacher in Sunbury, Ohio, said on Tuesday he planned to vote for his town's ban while allowing that "I think we kind of need them, just to keep up with the demands of all the things they do," objecting only that "it feels like there might be a better way to do it than putting it right next to schools and people's houses." The Sunbury ban passed in a landslide. That is the actual shape of the democratic position: not Luddism but siting discipline, a demand that the industry show its work. The voters were not voting against computing; they were voting against a bill nobody showed them — and showing your work is precisely what the apparatus is built to avoid, because the work shows the water.

What followed the vote followed the script. The corporate-apologetics playbook — no problem, then your fault, then fixing it will make it worse, then socialism — is running at stage three, where every reform is priced as an assault on growth: the securities filing, the warnings about construction employment, the timelines stretching. The industry understood the stakes, because it paid accordingly: Elon Musk poured nearly $500 million into the midterms, others in the sector gave tens of millions to help elect candidates more open to data-center development, and a political committee ran ads against David Sacks — the venture capitalist serving as White House AI adviser — portraying him as getting rich on AI investments while blocking the rules in his advisory role. By early Wednesday, Musk was posting on X that "Data centers in space looking pretty appealing right now" — the orbit being, apparently, the one jurisdiction left without a zoning board or an aquifer.

In Alaska, Mary Peltola led Senator Dan Sullivan late into the count, having made an issue of his eagerness to court AI firms, and one of her voters, an environmental scientist in Girdwood named Wendi Deschamps, summarized her ballot plainly: "I just tried to vote anti-data center for every single candidate." Representative Greg Casar, re-elected in Texas on a platform of a nationwide moratorium, called Tuesday's results the early tremor of "a much bigger AI earthquake" — a repositioning this publication had already charted as [the data-center backlash forced both parties to move ahead of the midterms](/articles/2026-10-14-data-center-backlash-forces-bipartisan-repositioning-ahead-of-us-midterms/). Whatever the eventual size of the quake, the direction of the measurement is not in doubt: the public has started assigning the build-out a price, and until last week the price was zero. The industry treated democracy as a procurement problem this cycle. The procurement failed. That is not a crisis for the republic; it is the ordinary noise of losing one.

Harold Innis built the staples thesis on the fur trade and the pulpwood cut: an economy that draws a raw staple out of a hinterland, refines its value in the metropole, and leaves the region the emptied river and the short run. He spent his career on the corollary too — extraction economies never stay in one commodity, cod, fur, timber, pulp — and each staple reshapes the colony that ships it, with decision rights accumulating at the receiving end. The data center is the newest entry in that lineage, and the geography is unusually legible: the staple lands are concentrated exactly where the harvest was just voted down, and the refined product — the models, the valuations, the stock — accrues elsewhere. What stays behind is what staples regions have always kept: the drawdown, the tax-abatement brochure, and the promise that the employment numbers will look better before they do. American towns export water and electricity embodied in computation and receive back property-tax revenue and a bonus check, and the power to decide where the machine goes, what it draws and who pays stays in the Bay Area, where it was on Tuesday night, at the opera.

My father spent thirty years as a millwright at Manitoba Rolling Mills, in a Manitoba town whose economy was one machine. In 1995 Gerdau, of Porto Alegre, bought the mill, kept him, and sent a third of the line home; the uncles who left learned what a staple town learns when the metropole reprices the staple. The mechanism is new — the warehouse of computers, the interconnection queue. The playbook — take the surplus a landscape and a workforce built, keep the decision rights, find the next town — is the oldest one on the continent.

Cory Doctorow has spent years cataloging the four constraints that used to keep concentrated platform power in check — competition, regulation, self-help and labor — and the industry's patient dismantling of each. Tuesday reinstalled the second one, not by a tribunal with a docket number but by the people the constraint exists to protect, at the one level of government that cannot be lobbied past cheaply: the municipal ballot. Regulation bites where the permit lives. For years the bites were scheduled elsewhere — federal committees, trade agreements, hearings in Washington — which is to say, nowhere. The towns have corrected the venue. The federal picture shifted overnight, and [we traced the new Congress's technology outlook yesterday](/articles/2026-11-04-democratic-gains-shift-u-s-technology-policy-outlook/), but the levers that bite this winter are local, and they are not mysterious.

The remedy is not to stop the machines; it is to price them. Treat hyperscale compute as what its own filings already say it is — essential infrastructure — and regulate it as infrastructure. Require the water budget and the grid-impact study as a condition of the permit, so the ledger is public before the ground breaks rather than after the drought. Put the cost allocation where the contracts already say it can go: the buyer of the compute pays the grid upgrades and the water infrastructure, at rates set by the state utility commission, not the residential ratepayer — the twenty-year purchases financed on the buyer's ledger rather than the ratepayer's. Close the tax-abatement auction. Protect the local referendum right against state preemption. And leave the local veto exactly where Tuesday left it, as the default, with the moratorium as the remedy wherever the disclosure does not come. Let the firms that want the river bid for it openly, at full cost, in public. None of this bars the industry from the field; it is the price of staying on it.

It should be recorded, since the victors will not record it, that the winners offered few specifics beyond pledges running from outright bans to greater scrutiny; a pledge is a mood, not a permit standard, and the engineering the campaigns never supplied is precisely the work that now falls to the towns. The industry's filings now list democracy among the risks to compute; the voters, reading the same ledger, reached the opposite conclusion — the risk was always the unpriced build-out — and on Tuesday, in Sunbury and in more than a dozen towns like it, they filed their disclosure. The industry can still build. It will have to build as if it owes the water an answer, and after Tuesday the water has people who speak for it.

The fights now move where fights of this kind always move: to permit hearings through the winter, to state utility commissions, to the water board's monthly agenda. The industry has long queues of its own — an interconnection request for a large data center can take years — but it has just learned that the queue at the zoning board runs on a different clock, one that answers to people who drink the water. There is a rule in any workshop worth the name: you see the torque specs before you let a machine run unattended overnight. A company that will not show you its water budget is not showing you its torque specs. It is showing you the opera.
