Iran is planting bombs under ships full of civilian sailors. A South Korean vessel sits damaged in the Strait of Hormuz, and merchant ships are still taking hits there despite the U.S. Navy's presence. Steve Cameron, a former merchant navy officer now working as a maritime consultant, laid out what a Hormuz transit costs a captain now: agreement between the master and the crew to take the risk, war-risk insurance, the special coverage a ship buys when the route is dangerous enough that ordinary insurance will not touch it, and a final yes-or-no that belongs to the man on the bridge alone. What a captain wants, Cameron said, is evidence the mines are not there.

A naval mine is a bomb that waits. The modern bottom mine settles on the seabed and listens for a passing hull's noise and metal, and then it fires, and the pressure wave breaks a keel, and the keel is what stands between a crew and the sea. You do not have to sink many vessels. The hardware is cheap. Ian McFarlane, a naval architect with the French defense group Thales, said it straight: "Mines are a very cheap way of waging a naval war, as the Royal Navy is acutely aware." And the weapon's first payload is not even the explosion. It is the doubt. The bare possibility of a mine can park a tanker fleet at anchor, and Neil Roberts, who speaks for the marine insurers at Lloyd's, says the psychological impact of the mine threat is inescapable, as is the uncertainty about how many mines, if any, are present.

Michael Walzer built *Just and Unjust Wars* on a rule any crew member can carry: force is directed at soldiers, not at civilians, and a weapon that cannot tell them apart, planted where civilians must pass, attacks civilians by choice. His case was the V-2 rocket, which could hit a city and nothing smaller, so whoever launched it at London attacked whoever happened to be home that night. A mine seeded into the Hormuz lanes cannot aim at a warship over a tanker, and those lanes are where the world's merchant traffic has to go, much of it crewed by Filipinos, Indians, and Indonesians, men and women who never enlisted in anybody's navy. A mine in a civilian shipping lane discriminates about as well as a landmine seeded into a market road. The men who laid them knew which lane they were seeding. The decision to put the bomb there is the decision to break whichever hull comes along. That is not a flaw in the technology. That is the choice, and the choice is the crime.

Nobody outside a few briefing rooms knows exactly what is on that seabed, and this publication has carried the competing claims for weeks. What is documented is the deployment: [Iran's forces have put drones, grenades, and mines against Hormuz shipping, month after month.](/articles/2026-11-04-iran-deploys-drones-grenades-and-mines-against-hormuz-shipping/) And the record says this is a habit. In July 1987 the supertanker Bridgeton, sailing under U.S. Navy escort, struck an Iranian moored mine in the Gulf, and the mine won the exchange. In the spring of 1988 the frigate Samuel B. Roberts hit an Iranian mine, and the Navy answered with Operation Praying Mantis. Four decades on, the same government is at the same work. In September, U.S. forces struck the Iranian mine-launch sites near the strait, as [this paper documented](/articles/2026-09-01-u-s-forces-strike-iranian-mine-launch-sites-near-strait-of-hormuz/), and the strikes did not settle it. The drones, the grenades, and the mines kept coming.

I know this weapon from where I sat in the gunner's seat south of Baghdad in 2004. The roadside bomb was the same machine in a different medium: cheap, patient, blind, aimed at nobody in particular, which meant it was aimed at whoever drew the route that day. You never saw the man who put it there. You ran the route anyway, because the route had to run. Somebody has turned that weapon loose on the sea, and the crews who draw the Hormuz run now live the way we lived, running the arithmetic between the cargo and the seabed every single watch.

The machines sent against this are doing honest work, and I will not pretend otherwise. Mine hunting used to mean ships with eighty sailors aboard, and before that it meant divers swimming down to armed bombs with their hands. Now the Royal Navy and France's navy, working with Thales, run a robot launch that tows a five-meter imaging box through the water and builds three-dimensional pictures of the seabed, with software trained to flag anything shaped like a mine, including the new kinds built to hide. A crewed minehunter, the slow ship whose whole job is finding mines, becomes a control room of about twenty people working ashore in shifts. The Navy ran a torpedo-shaped robot called the Remus 100 through Iraqi waters in 2003 for the same reason it runs these now. Faster answers, fewer funerals.

But hold one thing in view while the good news runs: the machines do not end the war in the strait. They make the war cheaper to continue. That is a mercy for the crews and a subsidy to the war, and both are true at once.

The capture this fall tells you the design. An Iranian patrol hauled one of ours out of the water and published the photograph: an extra-large uncrewed underwater vehicle, a big robotic submarine that runs itself, built by the defense-technology firm Anduril. The Pentagon says it was an older, malfunctioning model carrying nothing classified. Give that description the salt you would give any belligerent describing its own equipment in a war it is fighting. What is not in dispute is the shape of the outcome. No sailors went out with the machine, no sailors were taken, and the naval analyst William Lupton called the lost hardware "a drop in the ocean compared to having prisoners taken." Navies can wipe these computers remotely, and the Anduril drives may have wiped themselves in Iranian hands. That is the design principle stated out loud: the machine absorbs the loss so the crew does not. Every one of those arrangements is rational, humane, and load-bearing for the war. For the people who used to do this work in the water, it is not a procurement story. NATO navies, which cannot fill their recruiting goals as it is, know exactly what those divers were.

Because here is the pattern the war colleges do not put on a slide: when the fighting gets cheap for one side, the fighting does not get short. It gets long. Andrew Bacevich, who served a career in uniform and buried a son in Iraq, wrote the standard account of it in *Washington Rules*: the permanent American war survives not because it wins but because it has been made bearable to the country paying for it, and the technology has always carried that promise. Precision weapons, stealth, robots, war at a discount with sacrifice optional. A quarter-century in, the discount is the product. Eisenhower named the machinery in his farewell address of January 17, 1961, when he warned against unwarranted influence, whether sought or unsought, by the military-industrial complex. His own drafters had first written military-industrial-congressional, naming the branch that funds the machinery, and that word was cut before the speech went out. Restore the cut word and look at the strait: contractors building the robots, a think-tank fellow laying out the hybrid fleet, Congress funding every piece of the machinery while voting on none of the fighting.

Steve Prest, who introduced uncrewed mine hunting into the Royal Navy and held its first fleet robotics job, describes the destination plainly: "Uncrewed or autonomous vessels are not the operating system. They are applications running on it." Translate the term of art: one distributed force, one nervous system, every hull and drone a nerve ending. Behind my parts counter I have answered one question my whole working life: will it make the trip? When a man rolls in with a bubble in a tire's sidewall, I cannot tell him it will blow, and I will not tell him it won't. He asks whether it will make Houston, and the honest answer is that Houston will find out. A sweeper fleet reduces risk. It cannot prove a seabed empty, and Prest says the same thing in so many words: "you can't prove the negative, that no mines are there, you can reduce the risk." It is genuinely impressive engineering. It is also, read honestly, an apparatus for fighting a war with the smallest possible number of Americans in the water and the smallest possible number of Americans watching.

Now look at who carries what. The crews transiting the strait did not enlist in anybody's navy. They are merchant mariners from the countries that man the world's tankers, moving the fuel the world burns and getting shot at for it. The old song had it right about who fights, and this war has added a verse, because the ones in the water did not even join up. Washington and Tehran have outsourced the last decision of their war to underwriters at Lloyd's and to the captain of each ship. A man fixing brakes in Georgia does not decide whether his route through a bad part of town is an act of war. A tanker master in Hormuz decides it tonight, and signs alone.

The assurance on offer is thin. The Pentagon says the Omani route has been cleared and shipments are running at about seventy-five percent of pre-conflict volume, a claim relayed through the marine-insurance desk at Lloyd's. Hear it for what it is: a belligerent's word about its own war, passed along by the men selling the insurance. A quarter of the world's most important oil lane is still not flowing. In August, [transits through the strait fell to fourteen](/articles/2026-08-12-strait-of-hormuz-crossings-fall-to-14-as-trump-claims-u-s-control/) in the same month the President announced that America controlled it. The water had not read the announcement. Claimed control is not a swept lane, and the captain's signature is the one that has to carry the crew. From that same seat in Iraq I learned to read a briefing slide as the first draft of the story the briefer needs. The machines have only gotten better since. The briefings have not changed.

Ships attacked in the strait despite the Navy's help. A South Korean vessel among the damaged. Crossings down to fourteen in August while the President claimed control. Shipments at three-quarters of before, according to the only party with an interest in the number looking good. Captains buying special insurance and signing personal risk acceptances to pass through a lane their governments cannot keep quiet. Eighty sailors replaced by twenty people ashore, every savings booked, and no line on the ledger for the quarter of the oil that stopped. None of that pencils out for the people doing the work. It pencils beautifully for the people running the war.

The Constitution is not a hard read on this one. Sweeping a live minefield, escorting tankers through it, and striking launch sites on another country's shore, month after month, is war at sea against Iran, and Article I puts the declaration of war in the legislature's hands. Robots do not amend the Constitution. Congress holds the power to declare it, and the President holds the duty to report it under the War Powers Resolution, the law that requires him to tell Congress and the country what the armed forces are fighting. The 2002 authorization for the Iraq war is leftover paper from a regime that fell in 2003, and a quarter-century of treating old authorizations like blank checks does not make one cover robot submarines in the Gulf. Either Congress votes, on the record, or the fighting stops. War on the installment plan does not pencil out under Article I, and it does not pencil out for the crews who pay the premiums in person.

The mines are cheap. The robots are cheaper than funerals. The captains still sign alone, and the quarter of the oil that stopped is priced into somebody's spreadsheet at a desk that never smells salt water. Iran planted the cheapest bomb it could buy in the lane the world's crews must run and let the seabed do the rest. Washington built the machinery that makes the war cheap to keep. The bill for both goes to crews who never voted for anybody's war, and the crews are the ones who find out whether the sweep missed. That is the arithmetic of the strait, and the arithmetic is why it does not end.
