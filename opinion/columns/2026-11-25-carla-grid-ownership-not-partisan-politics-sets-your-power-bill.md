The real climate fight is not Democrats versus voters; it is who owns the grid, who gets paid to rebuild it, and who carries the power bill. Daniel Turner’s [“Voters barely care about climate change, but Democrats push it anyway”](https://www.foxnews.com/opinion/voters-barely-care-climate-change-democrats-push-anyway) in Fox News Opinion argues that climate change registered at just 1% in a September Fox News survey, that a growing bloc of Democratic Socialists is using the Green New Deal as a socialist Trojan horse, that newly elected Democratic governors will use disasters to escape blame, and that a large climate advocacy and litigation network will keep pressure on officials.

Turner is right about two things. Governors do use disasters to dodge responsibility, and climate politics now has an industry with real money, real lawyers, and real influence. Climate science does not repair an offline reservoir. And the twentieth-century communist regimes were a catastrophe; that verdict is settled. None of that tells us whether a modern grid should be owned by shareholders, a public utility, or the people who use it.

Here is the tell: Turner mistakes issue salience for material consequence. A survey asks what people say when you hand them a list of issues. It does not tell us what their money is already paying for. Climate damage can reach the kitchen as insurance withdrawal, a higher power bill, a harder mortgage, a washed-out road, or a plant closure. Plenty of people will rank “climate change” near the bottom and still rank not being ruined by it near the top. That is like asking people whether they care about oxygen and then watching them decline to list it. One percent is an efficient measure of what people say when asked to rank a dozen problems. It is a useless measure of what they pay for.

Turner says as many as six Democratic Socialist House members will make climate policy a Trojan horse. Six members tell us what a faction will advocate. They do not tell us who owns the resulting grid, what reliable power costs, or whether voters can hear the plan and reject it. The democratic test is whether the plan can lose. The economic test is cost and ownership. The “Trojan horse” label replaces both with a party sticker. A party sticker is not an ownership plan. It is also not a power bill.

The same problem runs through the disaster examples. Turner points to what he says Newsom ignored after the Palisades fire: overgrown forests, the lack of brush clearance by the state agency, and an offline reservoir. Then he praises Abbott for not finger-pointing after the Texas floods—a flood that killed 136 people filed under “Every football team makes mistakes.” The New York Times, not Turner, is the one doing the scolding. But both governors use language to soften responsibility. Turner objects when Newsom reaches for an excuse and rewards Abbott when Abbott reaches for the same kind. The difference in party does not change the maintenance schedule or the voters’ ballot. If either man keeps failing, voters can vote him out. A party label can predict an excuse. It cannot repair a reservoir. When the weather becomes a convenient villain, that is ordinary evasion with better branding.

Turner also notices something real: climate politics has an industry around it. His figures show scale, not control. More than 34,000 organizations, more than 130,000 employees, and nearly $30 billion in annual revenue. That is a large industry, and yes, large industries can do large things. “Advocacy complex” is not an ownership analysis. Thirty billion dollars will fog a room. It still does not tell us who owns the wire.

The same question applies to the lawsuits. A climate-damage case can make a polluter pay for damage it shifted onto the public. Turner alleges that the lawsuits also drive up costs for ratepayers. That can happen, but which effect occurs depends on who funds the case, who receives the settlement, who pays the lawyers, and what the law lets them recover. He gives us the number of pending actions and calls the result a green hurricane. The number of suits does not tell us who pays. Show the money. Until then, ideology is wearing a hard hat. Anyway.

Now follow the bill. Some fossil extraction looks cheap because pollution, public-health damage, and climate risk are pushed onto people who never agreed to carry them. Make the firm pay for the pollution, and the old margin shrinks. That does not prove every climate rule is well designed. It proves that private cheapness can be public damage.

The same discipline applies to the clean rebuild. If households pay for transmission, public loans, or tax incentives while private developers and utilities keep the assets and profits, the public has financed somebody else’s balance sheet. That is extraction wearing green colors. If public or member-owned utilities build the grid, own the generation, and recover reasonable costs while sharing the surplus, the same money buys shared assets. The policy label does not decide this. Ownership does. A financing plan without an ownership clause is a wish with a spreadsheet.

Turner can keep his one percent. Either show that changing who owns the grid does not change the bill, or retire the survey as an argument.

I am not against markets or a clean grid. I am against public money carrying the risk while private hands keep the reward. The answer is not a command economy. It is to decide the owner before the money moves.

The weather is a hazard. The grid is a choice. So what do we build?

America already has part of the template. Roughly 900 rural electric cooperatives serve 42 million people across 56 percent of the country’s land. They are member-owned. Washington can keep treating ownership as an ideological insult. The co-ops have treated it as plumbing since the New Deal. That is what a functioning alternative looks like: not a green hurricane. A transformer.

Let local co-ops and public-power utilities buy or build generation and transmission. Markets can choose the contractor. They cannot decide who owns the road if the law refuses to say. If public capital takes the risk, the public should keep a share of the asset or the return.

Norway is not a model for climate politics. It is a receipt for competent public ownership of capital. It did not spend the oil windfall as a boom. The Government Pension Fund Global now holds more than $2 trillion, preserves the principal, and spends the expected return. A grid built for the public can be owned for the public.

Worker voice has to be bargained in, industry by industry. Only about 11 percent of American workers are covered by a union contract, and that will not carry a national rebuild on its own. Build the bargaining structure as we build the wires.

It will be harder here because investor-owned utilities and state regulators have had decades to make the investor-owned model the default. Federalism leaves the concrete grid to states and municipalities while spreading national financing fights across Congress. We can start with existing co-ops and public-power utilities, but ownership will have to move one state and one utility at a time.

The weather will not care which party owns the talking points. It will care who keeps the lights on, who receives the bill, and who can be removed when the grid fails. Build it as shared infrastructure: public and cooperative ownership, patient public finance, worker voice, and local accountability. Then own the switch.
