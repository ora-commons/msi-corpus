Congress has spent fifty years starving the families of the soldiers it sends to war.

At the Armed Services YMCA in Harker Heights, Texas, there is a swimming pool, a gym and a coffee shop. There is also a food pantry at the far end of a hallway on the second floor, where active-duty soldiers stationed at Fort Hood can take groceries home. More than a dozen such pantries ring the base, home to about 40,000 soldiers.

On a July morning, Sgt Miguel Pérez, 39, an army combat engineer, carried a heavy reusable bag across the parking lot to his pickup: sweet potatoes, a box of elbow macaroni, a 5lb bag of frozen chicken breasts, enormous beets, a dozen eggs. About $100 of food, by his reckoning. He makes the trip twice a month. It shaves roughly $200 off a grocery bill that had no room in it to begin with.

"We need it," he says. "When you come early, you see a lot of military people."

One of his soldiers put the squeeze into a single sentence: he could not pay a speeding ticket, because if he paid it he would not be able to buy groceries.

That is not a story of soldiers making irresponsible choices. It is a story of a country paying its military families through a compensation system that makes no sense for the households it now expects them to sustain.

At the Killeen pantry, which delivered 500,000 pounds of food to more than 19,000 families in 2025 — an average of about 26 pounds per family across the whole year — roughly 80% of its customers were active-duty personnel or their families. Demand has climbed 12–19% every year. It opens on Thursdays and frequently runs out by early afternoon. On a shelf of peanut butter, a sign asks families to limit themselves to one of each kind.

"It's the most heartbreaking thing for a soldier in uniform to walk in here at one o'clock and our pantry is already empty, and I have to turn them away," says Sheri Yerrington, executive director of the Killeen ASYMCA. "That is the worst feeling, and I have had it."

The ASYMCA's food-distribution programs, serving more than 50 military installations, saw demand rise 23% in a year. In 2025, nearly half of active-duty military families experienced food insecurity, more than double the rate in 2023, according to the Military Family Advisory Network. Almost 57% of enlisted families reported it, against 14% of officers. One in five active-duty families said they had eaten less than they felt they should because there was not enough money for food.

Here is the kitchen-table version of that number.

Pérez brings home about $4,800 a month — just shy of $58,000 a year — including roughly $1,600 in housing allowance and $476 for food. His rent is $1,900. He is raising two sons, 16 and three, after a divorce, and he does dangerous work: combat engineers emplace and destroy obstacles on behalf of infantry and tank units, with explosives and grueling physical labor. "I always wanted to do this," he says. "It's hard today for us."

The government pays him $476 a month for food. The volunteer pantry hands him about 42% of that. The government pays him $1,600 for housing and his rent is $1,900, so he is $300 short before he buys a gallon of milk or a box of diapers. He is not earning some exceptional salary that has failed to keep pace. He is receiving the system as intended and discovering that its arithmetic cannot support a family.

I do the same math in my own kitchen, with two kids and a spreadsheet open at eleven at night, and I want to be careful about what my math can prove, because my household has buffers his does not. My parents were college-educated and handled my financial-aid paperwork, a load first-generation students carry alone. We are white, we are two-income, and those are buffers. Which is exactly the point. A household with buffers can absorb a shock: a rate, a layoff, a school year, a bad month. A household living on $58,000 with a $300 housing gap does not have a buffer. It has a Thursday.

And I have to be honest about the family-wage math, because my father spent thirty-eight years as a supervisor at the U.S. Postal Service and raised three of us on one income in Lansdale, and we did not go hungry. He was a federal employee. The federal government was his employer. The same government that set his scale now sets the scale of a sergeant whose groceries come home in a bag from an Armed Services YMCA.

The tradition I grew up in already had the doctrine, and it is 135 years old: a wage ought to support a household, not just the worker. That is the argument of *Rerum Novarum*, Leo XIII's 1891 encyclical: "Wages ought not to be insufficient to support a frugal and well-behaved wage-earner." A wage that cannot support a household is not a wage; it is a recruiting poster. Congress has been arguing with Leo XIII since before my father was born, and the argument always goes badly for the service member.

Then the country changed its mind about its army and never changed its budget.

The foundations of the current compensation structure date to the Career Compensation Act of 1949, when about 30% of enlisted army soldiers were married and the enlisted force was a rotating draft. It was designed to support an 18-year-old recruit eating at a dining facility. Shannon Razsadin, co-founder and CEO of the Military Families Advisory Network, puts it plainly: "The reality is that the military compensation structure is not designed to sustain a full household."

The all-volunteer force arrived in the early 1970s and turned enlistment into a career. Enlisted marriage rates climbed to nearly 60% by the 1990s. In 2024, about 44% of enlisted troops across all branches were married, roughly the civilian rate, and about a third had children. The force became a family institution. The money to feed that family was never rewritten to match.

Congress has not been absent from this crisis. It has been present at every stage, watching the evidence accumulate and choosing policy that cannot reach the families carrying the burden.

The Gates Commission, appointed by President Richard Nixon in 1969 to examine the move to an all-volunteer military, flagged the inequity of military pay against civilian wages as a barrier to recruitment. For a few years in the 1970s, raises for junior ranks actually outran comparable civilian wages. Then they stopped outpacing them.

By the late 1990s, Sen John McCain — Republican, veteran, and no man for soft language — was attacking the fact that tens of thousands of service members were collecting food stamps, calling it a "national disgrace." In 2000 he authored a bill to put a monthly nutritional allowance in the paycheck of any service member eligible for food stamps. "We must end the days of a 'food-stamp Army' once and for all."

He lost. But the bill's defeat produced the 2001 National Defense Authorization Act, which put the basic allowance for subsistence into every military paycheck and created the family subsistence supplemental allowance, designed to lift qualifying families to 130% of the federal poverty level: $18,100 for a family of four in 2002, about $33,000 by 2026 after inflation.

It failed exactly as written. Restrictive criteria and a labyrinthine approval process meant few families applied and nearly all who did were rejected. In 2013, 620,000 military households sought some form of food assistance and nearly $104m in food stamps were spent at military commissaries. That year the FSSA denied 97% of applicants. Congress scrapped the program in 2016.

Then came the Basic Needs Allowance, approved in 2021 for service members with dependents under 200% of the poverty line. Still applied for through the chain of command. Still baffling applicants. In its first reported year it enrolled seventy-seven families.

Seventy-seven. Nearly half of active-duty families were struggling to afford food.

This is not merely an administrative mess. It is a political choice. Congress has repeatedly created a benefit and then made it nearly impossible to receive, because serving the people who need it is more troublesome than announcing it.

The wider safety net has the same design flaw. The agriculture department counts the basic allowance for housing as household income when calculating SNAP eligibility. The allowance is pegged to local rents, so count it as income and a family in California, Hawaii or Alaska gets pushed over the line. The line item that doesn't show up in the press release is the housing allowance itself — the money the family is supposed to live on, counted as income against food assistance. "They never see that money," says Liza Lieberman, vice-president of communications at the anti-hunger nonprofit Mazon. "There were Republicans and Democrats on the hill that were laughing us out of the room. No one wanted to acknowledge or confront the fact that we were not paying our troops enough."

Washington can find money when it decides to find it. Congress is currently [weighing proposals to narrow Social Security's shortfall](/articles/2026-10-24-rubin-and-dapena-examine-proposals-to-narrow-social-security-s-shortfall/) — a serious business in a budget year. The military's version of that same deliberation produced a 97% denial rate and a replacement program that served seventy-seven families. The money has been identified, appropriated, debated and starved on both sides of the aisle for five decades. What it has never been is funded on purpose.

Then, in December 2025, Pérez and all but the most senior officers received a one-time $1,776 bonus, the so-called "warrior dividend." The administration said tariffs would pay for it. They did not. The money came out of the $2.9bn Congress had appropriated to repair the military housing allowance — the exact line item that three years of work by Rep Don Bacon's office had targeted at troops living off base in expensive markets.

"There is one area where the Hegseth team has hurt us," Bacon, a Republican from Nebraska and a veteran on the House armed services committee, says. "Congress appropriated $2.9bn to address this, but the Pentagon took that money and distributed it evenly to everyone as a bonus, which meant those living off base received less than we recommended."

A flat bonus spread evenly across everyone is the same thing as no raise at all for a family whose rent outstrips its allowance. It is a check. Checks do not repair a housing allowance. A bonus gets a photograph. A housing allowance does not.

A one-time $1,776 payment is $148 a month spread across a year. The pantry gave Pérez roughly $200 a month, for free, on two Thursday mornings.

There is a mechanism for what happened here, and Naomi Klein named it in *The Shock Doctrine*: crises used to advance policies that could not have passed in ordinary times. Klein is a left source and I am borrowing the mechanism rather than the polemic, but the mechanism is the point. Congress appropriated the housing money. The Pentagon spent it on something else. A bonus requires no signature, no formula, no local market study and no follow-up question about why rent in Killeen is what it is. It requires a press release and a check with a flag on it.

When the October 2025 government shutdown threatened missed paychecks, the Military Families Advisory Network launched an emergency food program and took more than 50,000 applications in 72 hours. "So many military families are living in a bubble of just getting by, and then something happens and it throws everything off kilter for them," Razsadin says.

There is a Taylor Swift song called "You're On Your Own, Kid" that I have spent four years treating as a breakup song. It is also the mission statement of American care infrastructure, and the title line is exactly what the compensation system says to a sergeant with two sons and a $300 housing gap. The redemptive turn in that song is the friends who show up; the American version is the YMCA on Thursday morning with its sign on the peanut butter shelf, and 50,000 military families applying for emergency food within 72 hours because the paychecks stopped. That is not nothing. It is also not a compensation system.

A bubble of just getting by. That is what a pay structure that does not cover the household looks like from the inside: a bubble held together by a volunteer pantry and a second paycheck, waiting for one emergency to pop it.

Meanwhile the 14.5% raise lost the war with everything a family actually buys. In 2025, Congress approved an exceptional 14.5% pay increase for junior enlisted ranks and 4.5% for all other troops. A historic raise. Average base salary for an enlistee with less than two years of service went from about $22,000 in 2022 to nearly $28,000 in 2025, and then the grocery store did what grocery stores do. "There's a misconception that they got a pay raise, so the issue is resolved," says Lieberman. Rep Veronica Escobar, whose El Paso district includes Fort Bliss, calls it "just a Band-Aid on the overall challenge."

Residential rents are up about 40% since 2019. Childcare costs are up more than 36% since 2019. Vehicle insurance has surged 48%. Energy has jumped 16% in a year, and gasoline is up about 40% since the war on Iran began in February. And according to the army's own Directorate of Prevention, Resilience and Readiness, only 37% of soldiers could find housing within the limits of their allowance in 2025, down from 58% in 2020. Sixty-three percent could not.

Because this is a family crisis, it is also a labor crisis, which makes it a readiness crisis. More than 20% of military spouses are unemployed and actively seeking work, and 67% cite a lack of childcare as a barrier to work or education — a spousal unemployment rate five times the civilian rate. Deployments spike pantry demand, because a spouse with a job suddenly becomes the only parent. "Often, a military spouse may have a job and then can't work once their service member deploys because they have to take over 100% of childcare," says Dorene Ocamb of the ASYMCA. The Killeen pantry answers with low-cost before- and after-school care at 30 area schools and free short-term childcare during medical appointments.

Affordability has become a campaign message. [Arkansas Democrats are pressing the affordability message in the final midterm stretch](/articles/2026-10-25-arkansas-democrats-push-affordability-message-in-final-midterm-stretch/). At Fort Hood, affordability is not a campaign message. It is a Thursday morning line that runs past crates of produce.

Which is where the numbers become a person.

Becky Maurer, 21, is an active-duty army supply specialist, married to another soldier, pregnant with a second child, with an 18-month-old daughter named Chloe. Two military salaries. A "frugal" approach to spending. And still, on a Thursday morning, her first trip to the pantry: "I didn't go for a really long time because I thought I could be taking from someone that needs it more than me, because I have a job and my husband has a job." It did not feel good.

She pays nearly $800 a month in daycare, expecting that to climb to $1,400 when she returns to work after the second baby, plus about $140 a month in diapers and wipes. "Diapers, oh my God," she says. "It's manageable," she says, "but barely."

Dorothy Day put the whole thing in one sentence: "The Gospel takes away our right forever, to discriminate between the deserving and the undeserving poor." Maurer did the discrimination to herself, in the hallway of a YMCA, with two jobs and a second baby on the way. The county does it too, every time it counts the housing allowance as income.

The Marine Corps has a running joke: "If the Marine Corps wanted you to have a wife, they would have issued you one." It is a barracks joke, and barracks jokes are how an institution says what it believes about itself: the family is a curiosity, a private arrangement, a rounding error. Congress did not issue them a wife. It issued a housing allowance 63% of soldiers cannot find a home within, and a $1,776 check with a flag on it. In 2021 the Marine Corps commandant David Berger set out a new strategy and said: "We have to treat people like human beings instead of inventory."

The military knows what all of this does to retention. In 2021, an army survey of 38,000 soldiers found that more than 40% cited the impact of military service on their families' wellbeing among their top five reasons for declining to re-enlist. A 2025 army report concluded that "the distraction of financial stress can lead to decreased operational efficiency and mission readiness." "Absolutely it has a direct impact on our national security," says Escobar. "We want service members focused on the mission, on global threats, on their training, and on cohesion."

Pérez, who came to the army at 34 after losing his casino job in the Covid-19 pandemic, was ready to stay. Maurer has already decided to leave when her contract expires in September, planning to use her VA benefits to study psychology online. One salary, one baby on the way, one more person on the Thursday line.

None of this requires a theory about whether military families deserve charity. They do not need charity. They are owed a wage. This is a question of whether the government pays the people it asks to risk their lives, and the answer for decades has been no — or, more precisely, only if the pay happens to cover rent, groceries, childcare and the rest of living a military household.

Congress can change that, and none of what follows is partisan. The AEI–Brookings compromise on paid leave — eight weeks at 70% wage replacement up to $600 a week — is what center-left and center-right policy economists actually agreed on before the political class buried it; the Hamilton Project's Cascio has a refundable federal childcare tax credit indexed to a family of four with children under five, paired with quality investments and universal pre-K. Raise basic pay to the family wage, index housing allowances to actual local rents, fund childcare so a spouse can work without another family absorbing the cost, stop diverting the housing line into flat bonuses, make food assistance automatic and confidential and available without a humiliating trip up the chain of command, and publish the enrollment figures, because an invisible benefit is an inadequate one.

On Thursday the Killeen pantry opens, and by one o'clock Sheri Yerrington is turning soldiers away. At Harker Heights, families wait in a line that runs past crates of produce. Pérez will go back next week, and the week after, because $1,776 does not cover a year of groceries and $476 does not cover a month.

The people in that line are not asking for charity. They are asking for the salary they were promised by a country that decided decades ago that their families are a rounding error. Every empty shelf is Congress making its choice again, and the choice is starvation. The money was never missing. The willingness was.
