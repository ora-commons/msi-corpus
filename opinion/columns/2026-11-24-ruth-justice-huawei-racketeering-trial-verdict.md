Huawei stole trade secrets and defrauded banks, and on Tuesday a New York jury convicted the telecommunications giant of conspiracy to steal trade secrets and bank fraud while acquitting it of criminal racketeering. The convicted defendant is a corporation, which is to say, a thing no jail can hold. The [jury’s split verdict](/articles/2026-11-24-huawei-acquitted-on-racketeering-in-trade-secret-and-bank-fraud-case/) narrows the charge. It does not launder the convictions.

The United States did put hands on a human being in this affair: Meng Wanzhou, Huawei’s finance chief and the founder’s daughter. Canadian authorities arrested her in 2018 after U.S. prosecutors accused her of misleading HSBC about Huawei’s business in Iran. She was held nearly three years, returned to China in 2021 in a prisoner swap that freed two Canadians, and the United States dropped its case as part of the deal. Prosecutor Meredith Arfa told the jury: “The tone was set at the top by Huawei leaders and executives who themselves engaged in criminal conduct, like lying to Congress, lying to banks, violating U.S. sanctions and trying to interfere with the very criminal investigation that led to this case.” The prosecutor put the conduct at the top of the company. The verdict reached the letterhead.

The [trial had opened with prosecutors’ “criminal enterprise” claim](/articles/2026-09-10-huawei-racketeering-trial-opens-in-brooklyn-with-criminal-enterprise-claim/). The indictment’s own inventory, as prosecutors laid it out: someone working for Huawei slipped into a rival’s booth at a Chicago trade show and photographed the circuits inside another company’s networking device; a Huawei worker carried a robotic arm out of a rival’s laboratory to have it photographed and measured; and Huawei sold routers containing a competitor’s copied source code. Court records named Cisco Systems, Motorola Solutions, T-Mobile, Quintel USA and Fujitsu among the victim companies. Prosecutors also alleged that Huawei did business in sanctioned Iran and North Korea and made false statements to U.S. officials and banks to conceal it.

Much of the disputed material was public, defense counsel Brian Heberlig argued. He said prosecutors had spun isolated episodes involving a few of Huawei’s more than 200,000 employees into a grand conspiracy. He called the proof “innuendo and facts ripped out of context,” assembled from former employees “with a bone to pick.” Huawei succeeded through “hard work, innovation and competition,” he said. The defense calls that correction. I won’t dwell on the licensing arithmetic: billions of dollars, 75% of it to U.S. companies; employees reprimanded or fired; a “compliance culture that permeates Huawei today.” Lines of Cisco source code, he said, turned up in Huawei software more than two decades ago. When the copying was discovered, Huawei recalled the routers it sold in the United States and offered to replace them with Cisco routers. “You can’t steal a secret that isn’t a secret,” he said. Then the recall was a curious expense. “Huawei didn’t steal its way to success.” The jury did not accept the clean copy.

The class is powerful corporations that turn criminal conduct into administration. That is how the code baron works. According to the indictment, a worker takes the photograph, a false statement reaches the bank, and the company sells the product. Scale does not merely buy better lawyers. It buys enough distance between the worker and the corporation to make responsibility harder to place. That is how theft becomes administration. A corporation can pay a fine; it cannot do time. The fine comes out of a corporate till. The people who gave the orders need never touch it. Ambrose Bierce had the definition a century ago: “CORPORATION, n. An ingenious device for obtaining individual profit without individual responsibility.” The device now stands convicted twice over.

The jury has other nouns. The law binds the kid who shoplifts; it can convict the company, and it did let an executive walk out through a prisoner exchange. Meng’s charges were dropped. That is the machine, entire. I have watched this movie since Nixon. A shoplifter with a shipping department: that is the honest job description, and the shipping department is why the shoplifting ends in filings instead of fingerprints.

Again. In the savings-and-loan era, the law could jail the powerful individual. Charles Keating, asked whether the $1.3 million he gave five U.S. senators bought influence, answered: “I want to say in the most forceful way I can: I hope so.” He served about four and a half years before his convictions were overturned on a technicality, then pleaded guilty in 1999. Impunity is not a law of nature. It is a purchase.

After that, the corporate docket filled with fines and deferred prosecution agreements. HSBC admitted in 2012 to laundering money for Mexican drug cartels and sanctioned regimes. It paid $1.9 billion under a Justice Department deferred prosecution agreement, including $1.256 billion in forfeiture, and produced not one individual defendant. Sen. Grassley said HSBC had “quite literally purchased a get-out-of-jail-free card.” Wells Fargo opened millions of unauthorized accounts under sales quotas and resolved its case with $3 billion in 2020; the Justice Department’s agreement was “with the bank itself, not with any individuals responsible for the fraud.” General Motors hid a defective ignition switch for more than a decade, a defect tied to 124 deaths; GM agreed to a $900 million deferred-prosecution forfeiture, and no individual was charged. Purdue Pharma pleaded guilty in 2007 and 2020; the Sacklers, who extracted billions from OxyContin, have never been criminally charged; and about $850 million of a 2025 $7.4 billion settlement was set aside for individual victims over 15 years. The law kept billing the company, and the shareholders carried the check.

The criminal-racketeering count, the kind prosecutors typically use in organized-crime and drug cases, fell. It was the only count that brought all the allegations together, Heberlig said, so he called the acquittal vindication. Huawei will appeal. A motion to appeal is not a receipt erased. Chinese officials call the prosecution “economic bullying.” The “bullying,” as enacted here, was a lengthy trial and a verdict from ordinary citizens.

That has not put the company out of business. Huawei has been barred from selling equipment to U.S. carriers for years over security concerns and has been blacklisted by Canada and Britain. It still sells smartphones and telecom equipment worldwide. It has also moved into chipmaking as the AI boom makes it an important alternative to Nvidia in China. The code baron can be convicted and keep selling.

Tonight, a child may steal a jar of formula and wear the record for it. Huawei has a conviction, a motion to appeal and a market in which it still sells. The child gets a record. Huawei gets an appeal. No executive gets a prison sentence. Gavel.
