Efren is a visa in my crab house, and I solve the labor shortage by keeping his passport in the safe beside my desk. James Freeman writes in [the *Wall Street Journal*](https://www.wsj.com/opinion/more-hiring-at-small-firms-f19a0080?mod=rss_opinion) that small firms are hiring again, that raises are getting more plentiful, and that good help is still very hard to find. He's right on all 3 counts. I hold a fourth, and it's the only one that pays me: own the exit. You eat the crab. It's in your crab cake, in the bisque, in the dip you're carrying to somebody's party on Saturday. Efren picks it at $9.15 an hour. A man who could walk out at noon costs me $14.40, so Efren is worth $5.25 an hour to me before he lifts a claw. The drainage mats for the picking floor run $14.60 a station. I don't buy them. God gave the sea the crab and gave me the hands that pick it, and He never named a wage.

Here's the whole trade, in 2 lines. The visa belongs to my plant. The man belongs to the visa. Efren's boots are wet through by 9 in the morning, and the visa doesn't care, and neither do I.

The statute names 66,000 of these men a year, and Congress keeps appropriating more on top of the cap, which tells you the country already conceded the principle and is only haggling over the volume. Efren's recruiter in Manila fronted the airfare, the physical, the consular fee, and the placement itself. The total comes to $8,400 against his first 11 months of wages, and he signs for it before he boards. The visa names my plant, so he can't change employers. The debt follows him, so he can't go home. A complaint is a request to be flown back to the debt with nothing in his pocket, so he doesn't complain.

That isn't a fence I built. It's a fence the form built, and I hold the form.

The trick was never finding them. The trick is the terms you attach once they arrive, because a worker who can leave at any time isn't a worker. He's a weather event. So you attach the terms. Should he be able to change employers? No. His visa is mine. Should he be able to leave the country? Not without my signature. Should he owe me for the ticket and the physical and the recruiter? All three, off the first checks. Should he live in my bunkhouse? Yes, at $90 a week, off the top. The $90 buys a bunk, a bulb, and a lock that turns from my side, and I stopped itemizing it years ago, the way you stop itemizing a subscription. Should he complain about the ammonia? He can. He can also be on a plane by Friday. Read those four answers again as a balance sheet. That's all a visa is once I'm the one holding the pen.

Let me do you a kindness, because I can hear you starting to feel something. Feeling is the expensive habit. You're going to want to spend it on Efren's feet, and feet don't bill, so the feeling buys you nothing and costs you the mat. Sit with me a minute. I'll teach you to look where the money is instead. It's easier than you think, and once you've learned it you won't be able to unlearn it, and you'll be calmer for the rest of your life.

The picking room runs at 91 degrees. The cookers throw ammonia and the floor sits under half an inch of water at 41. He stands in it 10 hours.

By the second month, his feet won't go back into the boot at the end of a shift without his thumbs.

By the fourth, the nails lift.

By the sixth, there's a fungus in the webbing he'll be carrying when he's 50.

It isn't a wound. So it isn't a claim. So it isn't on my books.

I call him a sponsorship. That's the word in the schedule column. A sponsorship. By which I mean a man whose feet won't go back in his boots. I use the word because it fits in a cell and the other thing doesn't.

$14.60 is the mat. 40 stations is $584, and again in the fall when they rot through. His feet go on the scale. The $584 goes on the scale. The $584 wins, and it wins again on Monday, and I'd keep it a third time. The only part of this that has ever cost me an evening is my foreman raising it at the end of the quarter, and my foreman is a decent man, and decent men are the reason I hire through a recruiter.

The country keeps promising to import more of the world's greatest natural resource. I've imported it for 19 years, and I can tell you what the welcome costs. Hand a man the freedom to leave and you haven't ended my shortage. You've bought my raise. The market clears and my wage doesn't move. More bodies hands me the worker. It doesn't hand me the leash, and a worker without a leash is a customer with a lawyer.

Same terms, different crop, because the principle travels. Aurelio picks mushrooms in a house I own outside Kennett Square, Pennsylvania. He's Efren with a different product and a darker room. Same tie, same deduction, same math to the decimal.

Aurelio is a picker, by which I mean a man whose cough started in March and hasn't stopped. The mushroom house runs dark and wet, which the crop likes and the lungs don't. The spores hang in the air and there's no light to see them by, because mushrooms don't want it. He picks by the pound. The pound pays $0.11. The cough has a name. Hypersensitivity pneumonitis. The trade shortens it to mushroom worker's lung. I use neither name in the house, because a name makes a man ask what it costs to fix. The fix is a $1,200 ventilation fan. I keep the $1,200. I keep the $0.04 a pound too, the $0.04 I'd have to add to $0.11 to make it a wage. I don't add it. I've never added it. I'd keep it again.

Here's the part I charge tuition for. The fan isn't a cost decision. It's a scope decision, and scope is where the margin sits. The fan doesn't pick a single mushroom. So the fan stays in the catalog, and Aurelio stays in the dark, and the cremini in your cart is $2.49 a package with the best margin in 3 years. God bless the harvest. That's not a feeling. That's the cost basis, stated correctly.

And don't file me under "one strange man," the way a voter likes to file people so he can be finished with me by November. I'm printed. Chapter 9 of *Calibrated Deprivation* is called "Terms of Retention," and it hands the manager a worksheet: four questions, four answers, one column for what the man keeps and one for what I keep. The clerk who typed Aurelio's visa terms was working from page 214 of the 3rd edition, and he crossed out 2 of my examples and wrote "too generous" in the margin. He was right. I teach his amendment now. He sets the starting rate where you work, and you've met him, and he is perfectly pleasant.

Lift your eyes off the 2 floors, because the law is bigger than one bunkhouse and one safe. I'm not new. I'm the oldest arrangement there is, wearing this decade's vocabulary. The company town, the crop lien, the Bracero contract, the kafala system of the Gulf, the H-2A tie, the plantation. The names change. The arithmetic doesn't. You bring the body in, you tie the body to the place, you deduct the cost of keeping the body alive from that body's own wage, and you keep the difference.

The law has always had a face. Under the crop lien the storekeeper set the year's furnishings against the cotton before the cotton was weighed, so the farmer walked out of the weigh house already owing the difference between what he grew and what he had spent. The padrone held an Italian's passage money and his papers in 1905, and the Dillingham Commission wrote 41 volumes about him. The Bracero contract sat in the employer's file from 1942 to 1964, and when the men tried to bargain the government shipped them back and reopened the line the next season. The Gulf calls its version kafala and gets lectured about it at Davos. I call mine a guest worker program and get a credit against my state tax. It's one instrument in 3 alphabets, and I'm not new. I'm the padrone with a database.

Scripture didn't abolish any of it. It regulated it, and then it wrote it down:

> "Your male and female slaves are to come from the nations around you; from them you may buy slaves. You may also buy some of the temporary residents living among you and members of their clans born in your country, and they will become your property. You can bequeath them to your children as inherited property and can make them slaves for life, but you must not rule over your fellow Israelites ruthlessly." — Leviticus 25:44–46

Read the last clause again. The Lord set a limit on the master's hand against his brother and put the foreigner outside the limit, and He wrote both in the same breath, in the same statute, on the same page. The guest clause isn't a loophole I went looking for. It's a line item, and it has been in the document for a very long time. The temporary resident. The stranger among you. Freeman's "world's greatest natural resource" is the same resource that verse is pricing. The century is different. The arithmetic is the same.

I don't care where Efren is from or where Aurelio is from. I care that neither can leave. The passport is the product. The man is the delivery mechanism.

Which brings me to the part I'm told is the problem and which I find restful. Efren will never buy a crab cake. He lands owing $8,400 and leaves with a foot he can't feel, and not $1 of that goes back across the deli case. Multiply him by the floor, by the parish, by the coast. The Gulf retail numbers cross my desk on the 1st of the month. I read the cost line and I leave the rest of the page face down. The county clinic keeps a waiting list, and his foot will be on it, and so will Aurelio's cough. I don't use that clinic, so I have nothing useful to offer on it. My cooler is full, my payroll is clean, and I have no complaint to file this quarter. It's been a fine season. I take the boat out Sunday.

Aurelio coughs in the dark. The fan stays in the catalog. The cremini ships.

Efren's replacement lands Tuesday. Same recruiter, same $8,400, same safe. The new passport goes in beside the last one, and there's room, because I bought a bigger safe in 2019 and it has never once been full.

The mat costs $14.60. I have 40 stations. Do the multiplication, and then tell me what his feet were supposed to be worth.
