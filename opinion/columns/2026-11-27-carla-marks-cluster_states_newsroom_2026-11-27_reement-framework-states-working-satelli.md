The new broadband deal doesn’t cut red tape. It cuts the oversight. In November, the National Telecommunications and Information Administration released a [model agreement for satellite providers](https://www.newsfromthestates.com/article/feds-offer-new-agreement-framework-states-working-satellite-providers-broadband-program) working with states under BEAD, the federal broadband program. News From The States reports that low-earth-orbit satellite companies may meet the federal service and testing requirements while declining BEAD subsidies. A state that refuses the agreement must find another provider at comparable cost. Nebraska has received roughly $57 million of its $405 million award, leaving more than $300 million in “non-deployment funds” that may never come. About 23 percent of Nebraska’s BEAD technology mix is satellite, and Starlink is the provider at the center of the change. The paperwork is supposed to be simpler. The harder question is who gets to serve the household without answering for it.

Give the government its true half first. Contracting has real costs. A fifty-state patchwork of bespoke requirements can slow a build to a crawl. Satellite is also a genuine tool. For the farmhouse eight miles from its nearest neighbor, a dish is a connection this year instead of a fiber route someday. Nobody should ask a farm kid to admire the future while her homework waits on a cellular hotspot. A rule nobody can sign is not policy. It is paperwork with a pause button.

I’m not against satellite broadband. I’m against public money buying private reach without public accountability.

Now watch what “simplify” is doing in that sentence, because it is the whole trick. The federal baseline stays. The state-specific obligations can be left behind. The paperwork even has a name now: the Model Zero Outlay Agreement. Zero outlay. Nobody printed “zero oversight” on it, but that appears to be the bundle.

Emily Haxby, a Gage County farmer and vocal critic of the program’s remodeling, says the agreement would remove the accountability measures meant to ensure that Starlink serves Nebraska’s BEAD locations. She suspects its supporters will call it a cost-saving opportunity. She also doubts Nebraska can find another company offering comparable service at comparable or lower cost. Her proposed federal policy, quoted for accuracy, is for Washington to “keep their grubby paws out of it.” She is a farmer watching her own broadband. She has earned the phrasing.

If the patchwork is truly what is slowing service, name the obligation that failed to deliver a connection. The obligations were how Nebraskans could check. The simplification is the escape from being checked.

The subsidy, for the company at the center of this, was never the point. Claude Aiken, chief strategy officer at Nextlink, did the arithmetic: SpaceX’s entire nationwide BEAD award, he said, is less than two weeks of the company’s revenue. A competitor with every reason to measure his words puts the incentive more bluntly: “The long-term benefit for them is keeping other providers from receiving subsidies for these locations.” Haxby’s observation completes the picture. Starlink was already serving Nebraska locations, subsidy or no subsidy.

SpaceX can keep serving eligible rural locations while declining the federal subsidy, and BEAD can make the states absorb the consequences. That is not a contracting shortcut. It is a corporate veto over public money.

The omitted fact is simple. A provider can keep access to a publicly planned location while shedding the obligations attached to it. If the dominant provider says no to public money, the state must solve the harder problem: find someone else at comparable cost. If nobody can, the location remains without service and the unused subsidy may remain unused. The reach stays. The obligation walks.

Nebraska cannot make its no cheap if rural homes remain unserved. Aiken’s point is not merely that the subsidy is small. It is that declining the money lets one provider keep the map while making the state’s replacement problem more expensive. Whether Starlink intended that result is beside the point. The incentive is built into the form.

The two complaints can also be true. Nebraska might have managed its program badly. Haxby says the state could have done better, and she says the federal changes “did not help us at all.” A bad state contract does not make a federal waiver the cure. If simplification is the goal, states must be able to name the accountability the agreement preserves. If they cannot, this is not simplification. It is a waiver with a procurement department.

So Nebraska may end up with satellite reach delivered as a subscription instead of an asset. The fiber line is capital buried once, owned locally, and able to outlast everyone who paid for it. The satellite dish is a monthly bill for a connection that lives on someone else’s network, under someone else’s terms. Public capital is supposed to buy the thing that compounds. A subscription doesn’t compound. It bills.

The procedural record tells its own story. Federal officials spent late 2025 changing BEAD through the “Benefit of the Bargain” initiative. Washington rescinded and then [approved Nevada’s $170 million broadband plan](https://www.nationalreview.com/2026/01/09/trump-administration-approves-nevada-s-170m-broadband-plan-after-earlier-rescission/), and Texas [paused BEAD grant disbursements after Starlink favoritism allegations](https://www.nationalreview.com/2026/08/12/texas-pauses-bead-grant-disbursements-after-starlink-favoritism-allegations/). The federal program keeps changing the ground rules while state plans sit in the balance. A program this busy rewriting itself is not a program being slowed by paperwork. The motion, measured across the year, has mostly been the motion of the rules changing.

So what do we build? Not a bigger waiver.

First, any provider serving a location planned through the program should face one common performance and accountability standard, whether or not it accepts the subsidy: the same uptime test, the same duty to report and answer when service fails, and the same service-performance reporting. If a company wants fewer obligations, it can compete for customers on private terms. Public funds should not purchase private reach and public silence.

Second, if Nebraska’s non-deployment money returns, the state could help build a public option: a state- or community-owned open-access network. Several providers use the same public line and compete for customers. Nebraska retains the asset. If one provider fails, another can take its place. Satellite companies can compete over the network. They just cannot convert public infrastructure into a private toll road by declining the obligations attached to it.

This is harder here than signing a model form. A public option requires capable administration, contracts, metering, maintenance rules, and a way to replace a failing provider. Rural electric cooperatives are not a ready-made broadband plan. They are a working precedent for the thing BEAD keeps dancing around: public capital, local ownership, and service somebody has to answer for.

The 1936 answer, updated for wire instead of wattage. The Rural Electrification Act provided long-term federal loans to member-owned cooperatives because investor-owned utilities looked at the empty miles between farmsteads and declined the business. Rural electric cooperatives still serve about 42 million Americans across 56 percent of the country’s landmass. Roughly 900 of them are member-owned, and many grew from the same New Deal logic: if the market would not build it, route the capital to the people who will own it.

My grandfather sat on the boards of both an electric cooperative and a dairy cooperative in Minnesota. Nobody at that table believed they were operating an ideology. They were keeping the lights on. They were.

The model has already been extended to wire. Chattanooga’s city-owned EPB built a municipal fiber network and offered gigabit service. Missouri’s Co-Mo electric cooperative strung fiber for members the market would not serve. Broadband cooperatives now route patient capital toward member-owned networks that keep the asset in the county. Public power districts extend into broadband where they choose. Municipal networks do the same in towns that decide local ownership is worth the trouble.

Name the difficulty, because it is real. The density arithmetic that made rural electrification a public job makes rural broadband one. Fiber costs real money per mile when the nearest neighbor is a section away. Cooperatives need patient capital, engineers, and durable rules at a moment when the federal machinery keeps tilting toward the incumbent. The model agreement is voluntary, and Nebraska’s Broadband Office is still weighing its options. Declining it means the harder road. Haxby may be right that no cheaper satellite substitute will appear for the most scattered addresses.

Fine. Let satellite carry those addresses as the bridge. But buy the bridge with the strings on. Stop selling it as the destination.

My grandfather could ask the two useful questions. Who owns the line? Who answers when it fails? Those are the questions a subsidy must answer.

The grid running out past Gage County was never the weather. Somebody built it on purpose, with borrowed federal money and local ownership, and it is still standing. The economy is a set of choices, not the weather. The poles remember who made the good ones. All Nebraska has to decide is whether the next set of wires belongs to the people who will use them.

Build the pipe. Compete over service.
