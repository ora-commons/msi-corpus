The Bank of England is cutting staff to fund its own offices.

Here are the numbers, as The Guardian reported them on 19 November 2026. The capital scheme carries a £160m figure. The Bank says cash will come from an estimated £100m of savings in each of three years, or £300m in total, arising from 450 redundancies and other cost savings. A capital project is therefore being financed, at least in part, by reductions in the institution's operating resources.

If the three-year estimate is realised, the savings stream exceeds the scheme's headline cost by £140m. The Bank has not published an itemised bridge showing how that £300m is allocated between the building and other uses. Nor does it identify the "other cost savings" or show what they are worth. The difference is not necessarily a hidden cost. It is an unanswered allocation. A funding plan names where the money comes from. This one names 450 departures from the Bank's payroll and a category.

The same report puts people inside that funding line. 3,500 staff will remain on site at Threadneedle Street while the build runs. At the Bank's second site, 20 Moorgate, 1,400 staff face relocation, voluntary redundancy, or an offer of a post at the Leeds outpost. One member of staff, speaking on the record, put it to The Guardian this way: "It's like there is a leaving party every week."

The savings line is not idle money, and the reason has nothing to do with wallpaper. A central bank that raises its policy rate to hold down inflation pays that rate on the reserves commercial banks hold at it. The Bank also owns a gilt portfolio running to hundreds of billions of pounds, most of it bought under its Asset Purchase Facility at yields far below the rate it now charges on reserves. When the rate it charges exceeds the yield on its own portfolio, the difference does not disappear; it arrives as an operating loss, which is money out of the Bank's accounts that does not come back. That is negative carry. It is why a plan to take £100m a year out of running costs is a plan to shrink a loss the Bank is already running, and why the redundancy half of it is the part being contested. Whether negative carry justifies 450 departures is the Bank's argument to make, and it has not made it. The alternative to closing the loss is not a smaller Bank; it is a request to the Government, which is the Bank's other route to a balanced account.

The programme's paper trail is where the story is. The Bank announced the project in July 2025 and appointed its architects in November of that year. It says there will be no delays, and the evidence it offers is the appointment of architects. A project manager, a cost consultant — the firm that prices the work — and a firm of engineers were also signed up, and they have begun evaluating budgets and timescales.

Work was due to start this month. In October, with a month to spare, the building contract went to Sir Robert McAlpine. The scheme has been described as an "incredibly complex project." From announcement to contractor, the procurement sequence has proceeded. What has not been published is the detailed cost plan tying £160m to a defined scope, money set aside for unpriced work, and the final cost. The number has a title. It does not yet have an audit trail.

The main scheme has not been submitted for the consent that governs it. Threadneedle Street is Grade I listed, the highest grade of protection England offers a building, and the work must be sanctioned by the City of London Corporation and Historic England. The architectural plans have yet to be agreed with either. Anna Joynt, a senior architect and heritage specialist at the London practice of Allies and Morrison, told the Architects Journal that there may be "bones of the future scheme in place" while the wider scheme is not developed enough to submit for listed-building consent, "which needs a lot of detail."

Splitting out simpler preparatory works and obtaining permission for them ahead of the main scheme is common practice on projects of this size. It means work can start earlier and save money, "as the longer you wait the more things cost." That explains how the programme can begin before full consent. It does not supply a published estimate for the scheme as a whole. The last clause is an argument for starting earlier. It is not the project's cost estimate.

The October contract award allowed the planned start to proceed. It did not supply consent for the main scheme. Last year the Bank secured planning permission for one small section: the former governor's secretaries' offices, whose plasterboard walls are to be taken out and the space opened up. The roof of the main portico is up in scaffolding for repairs. For the rest of the 1.4-hectare site, staff must wait and see.

The comparison the Bank wants is the right warning and the wrong forecast. The Federal Reserve budgeted its Washington headquarters renovation at $921m in February 2020. The figure was revised several times before December 2024, when it reached $2bn, and the final cost is likely to be nearer $2.5bn. Construction was supposed to be finished by mid-2024 and is now expected to run until December 2027. Donald Trump threatened the Fed with court action over the overrun, and the clash with Chair Jerome Powell played out on camera.

That is a record, not a multiplier. Applying the Fed's overrun to the Bank's scheme would produce a forecast without a building-specific method, a scope analysis or an estimate of the Bank's contingency. The Fed shows what happens when a central-bank headquarters programme acquires delay, revised costs and political salience: the estimate stops being an estimate and becomes the thing elected officials fight over, and the fight is conducted on camera without anyone having read the cost plan. It does not show how the Bank's project will end. The BoE's comparison is to establish scope, consent and cost before the work starts. It has begun procurement while the main consent remains outstanding.

Much of the bill is not optional. Bailey's team, based in a grand part of the Bank known as the Parlours, must meet fire-safety, accessibility and electrical-testing rules, replace plumbing and heat the building from renewable sources, preserve the Roman mosaic floors, the stone columns and the vaulted ceilings, keep the exact floorplans under wraps for fear of terror attacks, and secure 400,000 bars of gold worth more than £200bn and £100m of bank notes in the vaults while all of it happens — with 3,500 people at work in the building throughout.

Fire-safety and accessibility work in a building the Bank has occupied since 1734 carries real costs. The Bank largely demolished and rebuilt the interior once before, in the 1920s. The Notre Dame fire, mid-renovation, is still fresh in memory. Nobody should fight that line.

The discretionary line is the one some staff hope for: a "Google-like workplace" of friendly office spaces, break-out rooms and in-house cafés, and a frontage fought over inside the Bank between the museum, which draws 100,000 visitors a year, mainly schoolchildren, and café space whose argument is that it makes the Bank appear more modern and appealing. None of that requires a Google-like workplace. None of it requires that necessary maintenance be financed through a discretionary amenity programme whose single named funding line is a payroll. The offence is the funding structure.

Vivienne Grafton, the executive director of central operations, calls the project "an opportunity to breathe new life into a legacy building, not through radical reinvention, but through thoughtful, intelligent transformation." Thoughtful. Intelligent. And still not submittable for consent.

Earlier public works supply reasons for scrutiny, not forecasts for Threadneedle Street. Elizabeth Tower was budgeted at £80m, then doubled, then rose by a further 20%. The Palace of Westminster may cost £60bn. Neither tells us how the Bank's project will end. Together with the Fed's record, they show why the baseline, scope and contingency should be public before the first invoice.

So here is the sentence that has to carry a £160m project: "All our procurement processes aim to deliver value for money," a Bank of England spokesperson said. Aim is not a number. The Bank's other formulation is that it is "considering how best to renovate the site and optimise our running costs."

A capital programme — money spent on the asset rather than on the running of it — whose early procurement precedes full consent, and whose funding plan relies on an unitemised payroll reduction, is not an optimisation that the public can verify. "Value for money" is asserted exactly where a published costed plan should stand. A published estimate gives the public something concrete to check; a concealed estimate gives critics only a target.

The remedy is dull and available. Publish the scheme, publish the consent application when submitted, publish the savings ledger and the redundancies standing behind it, and publish the estimate against a defined set of work: the budget, money set aside for unpriced work, the cost consultant's numbers and the schedule. Give the public a named starting point and a named final cost, so that anybody can check it, and check it again when the work changes.

If the Bank wants break-out rooms and in-house cafés, identify them as a separate discretionary package, with its own price and its own approval. Do not hide them inside a building bill and charge the price to staff departures. The conservation work should be identified separately and justified on operational grounds. Every redundancy should be justified separately, on operational grounds, with no building attached to it. The 1,400 staff at 20 Moorgate — relocation, voluntary redundancy or a post in Leeds — are owed the same itemised arithmetic as everyone else.

The Bank of England has £160m on the table for a headquarters whose main scheme it cannot yet submit for consent. Its evidence for the schedule is the appointment of architects, the engagement of a cost consultant and the award of the building contract. Its evidence for the funding is 450 departures and an unitemised category. It has a headline number in public and no arithmetic anyone can check.

The building belongs to the Bank. The savings come from its operations. The public is owed the arithmetic. The Governor can announce the scheme and defend the procurement in front of a select committee. He cannot make the numbers checkable. The estimate is the estimate. The Bank of England does not get to grade it.
