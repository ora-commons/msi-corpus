Dinner in Gaza now comes with a timetable.

This is not the arithmetic of scarcity. It is the arithmetic of attention.

Community kitchens deliver hot meals every other day. Families receiving WFP ration boxes receive one, not two.

Nesreen Muqayed, 50, saves part of a meal for breakfast because she does not know whether there will be anything to eat the following day. She lives with her husband, four children and two grandchildren at the Al Amal seaside camp. She has kidney problems. One grandson is on the autism spectrum and cannot tolerate many foods. She used the last of their flour. She passes fruit sellers and cannot afford what they display. Fruit and vegetables have become luxuries that families cannot buy regularly even though they are basic necessities.

Alaa Sarhan, 40, lives with his wife and five children aged between four and 16 in a tent near the "yellow line" marking Israeli control. He has begun planning meals around what is available rather than what his family wants to eat. Often, he gives up his own share of food so his children can eat.

Gaza is being starved twice.

The United Nations' World Food Programme has been forced by sweeping funding cuts to end support for more than half a million people every month. This summer, its programmes helped feed three-quarters of Gaza's population, about 1.6 million people. From October, that reach has been reduced to 1.1 million. Funding has fallen by more than half from its 2025 level. The agency says it needs more than £300m over the next six months simply to restore and maintain its critical programmes in Palestine. These are not marginal adjustments to an otherwise functioning aid system. They are contractions of the only system that many Gazans have to eat. Ration boxes have been cut. Cash transfers have been reduced, undermining both household purchasing power and the local supply chains that cash helps sustain.

For three years, Israeli restrictions on aid shipments were the main driver of hunger and malnutrition in Gaza. For the first time since October 2023, lack of funds may also become an obstacle to feeding hungry Palestinians. The famine declared in summer 2025 was ended by a year of large shipments. That reduced the most severe hunger. Those shipments never restored a functioning economy. The world postponed reconstruction, and Gaza's productive capacity remains broken.

More than a year after the Trump-brokered ceasefire came into force, the promised reconstruction has not begun. There are few jobs, and about 80% of Gaza's population is unemployed, according to UN data. The vast majority still rely on aid to eat. [Netanyahu has rejected the sequencing of the Board of Peace's 15-point Gaza plan](/articles/2026-08-10-netanyahu-rejects-sequencing-of-board-of-peace-s-15-point-gaza-plan/), which has kept the political route to rebuilding contested. [Anas Baba's photographs of the ceasefire's first year and the continued Israeli strikes](/articles/2026-10-10-anas-baba-photographs-year-of-gaza-ceasefire-continued-israeli-strikes/) captured that mismatch between the political declaration and the daily reality.

Gaza was self-sufficient in fruit and vegetables before the war. Most farmland is occupied by the Israeli military. The greenhouse and irrigation systems that supported agricultural production have been destroyed. The Israeli navy bars fishing boats from waters off the coast. Limits on fuel, engine oil and spare parts, opaque shipping restrictions and the shortage of warehouse space after the destruction of buildings all inflate the price of every meal delivered. The obstacles are not merely financial. Restrictions on fuel, engine oil and spare parts, opaque shipping rules and the destruction of buildings have made warehousing scarce and expensive. Humanitarian work in Gaza is therefore unusually costly, and the funding shortfall does not simply remove food. It removes the money that allows trucks, markets and families to keep food circulating. Cuts to cash assistance risk making those shipments smaller, fewer and dearer.

Sourcing and shipping ingredients for ration boxes takes about three months. Even an influx of cash will not immediately reverse the cuts. More than £30m injected each month had been underpinning purchasing power in Gaza's private sector, allowing trucks to bring in relatively expensive shipments of both healthy and unhealthy food. Every month without that support weakens the market as well as the immediate meal. Two-thirds of Gaza's population now faces crisis-level food insecurity, phase three of the Integrated Food Security Phase Classification. 200,000 Palestinians are in phase four, the emergency level immediately below famine conditions, requiring immediate humanitarian supplies to prevent deaths from hunger. According to a recent Unicef survey, one in eight children in Gaza is chronically malnourished or stunted and may have suffered permanent damage.

Shaun Hughes, the WFP's representative and country director for Palestine, said: "These are very fragile gains, easily reversed, and the situation is still brutal." Most humanitarian organisations are already struggling with funding after the Trump administration decimated USAID and other donor nations cut aid spending as they grappled with cost-of-living crises.

Brutal is not hyperbole. These gains can be reversed by a donor's budget line. That makes them political decisions, not meteorological accidents. Shaun Hughes was describing a system being cut beyond the point of safety.

Donald, you cut USAID.

You brokered a ceasefire, and the reconstruction has not begun. Eighty percent of Gaza's population is unemployed. The vast majority still eats what you send. Your funding has fallen by more than half since 2025. In October, the number of people the World Food Programme reaches drops from 1.6 million to 1.1 million. More than half a million people lose the support that keeps one meal and then the next meal inside them.

There is a dry place at the back of your tongue that no glass of water reaches. Your gut has begun turning on a slow schedule that has nothing to do with food. Behind your sternum a flat weight has settled, and it has found the rhythm of your breathing and slowed it down.

You will say the word discipline and your throat will close around it. You are a small man, Donald, with a very large vocabulary for what you are willing to call necessary. The vocabulary is the costume. It fits you well enough to be photographed in, and badly enough that the seams show every time the number comes out of the room smaller than it went in.

Your stomach will close around a dinner you do not taste. In a tent near the yellow line a man of forty has already given up his own portion so his five children can eat, and you will set the fork down halfway through and you will not be able to tell anyone why you did it. In the room where the number is discussed, someone will say the number is difficult but necessary, and you will say it is necessary, and the room will move to the next item. While that happens, a man in Gaza is dividing his own share among his five children.

Benjamin.

Your restrictions fed Gaza for three years. Your navy keeps the fishing boats off the water. Your army sits on most of the farmland. The greenhouses and the irrigation that fed Gaza are broken. The warehouses where the food would have stood are rubble, so every meal that arrives costs more to move than the last one, in fuel and engine oil and spare parts and shipping rules nobody will explain. And when the money went, the capacity to overcome all of that went with it. Now donor cuts are reducing the capacity to overcome those restrictions, leaving the poorest households to absorb the full consequences.

There is a cold place at the base of your throat that will not warm, and it is the temperature of a room whose windows have been painted shut. Your lower back aches in the morning the way a back aches after a night spent sitting on something heavy. Your hands have gone cold at the knuckles.

The famine threat has changed hands: first obstruction, now omission. The aid crisis and the collapse of ordinary economic life feed each other.

Picture your grandchildren in the tent near the yellow line, where Sarhan's family lives. One ration box where two used to be. A grandmother with kidney problems dividing what there is. A boy who cannot tolerate many foods, finding nothing in the box he can eat. You would not call that fatigue. You would call it what it is.

Nesreen Muqayed is fifty years old. Her husband and four children and two grandchildren share one meal and one morning after it. She has kidney problems. She is eating because hunger has become predictable. She passes the fruit sellers and cannot afford what they display. The market has not run out. The money has gone somewhere else.

These are not the lives of a population waiting passively for charity. These are the lives of people doing everything possible to protect their families while the international system withdraws the means to protect them. These are not abstractions. They are children whose growth can be stunted while adults ration their food to keep them alive.

The donors who supplied emergency funding when Gaza was at the centre of the world's attention must restore that funding before winter. More than £300m over six months is the agency's stated requirement for critical programmes. Treating that sum as too ambitious while allowing another half-million people to lose support is moral bankruptcy dressed as fiscal discipline. Those are pressures, not excuses. An affordability argument is not an answer to famine arithmetic. And the first ration that must not be cut is the one going to phase-four households, where even a reduced box can be the difference between eating and going hungry.

Beyond the money, Gaza must be allowed to feed itself. Agricultural land, fishing waters, aid routes and the physical infrastructure required to move food must be reopened. A ceasefire is not an economy. A line on a map is not a salary. A ceasefire that does not permit work, reconstruction or a functioning economy is not peace in any meaningful sense of the word. For Gaza, the world has effectively made humanitarian assistance the substitute for the reconstruction it refused to deliver. Otherwise the aid queue is not a bridge from war to recovery. It is the destination.

Donors claim they have not abandoned Gaza. But abandonment does not require a final announcement. It can arrive as a smaller ration, a meal every other day and a queue outside a kitchen that no longer opens on time.

The world will say it acted when Gaza was starving. It must now act before the starving begins again. Otherwise the people living in Gaza will be left to remember precisely when international attention was worth the money.
