There's a moment every student in Darrell K. Royal-Texas Memorial Stadium will remember. Forty-one seconds into the final quarter on Saturday, the No. 4 ranked University of Texas at Austin was down 23-3 against No. 1 Ohio State. Quarterback Arch Manning brought the Longhorns alive with a 91-yard drive that ended with a touchdown pass to wide receiver Ryan Wingo. Then a 72-yard drive, then a 73-yard drive, each ending with running back Hollywood Smothers scoring. Texas went up 24-23. The Buckeyes had 25 seconds. Texas held. Defensive back Graceson Littleton intercepted the last-second bomb, the clock ran out, the cannon boomed, and the stadium went wild.

That's how Karl Rove opened his Wall Street Journal column last week. Not with the bigotry he was about to denounce. With the game. Fourteen paragraphs about political racism against a sitting Republican candidate for Texas Railroad Commissioner, and the lede is Arch Manning's fourth-quarter drive.

I know why. I built versions of this lede for two decades. The football play is the costume — the writer cannot be the attack-dog partisan if he has already established himself as the fan first, the Texan, the normal person who happened to be in the stadium. The normalcy frame is the first technique deployed: frame-engineered relabeling applied to the author's own identity. Rove relabels himself as the fan before he relabels himself as the conscience prepared to vote for a Democrat. He came to Texas in December 1976 as a professional political operative, not as a spectator. He is the Architect. The game is stage dressing.

Then comes the rube. Bo French — UT grad, Republican candidate for the commission that regulates Texas oil and gas — had marked the win by posting a photo of celebrating burnt-orange students. Referring to the South Asian and Hispanic students in the foreground, he wrote: "I heard UT graduation this year looked like this. I didn't believe it. The problem is now obviously far worse than anyone imagined." The subtext was text. No plausible deniability, no dog whistle. Rove did not need to interpret what French meant because French had already said it.

Rove did not need to advance an argument about whether French is a bigot. He needed to select the most indefensible version of the party's populist wing and put that version in the crosshairs so his own condemnation earned maximum righteousness at minimum risk. This is strawman at its cleanest — not of a progressive position but of his own coalition. He sacrifices a pawn to protect the king.

The denunciation list followed: Senator John Cornyn called French's post "intolerance and racism" and warned "this is how great political parties self-destruct." Other legislators called it "racism, plain and simple," "disgusting," and "well below the standard set by Christ." Rove did not name them to move the argument forward. He named them so the credentialing work would be visible — the responsible conservative apparatus doing the responsible thing. Multiple-audience-targeting at the paragraph level: the elite reader sees the party's institutional leaders behaving; the political class sees the approved talking points; the populist base sees the establishment ganging up on their guy. One paragraph, three audiences, zero analytical content. Which brings us to the question Rove's column will not ask. Cui bono. Who benefits from a Republican Party in which a candidate for the Texas Railroad Commission can post a Jew-Muslim threat poll and still be the Republican nominee for a statewide office? The concentrated beneficiary is the donor class that needs the base activated every cycle to pass the agenda the donor class writes.
