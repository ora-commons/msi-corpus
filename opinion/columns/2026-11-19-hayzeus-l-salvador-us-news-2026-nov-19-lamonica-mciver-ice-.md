The Trump administration is weaponizing ICE and the Justice Department to suppress congressional oversight.

The Justice Department is prosecuting a congresswoman for doing her job.

On the sidewalk outside the locked gate at Delaney Hall in Newark, a federal agent tried to arrest Newark mayor Ras Baraka for refusing to leave. Representative LaMonica McIver had come to the facility in May 2025 with two other members of Congress and Baraka for a congressional oversight visit. They were barred from entering. When the agent moved to arrest Baraka outside, McIver intervened, according to a homeland security agent’s declaration. Federal officials say she slammed her forearm into the agent and forcibly struck another deportation officer. She was charged with assaulting, resisting, impeding and interfering with federal officers.

On Thursday, the Third Circuit declined to rehear her case. A three-judge panel had rejected her arguments in August, including her claim that the Constitution protects her from prosecution. She now faces a likely trial. McIver says: “Because of a weaponized Department of Justice, and because I dared to do my job and conduct oversight of ICE, I am facing 17 years in prison.” She [gave birth while the deadline in her federal Delaney Hall case was closing](/articles/2026-10-06-mciver-gives-birth-faces-oct-9-deadline-in-federal-delaney-hall-case/). Baraka’s charges were dropped. McIver is the only elected representative facing charges from the scuffle.

I know how easily a community can leave its moral accounting to someone else. Churches, including my own, have sometimes made institutional silence sound holy; my own community has sometimes treated migrants as labor supply instead of neighbors. I have shared in that accounting. That is why I cannot make this easier than it is. This is not neutral law enforcement. It is punishment aimed at scrutiny.

A fair accounting cannot amputate the sequence. Access was denied. An arrest was attempted. Baraka’s charges were dropped. McIver alone remains charged. That makes criminal process part of the shield. It makes the denial of access and the attempted arrest part of the prosecution built on what happened between them. This is institutional cruelty and betrayal of the public duty to oversee.

You can prosecute assault. You can prosecute interference. But you cannot pretend the arrest at the gate and the denial of access are separate from the prosecution built on what happened between them.

The machine is older than one administration. In El Paso, pre-dawn raids emptied classroom desks, and in rooms where men sorted human worth by family, my silence was one way I helped the men who carried the cuffs. The standard must not move with the party holding the prosecutor’s office. If a lawmaker from any party blocked an ICE oversight visit and assaulted an officer, I would condemn that conduct and demand due process. Put a Republican congresswoman behind that same gate for the same oversight and I would demand her release, too. That symmetry does not excuse this prosecution; it shows that the machinery of punishment, not the party in power, deserves the indictment.

Its architecture is plain: a fence, detention beds filled by contract, private operators paid when the beds are occupied, and immigration dockets that become queues. A docket that becomes a queue is not due process. It is a verdict at the end. A government that prosecutes the representative who asks what is happening inside the fence while treating the question itself as interference has chosen enforcement over truth.

The building behind that sidewalk is why to care. Delaney Hall resumed detaining immigrants arrested by ICE in 2025. At least three people detained there have died. Earlier in 2026, major clashes broke out between ICE and protesters outside the fence during demonstrations supporting a hunger strike by the immigrants held inside.

At Wednesday’s House hearing, McIver raised the conditions and the deaths. Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin said he had visited Delaney Hall and that it was in “very good condition.” He told her that her claims were false. She told him twice: “You are killing people.”

Three deaths do not settle McIver’s charges. They make “very good condition” an insult to the dead and a refusal to answer the living. Three deaths are not a condition report. A condition described by the official responsible for the system is not a condition. It is a receipt for the gap between his claim and the three deaths. He was not asked to grade the building. He was asked to answer for the people inside it. A secretary can walk through a building. He cannot restore three lives by calling the building good. This is what prosecuting the oversight while three people died at Delaney Hall looks like.

Matthew 25 does not permit the state to make a detained person disappear into a file. Jesus says, “I was a stranger and you did not welcome me” and, “Truly I tell you, just as you did it to one of the least of these who are members of my family, you did it to me.” John XXIII wrote in *Pacem in Terris* §25: “The fact that he is a citizen of a particular State does not deprive him of membership in the human family.”

The people inside Delaney Hall are not props in McIver’s prosecution. The agent who tried to arrest Baraka, the mayor who refused to leave, the representative who intervened, and the people now held inside are all human beings. Justice must ask what each person did. It must also ask what power assembled them at that gate.

On March 23, 1980, Óscar Romero faced soldiers ordered to kill their own brother-peasants and told them: “No soldier is obliged to obey an order against the law of God.” Then he named the murder and ordered: “¡cese la represión!” Stop the repression. Both sentences belonged together: conscience before the order, and the hand still open to the person who bore it.

The uniform changes; the moral question does not. Repression can wear a federal badge, use an arrest as its trigger, and threaten a legislator with prison because oversight became inconvenient. The legal machinery is directed at a public servant who went to the gate to conduct oversight and later raised Delaney Hall’s conditions and deaths. That makes the machinery more dangerous, not less.

The agent at the gate had a uniform, a chain of command, an assignment to arrest the mayor, and a choice about what to do when force met a duty. That door remains open to the agents, the prosecutors, and the officials who built the system. Stop the repression. Your conscience comes before the order.

A humane immigration policy can enforce the law; it cannot enforce it by denying meaningful review of detention, leaving death unexplained, forbidding congressional oversight, or making asylum depend on the luck of where a person crosses a border. It must require a lawful basis for detention, meaningful judicial review, independent investigation and a full public accounting of every death in ICE custody, genuine congressional oversight, counsel and fair hearings, and lawful pathways for people who flee violence and work here. Congressional overseers must be able to inspect detention facilities and leave without being charged merely for their presence; asylum claims must receive hearings instead of queues; no government may call coerced self-deportation a voluntary choice; and borders must not turn the stranger into an expendable person.

Secretary Mullin, the door is still open. Open Delaney Hall to lawful oversight, investigate the deaths, release anyone detained without lawful basis, and let Congress do its work. You do not need to love the stranger to stop treating people as disposable; begin by letting those inside speak and be seen.

Outside Delaney Hall, demonstrators gathered in support of the hunger strike inside. Outside the locked gate, McIver’s microphone waits for an answer. The gate between public oversight and private custody should open both ways.
