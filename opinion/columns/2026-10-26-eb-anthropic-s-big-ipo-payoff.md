On Monday, Anthropic rolled out the prospectus for its long-awaited IPO. It runs to more than 300 pages and repeatedly warns of financial, regulatory and legal risks. The cumulative message is not “stay away.” It is: “Here is what serious capital would be financing.” After years of hearing AI described as humanity’s salvation or extinction, Anthropic is offering investors something rarer than either promise: a candid account of what could go wrong.

There has never been a public IPO like Anthropic, with a technology this ambitious and a prospectus this candid. Its virtue is laced throughout the more-than-300-page document: Anthropic repeatedly says it can benefit humanity and solve humanity’s biggest problems. It also says AI may pose “catastrophic or existential risks to humanity.”

That is not CEO Dario Amodei’s public contradiction. It is his public consistency. Now that he is selling shares, the market can judge that consistency for itself. As a friend quips, perhaps he should ask Claude. Prospective shareholders should ask the prospectus.

The largest commercial test is whether Anthropic will generate enough revenue to cover the soaring costs required to stay at the AI frontier. But look at the starting point: Anthropic reported $16.3 billion in revenue in the first half of this year, up from $1.2 billion in the same period last year. That is not a company waiting to discover whether customers will pay. It is a company whose central challenge is scaling a business already producing extraordinary revenue.

Anthropic says its customers include more than half of the Fortune 500 companies, including the 10 largest internet firms, nine of the 10 largest pharma companies, the 10 largest banks, nine of the 10 largest semiconductor companies and eight of the 10 largest healthcare firms. The reach is not incidental. It is the operating substance behind the filing.

The prospectus says a “significant portion of our customer base consists of startups and technology-oriented enterprises that may be more sensitive to adverse macroeconomic conditions.” A major economic slowdown would test that mix. It would not erase the revenue growth or the customer reach already achieved. It would make the IPO’s timing challenging, not irrational.

Competition, meanwhile, is evidence that Anthropic operates in the market everyone wants to lead. The prospectus names Google and OpenAI as chief competitors on the frontier, while open-weight and open-source AI models “may capture a portion of the market for certain use cases, which could exert pricing pressure on our offerings or lead certain customers to adopt open-source alternatives for those use cases.”

The competitive map also includes security. Large banks and infrastructure providers may be reluctant to use Chinese models because of potential security risks. But Nvidia and Meta are pushing their own open-weight models, which can be attractive because of lower costs. Companies needn’t worry about sharing proprietary data if they use open models.

A recent report found that open models account for nearly 60% of AI use in the U.S., up from 10% a year ago. On paper, that makes Anthropic vulnerable. Read more carefully, it shows AI use spreading faster than many incumbents expected. The market is not merely becoming cheaper; it is becoming larger.

Anthropic is right that if open-weight models proliferate, it could struggle to pay for its computing build-out. Its estimate that it has a minimum of $417 billion in future payments, mainly owed for computing and infrastructure, deserves scrutiny. It is also the reason to tap public markets now. The burden is enormous. So is the opportunity to finance it.

The regulatory risk is real, but it is not the risk it first appears. The prospectus warns that “recurring narratives concerning the potential adverse societal impacts of AI, including that AI may displace or eliminate jobs, may be misused or otherwise cause harm, and may pose catastrophic or existential risks to humanity” could hurt Anthropic’s reputation and deter customers.

Mr. Amodei is a leading promoter of such narratives. That is not something to conceal. It is why he understands that companies cannot claim the benefits of AI while leaving the public to absorb its consequences without explanation.

Nor is the political risk trivial. In June, the Commerce Department imposed worldwide export restrictions on Anthropic’s Fable 5 and Mythos 5 models, effectively forcing the company to disable them. The government restrictions were later lifted, but “similar export controls could recur, could remain in place longer, or could extend to additional models or capabilities.”

That is not a reason to hide the models behind private capital. It is a reason to require full disclosure before shareholders and regulators are asked to bear the risk. The prospectus also omits that the risk of Washington expanding political control over AI will increase if Democrats win the White House in 2028. That omission should be corrected. Public shareholders should see that risk in the open, not infer it.

Anthropic names the privacy-and-data-protection burden plainly: “we are subject to complex and evolving laws, regulations, and other legal obligations related to privacy and data protection.” Chinese AI labs face few of these barriers. That is not a competitive model to celebrate; it is an argument for giving firms capable of meeting stricter standards the capital to expand. Anthropic is already submitting itself to those standards, and public capital can help it expand.

Becoming a public company will increase Anthropic’s legal exposure. By now Mr. Amodei must know that anything he says can and will be used against him by regulators and the plaintiff bar. If an Anthropic agent causes damage, the tort and securities bars will pounce. Good. Public markets are not a place to hide from liability; they are where liability becomes legible to investors.

On Friday, the owners of Elvis Presley’s music sued Anthropic, alleging that it trained its models on more than 200 of his songs without permission. They are seeking more than $41 million in damages. “Costs of defending against such litigation, regardless of merit, are considerable,” the prospectus says. Yes, they are. They are also the visible price of operating in a market where the rule of law applies to a company of Anthropic’s scale. That is a problem for the balance sheet, not a reason to pretend the risk is absent.

Given such enormous risks, why is Anthropic going public now? The cynical guess is that it is trying to take advantage of the AI boom to let employees with stock options and early investors cash out before its edge erodes, or before more risks bite. The cynicism misses the point. Liquidity is one purpose of an IPO, but capital is another. Employees and early investors can convert private stakes into public currency while Anthropic raises the money required to keep building.

An underplayed hint of strength is Anthropic’s insistence on its “public benefit purpose,” which includes ensuring “the hardest problems facing humanity are not left to market forces alone to solve.” That is not an admission against public markets. It is the argument for them. If the hardest problems will not be left to market forces alone, then capital, governance and accountability should not be either.

Anthropic will continue advocating a bigger government role in controlling AI. Investors and taxpayers, take heed—and take a seat. Public shareholders are financing a company in plain sight: the prospectus, regulators, plaintiffs and shareholder scrutiny make consequences harder for the builders to evade.

Anthropic’s IPO does not make the risks disappear. It makes them visible, priced and accountable. That is the payoff: not a promise that AI will behave, but a public-market mechanism to ensure that the people building it cannot escape the consequences of what they create.
