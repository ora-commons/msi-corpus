*A problem purchased is worth more than a problem solved.*

Russia is charging $100,000 a drone and the Pentagon is paying $2 million in Patriot missiles to shoot them down. That is not a mismatch. That is a profitable arrangement for the companies that make the Patriot.

RTX builds the Patriot interceptor. Lockheed Martin builds the missile-defense systems. Northrop Grumman and General Dynamics fill the rest of the portfolio. Their lobbyists work the appropriations committees in both chambers. The contractors do not need the system to be efficient. They need it to be bought.

Here is the arithmetic that matters to a family in my part of Georgia. A single Patriot missile that gets fired at a Russian drone costs more than some folks earn in five years. Multiply that by the number of interceptors Ukraine is burning through on a weekly basis, and you are looking at billions that are not building a school, not keeping a rural clinic open, not easing the rent squeeze on a factory worker's household. That money is a line in a federal budget, and every line in that budget is a choice.

Russia has been iterating on these drones for two years. They started with the Iranian Shahed, a slow propeller job that Ukraine had already learned to shoot down. Russian engineers added jet engines, hardened the navigation, built communication links designed to resist jamming. The latest version, the Geran-5, looks more like a missile than a drone. DroneSec chief executive Mike Monnik called it "essentially a poor-man's cruise missile." Development to mass production: roughly two years.

Both the Geran-5 and the Banderol cruise missile use Chinese jet engines, Chinese navigation computers, and Chinese communications electronics. Ukrainian wreckage analysis and outside experts confirmed the sourcing. Most of those parts are commercially available dual-use components. DroneSec estimates the Chinese jet engines cost $45,000 or less per unit. Beijing says it regulates exports with military uses. The parts keep arriving at Russian factories anyway.

A sanctions regime that cannot stop the supply chain is not a supply-chain strategy. It is paperwork.

And here is what stops me. Russia is also dropping glide bombs from warplanes onto Ukrainian positions. Thousands of them. Almost no way to stop them. Royal United Services Institute research fellow Justin Bronk said, "While the drones are a problem, by far the biggest problem for Ukraine is glide bombs." Those weapons are threatening Kyiv's ability to hold its front lines.

So the picture is this: expensive interceptors are draining budgets to stop drones, and glide bombs are hitting targets with nothing in the way. One problem eats money. The other eats people.

NATO pledged more than $40 billion over five years for counter-drone capabilities, according to Army Col. Martin O'Donnell. That is real money pointed at a real problem. It also has to pass through 32 allies and years of committee work and contractor lobbying before a single new interceptor rolls off a line. Russia will have fielded its next version before the paperwork finishes.

The problem is not that nobody can build a cheap interceptor. Companies like Auterion are working on high-speed interceptors. Merops, backed by former Google chief executive Eric Schmidt, has been deployed along NATO's eastern flank and is being redesigned for jet-powered capability. Bronk has proposed a dedicated multinational counter-drone force using cheap radar-guided antiaircraft guns and simple jet trainers that already exist. He said, "It's not an inherently difficult problem. It's just not one we're optimized for."

He is right. And the reason the system is not optimized for it is not technical. It is financial. Cheap interceptors do not generate the margins that keep shareholder returns climbing. They do not generate the donation cycles that keep the appropriations committee members comfortable. RTX does not need a $15,000 solution when it can sell a $2 million Patriot. The lobbyist does not need a problem solved when a problem purchased is worth more.

Back in Georgia, a man I know who runs a body shop was complaining last week about his insurance premiums going up again. He is paying more for coverage, more for parts, more for everything. He did not connect it to the defense budget, and I did not push him on it. But the math connects. Every Patriot that gets fired is money that does not come back to that man's town, his shop, his kids' school. The connection is not abstract. It is a budget line that somebody chose.

Eisenhower, in his farewell address of January 17, 1961, warned that the military-industrial complex could acquire unwarranted influence in the councils of government. He said an alert and knowledgeable citizenry was the only real check on that influence. He was not talking about defense. He was talking about a machinery that could mistake its own expensive habits for national security.

Sixty-five years later, the machinery is still running. It is running on the same fuel: the gap between what a weapon costs and what the company charges, multiplied by the number of times Congress writes the check, multiplied by the number of times the contractor says the alternative is unacceptable.

The $40 billion is the seed capital. The Chinese supply chain is a known vulnerability. The cheap-interceptor market is open. The Bronk proposal is on the table.

What the system is not optimized for is building what is needed at the price it should cost, and building it fast enough to matter. The defense establishment has spent decades perfecting exquisite weapons, extending development timelines, and treating battlefield adaptation as a briefing-slide virtue. Russia iterated in the field, learned from wreckage, and shipped the next version. That is the loop the West should have been running.

Kateryna Bondar at the Center for Strategic and International Studies put it plainly. "Here in the West, we tend to underestimate Russia, but they can go very fast from idea and experimentation to production. This loop is something Western militaries should learn from Russia."

The question is whether that lesson arrives before the next batch of Patriot interceptors does.

The core problem sits in the economics of interception. The attacker spends a hundred thousand. The defender spends two million. That ratio advantages whoever can produce more targets. It turns every successful interception into a budget wound, and the wound comes straight out of the tax base that funds everything else.

Russia has already crossed into NATO airspace, through electronic-warfare interference or deliberate probing. The same design logic is showing up in Iranian weapons programs and North Korean production lines. The cost gap is not Ukraine's problem. It is NATO's problem.

The question is whether NATO keeps answering a hundred-thousand-dollar drone with a two-million-dollar missile.
