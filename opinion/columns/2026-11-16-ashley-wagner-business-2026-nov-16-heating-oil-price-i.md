Trump is making American families pay six dollars a gallon to stay warm. Nikki Papadopoulos of Piscataquis County, Maine, told a reporter this month that her family keeps the thermostat at the lowest setting available, washes dishes in cold water because the water heater runs on oil, wears sweaters and jackets indoors, and is — her words — often cold in their own home. She is one of 4.79 million American households that heat primarily with oil; 82 percent of them are in the Northeast, and in Maine oil heat is half of all households, the highest share in the country. The cold in that house is a fact about a tank gauge. The tank gauge is a fact about policy.

Start with the number that decides the winter. Heating oil averaged over $6 a gallon this fall, up 74.2 percent from September 2025 to September 2026; pump gas is up about 30 percent over last year. The Energy Information Administration projects oil-heat households will pay 21 percent more this winter than last, and that projection already assumes a milder winter soaking up part of the increase. The National Energy Assistance Directors Association puts overall heating costs up more than 7 percent nationwide — [a projection my own paper covered when it landed](/articles/2026-11-11-average-us-heating-bills-projected-to-rise-more-than-7-this-winter/) — and up 16.9 percent in the Northeast, where the oil tanks are. Those are not weather reports. They are invoices, and they arrive whether or not anybody in the house got a raise.

Then the household math, which is the math I run at 11 PM. Heidi Unruh, 61, of Kittery, Maine: her costs have doubled this year, and it takes more than $1,500 to fill her 250-gallon tank, which needs a refill every 47 days in deep cold. Run it — December through March is roughly 120 days, so call it two and a half fills, about $3,750 to keep one house above freezing for one winter, against roughly half that last year. In Greenville, Rhode Island, the electrician Bob Carpenter paid $1,425 for about 240 gallons last month — $5.94 a gallon, roughly $600 more than he paid in previous years — and his electric bills have drifted from around $250 a month to around $350. His summary of the mechanism is exact: when necessities take more of the budget, there is less left for the unexpected car repair or the roof, and less left for his children to save. A cost that gets cut does not disappear. It moves — into a sweater worn indoors, into a body kept at the lowest setting the thermostat allows, into a roof that waits until it becomes a ceiling problem.

The price is not the thermometer. A trading route closed in the war in Iran; Russia pulled diesel off the market amid its war with Ukraine, and Russia exported 10 percent of internationally traded diesel in 2025; the Trump administration went looking for a deal to bring Russian diesel to the United States and has not got one. The cost landed where costs land — on the households that cannot switch. About 3 percent of American households heat with oil, and roughly half of all homes in Maine. In Piscataquis County the Papadopoulos family split wood and moved to a wood stove, cut the hot water, and turned the thermostat down to the lowest number the machine will hold, wearing layers indoors just to stay warm. Her husband got a raise. Nearly all of it went into the tank.

The people delivering the fuel say it plainly. Justin Barnes, a fuel driver in the Bangor region, has been handing residents bills much higher than recent years: "It seems like these politicians ain't doing a lick to lower the prices or make our lives easier, which is their responsibility." Adjustment is a word for what somebody else does to your budget, and no one at a kitchen table has ever used it. Unruh grew most of her own food this year and Papadopoulos split wood, and both did it well — the resilience script asks households to thrift harder, grow more zucchini, need less. Taylor Swift wrote the chorus that script hands every cold family: "It's me, hi, I'm the problem, it's me." The structural gets worn so long it arrives as character, and the instructions go with it. It still wasn't enough, because the script was never the mechanism.

Nothing about that price fell out of the sky onto Maine. Annie Lowrey wrote that "poverty in the United States is a choice" and that "stagnant middle-class incomes are a choice" — the issue is not that the United States cannot pull its people above the poverty line, but that it does not want to. Unruh puts tariffs in the same breath as oil prices, food and electricity, and says the bills were already impossibly high in Maine before Trump took office; the choice since has been to leave households alone with the rise. Between the shock and the household sits the federal heating aid that exists to catch the difference, which Congress has left at its old size while the price ran away from it. The administration set its tariff. Congress set its aid budget. Between them, they chose where the gap would land: children's bedrooms.

The CPI says four-tenths of a percent for October ([my paper ran that number too](/articles/2026-11-10-u-s-consumer-prices-rise-four-tenths-of-a-percent-in-october/)). The tank says $1,500. There is an editorial habit of calling this consumer adjustment, as though a household choosing between the thermostat and the grocery cart were exercising a preference; in Lansdale we called it cutting back on stuff the kids need. Maine, for what it's worth, already voted on this — the incumbent senator lost her seat to a logger in a midterm widely read as a repudiation of the rising cost of living. The ledger got counted at the ballot box. It still has to be paid at the tank.

My own flag, because this argument sits on where I sit: I heat a 1,400-square-foot rowhouse in Fishtown on gas, and the oil trucks do not come to my street. My thermostat arguments are about money rather than temperature. My kids have never been cold inside that house. That is the whole difference between my winter and the ones above — where turning the thermostat down is not a conservation measure, it is the plan.

The formation I came up in ran on a rule that was never written down as policy: when a family's heat got cut, the help arrived quietly and nobody's name was spoken in the hall. Dorothy Day wrote that the Gospel takes away our right forever, to discriminate between the deserving and the undeserving poor — and the list we memorized in religious ed had "naked, and you covered me" on it, cover people, keep people warm, a work of mercy centuries before anyone said "energy burden." Warming a house is shelter on that list, which makes the answers legible as a catalog: fund the [$3bn heating-aid boost an energy economist urged](/articles/2026-10-30-energy-economist-urges-3bn-heating-aid-boost-as-winter-bills-climb/), weatherize the housing stock so a cold snap stops being a 47-day emergency, and stop grading families on how little heat they can endure.

Swift has a line for the safety net these families actually hold, and it isn't LIHEAP — it's the title "You're On Your Own, Kid," which reads less like a song title every year and more like the mission statement of American care infrastructure. The lateral net is real: the neighbor with the log splitter, the sister who takes the kids during a fill-up week, the group text that knows the tank is low. I believe in it. I live on parts of it myself. But a wood stove is not a policy, and a group text cannot fill a 250-gallon tank.

In Piscataquis County the thermostat is set to the lowest number it will hold and a kid is doing homework in a jacket. That is the policy. Everything else is commentary.
