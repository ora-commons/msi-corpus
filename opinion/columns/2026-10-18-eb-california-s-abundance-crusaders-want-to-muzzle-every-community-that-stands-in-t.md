*He called it abundance. He never promised you an affordable unit.*

A renter in Los Angeles pays $3,000 a month for a studio. Proposition 45 promises her abundance. It does not promise her a single affordable unit. It promises her developers a faster permit and a quieter courthouse, and it calls the difference between those two things a housing plan.

The crusaders behind it are barreling into the November ballot dressed up as the friends of renters and renewable-energy builders. Read what they are actually pushing in Sacramento: a procedural straitjacket that will silence every Californian who has ever used the state's environmental review laws to defend their neighborhood. Prop. 45, marketed as a permitting panacea, would muzzle the very communities, workers, and greens who have held reckless development accountable for more than half a century.

The 1970 California Environmental Quality Act is not the bane of California. It is the spine. It mandates environmental review for major projects. It opens the process to public hearings and written comment. It gives neighbors a chance to challenge inadequate plans in court. It is why California has led the nation in clean-air disclosure, coastal protection, and public participation in land use. This is not a "bane of businesses." This is democracy in action.

Strip CEQA's teeth and you do not get more housing. You get more pollution, more displacement, and more wealth extraction for the few.

The homeowners who file objections to a housing plan they fear would clog their streets or crater their property values are not "weaponizing" the law — they are using it exactly as it was written to be used. The building trades who litigate to enforce prevailing wage are not "shaking down" developers — they are shielding a generation of construction workers from a race to the bottom that has already gutted middle-class trades. The greens who challenge water-storage schemes and energy mega-projects are not the obstacle to a modern California — they are the only faction insisting that "modern" not poison the groundwater or scorch the desert. They did not invent NIMBY litigation; they defended the only forum where a grandmother in West Oakland or a family in the San Joaquin Valley can fight a warehouse dropped on their block.

A gas station proprietor who sued a decade ago to block a competitor across the street from adding new pumps was using CEQA exactly as it was designed: as a check on runaway development on his own block. Even bike lanes, food banks, and solar farms get ensnared by the law, and that is precisely the point — a serious environmental review process protects every kind of community asset, including the ones the developers' lobbyists claim to love.

Proposition 45 would silence all of them. Localities would have to "specify all information" developers must submit at the outset of environmental review, locking reviewers out of asking new questions mid-process — tidy on paper, until a developer's lawyer argues later that anything not on the pre-set checklist is foreclosed. Opponents would get 45 days to file public comment on a draft environmental impact statement: 45 days to read a thousand-page filing, hire a consultant, organize a neighborhood, and respond in writing. After approval, opponents would have only 30 days to file suit. Courts would have 270 days to rule. And judicial review would be narrowed to whether the approval "complies with objective existing laws" — language carefully drafted to strip judges of any meaningful oversight. Even when a judge finds the environmental review fatally flawed, the project goes forward; the judge is forbidden from blocking it "en toto."

This is not streamlining. It is a loaded gun pointed at community input.

The California Building Industry Association — that is, the developers who would be the direct beneficiaries — estimates Prop. 45 would shrink new-home construction costs by $75,000. Of course it would. Remove the cost of environmental review, public comment, and the right to sue and you remove the friction that turns a developer's profit into a community's expense. Read the estimate again: it is the developer's savings, projected as the renter's gain. Not one dollar of it is required to reach the renter paying $3,000 for a studio. There is no mechanism in Prop. 45 that requires any unit built under expedited review to be affordable to anybody.

The "yes" coalition lines up exactly as you would expect: renewable-energy trade groups, affordable-housing nonprofits, and California's local NAACP — organizations that should know better than to endorse a measure whose procedural rollback will outlast any near-term housing gain. The no coalition — greens, building trades, and plaintiffs' attorneys — is derided by the pro-45 campaign as caring only about "leverage." The trial bar is not a leverage machine. It is the courthouse door. These are the people who have shown up, year after year, to defend the right of ordinary Californians to be heard in the planning process. Their commitment is the point, not the scandal.

The no campaign has been bankrolled in part by Jane Fonda and Wendy Schmidt, wife of Google co-founder Eric Schmidt, who with her husband purchased a 56,500-square-foot Los Angeles home last year for $110 million. The proponents want you to snort at that and move on. Don't. Disqualifying the opposition because they are rich is the oldest trick in the donor-class playbook — and by that logic the CBIA's $75,000 estimate should be disqualified too, except the proponents want you to take that number at face value. When the pro-developer side finds itself attacking two funders instead of defending Prop. 45's text, the text has already lost.

Opponents warn that Prop. 45 would grease the way for data centers and warehouses. Supporters retort that neither is listed as "essential." Read the list: housing, transportation, water, broadband, renewable energy, schools, healthcare facilities, wildfire mitigation. The category is broad enough to swallow nearly any project a major builder wants to fast-track. And what is not vague at all is that Prop. 45 caps judicial review at "whether the approval or authorization complies with objective existing laws" and forbids judges from blocking projects "en toto" even when they find fault. That is the gift to data centers and warehouses, whether or not they are named in the text.

A recent Public Policy Institute of California survey found voters split, with the measure leading by a large margin among those making less than $100,000 and renters, while trailing among the affluent and homeowners. Read that carefully. It leads among the very people who will bear the consequences of a gutted CEQA — renters and working families who do not own the land, do not hire the lawyers, and cannot fight a warehouse alone. It leads because the proponents have sold "abundance" as a synonym for "cheaper rents." It will deliver cheaper projects.

Gov. Gavin Newsom has not taken a position. Good. A governor who has spent four years presiding over a housing crisis he cannot solve should not be stampeded into endorsing a ballot measure that hands the fix to the same developers who caused the crisis. Let him ask the proponents one question: where in Prop. 45 is the affordable unit, the renter protection, the community-benefit agreement? When they cannot show him, he will know what the opposition already knows.

California's real liberals — the ones who actually live with the consequences of unchecked development — should say no to the corporate-backed "abundance" agenda. This is not abundance. It is extraction with a marketing budget. Prop. 45 is the quietest, most procedural form of dispossession ever put on a California ballot.
