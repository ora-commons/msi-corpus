Somewhere in this country, a child was sent home from school this year for a dress-code violation. The federal government counted it — one incident among millions, in the most comprehensive civil-rights survey it runs. Then it sat on the count for six months.

The 2023-24 Civil Rights Data Collection — 49.7 million students across nearly 97,000 public schools, the fullest picture the federal government possesses of how American schools treat American children — was supposed to be released long ago. It arrived on Aug. 31, six months late, stripped of the First Look report that ordinarily tells a lay reader what the numbers mean. The administration held the numbers, then handed them over in a form it hoped nobody would read.

Ask who benefits from sitting on a country's civil-rights data, and you have already begun to read the report. The six-month silence is the first finding in it — the government's own conduct as a data point.

Start with the genuinely good news. Public preschool enrollment climbed 12 percent, to roughly 1.5 million children. California's universal transitional kindergarten alone added nearly 36,000 four-year-olds in a single year. Colorado and New Mexico are pouring money into the same expansion. That part of the story belongs to the states that finally ponied up.

In the same window, K-12 enrollment fell almost 1 percent. Birthrates explain some of it. Not all of it. Families are pulling children out of the public system — into homes, into private classrooms — and the children leaving are not a random sample, and anyone pretending otherwise is not paying attention.

The system that calls itself the great equalizer is expanding at its earliest beachhead, where four-year-olds cannot yet opt out — and hemorrhaging families by middle school, when parents have learned the price of staying. Parents are voting with their feet. It is a verdict.

Now the part the country would rather you skip past.

Out-of-school suspensions hit 2.5 million students. In-school suspensions hit 2.3 million. Both numbers are up by roughly 100,000 from two years earlier. Lost instructional time: more than 14 million days. A decade ago, the count was 11 million. The number is going the wrong way.

The offenses are truancy, repeated tardiness, dress-code violations. The operating theory of American school discipline is still the removal of the child from the institution that is supposed to lift the child. The lost days are not a side effect. They are the policy's content. The framing that treats the consequence as the crime has it backward: the consequence is not the child's crime. It is the system's own work product.

Black students lost more than four times as many days to out-of-school suspension as white students. Four times. Not two, not three. Four. This is not a data point. This is a country, every year, making the same call in thousands of buildings it calls schools.

Nineteen percent of public school students now have a documented disability, up from 17 percent two years earlier. Boys make up nearly two-thirds of that population.

Part of that rise is the system finally working. Identification rates that lagged for decades are catching up to actual prevalence. Children who used to be punished for behaviors caused by undiagnosed needs are now being seen and served. Concede it: that is progress.

Then read the rest of the data. Students with disabilities are suspended at disproportionately high rates — and when they miss school, they lose the very services that make school work for them: counseling, physical therapy, the structure of the school day. They are also among the least likely to be in Advanced Placement classes. Not because most of them cannot do the work. Because the system has decided they are not expected to, and it operates accordingly.

For the first time since the 1970s, the collection broke out the teaching workforce by race and ethnicity: 75 percent white, 9 percent Black, 11 percent Hispanic. The share of Black and Hispanic teachers has been climbing for two decades. That is not the scandal. That is progress, and it deserves to be named.

The data also exposes the real civil-rights scandal: more than 720,000 students attend schools where fewer than half of the teachers meet state certification requirements — and 73 percent of those students are Black or Hispanic. We are sending the least-certified adults into the buildings serving the children this country has decided don't matter, and we are doing it on purpose.

The fix is not a quota war. The fix is certification. Hire, train, and certify the teachers. Then watch the mix sort itself out.

Then there is the part where the government tells you what it knows by refusing to say it.

The Biden administration required schools to collect data on nonbinary students. The Trump administration collected the numbers and then refused to publish them, citing an executive order directing the federal government to "recognize two sexes, male and female." For the 2025-26 cycle, every question about nonbinary students has been removed from the survey. So have the questions about whether students are harassed on the basis of gender identity. The federal government is no longer asking.

The defense is that this is a sliver. Roughly 3 percent of children ages 13 to 17 identify as transgender, including nonbinary youth, by one UCLA analysis. A sliver. But civil-rights data has never been a majority business. The entire instrument exists to count the people the majority forgets — the Black child losing four times as many days, the disabled child losing therapy to a suspension, the nonbinary child erased from the federal ledger. The size of the category has never been the test. Discrimination against a sliver is still discrimination. The researchers who spent years building this data infrastructure say they have been blinded. The country has not been spared. It has been blinded.

If you stop counting, the cruelty becomes invisible. If you stop counting, the next report will say nothing happened — because nothing was measured.

This is not an isolated decision. The same playbook is running across the federal data apparatus. At the Commerce Department, the scientific-integrity apparatus has been quietly gutted — the language about political interference stripped from the charter. The pattern is consistent: when the data shows what the administration does not want shown, the data is delayed, scrubbed, or deleted. The Education Department is running the same play.

The instinct to scrutinize institutions that affect children does not stop at the schoolhouse door. It reaches into the safety records of consumer AI products aimed at teenagers and the addictive features of the platforms competing for their attention. None of these institutions — not the platforms, not the schools, not the administration that sat on this report — are above the data. They are only as good as the country's willingness to look at it.

The data does not belong to any one lobby. The 49.7 million students do not, either. Read it again, without the script, and a different picture emerges: a system failing some of its children badly, serving more of them than the loudest readings will admit, and living under an ideological project whose move, when the numbers turned against it, was to stop counting.

We will read this survey again in two years. If the next one is as delayed and as scrubbed as this one, the country will know that the administration is no longer pretending to look.
