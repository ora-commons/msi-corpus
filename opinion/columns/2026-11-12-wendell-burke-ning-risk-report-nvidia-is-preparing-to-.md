The world's markets now flinch at the private conduct of one man, and the system that made his fidelity a macroeconomic variable calls itself the freest economy in history. The Wall Street Journal's [Morning Risk Report](https://www.wsj.com/risk-compliance-journal/the-morning-risk-report-nvidia-is-preparing-to-oust-ceo-jensen-huang-324bfbd1) opens with Nvidia's board convening Wednesday to weigh the future of Jensen Huang after reports of two extramarital affairs — one with an employee, one with a suspected Chinese spy — a departure that would, the report says, bring down the curtain on the man who stoked the global artificial-intelligence boom and became, in its words, a geopolitical star, leaving one of the world's most valuable companies in need of a new leader. [As my colleagues reported, he has since resigned after the reports surfaced](/articles/2026-11-12-nvidia-ceo-jensen-huang-resigns-after-reports-of-two-extramarital-affairs/), and the shares [sank seven percent before the bell on the rumor of it](/articles/2026-11-12-nvidia-shares-sink-7-premarket-as-reports-put-ceo-jensen-huang-s-tenure-at-risk/). The same pages carry United Airlines barring personal AI agents from its booking systems over privacy exposure and Federal Aviation Administration rules, and a Texas oil-field outfit disclosing settlement talks with the Justice Department and the Treasury over payments it likely made to Mexican cartel affiliates. It is a clean ledger of what the arrangement now costs.

"There is very little that can be done to prevent a global recession should the AI boom collapse." That is Frederic Neumann, HSBC's chief Asia economist in Hong Kong, quoted in the same newsletter, in the paragraph beneath the scandal — the paragraph that ought to worry every household in America. Squeezed by the energy crunch of the war, the world has leaned on American data-center investment to power otherwise lackluster growth; a sustained slide would punish exports in economies with few other signs of life, crush the mom-and-pop investors who piled into technology stocks expecting bumper returns, and land on governments with overstretched budgets and no room to spend. The cable scroll will spend the week on the spy and the marriage. The scandal that deserves attention is architectural: we built an economy in which one man's weakness is everyone's exposure, and we did it in the name of freedom.

Before any of that, the honest word, because a man who traded paper owes the truth about paper. The build-out is not a phantom: data centers are poured and wired, copper and turbines are bought, the payroll is real. Huang built a company that makes real things — the silicon, the boards, the engineers' labor are as concrete as anything my county ever shipped — and there is something wholesome in the instinct that sent ordinary households to buy shares in it. Broad ownership was never the enemy. A man with a little money invested in the productive work of his country is nearer the distributist's ideal than a man with nothing but a paycheck and a subscription. Manias have always been how this country front-loads its infrastructure, and my own county carries both halves of the proof: in the 1850s the La Crosse & Milwaukee took farmers' mortgages for stock in a railroad that died in the Panic of '57, leaving families holding paper for a line that never came near them; a half-century later the Chicago & North Western actually built — the yard, the roundhouse, the division point that turned Adams into a town of union wages. Speculation is how a young country pays for its railroads in advance. It is possible the mania for compute leaves behind fiber and power the way the rails left depots. I concede every word of it. My objection is not to the boom. It is to the shape we let it take: one man, one stock, and everyone's savings underneath.

Conserve what, exactly? For two generations the movement that promised to conserve told the working country that its security lay in owning shares — the index fund, the retirement account, the tech stock climbing — while it presided over the closing of the things those people used to own outright: the family farm under contract to the snack-food giants, the Main Street storefront leased to an absentee, the roundhouse at Adams with the yard running light. I used to trade the corn before it was planted, and I know precisely how little the men in that building think about the men in the field. The paper I sold was a claim on a crop someone else grew, a thousand miles from the dirt. That is what a share is. Nobody here owns Nvidia; a household holding its stock owns a claim on a boardroom it will never see, governed by men whose private decisions set the price of its savings. They put a casino in every man's pocket and called it liberty, and then they were surprised when the whole country woke up one morning dependent on a vote it had no part in.

Consider what kind of machine requires a founder's bedroom to be a market variable. The tradition I belong to built its whole architecture against exactly this. Burke called society a partnership between those who are living, those who are dead, and those who are to be born; the point of the institutions that partnership erects is that no single man is load-bearing. Men sin; men die; men fail. A parish survives its pastor, a town its mayor, a cooperative its manager, because the institution carries the rules and the memory so that the people inside it need not be incorruptible for the thing to hold. What forty years of the movement's triumph built instead is a set of enterprises where the institution is the man — succession as crisis, the board in emergency session over his conduct. Hayek taught conservatives that the market's whole virtue is dispersed knowledge, that no central mind can gather what millions know in fragments. This economy runs the lesson in reverse: the one fact every portfolio on the planet needs on Wednesday morning is what one boardroom decides. That is not the market Hayek described. It is a planned economy with a ticker, and the plan lives in one man's calendar. Brandeis said it plainly a century ago: we may have democracy, or we may have wealth concentrated in the hands of a few, but we cannot have both. A man is not an institution. Men are exactly what institutions exist to outlast.

That dependency is the actual scandal, and it is old news to anyone who has watched a place hollow out. The last *Twin Cities 400* pulled out of Adams on July 23, 1963. The Wisconsin Rapids mill idled on June 9, 2020, and took near a thousand paychecks with it. What replaced that work was lake lots and a golf resort and the 48.6 percent labor-force participation rate measured in 2017 — the lowest of any county in this state — and into that hollow the country's households were told to pour their savings, so that an energy shock and a board vote in California could reprice their retirement in the same week. Pius XI called it a grave evil to assign to the higher what lesser organizations can do; we have assigned the economic life of whole nations to a handful of firms and one board. My Church teaches that property is real and good, and that the earth was given for all — ownership is stewardship carrying a social mortgage, not a wall. After a family's needs are met, Leo taught, what remains owes something to the poor. No paragraph of that tradition imagined an arrangement in which the widow's retirement requires a stranger in California to keep his vows. Property in many hands absorbs a shock. Paper in every hand transmits it. These forty years we did not distribute ownership. We distributed exposure.

And what does the county hold of the boom itself? Nothing but the risk. The data centers rise where the power is cheap and the fiber runs; nobody routes them across the sand plain. What came here instead was a golf resort built on the old barrens with Chicago money — several hundred real jobs, the biggest employer this county has had in a generation, in what its own founders called the weakest local economy in the state. The median age here is in the mid-fifties, better than a third of the county past sixty-five, nearly half our houses sitting empty most of the year because they belong to someone else's weekends. A town surviving as other people's playground and a world growing on other people's machines are the same condition at two scales: the working place converted to an amenity, the working economy converted to a sentiment. When the sentiment breaks — and Mr. Neumann's own sentence concedes almost nothing could stop a collapse from becoming recession — it will arrive at the co-op counter and the implement dealer in towns that never saw the boom, exactly as the mortgage bust of 1857 arrived on farms that never saw the railroad. The paper is paper until the day it is invoices.

Say plainly what I will not take, because both camps are already reaching. Those who built this arrangement will call the concentration freedom and let it ride until it breaks. Their opponents will promise a federal backstop — Washington guaranteeing the paper so the widows are made whole and the machine that endangered them is preserved. Both cures feed the disease. A guarantee that socializes the losses of levered sentiment is the servile state with a retail window; it teaches every household that ownership means exposure someone else will eventually absorb, and it hands the few a permanence no free people should grant. Concentrated state power is the same disease in two coats, and the post-liberals who diagnose this tumor with such skill always reach for another one to cut with. And no — the answer is not a bureau of virtue either. Institutions are not built to abolish sin; they are built to survive it, which is the one thing this week's arrangement cannot do. Nor do I blame the households that bought: they were told that buying *was* owning, by the same people who had spent forty years making sure the real thing was never on offer. What I am asking for is smaller and harder. Claims you can hold.

The old answer still has an address. On East Lake Street in Friendship stands the Adams-Columbia Electric Cooperative, the largest rural electric co-op in Wisconsin, some 31,560 member-owners across twelve counties, governed by a member-elected board, its surplus staying with the people who use it. When ice takes a line in February, the crew that comes out is from here, and no member's retirement depends on a founder's vows. The form has a lineage. Capper-Volstead, in 1922, let farmers bargain as farmers against concentrated buyers without being prosecuted as a cartel. The Rural Electrification Act of 1936 lent to member-owned cooperatives and lit up a countryside the investor utilities had judged unworthy — roughly one American farm in ten had central-station electricity in the mid-thirties; a generation later, most farms did. In La Farge, west of here, Organic Valley binds together more than sixteen hundred family farms and calls itself the largest organic farmer-owned cooperative in North America. Land O'Lakes, the credit unions and the mutuals all run on the same grammar — one member, one vote, no absentee, no extraction. In Spain, Mondragon runs on some seventy thousand people, most of them worker-owners, with a pay ratio near five to one. None of these can be brought down by one man's weakness, because none of them is one man.

I will not romanticize the form. A cooperative is slow. A cooperative is boring. The share in the elevator will never compound like the chip, and a cooperative can decay into a manager's fief if the members stop watching — a 2022 review of eighty-three cooperative enterprises found only about one in ten had fully degenerated, but the vigilance is the price of the form. You cannot get rich leaving one. That is the point, and it is also why the century's money went the other way. It is harder here than almost anywhere: a county with no capital to spare, nearly half its houses empty most of the year, its young people gone somewhere with work. I make no pretense that a village cooperative etches wafers; some things take scale. The question was never scale. It is whether the world's security may ride on the conduct of one man inside the scale. Chesterton's shorthand was three acres and a cow. The substance is older than Chesterton: property in many hands, decisions at the lowest level that can carry them, security that does not require anyone's virtue. The disposition my tradition calls conservative — the familiar over the speculative — has an address. It is lit at night on East Lake Street, where the owners are the members and the members are neighbors. The boom may yet lay its fiber and light its racks; let it. The answer to an economy hanging on one man was never a better man. It was more hands on more deeds. Leave the town its life, and the town holds its own future in its own hands — not in a room in California where a board meets on a Wednesday to decide a future no one in this county was invited to vote on.
