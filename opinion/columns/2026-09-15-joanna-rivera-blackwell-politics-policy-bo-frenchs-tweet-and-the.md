*He called it an admissions problem. He meant the faces.*

Bo French racialized a crowd and marked nonwhite people as a problem.

The Republican nominee for Texas railroad commissioner posted a photograph of nonwhite University of Texas fans celebrating a football victory and wrote that UT graduation “looked like this,” adding: “The problem is now obviously far worse than anyone imagined.”

That is not a neutral observation about university admissions. It is a public candidate marking the visible presence of nonwhite people as a crisis. The photographed fans became evidence in a racial complaint. Their faces were turned into a warning.

I know the machinery because I once watched it work from inside the church. We learned to place a respectable noun over an ugly implication and then defend the noun: standards, heritage, merit, order, tradition. The question is never allowed to remain where the photograph put it. Who is being named as the problem? Who is being invited to understand that their presence has become intolerable?

The admissions rule French invoked does not rescue the post. Texas law requires 90% of UT freshmen to be in-state, leaving 10% from elsewhere. That is an administrative rule about residency. It is not a racial classification, and it does not establish that a crowd of nonwhite fans represents foreign control of the university. French used the rule as cover for the point he was actually making: that the university’s nonwhite presence itself signals something gone wrong.

The distinction matters. A student’s race is not proof of residency. A student’s appearance is not proof of citizenship. A cheering crowd is not an admissions dataset. The 90% rule cannot turn a racialized insinuation into policy analysis merely because the insinuation is dressed in the language of enrollment.

The community made vulnerable here is not abstract. It includes the fans whose photograph was used without their consent as political evidence. It includes international and out-of-state students who are told, directly or by implication, that they do not belong in the institution they attend. It includes Texans of color—students, graduates, workers, and voters—who must now hear a nominee for statewide office describe people who look like them as “the problem.”

A candidate for railroad commissioner does not possess a private megaphone. He is asking for public authority. His words travel ahead of his office. A student who sees that photograph understands the message even if French later retreats into arguments about demographics: your face can be used to accuse your university; your presence can be described as a worsening condition; your belonging can be made provisional by a man seeking statewide power.

Sen. John Cornyn condemned the post as intolerant and racist and asked whether Republican officials would condemn it or allow it to become normalized. That is part of the record. The relevant question is not whether Cornyn’s rebuke was politically convenient. The relevant question is whether the rebuke accurately named the breach. It did.

The breach is not that French raised a question about admissions. Public institutions may be questioned about residency rules, enrollment, tuition, academic standards, and access. The breach is that he selected a photograph of nonwhite people, described their visible presence as a “problem,” and then attached an admissions rationale after the racial meaning had already been made plain.

Matthew 25:35 says, in the words of Jesus, “I was a stranger and you invited me in.” The surrounding passage does not praise the stranger as a demographic abstraction. It places the stranger among the hungry, the thirsty, the sick, and the imprisoned—people whose treatment reveals what a community is. In Matthew 25:40, Jesus says that what is done to “the least of these” is done to him.

The legalist reading narrows the stranger until the verse becomes harmless: the stranger means someone acceptable, someone inside the right boundary, someone whose presence does not trouble the settled community. The plain-language reading refuses that narrowing. The stranger is the person whose belonging the powerful are tempted to question. The text does not give religious or political insiders permission to turn that person into a warning sign.

I taught passages like this for years while leaving their social force at the church door. I could speak warmly about hospitality and then accept a public language that treated some neighbors as contamination. That was not a failure of vocabulary. It was a failure of obedience to the text as plainly read.

The same discipline applies to James 2:1–4, where the church is warned not to show favoritism by honoring the well-dressed while humiliating the poor. The passage names the act before it names the excuse: partiality is judged by whom the community elevates and whom it makes to stand aside. The problem is not that a community notices difference. The problem is that it converts difference into rank.

French’s post does exactly that. It converts the racial appearance of a crowd into evidence of institutional decline, then uses a residency rule to make the conversion sound administrative. That is racial coding in its most familiar public form: the speaker need not say the forbidden sentence if the photograph, the noun, and the insinuation will say it together.

No one needs to decide what God finally intends by this episode in order to name what happened. The record is sufficient. A statewide nominee publicly marked nonwhite people as a problem. A rule about in-state admissions was used to launder that marking into policy language. The people displayed in the photograph were made to carry the political cost.

For the student from abroad, the student from another state, and the Texan of color who sees the post and wonders whether the candidate would govern them as a neighbor or as an intrusion, this is not a tempest in a teapot. It is a warning about who is permitted to belong.

And for those of us who still know the words to “They’ll Know We Are Christians by Our Love,” the standard is not difficult to locate. We do not need another argument about whether the photograph was strategically useful. We need to ask whether the people in it were treated as neighbors or as material for a campaign. On that question, the post has already answered for itself.
