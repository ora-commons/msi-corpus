On Nov. 25, The Wall Street Journal reported that high costs and private-sector opposition had constrained incoming congressional candidates and leaders seeking full Medicare for All, leaving their early proposals short of the comprehensive care described on the campaign trail. A Progressive Change Institute analysis of 64 winning battleground House and Senate Democratic candidates found roughly 20 percent campaigned on Medicare for All, while 60 percent campaigned on a Medicare Buy In or public option. More than a dozen incoming supporters of full expansion were unlikely to overcome President Trump’s veto. In July, a congressional hearing centered on single-payer legislation even as [the uninsured ranks grew](/articles/2026-07-22-progressive-democrats-hold-medicare-for-all-hearing-as-uninsured-ranks-grow/).

Sen. Bernie Sanders proposed a [four-year transition](/articles/2026-11-18-sanders-lays-out-four-year-medicare-for-all-transition/). In year one, Medicare’s eligibility age would fall to 55, public coverage would extend to all children, and dental, vision and hearing benefits would be added. The age would fall to 45 in year two and 35 in year three before public coverage extended to all Americans in year four. Private insurers would be barred from selling coverage that duplicated the government’s benefits.

Hakeem Jeffries endorsed universal health coverage for children, expanded dental, vision and hearing benefits under Medicare, and lowering the eligibility age, though he did not specify how far. Earlier this year, he said he did not support Sanders’s Medicare for All proposal. Sen.-elect Abdul El-Sayed favored guaranteed public coverage for all Americans while allowing private and employer-sponsored insurance to coexist, though how the two would operate together remained unclear. Democratic leaders placed incremental steps on [the new Congress’s agenda](/articles/2026-11-23-democratic-leaders-put-medicare-for-all-steps-on-the-new-congress-agenda/). No consensus had formed on how far public coverage should extend.

Under full Medicare for All, a government-run program would replace most private coverage and guarantee comprehensive benefits without premiums, deductibles or copays for covered services. The federal government would assume responsibility for financing care now paid by employers, households and private insurers. That private insurance market covers more than 200 million Americans, and federal data cited by the Journal placed spending on private health insurance at nearly one third of U.S. healthcare expenditures. A 2022 Congressional Budget Office projection estimated that lowering Medicare’s eligibility age to 60 from 65 would add $155 billion to federal deficits over six years, assuming the change took effect in 2026. Dr. Ezekiel Emanuel, the oncologist who helped craft the Affordable Care Act, named the barrier: “Healthcare is a $6 trillion industry.” Moving it to the federal budget, he said, was “a major, major barrier.”

Health insurers and pharmaceutical companies helped finance lobbying and advertising against Medicare for All through the Partnership for America’s Health Care Future. The U.S. Chamber of Commerce separately launched Protecting Americans’ Coverage Together in 2020 with other business groups. That coalition opposed Medicare for All, a public option and a Medicare Buy In. Lexi Branson, the Chamber’s vice president for health policy, said, “Americans want more healthcare, not higher taxes, fewer choices, and government control over their coverage.”

El-Sayed sought Trump’s support by posting about “TrumpCare for All” and sharing mock-ups of a gold TrumpCare card bearing the president’s name. Trump said Tuesday that he planned to meet with El-Sayed to discuss possible cooperation. “He’s a smart guy,” Trump told the New York Post. “Maybe there’s some place in the middle that we could go.” Days earlier, he said, “Now, can it be what they want? No, but it can be a combination of what we all want.” Republican Sen. Rick Scott of Florida said El-Sayed’s push for Medicare for All would “destroy the Medicare program.”
