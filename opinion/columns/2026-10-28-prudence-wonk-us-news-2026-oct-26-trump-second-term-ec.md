Trump is laundering Wall Street’s boom as national prosperity while households pay the price.

At the pump, the denial becomes $4.32 a gallon. At the mortgage table, current rates are above 7%. In the payroll ledger, 181,000 jobs stand against 2.2 million the year before. At a rally in Montana, Trump reduced the whole ledger to: “We’re richer now. We don’t have inflation.” The unemployment rate is just over 4%. The S&P 500 has soared. Those facts are real. Trump’s conclusion that they describe a national economy without inflation is false.

With the midterms days away, the president’s pitch is that the economy is working and the prices are calm. The receipts describe a narrow boom surrounded by household strain.

Here are the numbers. Start with the number most favorable to his claim. The unemployment rate has remained just over 4%. That is the administration’s cleanest labor-market fact. It does not measure how many people employers are hiring, what prices have done since 2021, or whether a household can absorb a surprise bill.

Nearly two-thirds of Americans told a recent Harris Poll that the country is experiencing a recession. A third said one surprise bill could sink them financially. Americans also told pollsters they were having trouble saving. The unemployment and market figures are true. The poll is also a real measurement—of household strain, rather than of the technical recession indicator. None of them, separately or together, establishes Trump’s national claim.

Look at the inflation line closely, because the rally claim is technically defensible in isolated figures and practically beside the point. Inflation peaked at 9.1% in June 2022, a 40-year high. It was 3% when Trump took office in January 2025 and 2.3% by April, the lowest since 2021.

The decline was already underway before Trump entered office. The defensible sequence is narrower than his pitch: the tariff-driven price increases interrupted that progress, and the Iran-related oil shock lifted inflation again. After many tariffs were paused and courts found some unlawful, inflation eased to 2.4% in January and February 2026. Then the war with Iran hit the oil markets. Inflation jumped to 4.2% in May and has hovered above 3.4% since.

Here is what the rally leaves out. When the rate of increase falls, the price level does not fall back. The aggregate price level rose cumulatively over those nearly six years even as the annual inflation rate fluctuated. A lower inflation rate means prices are rising more slowly, not that earlier increases have been refunded. One sentence measures the speed; the other measures the accumulated bill.

Trump won in 2024 promising to lower inflation and undo the damage he said he had inherited. The data show progress before he took office, a tariff-driven reversal after he did, and another reversal after the oil shock. They do not show a sustained reduction attributable to his policy. The price level did not fall back.

Gas is where the inflation rate meets the consumer, and the proof arrives fastest at the pump. Soon after the war with Iran began, the Strait of Hormuz—the route through which a fifth of the world’s oil normally passes—was blocked. Prices surged around the world. The United States is a major oil producer, and it made no difference to the driver. The average price of gasoline was $4.32 a gallon in mid-October, according to the American Automobile Association, nearly $1.30 higher than a year earlier. In Texas, an oil-producing state, a gallon ran $3.81, against $2.64 a year earlier. In California, which imports much of its fuel, drivers were paying $6.21.

And 83% of Americans told the Harris Poll that gas prices were making a dent in their current finances. More than four in five.

Then there is the job market, where the reassuring headline is hiding a freeze. Unemployment has stayed just over 4% while separations remained low. Gross hiring—the number of workers hired before layoffs, quits, and other separations are netted out—slowed sharply. Payrolls added 181,000 jobs in 2025, against 2.2 million in 2024. Economists call it a “low-hire, low-fire” economy: employers neither hire nor dismiss workers as tariff uncertainty and higher supply prices make expansion too expensive to bet on.

The unemployment rate is calm. The payroll flow is not. A 4% unemployment rate describes workers retained, not workers being brought in.

Now the asset side, because “we’re richer now” comes from here, and the gains are real for the people who hold the assets. The S&P 500 has continued to soar since Trump took office, recovering from the dips around his initial tariff announcement and the early days of the Iran war. Economists largely credit AI investment: technology companies spending billions on datacenters and the chips that power them. The boom is not evenly held. The top 1% of Americans hold nearly half of all stock-market investments.

Economists call the result a K-shaped economy. The upper arm of the K is a portfolio compounding. The lower arm is a paycheck stretched against $4.32 a gallon and a grocery bill reflecting years of cumulative price increases. That is the difference between owning the assets and filling the car. “We’re richer now” is a sentence about the upper arm, delivered to a country living on the lower one.

Then came the rate that quietly broke for households. When Trump entered office in January 2025, the Federal Reserve was in the middle of a series of interest-rate reductions. Officials said they were seeing inflation cooling and slower growth in the labor market. The policy rate stood at 4.25% to 4.5%, a point lower than a year earlier.

Tariff uncertainty made the Fed pause its reductions. It resumed cutting in fall 2025, then held off for months while the Iran war worked through the oil markets. Here is the tradeoff erased by the rally: slower hiring argues for lower rates; inflation above 3.4% argues against them. The president was still demanding lower rates. In September, the Fed hiked for the first time since 2023, by a quarter point. The Trump-appointed chair, Kevin Warsh, gave the reason: “inflation is too high and has been for too long.” That is not a partisan objection. It is the president’s own appointee contradicting him in his own voice.

The mortgage channel reaches different households in different ways. Existing fixed-rate borrowers are not repriced merely because the market rate rises. Prospective buyers and borrowers seeking a reset or refinancing face current rates. For those households, the increase is direct: 30-year mortgage rates dipped below 6% in February, the first time since 2022, and are now past 7%. New mortgages and refinancings are priced above what borrowers paid in the spring. For fixed-rate borrowers, the pain does not arrive through an immediate reset of the existing payment; it reaches housing through affordability and credit conditions.

Rents move through a separate, slower channel. Higher landlord financing costs can make new apartment construction less attractive and can feed into rents, though the timing and magnitude are less certain than the direct reset on a mortgage. The Fed’s September hike did not raise rent checks automatically. Its effects reached housing through financing, construction, and property decisions.

The policy answer begins with refusing the split: defend price stability without pretending a frozen labor market is full employment. Report growth together with the price level, hiring flows, mortgage rates, and household financing costs—not GDP alone. Then “boom” must survive the entire record.

That brings us to why the campaign is talking about almost anything else. Only one of 54 Republican economy ads in the closest races mentions Trump, according to [the linked advertising analysis](/articles/2026-10-23-only-one-of-54-republican-economy-ads-in-the-closest-races-mentions-trump/). The observed pattern supports an interpretation that the party is running on its economic pitch without attaching the president’s name to the advertisement. It does not establish who made that choice or why. The result is an economic message without a presidential signature.

Trump’s claim in Montana is false as a statement about the national economy, even if it describes gains concentrated among asset-rich households. The top 1% are richer; they own nearly half the market. The rest of the country is staring at $4.32 a gallon, a mortgage rate above 7% for new and reset loans, and a job market that has stopped adding at the earlier pace.

By election day, the economy will have one growth rate and one bottom line. The stock index is the banner. The household ledger is the bill. That is the laundering.
