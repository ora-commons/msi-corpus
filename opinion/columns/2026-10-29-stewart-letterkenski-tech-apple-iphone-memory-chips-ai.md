Scott Nelson stopped his dirt bike in the Southern California desert, pulled out a rifle and shot his old iPod. Then he kept the broken pieces on his desk. Nelson sold flash storage to Apple — the chips inside iPhones that hold your photos, your music, your apps — and for two decades he was the man on the wrong end of every deal. His employer, Kioxia, had no leverage against the world's biggest buyer of flash. Apple squeezed every cent, forced suppliers to open their books, and audited their factory floors down to the output of individual machines to make sure nobody else ever got a better price. Nelson, it will surprise no one, used Android phones.

Those shattered iPod pieces sat on that desk as the only version of the story where he lost. Then AI datacenter buyers quintupled the price of flash in a single year — TechInsights tracks the jump — and the fragments turned into a trophy. They should be. What they document is not a vendor dispute. It is the autobiography of a company that treated the most boring line item on the bill of materials as a private money-printing press, and the moment the press jammed.

Run the arithmetic one more time. Flash cost $68 a gigabyte in 2005, fell to 5 cents by 2023, and now sits above 30 cents. That is a 550% jump. On the iPhone 17 Pro, the extra storage Apple sold a buyer for $200 — many forked over $200 for an extra 256 gigabytes, and as much as $800 for an extra 1.75 terabytes — cost the company $15 at the time and costs $66 today. Apple booked more than 80 cents of gross profit on every dollar of iPhone and iPad storage upgrades through 2025, and nearly 80 cents for Macs sold since 2010 — a number so obscene it reads like a typo. Roughly 100 million buyers a year, IDC's estimate, walked into it at an 85% margin, pushing total upgrade profit past $10 billion. In 2023, when flash bottomed out to a nickel a gigabyte, Bernstein Research estimated the entire flash industry lost $19 billion in operating profit while Apple cleared $14 billion selling that same silicon onward. The suppliers built the factories; Apple collected the rent.

This was never a margin story about Apple's costs. It was a story about Apple's power, and the power had a machinery. The relationship stretches back to 2001, when engineers at Toshiba — Kioxia's predecessor — showed two Apple executives a 1.8-inch, five-gigabyte hard drive they had developed but did not know what to do with. Apple bought exclusive rights, put it in the iPod, and went on to become the industry's dominant customer, prepaying $1.25 billion for flash supply through 2010 from five companies. Suppliers built entire factories on Apple's patronage. Apple repaid them by refusing deliveries when prices fell and leaving them holding piles of unsold chips. Contracts demanded "most favored customer" clauses that guaranteed nobody else paid less, enforced by audits so relentless that Apple negotiated a volume discount with Deloitte, its own auditor. When you audit the auditor at a bulk rate, you have built something. It was not a partnership.

When Apple caught Micron selling flash cheaper to another customer, it won an arbitration settlement worth tens of millions of dollars. When Western Digital had the gall to charge a reservation fee for chips Apple never committed to buy, its executives were marched back to Apple's offices a year later to hand over a check, told to pay Apple to test the merchandise — with no obligation for Apple to buy anything — and then escorted out by security. Kioxia's people joked they were Charlie Brown and Apple was Lucy with the football. Around the end of 2015, Sandisk refused Apple's request for more supply because it already had customers willing to pay more; a person familiar with the call says Blevins threatened retaliation, promising to have Sandisk's chief executive, Sanjay Mehrotra, "crawl on his knees" back to Apple. When flash tightened around 2017, Blevins circled the date of Western Digital's reservation fee on his office whiteboard, photographed it, and texted it to an executive with a promise of payback.

The architect of the arrangement was Tony Blevins, Apple's head of procurement until 2022 — the "Blevinator," a nickname suppliers used the way you name a tornado. He scheduled back-to-back supplier meetings so rival sales teams would run into each other, stoking competitive tension. Sales reps packed nutrition bars for sessions that ran past midnight without a dinner break. And Blevins, the one man who operated the machine, walked out of Apple in 2022: the muscle memory of the squeeze retired with him.

So the schadenfreude in Santa Clara this summer, where suppliers traded satisfaction in private at the industry's biggest conference, is earned. "There's no shortage of satisfaction among suppliers now that the balance of power is shifting," says Walt Coon of TechInsights. Meta, Microsoft and Amazon have signed long-term Kioxia contracts at higher prices — the kind of commitment that protects a supplier's profits if the market turns — which means the suppliers now have a guaranteed alternative buyer whose cost accounting treats flash as a rounding error on a datacenter rather than one of the largest single line items in a phone's bill of materials. Hyperscalers buy double Apple's share of the market. Two years ago Apple bought nearly twice what all four of them combined did. That reversal is the whole story: Apple's leverage was never ingenuity, it was volume, and volume is exactly what AI ate. Apple never needed contracts like those, because Apple never had to. Apple made everyone else eat the risk.

Which is why Apple's position here is worse than a bad quarter. We flagged the trap back in [Apple faces unavoidable price increases as AI demand drives up memory chip costs](/articles/2026-06-21-apple-faces-unavoidable-price-increases-as-ai-demand-drives-up-memory-chip-costs/) — the squeeze was foreseeable, and procurement was warned. According to people familiar with its practices, Apple ignored analysts' warnings because executives believed they knew this market better than anyone. Tim Cook's "100-year flood" framing is the tell: right metaphor, wrong victim. A flood is something that happens to you, and a company that thinks it is being acted upon cannot see that it spent twenty years ensuring suppliers would have no reason to protect it. Apple is now expected to spend $25 billion on flash this year for roughly the volume it bought for $6 billion in 2025, and every lever Washington pulls to tighten that same market — the administration is [readying memory-chip tariffs to expand US production](/articles/2026-09-24-trump-administration-readies-memory-chip-tariffs-to-expand-us-production/) — lands on the side of the squeeze, not against it. It cannot audit hyperscaler contracts. It cannot dictate most-favored terms to a supplier that has Meta on a multi-year commitment. It cannot refuse delivery and leave someone else holding custom chips, because the custom chips are now spoken for.

Nor can it simply pass the cost through and be done. Apple has already raised iPhone prices an average of 13% — including older models that historically got cut, part of a broader wave as [device makers raise prices to offset memory costs](/articles/2026-09-23-device-revenue-climbs-as-makers-raise-prices-to-offset-memory-costs/) — and the storage upsell, the single highest-margin transaction in consumer hardware, is now the part of the machine most exposed to cost inflation. And when a device finally runs out of space, the machine charges again every month for iCloud — which only proves how thoroughly the model was built on engineered dependence. The consumer's answer to a 13% increase is to keep the phone another year. That is the question the quarter turns on, and it is the wrong question for Apple to be asking at all. The right question is what a company does when its most reliable profit center turns out to have been borrowed from suppliers who were always going to want it back.

Apple's official response was a monument of corporate composure: "We've worked with many of our suppliers for decades, and we deeply value their partnership over the years." The translation: we need them now. As Bernstein's Mark Newman puts it, "It's been a love-hate relationship between Apple and the flash industry." Apple sustained the manufacturers for years, Newman said, but its power to cap prices, while great for shareholders, left suppliers feeling "screwed." Love-hate is generous. For two decades, Apple treated the companies that make the guts of its products like wallets to be emptied: open the books, eat the unsold inventory, beg at the negotiating table — all so Apple's customers could pay prices that made its own suppliers wince. The suppliers took it because Apple's volume kept their expensive factories running. It was a dependency Apple mistook for a one-way street.

Flash is a commodity, commoditized by the world's most sophisticated buyer of commodities — and a commodity with one desperate customer is only cheap right up until the day it isn't. The day it wasn't arrived this year. Scott Nelson keeps the shattered pieces of that iPod on his desk. They used to be a trophy of how much Apple could take from the people who made its products. These days, they are a reminder of how much Apple is about to pay. Somewhere in California, a broken iPod is still on a desk, and for the first time since 2001, nobody at Kioxia is laughing at the joke.
