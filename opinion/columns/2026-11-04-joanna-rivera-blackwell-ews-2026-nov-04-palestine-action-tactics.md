The British government has criminalized conscience and called it terrorism.

Let me show you the scene, because the scene is the argument. This week the Supreme Court of the United Kingdom opened the appeal of Huda Ammori, a co-founder of Palestine Action, against the Home Office's decision last year to proscribe her organization as a terrorist group — the same shelf where the state keeps al-Qaida and Boko Haram. Outside the court on Wednesday, police arrested at least twenty-five of the people who had come to hold placards. The placards were variations on a single sentence: "God help us authors who support Palestine and Palestine Action." The sentence belongs to Sarah Helm, a journalist and author married to the prime minister's national security adviser, and the police examined her tweet and ruled that it did not meet the criminal threshold for supporting terrorism. Then they arrested Sir Jonathon Porritt — a man who advised the then Prince of Wales for more than thirty years and once directed Friends of the Earth — for holding the sentence with "authors" swapped for "CBEs." Others held it with "normal people." With "professors." With "Jews." With "holocaust survivors." With "bartenders." One of the last taken away held the version that reads, "God help us who are not married to the UK's national security adviser who support Palestine and Palestine Action." An elderly protester was carried off in a chair. One sentence. Lawful in one mouth. Criminal in cardboard.

There is a reason those last two phrases were chosen for placard board in this particular country, and I will say it plainly: within living memory, a European state began sorting its citizens with lists and labels, and Jews were first in the file. The protesters put that history on the cardboard the police were already carrying out of the square, and the arrests did the proving for them.

Inside the court, Raza Husain KC, appearing for Ammori, put the record before five judges: "Palestine Action never advocated for violence against the person and the home secretary has never advocated — and could not advocate — to the contrary." The uncontroverted evidence, he told them, shows that the suffragettes, anti-war groups, environmental groups, and animal-rights protesters used tactics similar to or more extreme than Palestine Action's and were never proscribed. He argued the ban is a disproportionate interference with speech and protest, and that it has already reached past the organization itself: the entire Palestinian solidarity movement, chilled, regardless of any connection to it — journalists, academics, artists looking over their shoulders at a statute built for the al-Qaida years. The Court of Appeal, whose ruling is under appeal, had answered that the group's tactics had "little or nothing in common with the suffragettes … or Iraq war protest groups." The five judges will hear the case through Thursday.

The Home Secretary's counsel, Sir James Eadie KC, defended the ban as lawful and proportionate, and fairness requires me to state his strongest facts plainly: four people have been convicted in connection with the Palestine Action raid on an Elbit Systems factory at Filton, near Bristol — one for grievous bodily harm, with a terrorist connection formally found — and in August the United States Treasury [sanctioned Palestine Action as a global terrorist organization](/articles/2026-08-26-treasury-sanctions-palestine-action-as-global-terrorist-citing-uk-attacks-since-/). Property damage is real. Injury is real. But mark what is being defended in that courtroom, because it is not the crimes. Nobody defends property damage there, and no one needs to: the ordinary criminal law prosecuted it, and it produced convictions. A court can punish what a person did. What is being defended is the category — a legal instrument that does not punish an act but re-labels a movement, so that membership and public support become offenses in themselves. Prosecution is aimed at conduct. Proscription is aimed at allegiance. That is the whole difference, and it is the difference between a justice system and a loyalty system. A state may charge a crime as a crime; that path was open yesterday and remains open tomorrow. What the state cannot do honestly is shelve property damage alongside organizations that murder civilians, tell the court the ban never touches words, and then arrest the citizens who repeat a sentence its own police cleared.

And the state's own counsel gave the five judges the reassurance: "As a matter of law, the proscription of PA [Palestine Action] does not prevent public expressions of support for the Palestinian cause or opposition to Israel and to the Israel Defense Forces, or demonstrations targeted at Elbit." That assurance was delivered across the street from officers carrying out of the square the citizens whose public expression of support was a sentence on cardboard. Read the record plainly, because the record is the argument: the crime, in practice, runs inverse to proximity to power. The woman inside the security establishment may say the words; the bartender may not. You may love the cause. You may not stand too near its conscience. Last month a payment processor [choked off the fundraising for this very appeal](/articles/2026-10-02-stripe-blocks-palestine-action-court-challenge-fundraise-over-us-sanctions/) under those same sanctions. The conscience now has to raise its legal fees through channels its adversary approves. The label does not stop at the protest. It pursues the courtroom.

I have to be plain about where I am standing when I say all that. I taught Romans 13:1 off a laminated handout for fifteen years — "Let every person be subject to the governing authorities" — in Sunday-school rooms and women's retreats across the South, and I cannot remember one Wednesday night when I assigned the chapter that comes before it. In my classrooms and my women's-study workbooks it was a standing order to bless whatever the state did, because in our circles the state and the Lord's order wore the same uniform. Submission was the lesson. The verse was the badge. Dissent was disorder. I am one of the people the submission-machinery worked on, and I am telling you how it worked.

Because here is what the chapter before it says. Romans 12 ends, "Be not overcome of evil, but overcome evil with good" — do not repay anyone evil for evil. The disciple's posture is non-retaliation, and Romans 13 carries that thought forward into how a disciple lives under a magistrate: taxes paid, respect given, a ruler Paul calls "the minister of God" — a minister, which is to say a servant held to the justice the rest of the letter demands, not a god to be obeyed into silence. Read whole, Romans 12 and 13 are about the disciple laying down the sword. The legalist reading takes verse 1 alone and hands the sword back to the state with a blessing on it. And Paul wrote that letter as a free man, to a congregation in the capital of an empire that would, within a decade, be arresting believers for the name they carried; the letters he wrote from custody name him "the prisoner of the Lord" (Ephesians 4:1). Submission was never silence. I taught it as silence, and I have said in print that the teaching was wrong. The same Bible that carries the submission verse carries Peter's answer when the authorities ordered the preaching stopped: "We must obey God rather than any human authority" (Acts 5:29). Both verses are in the book. The machinery teaches one and loses the other.

Now let me show you the passage the machinery loses hardest, because it could not be more on point this week. Exodus 1. Pharaoh gives a lawful command, through the proper chain of command, to Shiphrah and Puah, the Hebrew midwives: kill the boys at the birth stool. It is the king's order. It is the law of the land. The midwives "feared God; they did not do as the king of Egypt commanded them, but they let the boys live" (Exodus 1:17). And then the text does the thing the submission-machinery cannot survive. It does not rebuke the lawbreakers. It says God "dealt well with the midwives" — and read this slowly — "because the midwives feared God, he gave them families" (Exodus 1:20–21). When Pharaoh demands an accounting, the midwives mislead him to his face, and the text lets that stand too. The first act of civil disobedience recorded in Scripture is disobedience, by women, against a lawful order, and Scripture records God's blessing on it by name. I am not claiming to know where God stands on any British courtroom or any American sanctions list — that knowledge is not mine, and I will not pretend otherwise. I am telling you what the passage says in plain English, and that a lifetime of teaching told the pews the Bible says obey, when the Bible's own story about a king's lawful orders says otherwise and blesses the resisters for it.

That is the chasm, and I will name it without decoration. The Evangelical submission-machinery reads a Bible that has already ruled on the question of a believer under an unjust edict, and it reads the ruling backwards. It keeps Romans 13:1 and loses Exodus 1, Acts 5, and the midwives. The women in the pews were never the problem — I sat beside them for thirty years and they are the finest people I have ever known. The machinery is the problem. Machinery that blesses the state was built to keep the blessing flowing, and it will bend any verse into the shape the blessing requires.

The machinery loses one more passage hardest of all, and it is the one the pews need this week. "I was an hungred, and ye gave me meat: I was thirsty, and ye gave me drink: I was a stranger, and ye took me in … I was in prison, and ye came unto me … Inasmuch as ye have done it unto one of the least of these my brethren, ye have done it unto me" (Matthew 25:35–36, 40). This is the judgment scene, and the King does not open it with a doctrinal examination. No creed is recited. No citizenship is checked. No flag is saluted. The criterion is named one item at a time: fed the hungry, welcomed the stranger, clothed the naked, visited the sick, came to the imprisoned. Not the innocent prisoner. Not the prisoner whose cause history had since vindicated. Came. Whatever was done to the least was done to him, and whatever was withheld from the least was withheld from him. The judgment of the age to come turns on proximity to suffering, voluntarily assumed. I was raised on the reading that fences all of that off — "least of these my brethren," we were taught, meant only persecuted fellow believers, and the older translations' "my brethren" helped the fence stand while the newer renderings, "brothers and sisters," quietly take it down. The passage was domesticated into a charity verse, private generosity, nothing a nation could be judged by — because the plain reading judges nations. That fence is the interpretive machinery working exactly as it was built to work: the text says the criterion of judgment is solidarity with the prisoner; the machinery renders it a suggestion about casseroles.

Now set the plain language beside the statute. Under proscription, public support for Palestine Action is a criminal offense — a placard is an arrest. And the chapter says the King will judge the nations on whether they stood with the imprisoned. I do not claim to know God's verdict on any government or any movement; the text does not hand me that authority and I will not counterfeit it. But I can read what the text sets as its criterion, and a state that outlaws public solidarity with the imprisoned while assuring everyone that love of the cause remains free has placed itself on the wrong side of that criterion's plain language, whatever its courts conclude about proportionality. The prophets saw the arrangement a long time ago: "I hate, I despise your feast days … Take thou away from me the noise of thy songs … But let judgment run down as waters, and righteousness as a mighty stream" (Amos 5:21–24). Worship from a people whose courts grind the accused is a stench. The condemned offering is not the incense. It is the silence.

And mark what the same state venerates while it defends the ban. The suffragettes — the window-smashing of 1912, the arson and the bombs left in empty buildings, Emily Davison beneath the King's horse at Epsom in 1913, the hunger-strikers force-fed by a government that called them criminals and hysterics — are now the respectable dead, the right side of history, the veneration every democracy performs for dissent once the dissent has won. Their arson was prosecuted as the crime of its day, and the sainthood arrived only after the victory — the label is written by the winner and then rewritten by the museum. The lawyers told this court that the suffragettes used tactics similar to or more extreme than the proscribed group's; the lower court answered that there was little or nothing in common. Both cannot be true. The establishment of 1913 had no statute for what those women were doing, so it had psychiatry and prisons. The establishment of this year has the statute, and the statute is doing the work psychiatry once did. Britain's proscription list grew through the al-Qaida years under the Terrorism Act 2000, a roster built for organizations that kill. The label "terrorism" is younger than the veneration, and it is doing older work: it is what power calls the conscience it does not want to answer.

The American half of this operation carries its own religious plumbing. The Treasury designation came from an administration whose evangelical bloc reads Israel into the last-days charts of the Scofield Reference Bible — a theology assembled a little over a century ago out of John Nelson Darby's diagrams and Cyrus Scofield's notes, younger than the telephone — and Palestine Action was the group blockading the factories that feed the war that theology blesses. I do not claim anyone at Treasury consults dispensationalist charts before signing a designation; I am mapping the economy the label serves, and the map is not subtle.

And because the text does not check which party holds the palace before it rules, the midwives' sentence binds my own side with equal weight. If a government of the other persuasion had proscribed a movement I once funded — and I have funded movements — I would have cried persecution before the first gavel fell, and I would have been partly right: the crimes would have deserved prosecution, and the proscription would still have been an offense against conscience. If a government I were glad to see in office filed its critics under "terrorism," Exodus 1 would say this week exactly what it says next week. The category is not faithful when convenient and unfaithful when not; it is simply unfaithful. The test does not bend toward the cause I happen to hold. Neither does the silence. The pulpits on both sides of the Atlantic have had more than a year to say something about a state that criminalizes standing with prisoners, and I have mostly heard other subjects. The state told us what kind of instrument it has built by how far the instrument reached — to a placard, to a CBE, to a bartender.

The five judges will rule in their own season, and I will read their judgment like anyone else, ready to be corrected by it if the record requires. But whatever they decide about a statutory category, the women at the birth stool are not going anywhere. They have outlasted Pharaoh, every empire that copied Pharaoh's paperwork, and every pulpit that taught their story as a children's lesson about obedience. They are still in print, on the same page as the submission verse that was supposed to have settled them. So I leave you where a column like this ought to leave you: at your own kitchen table, with the Book open and the news open beside it. Some of you will read Matthew 25 this week and close it quickly, the way I closed it for years, and I am not going to shame you for that — the road is longer than a column and I am still walking it myself. The statute is new. The plain language is old — it has sat on your shelf for two thousand years, patient, waiting to be read by someone with the nerve to believe it means what it says. Read them aloud at your kitchen table this week, honey, and then read them again on Wednesday night. Read it before you decide which of them gets to call the other terrorism. "I was in prison, and ye came unto me." The Bible is not proscribed. Not yet.
