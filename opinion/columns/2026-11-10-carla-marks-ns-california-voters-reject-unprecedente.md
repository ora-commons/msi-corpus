California did not reject Proposition 40 because a tax on wealth is economically impossible. Billionaires and their allies made the rejection possible with a funding gap wider than ten-to-one, then tried to lock the door behind them. The [union’s conditional pledge to return in 2028](/articles/2026-11-10-union-leader-dave-reagan-pledges-another-california-billionaire-tax-for-2028/) and the [one-time 5% tax on more than $1 billion in net assets that lost](/articles/2026-11-10-voters-reject-one-time-5-tax-on-wealth-of-californians-with-net-worth-above-1-bi/) are not two unrelated stories. Money bought the first fight and bought an attempt to shape the next one.

California did not begin with a settled moral objection to taxing wealth. It was handed one by the people with the largest advertising budget.

The receipts are blunt. The Service Employees International Union–United Healthcare Workers West spent more than $30 million trying to pass Proposition 40. Sergey Brin, other technology executives, and billionaires poured hundreds of millions into defeating it. Opponents outspent supporters by more than ten to one.

Earlier polls showed modest support, with stronger backing among Democrats. Then opposition advertising reached Californians through television, cellphones, social feeds, and mailboxes. It portrayed the tax as a threat to ordinary retirement savings. Public opinion soured.

Cash is not an economic argument. Sometimes it does not need to be.

Of course, the budget hole is real. Trump signed Medicaid cuts into law last year. The union estimated Proposition 40 would raise $100 billion over five years to offset them. It would have created the nation’s first modern tax on wealth in a country where most individual taxes are assessed on income rather than assets.

And yes, a one-time charge on net assets has genuine design questions. Assets can be hard to value. Complex finances can be hard to untangle. Such a levy could alter investment decisions. Newsom warned that it could weaken California’s competitiveness. The opposition called the measure a “reckless experiment” and said it had more thoughtful solutions.

Those objections deserved answers. They do not explain a ten-to-one spending advantage.

California also had a real political split. The state Democratic Party endorsed Proposition 40 in August. Gavin Newsom, Xavier Becerra, the state’s largest teachers’ union, unions representing firefighters and construction workers, Planned Parenthood Affiliates of California, and groups representing doctors and health clinics opposed it. That fracture widened the opposition. Money did the rest.

Now walk me through the retirement-savings claim. Slowly. Which paragraph taxes a 401(k) as such? None. One time. Five percent. Net assets. More than $1 billion. A household below that threshold paid zero. The measure applied only to residents with more than $1 billion in net assets.

The advertisement put every retiree in the frame. The bill named the payer.

That does not make the competitiveness concern false. It makes the advertisement a poor account of the bill. That is politics, not economics.

Some opposition money also went toward two other ballot measures intended to undermine Proposition 40. One would add spending rules and audit requirements. The other would ban wealth taxes. One campaign. Two locks. Defending fiscal balance by banning future choices is not balance. It is gatekeeping.

The result is not a clean referendum on tax design. A policy dispute is supposed to feature arguments; by the end, this one featured receipts.

To turn one campaign result into a permanent economic law, the opponents have to show that no broader coalition and no recurring structure could ever win. One $30 million campaign losing to hundreds of millions proves that this campaign was outgunned. Rule out the coalition, or stop calling advertising a verdict on economics.

Money beats organization when organization is one check.

Rejecting Proposition 40 did not make the Medicaid cuts disappear. The projected $100 billion in revenue will not arrive. Becerra and the lawmakers still have the hole.

The cost did not vanish. It moved from a projected windfall into a budget hole. The advertising did not replace the revenue. It helped prevent the revenue. The care does not become less necessary because the advertisement worked.

So what do we build? Not a louder rerun of the same campaign. Regan has promised another ballot measure in 2028 if Sacramento does not solve the healthcare cuts by then. Good. But that pledge should be for institution-building, not a rerun.

California needs recurring revenue on the scale of the failed measure’s five-year projection. It should replace the one-time charge on net assets with recurring revenue from capital gains: require owners of very large unrealized gains to pay tax annually on the increase in value, credit that payment against tax due when the asset is sold, and defer hardship cases where the gain cannot yet be realized. That taxes gains, not principal. The payer is visible. The payment recurs.

California already has the rails: Medi-Cal and Covered California. Use them. Expand Medi-Cal. Add a public option through the state marketplace. Automatically enroll and renew eligible residents so losing a job does not turn access to care into a form hunt before the appointment.

Recurring revenue pays for the plan. Automatic enrollment gets the patient to the appointment.

A public option is competition with a floor. Private insurers can keep serving customers who want them. Nobody has to abolish private insurance. They just can no longer rely on the absence of a public alternative when they compete for customers and manage risk. The floor is public.

Collective bargaining has a separate job. Give hospital and care workers power to bargain over staffing, pay, schedules, and safety. Hospitals, clinics, and home-care agencies should not be able to undercut one another by starving their workers. Wages and standards should be negotiated across an industry, not one employer at a time.

American union bargaining covers only about 11 percent of workers. The Nordic arrangements cover roughly 80 to 85 percent. Those countries have their own strains, but the machinery gap is real. That is why this is harder here. We cannot vote a bargaining system into existence and call the election results a mandate.

Do not ask bargaining to finance Medi-Cal. Recurring revenue does that. Bargaining gives workers a voice in whether the building can keep the appointment from becoming a hallway.

If voters still want a wealth tax, its proceeds should seed a state public-wealth fund rather than disappear into one budget hole. Alaska put oil revenue into its Permanent Fund; the fund now pays residents annual dividends from investment earnings. Public wealth is not a Scandinavian import with a fake passport. California does not have an oil windfall remotely like Alaska’s, and pretending otherwise would be silly. A public fund would not replace $100 billion overnight. Its point is recurring ownership, not a magic first-year check.

The opposition’s great institution is money. Ours is supposed to be a coalition. Health-care unions, patients, and community organizations can build it member by member, rather than asking one labor organization to finance the entire argument with one enormous check.

I’m not against markets. I’m against letting private money buy the public rules that decide who gets care.

A tax campaign is a transaction. Governing is recurring. Build recurring revenue, a public option, automatic enrollment, a bargaining table, a public fund, and a coalition that sustains them between elections. Then hold the election.

The kind nobody photographs. Everybody uses.
