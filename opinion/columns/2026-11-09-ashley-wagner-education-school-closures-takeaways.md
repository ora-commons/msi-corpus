Congress and school districts are sacrificing children to chase phantom savings.

At eleven at night, my kitchen table holds the same two numbers it has held since March 2022: $2,400 a month for childcare for Eva and Ben, and $8,800 in combined take-home pay for David and me. Childcare is 27 percent of the money that actually lands in our account, before the mortgage, the utilities, the food, the rest of it. Those are our numbers, not a claim about every family — the national case rests on federal data, not my checkbook. But the household underneath them is the one my parents' life was built to protect, and I cannot rebuild it: they raised three kids in Lansdale on my father's postal supervisor salary, sent all three through St. Stanislaus and Catholic high school, paid off the house. David and I earn more in real terms than they did combined, and we cannot give two children what they gave three. A child is never merely a seat, and the school-closure crisis is that same gap arriving at the public door.

The count is not in dispute. Big Local News at Stanford University counted 1,156 U.S. school closures last academic year, the highest number since the pandemic. An Associated Press analysis of federal education data collected by Stanford economist Thomas Dee and sociologist Jaymes Pyne found public-school enrollment fell by more than 1.4 million students — roughly 3 percent — between fall 2019 and 2024, while the total number of schools declined only to about its pre-pandemic level. The buildings have not closed as fast as the rolls have shrunk, so the closure wave is chasing an enrollment decline it did not cause. Lower birth rates are the primary driver, and that is true and it matters. But the rest of the pressure has policy authors: intensified federal immigration enforcement, which [our prior reporting traced through ICE deportations accelerating enrollment declines](/articles/2026-11-06-ice-deportations-accelerate-enrollment-declines-at-us-public-schools/), and the post-pandemic expansion of private-school vouchers and homeschooling, which pull children and their per-pupil funding out of the system. Demography set the pressure; policy decides who pays. Fewer children are not a budget strategy.

How does a district "save" money by closing a school? It buses the students somewhere else, pays severance to pink-slipped staff, and keeps paying to secure the empty building — and research cited in the AP's reporting shows those costs often eat the savings entire. The missing variable in every closure plan is the cost of closure. Fewer students and fixed buildings are a real arithmetic problem, and consolidation can preserve teachers and concentrate resources when it does not strip children of their school, their ride or their instructional time. But calling closure a savings plan is false when the money does not vanish from the ledger — it moves from the operating budget into a bus route, a termination line, and a longer day for a child whose family never consented to the "right-sizing." Close the school, and the budget does not erase the work. It sends the work home.

Bellwether and WestEd estimate declining enrollment could strip schools of $11.5 billion in state revenue annually by 2030-31, prompting layoffs, program cuts and more closures. And the closure itself manufactures the next revenue hole: displaced families choose charters, private schools, neighboring districts or homeschooling, and every child who leaves takes a share of state funding along. The "saving" creates the loss; the loss demands more cuts; the cuts drive more families out. That is a public-school death spiral built into the budget model, and none of it is a savings strategy. Lydia Rainey of the Center on Reinventing Public Education has already told us what comes from the state side: "States aren't about to open the coffers and swoop in with additional funds to save schools."

Vallejo shows what the spiral looks like as an administrative plan. The working-class district near San Francisco served nearly 18,000 students in fall 2006; it serves about 9,300 now. Since 2025 its leaders have closed five campuses — a quarter of the district's schools, including [three elementary schools shut right after the district exited state oversight](/articles/2026-11-09-vallejo-district-exits-state-oversight-then-closes-three-elementary-schools/) — and placed up to one-third of all staff positions at risk to address a $36 million deficit. Divide the deficit by the students still enrolled and you get roughly $3,871 per child, before anyone counts the busing, the severance, the security or the learning that gets lost. At Glen Cove Elementary, weeks into the school year, a 32-child class lost its first-year teacher to an immediate resignation; the district plugged in a long-term substitute and moved on.

Superintendent Rubén Aurelio calls this "right-sizing a district that was built for significantly more kids," with resources concentrated on the children who remain. Fine. That is what the spreadsheet says. But watch what the spreadsheet does not count. Concentrating resources means busing a ten-year-old into a stranger's classroom with 31 other kids in it. It means Vallejo parents posting their houses for sale to reach a district that still has a school. The district calls it concentration of resources. It is concentration of harm.

None of this is a fable about one shrinking California city. Cleveland, Houston and Philadelphia are staring at the same arithmetic, and Philadelphia is my city. About 45 percent of K-12 funding comes from local sources, mostly property taxes, so fewer students can mean less revenue while the buildings, buses and boilers stay fixed. That is the trap: it is easier to close a school than to change the financing system that made the closure look necessary. And the children absorb what the budget cannot see. Marisa de la Torre of the University of Chicago Consortium on School Research points to studies showing displaced students often suffer long-term drops in reading and math, worst when they land at a low-performing school. "It takes a while to recover, if they recover at all."

Congress has its own line in this ledger, and it is a drain, not the statistical cause of the closure wave: Congress underfunds the federal promise while districts absorb what remains. It wrote IDEA in 1975 and authorized paying 40 percent of the extra cost of special education. The Congressional Research Service put the actual federal share at 12.7 percent in 2021-22 — a $23.92 billion annual shortfall — and it was still under 13 percent in 2024-25. The shortfall is bigger than the entire Title I appropriation. It does not appear as a federal line item. It appears as a district program cut, a larger class, a longer bus route or a closed school.

Anne Helen Petersen writes in the introduction to *Can't Even* that burnout "isn't a personal problem," and that it "will not be cured by a productivity app or a face mask." Neither is a closure. A parent handed a longer commute, a new building, a 32-child class and the bill for a district's "right-sizing" is not an optimization problem to be managed with better mornings and tighter routines. The institution has externalized its adjustment onto the household and filed the damage under personal resilience.

Rachel Cusk described the kitchen as a kind of cell, because the demand on a mother is to be there for everyone, which means being nowhere else. Closure policy adds institutional motion to that confinement. The mother becomes bus dispatcher, attendance tracker, forms wrangler and crisis manager for a school system that decided the spreadsheet mattered more than the schedule. Exhaustion is not a budget strategy. It is what happens when an institution offloads its logistics onto caregivers and then mistakes the resulting collapse for a lack of commitment.

I know another model, because I was raised inside it. My parents put all three of us through St. Stanislaus parish school at roughly $3,500 a child in the early 2000s — about two months of my father's net salary — with the price held down by fundraisers, classroom-mother rotations and my mother's Altar-Rosary service, all of it unpaid women's labor and pooled parish infrastructure. The old system excluded people and ran on burdens I will not romanticize. But it never pretended that the civic floor could be paid with parental guilt. Dorothy Day kept the works of mercy and the works of justice continuous on purpose: charity at the door cannot substitute for a just institution. A closure that converts every family into an emergency client — the ride you arrange, the teacher you lose, the move you finance — leaves mercy without justice.

Pope Francis's *Fratelli Tutti* names what becomes of people a society governed by market freedom and efficiency no longer counts: "not yet useful" or "no longer needed." Run a closure formula and that is exactly where you land. It starts with seat counts and ends with a child treated as excess. Public provision is not a warehouse to be optimized for revenue.

There is a version of this that is actually pro-family, and it has been costed. Congress can fund IDEA at the 40 percent it authorized, restore Title I's purchasing power, reauthorize ESEA and restore the Pell Grant's reach at public colleges. States can write foundation aid that cushions districts when enrollment falls instead of triggering a closure cascade. And the Hamilton Project's Cascio model — a refundable federal childcare credit indexed to a family of four with children under five, paired with quality investments and universal pre-K for four-year-olds — shows what treating care and schooling as public infrastructure looks like, built before the household crisis instead of trimmed after it, with the children who remain treated as people, not units of fixed cost.

At eleven at night I close the laptop and leave the cold tea where it is. The last line of the spreadsheet is one I add myself: "Cost of keeping a child in school after the closure." Taylor Swift compressed an entire era of American care policy into five words of a title, and "You're On Your Own, Kid" is the operating rule of a country that closes the school and hands the family the bus schedule. The title is not a promise of self-sufficiency. It is the consequence of institutions retreating while the bill for care comes home. The line item that never appears in the district's savings plan is the child who has to get there. That is how Congress and the school districts sacrifice children: they call the disappearance of a classroom a saving, then send the human cost home — a 32-child class, a longer bus route, a resignation letter in the parent portal.
