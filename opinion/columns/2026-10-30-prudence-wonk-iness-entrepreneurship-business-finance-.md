Merchant cash advance funders are harvesting the small businesses they say they rescue, and calling it credit. Federal bankruptcy Judge Caryl Delano in Tampa put it plainly: "Virtually every small-business case we see has at least one and sometimes several or more MCA funders." The numbers say it harder than any bench can. MCAs were creditors in 51% of Subchapter V filings in the first nine months of 2026, according to a Wall Street Journal analysis, while total federal small-business filings — firms owing up to $3.4 million, the kind that anchors a downtown, sponsors the local team, employs the neighbors — ran 36% ahead of the same period a year earlier, per the American Bankruptcy Institute. Put the two figures side by side and stop pretending this is an accident of the credit cycle. This is not a coincidence. It is the product working as designed. The product is not financing small businesses. It is feeding where small businesses die.

The entire edifice rests on one sentence: *we don't issue loans.* Because the transaction is styled as a purchase of future receivables rather than a loan, state usury caps don't apply, and funders can set effective finance costs in the triple digits and higher. One of Triways's advances carried a disclosed estimated APR of 2,190%. Disclosed. In writing. The disclosure regime that California, Vermont and New York already require — a dozen states have passed laws of one kind or another — produced a document nobody could act on, signed by a man waiting on a delayed federal tax refund for cash he did not have. You cannot inform a desperate person out of a trap built for desperate people. What actually constrains a lender is being treated as a lender: licensing, underwriting standards, interest-rate ceilings that attach to the economics regardless of what the contract calls itself. California already licenses MCA brokers and funders as finance providers; every state should, and any exemption from lending-law treatment should be conditioned on that license.

Look at what the money actually does. In November 2025, Triways took two advances in a single day; one obligated the company to pay an estimated $674,550 in exchange for $427,500, most of it immediately recycled into paying off three earlier advances and a small-business loan. Triways kept $5,815.60. That is not an advance. That is a foreclosure conducted in installments. Payment comes out of the company's bank account daily or weekly — before payroll, before rent, before the lights — starving the firm to service the pull. And when the firm stumbles, the funders reach past the company into its customers' pockets: UCC notices told Feast Kitchens and other debtors to hold any money owed to Triways and send it to the funders' law firms, with credit-card processors and payment platforms like DoorDash and Stripe capturable the same way. One notice redirected Triways's flows to a law firm holding funds for Horizon Capital NY; a second, from a different attorney, said Triways owed Square Funding Cali roughly $379,000. The borrower's revenue never touches the borrower again.

No company shows the trap closing better than Triways, a family-owned trucking and warehouse business in Jurupa Valley, California, which had survived nearly half a century of hauling goods, won industry awards, employed roughly 60 workers and booked nearly $10 million in revenue in 2024. When the Navy came calling with commissary contracts across four states, John Jauregui — 70 years old and not looking to grow — saw a once-in-a-generation opportunity. He could not get additional bank financing despite holding a line of credit, so he took the first advance in the fall of 2023 and planned to repay it quickly. He took more than 30 advances in roughly two and a half years. New offers arrived nearly every day. "It's addictive because it's easy to get," Jauregui said. "It solves the problem right away. Then, at the back end, it gets worse." A broker talked him into new advances to pay off older ones, and Triways ended up owing on both. That October a lawyer wrote to the landlord, a small-business lender and four funders, putting Triways's funder debt at more than $700,000 against net operating income of about $120,000 after rent — and warning the funders they might collect only a third of what they were owed if the company shut down. He signed two more deals anyway in November, and more in January, February and March. "That's how desperate I was to make the payroll," Jauregui said. A Triways customer service representative was texting his wife: "Mrs. Jackie the check did not go through."

The industry's economics explain why none of it is a mistake. Because the charges are so high, a funder needs only 30% to 40% of its contracts to pay off in full to be profitable — so a business that fails is not a loss to be worked out, it is a scenario already priced in. As Shane Heskin, the New York attorney representing Triways, put it, the strategy is to "cut off your cash flow so you have no choice but to borrow, rob and steal to pay them off, whether it's take out a home-equity loan, sell property, selling assets, taking out another MCA." Richard Cook, a North Carolina bankruptcy attorney, says that unlike conventional lenders, the funders "don't care whether the business makes it or not." Meanwhile the legitimate channel narrows: the cost of an ordinary small-business loan has been [little moved by rate policy](/articles/2026-07-19-small-business-loan-costs-little-changed-by-quarter-point-rate-rise/), [credit-card delinquencies are nearing their 2008 peak](/articles/2026-06-13-u-s-credit-card-delinquency-nears-2008-peak-amid-lighter-debt-burden/), and the Small Business Administration has announced it will no longer refinance merchant cash advances at all — having already [narrowed its own door further](/articles/2026-06-12-sba-bars-green-card-holders-from-its-loan-program-disrupting-immigrant-run-busin/). SBA Administrator Kelly Loeffler told lenders last year that borrowers who resort to these arrangements have a high fail rate, catching herself mid-sentence: "I almost said extortionist." Applicants know where the door still opens: 12% of small businesses seeking financing applied for an MCA last year, up from 9% in 2024. In October the Federal Reserve Bank of Kansas City reported that MCAs "have exacerbated the difficulties faced by small businesses." That is a regional Fed describing a segment of American finance as a net negative for the firms it touches.

Even with the offers coming daily, the company died. Triways filed for Chapter 11 in April, citing $250,000 a month in MCA payments against $2.1 million in assets and nearly $6.8 million in liabilities — more than $1.2 million of it owed to funders. A judge dismissed the case in June after the trustee reported missed payroll and lapsed workers' compensation insurance. The doors closed in September. In August, Jauregui's wife had emailed Horizon Capital NY asking it to release its roughly $280,000 lien: "If the company's final source of revenue is eliminated, everyone loses." A Horizon lawyer answered by asking to skip the lengthy emails and get to the economics — Horizon "needs a number that Triways will turn over and is willing to pay weekly." This month two funders went to a New York state court seeking default judgments totaling more than $585,000, and a third asked the court to confirm a roughly $415,000 arbitration award. In the span of about two hours on a Wednesday, Jauregui received a half dozen fresh funding offers by text. "You may qualify for up to 200k."

The industry's own words settle the question of design. The trade group, which puts the market at roughly $20 billion, says its members are "committed to making sure that businesses are successful and survive" while its executive director concedes there are "predatory companies that are just trying to get their money and don't care if the business ultimately survives." The chief editor of *deBanked*, Sean Murray, gave the more honest version in April: "These are the glory days… What is the state of the industry? This is the time to be in it." Both statements are true at once, and together they are the whole indictment. When an industry's own trade press calls this its golden age while federal filings jump 36%, the bad-actor framing is a dodge — that is not a description of the fringe, it is a description of the incentive structure, and the abuse is not a deviation from the model, it is the model performing correctly.

New York's $1 billion settlement with Yellowstone Capital, secured by Attorney General Letitia James, proved a determined attorney general can reach these arrangements as illegal loans in disguise — and some bankruptcy judges have simply concluded the transactions are loans. But that is post hoc: after the bankruptcies, after the payroll that never went out. Post hoc is not a policy. Classify an advance as what its cash flows say it is, license everyone who originates one, and cut off the lien machinery that lets a funder seize a customer's payment before the customer has been paid. The number will not fall on its own. Fifty-one percent is not a ceiling the industry reached; it is the next filing season's floor. The funders took Triways's receivables, its bank accounts, and finally the company itself — from a 70-year-old man who spent two and a half years trying to keep a half-century-old company alive, who watched his rent arrears climb past $1 million and his customers' payments redirected. The only thing they didn't take was the sales pitch.
