Texas pulled more than 8,100 books from schoolchildren's shelves in a single school year — part of a record 12,000-ban purge that hit American public schools in 2025–2026, the highest annual total ever recorded, according to a new tally from the free-expression nonprofit PEN America. The machinery of censorship has shifted from a slow grind to an industrial operation. Since PEN began counting in 2021, the cumulative total has now passed 34,000 bans. The 2025–2026 school year alone banned a record 7,000-plus unique titles — the work of more than 4,000 authors, illustrators, and translators — across 114 school districts, more districts banning books in a single year than ever before. And the pressure is not diffuse. It is legislative. Texas's SB13 strips broad categories of books from school shelves by force of law. Florida, which had held the No. 1 spot for three straight years, has been knocked off by Texas. The legal mechanism is not a mystery and not a grassroots revolt: a legislature drew a statute that removes entire categories of literature from public schools, and 114 school districts executed that statute in a single year. That is the record. That is the crime scene.

Here is what the number is made of. Fiction accounts for more than 90 percent of the banned young-adult titles. Manga, graphic novels, novels about race, sex, identity, and grief. The literature of adolescence is being treated, by adults, as contraband. The most-banned title of the year was *Sold*, Patricia McCormick's young-adult novel about a Nepali girl trafficked into sexual servitude — banned, in other words, for describing the very exploitation that a high school junior might need a book in order to survive. The most-banned author in America is Stephen King, for the second year running — and the breadth is not an accident of taste. In Utah this summer, [four districts triggered an automatic ban on a Stephen King collection](/articles/2026-07-15-four-utah-districts-trigger-automatic-ban-on-stephen-king-collection/): one law, one author's name, and everything behind it swept off the shelf.

McCormick's own statement, issued by PEN, is where the human cost stops being an abstraction. The bans, she said, dishonor the women who told her their stories: "To hear people, or book banners, say that their accounts are pornographic is so disrespectful to them." Women who survived sexual exploitation — who gave their testimony so the world could know what was happening — had that testimony classified as obscenity by adults who have never had to survive it. And the bans abandon the students who need that book most: kids with their own exploitation histories who might, through a story "six degrees away," find the language for what they have lived. "Six degrees away." That is what a banned book still does when it is banned — it reaches the child the adult in the room cannot bear to face. The record names the target and the victim in the same stroke: a law that removes *Sold* from a school library removes, from a trafficked teenager sitting in a high school classroom anywhere in this country, the one book that said what happened to her was not her fault.

Kasey Meehan, who directs PEN's Freedom to Read program, put the institutional betrayal plainly: "When reading for pleasure is in decline across all ages, bans on popular and engaging titles are exceptionally tragic." Schools, she said, "should be doing everything possible to connect kids with reading by introducing them to engaging stories and formats. Instead, many are doing the opposite." The opposite. Not a neutral act. An active withdrawal of the one thing a school library exists to hand a child.

And the same machinery keeps running at full speed. [The report lands in the same week PEN separately warned that US public libraries are "at breaking point" under the same pressure](/articles/2026-09-01-pen-america-finds-us-public-libraries-at-breaking-point-amid-censorship-push/) — the same censorship apparatus, the same state laws, the same institutional targets.

But here is the part the headline numbers don't capture: the resistance is wider than the bans.

Parents, librarians, students, and educators have been fighting district by district, school board meeting by school board meeting, and the results are starting to show up where it counts — at the ballot box. In Florida, the state that built the modern model of schoolhouse censorship, progressive school board candidates this year beat back a slate of rightwing incumbents. The same state that wrote the playbook is now, slowly, voting it out of office, one race at a time. It is a small but real repudiation of the politics driving the bans, and it is happening in the school board races that don't make the national news — which is exactly why it is happening.

Twelve thousand bans is a moral catastrophe. It is also a map. Texas wrote SB13 into law. 114 school districts executed it. 7,000 unique titles — 4,000 authors, illustrators, and translators — were removed from the shelves of American children in twelve months. Patricia McCormick's sources — the women whose testimonies are now classified as pornographic by the districts that banned their story — are the named victims of that legislation. The exploited teenager searching a school library for one book that tells her the truth is the unnamed one. Both are now reading in a country that has decided their stories are contraband. The statehouses that wrote SB13 are betting that nobody will notice which books disappear from which shelves. A book pulled from a school library is a book somebody in that town has to talk about, has to defend, has to elect somebody over — and a lot of somebodies, this year, decided to. Because somebody has to. The 34,000-ban milestone is not a plateau. It is the floor of what comes next.
