Trump taxes working families to replace revenue after the Supreme Court struck down emergency tariffs.

Here are the rates. The administration released the proposal early Wednesday, the day before Thanksgiving, through a senior official on a late-night Tuesday call with reporters. The official would not give a name. No implementation timeline came with it.

The proposal would add 20 percentage points to duties on Chinese goods, taking the total on some products to 45 percent. China and Vietnam would face 20 percent—Vietnam pending finalization of trade talks—while duties on other partners range from 10 to 18 percent. Brazil and China would face some of the largest increases. Brazil also faces higher duties on raw and refined sugar and a new 10 percent tariff on aircraft, which would reach Embraer, the country’s largest aircraft maker. These product-specific duties would not stack with the separate Section 232 tariffs on automobiles, steel and aluminum or with the July forced-labor levies of 10 to 12.5 percent on 60 trading partners.

This is [the administration’s second tariff step over industrial subsidies](/articles/2026-11-25-trump-administration-plans-second-tariff-step-over-industrial-subsidies/), not a fresh emergency measure.

Here is what USTR’s November 25, 2026 Section 301 announcement did: it scheduled 14 investigations—12 countries, the 27-member European Union and Taiwan—while Mexico and Norway remained pending. The rate schedule was sized. The findings were not. Section 301 permits retaliation against foreign trade practices that violate existing agreements, are unjustified by market forces or discriminate against American goods. The procedure is supposed to put the record before the remedy: investigate, publish a determination, take public comment, then respond. The sequence ran backward: proposed remedy first, record afterward.

The official’s disclosure made the method more concrete: Brazil had been singled out for additional duties after talks stalled, and the investigative record was still unpublished when the rates were disclosed. That is how enforcement becomes bargaining and collection.

The Supreme Court’s February 2026 opinion is the next receipt. It held that Donald Trump could not declare an economic emergency and bypass Congress to impose across-the-board tariffs. The administration now says this Section 301 proposal will replace tariff revenue lost after that ruling. Put the two records side by side: the public justification is foreign subsidies; the disclosed fiscal purpose is rebuilding lost receipts. The Court did not take the revenue. It closed the emergency route. That is not a trade remedy with revenue attached. It is a revenue machine wearing a trade remedy as its label.

That is intellectual laundering. A trade case corrects a prohibited practice. A revenue case fills a gap in receipts. This proposal asks Section 301 to do both. The administration is not merely enforcing a trade rule. It is using enforcement procedure to collect revenue and extract negotiating leverage.

The fiscal point is explicit. The Office of Management and Budget’s *Historical Tables*, Table 1.2, “Receipts by Source,” records $194.9 billion in customs duties in fiscal 2024. The proposal publishes rates and country lists. It does not publish a ten-year revenue estimate, a household-distribution table or an employment estimate. A rate sheet is not a score. The deficit is not the moral issue; the consumer tax inside the trade remedy is.

Trump has promised that import taxes will bring manufacturing jobs back and reduce the federal deficit. The announcement supplies neither an implementation date nor an employment estimate. A claim with neither is an advertisement, not a forecast.

Permanence controls the ten-year result. A temporary tariff yields temporary receipts; a permanent tariff is a permanent tax. The announcement supplies no implementation schedule against which to inspect a revenue line. Under the current-law baseline—the statutory convention that assumes scheduled expirations occur—a future renewal cannot be booked as current revenue. If the duties are meant to last, score them as lasting. If they are meant to expire, the deficit effect worsens when they do. The relevant cost is the consumer tax. If the policy is permanent, the cost is permanent. If the cost is not permanent, the policy is not permanent. Pick one.

A tariff has two incidence questions. Statutory incidence is the legal payer: the American importer of record writes the check to U.S. Customs. Economic incidence is the burden after prices adjust. If the importer absorbs the duty, its margin falls. If it raises the invoice, buyers bear more. The legal form cannot change the arithmetic.

The 2018 outturn is documented. Amiti, Redding and Weinstein’s “The Effect of the 2018 Tariffs on Prices and Welfare,” *Journal of Economic Perspectives* 34, no. 4 (Fall 2020), pp. 187–210, found that the duty moved almost completely into U.S. import prices. Pass-through means the duty entered the price instead of vanishing into the foreign exporter’s margin. The authors placed the incidence on U.S. importers. That is the 2018 outturn, not a forecast. It is the relevant answer to the administration’s promise that foreign subsidies, rather than American buyers, will pay.

China’s Ministry of Foreign Affairs spokesman Guo Jiakun answered: “A tariff war or a trade war serves no party’s interests.” He added: “The so-called overcapacity is a false narrative, and China opposes using it as a pretext for political manipulation.” The administration says the tariffs will not surprise Beijing and are unlikely to undermine the trade truce expiring January 10. A punishment the administration says will not surprise the target and is unlikely to disturb the negotiation is not a punishment. It is a price list with a press release.

Article I, Section 8, Clause 3 gives Congress the power to lay duties and imposts. Presidents have long used the Section 301 and Section 232 delegations to impose trade measures, but a delegation cannot cure an order written backward. If the administration wants a subsidy remedy, it should issue findings, take public comment and impose a targeted response. If it wants revenue, it should publish the collections and defend the consumer tax. One cannot claim the first cover while collecting the second.

A Section 301 remedy can be legitimate when the administration identifies a prohibited subsidy, the affected economic interest and a tailored remedy. Before any duty takes effect, the government should publish a ten-year revenue estimate on a current-law baseline, a Treasury table assigning the tax by household and industry, and a Congressional Budget Office estimate of employment effects. The duties should expire unless Congress renews the trade finding. Section 301 should decide trade violations, not fill a hole in customs receipts.

On November 25, Donald Trump’s rate sheet went out before the investigation files. In February, the Court told the president he could not tax by emergency; the answer was another statutory route, directed at rebuilding the revenue his emergency tariffs had stopped collecting. The paperwork names subsidies as the wrong. The checkout line is where the tax lands.

The Trump administration chose the tariff route. The Supreme Court closed the emergency route. Section 301 supplied the authority. The Treasury supplied the motive. The author of a tax does not get to name its victim.
