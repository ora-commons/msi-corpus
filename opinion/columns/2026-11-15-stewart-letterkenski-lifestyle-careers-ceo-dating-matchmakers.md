Tech executives pay half a million dollars to escape surveillance they built for everyone else.

The report is filed under lifestyle. It is a market confession, and it supplies its own receipts. Matchmaking for the C-suite starts at $75,000 and can reach half a million dollars; DateSpot puts the average cost up 26 percent between January 2025 and 2026. Matches are vetted under non-disclosure agreements. Dates receive scrambled biographical details and fake look-alike photographs, so the person opposite cannot work out who is sitting across the table. One client was instructed to rent a Prius and collect his date in it rather than drive the Maserati. Private members' clubs such as ZZs and Chez Margaux stage the first evenings — the table far enough from its neighbours, concierge coordination, separate entrances — and one luxury concierge once hired people to fill the booth at a stadium concert so two recognizable people could use the balcony without appearing to be on a one-on-one date. Call it what the vendors call it, a "controlled dating ecosystem," a "bodyguard." Then call it what it is: concealment infrastructure, priced as an amenity.

The people buying this product are the people who built its opposite. For two decades the industry line has been that exposure is a choice: users freely traded their location, their messages, their faces and their friendships for convenience, and anyone uneasy could simply opt out. Shoshana Zuboff named the arrangement "surveillance capitalism," and while her account of machine-learning mind control has always asked the technology to carry more weight than it can — the power in the arrangement comes from concentration, not cleverness — the market has just supplied her thesis with its best evidence. The men who told you privacy was dead are paying $75,000 a match to be dead to your databases. Nothing in a shareholder letter states what a product is worth as cleanly as the founders' own spending does.

It is true that nobody in this report is inventing the danger. A honeypot — someone planted near an executive under cover of romance to extract money, information or leverage — is a genuine counterintelligence problem, and OpenAI has been running training sessions warning employees about exactly that. The reporting landed the same week Jensen Huang resigned as Nvidia's chief executive after two extramarital affairs came to light, one with a woman suspected of working for Chinese intelligence and another with an employee; Nvidia did not respond to requests for comment. Andy Byron resigned as chief executive of Astronomer after a phone at a Coldplay concert caught him embracing the company's head of human resources. The physical fears are not theatrical: the shooting of UnitedHealthcare's Brian Thompson and the attack on Sam Altman's home sit in the same news cycle, and fear for a family is not a posture. Nor is the burden evenly distributed — the reporting notes it runs hardest on high-profile women, whose intimate photographs get stolen by hackers and whose faces get deepfaked, and for them a stalker is not a hypothetical. Don Aviv charges $4,000 on top of his standard rate to research a potential partner.

The trouble is what the money is actually defending. Michael Nalick's research at the University of Denver shows that executives are nearly five times more likely to be fired for personal misconduct than for financial misconduct. Read that twice, because it inverts the brochure. The apparatus of dossiers and non-disclosure agreements is not, at its centre, protection against spies; spies are rare. What it defends against is the board, the partner, and the stranger at the next table with a phone — the only witnesses with standing to make a chief executive's private conduct matter. A governance system that reaches for the private life five times as readily as it reaches for the books is not protecting shareholders. It is protecting reputation. Safety is the sales pitch. Reputation is the product.

Consider what the vetting actually compiles. Cody Shultz, who leads private client protection at Guidepost Solutions and previously worked at the CIA, describes the emphasis moving away from civil and criminal litigation history toward reputation: what the prospective partner has posted, what they have been party to in court, whether they are associated with hot-button social or political questions. That is a dossier, compiled for money, about a private person who has not been notified, cannot inspect it, and will not be told why the calls stopped coming back. One of Aviv's clients, an executive at a private-equity firm in New England, has him investigate every prospective date. The non-disclosure agreement in this arrangement runs in one direction: it binds the matchmaker to the executive's confidences and says nothing whatever about the person whose file is being built. Bruce Schneier's description of ordinary people's security as feudal — your data held at the pleasure of someone else's security posture — needs no abstraction here. A person is a tenant in a file they did not lease.

Cory Doctorow's privilege gradient describes how this normally runs: intrusive technology is imposed first on people with no standing to refuse, and then normalises as it climbs toward people who could refuse and have stopped bothering. The dating market runs the same curve from the other end, which is the finding buried inside the lifestyle copy. The ordinary user is the exposed party — they post, they are scraped, they are matched against models that, as a [Senate report found in its examination of Tinder's scam-detection models](/articles/2026-10-14-senate-report-finds-tinder-scam-detection-models-went-years-without-updates/), went years without updates — and no invoice for their protection appears anywhere. The executive buys his exit with cash and counsel. Everyone else's exposure is treated as the weather.

The concealment work is only necessary because the room is instrumented. Rachel Greenwald's formulation is that privacy used to mean avoiding a couple of photographers outside a regular haunt and now means "controlling the room": the person at the next table with a phone, a pair of smartglasses, the Flock automated-licence-plate cameras on the highway, the facial recognition at the stadium gate. Dan Fugardi asks his prospects to put their phones away before the first drink, on the reasoning that communications thrown "out into the ether" are potentially there forever — treating the other person's device as the leak vector, and their presence as the risk. Tim Wu's attention merchants built an economy out of harvesting who looks at what; this is the same market at the refund counter. Bryan Goldberg, who runs BDG, states the resulting absurdity better than any analyst could: "KKR's legal department cannot perform diligence at the level of a single woman on a first date." His line is the whole arrangement in one sentence, because diligence has displaced introduction as the operative task — and because the executives in question have already been treated as operating above the standards everyone else is held to, an argument about the Nvidia scandal and what it says about bosses who are not subject to the rules, made in [these pages earlier this month](/articles/2026-11-12-guardian-column-argues-nvidia-scandal-puts-tech-bosses-above-the-rules/).

It is true that a relationship capable of moving a share price is a legitimate subject for a board. Bernard Looney left BP in 2023 after admitting he had not been "fully transparent" about "historical relationships with colleagues"; Nestlé dismissed Laurent Freixe over what the company called an undisclosed romantic relationship with a direct subordinate, a dismissal his representative says he contests, on both the grounds and the manner; and John Overdeck's divorce proceedings risk rewiring the C-suite of his own firm. Bob Cohen prepares about twenty-five cohabitation agreements a year for top executives, and their operative content is the right to remove a significant other from a shared residence. The trouble is what the apparatus reaches for first, and what it never reaches for at all.

The completed version of this logic is reported by the matchmakers themselves: the AI founders who come to Amy Andersen for help and then do not make room for dating, who "literally do not leave work" and are, in her phrase, "painfully single". When a private life has been reclassified as a liability to be managed, the cheapest management is to have none. The machine eats everything, including the date.

My father spent thirty years as a journeyman millwright on the bar mill at Manitoba Rolling Mills, where the cameras pointed at the workers and the offices had doors — and in 1995 a firm from Porto Alegre bought the place, kept my father, and let several of the men I called uncles go. Nobody needed a white paper to explain the politics of that building. The people with the least control over their own information were the ones closest to the machine, and the men who ran the machine watched everyone and were watched by no one. The gradient has not changed; the technology got smaller, cheaper and universal, and then the men who shipped it discovered they did not want to live under it — and, unlike the rest of us, they could afford not to. What they are buying at $75,000 a match is the office with the door. Everyone else is issued the floor.

The file is the part that ought to worry a Canadian reader, because Canadian private-sector privacy law starts from a premise the American one simply does not have: that a person holds rights in the information a business keeps about them. Under PIPEDA and Quebec's Law 25, an individual can ask an organisation for the personal information it holds about them and challenge how it is used. There is no equivalent lever in the United States, because there is no comprehensive federal privacy law to supply one. The remedies are unglamorous and they already exist: a federal privacy law with a private right of action, so that individuals rather than captured agencies hold the power to sue; bright-line limits on the face-recognition and plate-reader networks; closure of the data-broker trade that makes a stranger's history purchasable. That is the mass-market version of what the C-suite is buying for itself, and the people buying the bespoke version have demonstrated, at $75,000 a match, that they can afford the public one.

Andersen says she is there to protect the client, because "we can't get this wrong". "Protect" is euphemistic labeling — harmful conduct described in the vocabulary of benefit: the word names the client's safety and leaves out the file bought on a person who was never asked. To be fair, discretion itself is not a crime; people have always hired matchmakers, and the rich have always had doors, and nobody needs a licence to rent a boring car. The policy question is not whether a chief executive may be difficult to find. It is whether everyone else's life must remain the raw material that makes his scarcity valuable.

There is something almost tender in the detail about the Prius — a man from the industry that taught your phone to predict you, renting a dull car so a date will not learn he owns a Maserati. It is also the entire argument in one photograph. The men who built the machine will pay anything not to live in it, and they have chosen to pay it privately, for themselves, rather than unbuild it for anyone else.

Everyone else stays in the room. Somebody at the next table is holding a phone. That is not a security strategy. It is a confession, itemized, at $75,000 the first instalment.
