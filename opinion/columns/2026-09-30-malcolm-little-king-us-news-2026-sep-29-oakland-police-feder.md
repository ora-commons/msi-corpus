When several Oakland police officers in uniform sex-trafficked a teenage girl, the institution had already been under federal court supervision for more than a decade. The monitors were there. The compliance filings were being submitted. The 50-task checklist was supposedly being worked. And OPD officers were running a trafficking ring.

The city paid the victim a nearly $1 million settlement in 2017. Nearly a dozen officers were fired or suspended. The case was discovered not because the oversight regime caught it in its compliance reviews, but because an investigation into it took years to work its way into daylight. That is not a success story about internal accountability. That is a receipt against a department that could not stop committing serious crimes against a child while the people supposed to be watching it were literally in the building.

That receipt is the argument that now has no institutional counterweight.

After 23 years — the longest federal court-ordered police oversight arrangement in United States history — a federal judge has released the Oakland Police Department. The monitors are leaving. The compliance filings are closing. OPD answers to Oakland again.

That is precisely what OPD command staff, the Oakland Police Officers' Association, and every mayor who inherits a less-scrutinized force wanted. The monitors were an inconvenience. They compelled paperwork. They demanded data. They enforced deadlines. They made the department's internal life answerable to someone outside Oakland's political machinery, and they did it for 23 years because the department could not be trusted to police itself — a fact the department proved repeatedly by continuing to commit serious misconduct under their noses. The union gets its grievance process back without a federal overseer looking over its shoulder. Command staff gets operational autonomy without having to justify decisions in a courtroom. Mayors get a force they can deploy politically without a compliance officer asking what the data shows.

The federal regime was not imposed because Oakland activists were being dramatic. It was forced into existence by the "riders" scandal of the early 2000s, when officers were exposed for planting evidence, falsifying reports, and using excessive force against primarily young Black men. More than 100 civil lawsuits followed. A 2003 settlement placed OPD under a reform program with more than 50 required tasks — racial-profiling data collection, faster complaint handling, updated community-policing practices — because the department had demonstrated, through its own officers' conduct, that it could not be trusted to do these things on its own.

The reform program was supposed to be temporary. Instead, it metastasized. When compliance remained uneven, the arrangement deepened in 2012. A federal judge took direct supervision of the department's major policy decisions. For more than a decade, OPD had to bring its institutional life to federal court and ask permission to move forward.

That is not a healthy permanent model of municipal government. It is a confession that local institutions had failed so thoroughly that outside authority had to occupy the space they abandoned. The formal exit was already anticipated, and OPD had been [on track to leave federal oversight by September](/articles/2026-05-30-oakland-police-on-track-to-exit-federal-oversight-by-september/) per the department's own compliance timetable.

But the fact that the department eventually cleared the federal requirements is also real. A 50-task checklist completed over two decades is not nothing. It is institutional change, however slow and expensive. The question is not whether the reform era produced anything. It plainly did.

The question is whether anything it produced survives the end of the regime that produced it.

The Anti Police-Terror Project put the case plainly: "Police cannot police themselves. Oakland's history has already taught us that lesson." They are right, and their warning carries the weight of 23 years of evidence. The riders. The trafficking case. The uneven reform arc that required repeated judicial intervention because the department could not sustain its own improvements. The end of federal supervision does not erase that inheritance. The federal monitors did not leave because OPD demonstrated it had become trustworthy. They left because the compliance timeline ran out.

The riders, the trafficking case, and the pattern of misconduct that made oversight necessary in the first place — that is the inheritance OPD carries out the door. The demand that Oakland now trust the department to hold itself accountable is the demand that OPD has wanted since 2003. The question is who benefits from that trust being granted, and the answer is the people who will be least visible when the misconduct next surfaces.

For 23 years, the monitors supplied a particular kind of authority: they compelled paperwork, demanded data, enforced deadlines, and made the slow institutional grind of compliance answerable to someone outside Oakland's political machinery. That authority is now gone. The next chapter belongs to Oakland's voters, its elected officials, its courts, its journalists, its organized civil society, and its sworn officers — and it belongs to them without a federal judge compelcing any of it.

That means litigation. The lawyers who represented riders-era plaintiffs and trafficking survivors must continue to file suits when misconduct surfaces, because private litigation is one of the few mechanisms that has actually produced consequences for OPD — the 100-plus riders lawsuits created the consent decree in the first place. Organizing. The Anti Police-Terror Project and allied groups must continue to demand public records, attend city council meetings, and make the department's internal data a matter of public knowledge rather than institutional discretion. Elections. Oakland's voters must treat police oversight as a governing issue, not an afterthought, and hold every mayor accountable for what OPD does between elections. Records fights. Every FOIA request, every complaint-data demand, every budget hearing is a site where the power that federal monitors exercised must be exercised by someone else — or the vacuum will be filled by the people who want it filled.

Cities that have walked this road before — Los Angeles after Rampart, Cincinnati after its consent decree, Cleveland after the Tamir Rice settlement — show why the years after monitors leave matter so much. Gains either harden into institutional habit or erode quietly back to baseline. Reform can become culture, or it can become paperwork that ended when the paperwork ended. The officers who ran a trafficking ring while the department was under federal supervision suggest which way the institutional culture has trended when no one was watching closely enough.

Oakland's civil society is organized around this question. The organizers, the litigators, the journalists, and the community members who kept pressure on OPD through 23 years of reform reports do not disappear because the federal file is closed. They are not obstacles to the post-oversight era. They are the only reason the post-oversight era has any accountability in it at all.

The harder truth is that even the most intrusive external monitoring could not make OPD police itself. It could impose standards. It could expose failures. It could force reforms into existence. It could make the institution answerable to someone with the power to compel answers. It could not substitute forever for the civic institutions that must keep those reforms alive.

The city has its police force back. The union has its autonomy back. The command staff has its operational freedom back. The question is whether anyone in Oakland is organized enough, angry enough, and persistent enough to make all of them answer for what they do with it.

That is not a question about civic goodwill. It is a question about power. And the answer has to be built the way every piece of accountability OPD has ever produced was built: by people who refused to let the department close its own books.
