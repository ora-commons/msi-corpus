New York lets employers fire victims of unlisted violent crimes.

Laza Susuni, 62, was working in the women’s ready-to-wear section of Saks Fifth Avenue in Manhattan on June 28 when a customer attacked her in a dressing room. Susuni said the customer kicked her in the stomach. She went to the hospital and was released without permanent physical injuries. She could not sleep. She feared the woman would return to hurt her or punish her through other people. Susuni asked Saks for five days off to recover from her anxiety. Susuni says Saks terminated her after she made the request.

The [June 28 arrest record in the New York County Criminal Court docket](https://iapps.courts.state.ny.us/nyscef/CaseSearch?SearchType=Party) establishes only that a woman was arrested at Saks Fifth Avenue on assault and harassment charges. It does not establish that Susuni was the person attacked, that the arrested woman attacked her, that any charge resulted in an adjudication, or that Saks fired Susuni because of the assault. The charge here belongs to the statute, not to an invented finding about motive.

Exemplar Luxury Group, Saks’s parent, says it takes employee safety seriously, does not retaliate against employees who report violations of policy or law, and investigates every report promptly and thoroughly. The published record contains no stated reason for Susuni’s termination and no disclosed investigative result. That absence does not prove retaliation. It leaves the governing law in view.

The strongest defense of the existing law is sound. Employers must be able to manage attendance, safety, and performance. If a worker’s absence was unrelated to an attack, or if later conduct fell below legitimate workplace standards, termination can be ordinary business. Current law preserves that management authority. The defect lies in the statute’s selection of protected victims.

New York did not leave this field unregulated. Its operative texts as in force on June 28 protect some victims and omit others.

[New York Executive Law § 296(1)(a)](https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/laws/EXC/296), the official text in force on June 28, says an employer “shall not discriminate against any employee because of the employee’s status as an individual who is a victim of domestic violence” or “because of the employee’s status as an individual who is a victim of sexual assault.” The clause protects enumerated statuses. It does not protect an employee’s status as the victim of every violent assault.

[New York Penal Law § 215.14](https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/laws/PEN/215.14), likewise the official text in force on June 28, protects an employee “required to attend a conference, or to appear before a court of this state in connection with any prosecution of such crime,” provided the employee gives advance notice of the appearance, meeting, and anticipated length of absence. The quoted language protects participation in a prosecution. It does not create general recovery leave after an attack. Susuni’s hospital visit and five-day request do not fall within it.

[New York City Administrative Code § 8-107(10)(a)](https://rules.cityofnewyork.us/rule/8-107/), in force on June 28, provides: “No employer shall discharge, demote, suspend or otherwise discriminate against an employee because such employee is a victim of domestic violence, sexual assault or stalking.” The verbs are broad. The protected categories are enumerated. A nonsexual customer assault is not among the listed nouns.

Alice Jump, a partner at Reavis Page Jump, told the published account that a nonsexual customer assault falls outside those categories and that no broad federal termination protection for crime victims filled the gap. The statutes do not require an employer to prove that an absence or performance problem was unrelated to the attack. The worker must establish retaliation while the employer controls the attendance records, leave communications, performance history, and internal files. Exemplar’s assurance cannot supply the missing record. A no-retaliation policy is not a statute.

The result is selection by legal name. New York wrote protection for domestic violence, court appearances, sexual assault, and stalking, then left other workers to the employer’s paperwork and the worker’s burden of proof. A worker attacked at work receives no general protection merely because the attack was violent.

Jeff Vinokur did what the legal system would not do. After about ten lawyers told him that no law protected his mother, he used AI to search for comparable complaints, hired three freelance researchers who spent hundreds of hours, and assembled the allegations on Saksfiles.com. He paid demonstrators to stand outside Saks from 1 to 7 p.m. every day through the holiday shopping season, using money from the $100 million sale of his edtech company. Vinokur describes his mother as a “disposable minimum wage worker” and Saks as a billion-dollar corporation.

Private wealth buys leverage that the law otherwise withholds. That is the resource asymmetry behind [AI’s lopsided edge for attackers](/articles/2026-11-06-experts-warn-ai-will-hand-hackers-a-lopsided-advantage-over-defenders/): technology magnifies the party that can afford to deploy it. The website records allegations; it does not adjudicate them. Vinokur’s other termination claims remain allegations. The placards calling Susuni “the 7th victim” state a claim, not a verdict. His money can force a legislature to confront a missing rule; only the legislature can enact one.

New York should prohibit discharge or discipline when a documented violent crime caused the absence or impairment at issue, require notice and verification, and preserve management authority over absences and performance unrelated to the crime. It should protect reporting, participation in an investigation, and court proceedings, and give workers access to the records needed to prove retaliation. When adverse action closely follows a protected report and the employer controls the motive evidence, the employer should disprove retaliation rather than leave the worker to prove it.

Vinokur’s money put Saks on the public record. Susuni did not obtain that leverage. The assault ended at the hospital. The legal danger began when she asked for five days.

New York wrote the law. New York still lets employers call retaliation management. It can write a better one.
