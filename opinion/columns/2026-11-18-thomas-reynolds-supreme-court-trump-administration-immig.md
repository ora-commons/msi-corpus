The Supreme Court is helping Donald Trump deport 600,000 Venezuelans without deciding the law.

The administration’s application presents a serious case for temporary relief. It does not ask the Court to declare that terminating Temporary Protected Status was lawful. It asks the Court to extend a temporary appellate stay before it expires Friday so the government can continue its challenge. In its Nov. 18, 2026 emergency application, [*Noem v. Centro de Trabajadores Unidos*, No. 25-1577](https://www.supremecourt.gov/search.aspx?filename=/docket/docketfiles/html/public/25-1577.html), federal attorneys say that “if the justices do not intervene again before Friday, the administration could be required to release about 1,800 Venezuelans from detention.”

The [Associated Press report](https://apnews.com/article/supreme-court-trump-administration-immigration-venezuela-34f5293fe85f3befb2a599f513308d60) confirms the stakes and the sequence. The government relies on the Secretary of Homeland Security’s published termination notice, the statute and regulations governing TPS, and the Supreme Court’s earlier allowance of termination. Its theory is concrete: Congress assigned the initial country-conditions determination to the Secretary; the Secretary followed the prescribed process; the district judge cannot use an interim order to restore TPS while the government appeals; and the Court should preserve the status quo until appellate review ends. At the stay stage, that is a recognizable position. A court can prevent immediate harm without deciding the merits. Friday’s deadline strengthens that argument. It does not turn the government’s reading of TPS into law.

The district court reached the opposite conclusion. Its November 2026 TPS-restoration order, *Centro de Trabajadores Unidos v. Department of Homeland Security*, No. 3:25-cv-02860-WHO (N.D. Cal. Nov. 2026), held that the administration’s termination was unlawful and restored the protections. The order treats the Secretary’s country-conditions determination and termination process as defective. That conclusion is not housekeeping. TPS determines whether roughly 600,000 Venezuelans may remain in the United States and work lawfully. If the district court’s order controls, the protections return while the government litigates its legality.

Congress created TPS in 1990 in [8 U.S.C. § 1254a](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=granuleid:USC-prelim-title8-section1254a&num=0&edition=prelim). When the Secretary determines that a designated country no longer satisfies the statutory conditions, § 1254a(c)(2)(A) requires the Secretary to “publish notice in the Federal Register.” The implementing regulations govern country designations in [8 C.F.R. § 214.12.4](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-8/chapter-I/subchapter-B/part-214/subpart-H/section-214.12.4) and terminations in [8 C.F.R. § 214.12.5](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-8/chapter-I/subchapter-B/part-214/subpart-H/section-214.12.5). For the ordinary reader, the government’s decision was not simply to announce a policy preference. It had to make and publish the required country-conditions determination and follow the termination procedures Congress assigned to the Secretary.

Ending TPS does not itself issue a removal order against every recipient. It removes protection from removal and the legal authorization to work. Those are substantial consequences, but legal consequences remain susceptible to judicial review. That is why the district court’s restoration order matters. It held that the executive had not lawfully ended the protection before the courts resolved the government’s challenge.

The Ninth Circuit then stayed that command. Its November 2026 stay order, *Centro de Trabajadores Unidos v. Department of Homeland Security*, No. 25-10337 (9th Cir. Nov. 2026), pauses the restoration order until Friday, Nov. 20. The government now asks the Supreme Court to extend the appellate stay and prevent the district court’s order from taking effect. If the Court grants the application, the administration’s termination will continue to operate while the underlying legality remains unresolved.

The request travels on the emergency docket, commonly called the shadow docket because applications arrive outside the ordinary merits calendar, without the ordinary schedule of briefing and oral argument. The Court can act quickly when an emergency requires speed. The application here asks for no ruling on whether TPS termination was lawful. It asks the Court to preserve the termination’s consequences while the Court of Appeals decides the case. The Court’s May 16, 2025 stay order likewise granted interim relief; it did not hold the termination substantively lawful. The present application asks the Court to maintain the practical result before the merits ruling arrives.

The pattern extends beyond Venezuela. In February, the administration had already [asked this Court to end Syrians’ protections](/articles/2026-02-25-trump-administration-asks-supreme-court-to-end-syrian-migrants-legal-protections/). In June, the Court allowed the administration to end TPS protections for Haitians and Syrians in a [ruling affecting 1.3 million people](/articles/2026-07-10-supreme-court-rules-trump-may-end-tps-for-haitians-syrians-affecting-1-3-million/). Venezuela is now the next country in the same sequence.

These orders need not announce a general rule to build a practical one. Each application produces immediate legal consequences before the ordinary appellate process resolves the statute. Each invocation of the shadow docket preserves the administration’s determination while the merits remain open. The cases accumulate an answer before the Court has stated one.

The government’s strongest point remains the imminent harm to the approximately 1,800 detainees it says must be released if the stay expires. That is concrete, and emergency relief exists to address concrete harm. But the immediate interest in preventing those releases does not require the Court to treat the legal status of roughly 600,000 Venezuelans as finally settled. The Court could address Friday’s deadline with a narrow interim measure while requiring the Secretary to defend the country-conditions determination through ordinary briefing, argument, and appellate resolution.

An honest disposition would put that statutory question on the ordinary merits calendar. It would require the government to defend the termination under § 1254a and the implementing regulations and decide the dispute in a signed judicial opinion. The Court should preserve the status quo when urgency demands it. It should not let an emergency docket become the default tribunal for a mass change in legal status.

The Court is not auditing Donald Trump’s campaign to withdraw Venezuelan TPS protections. It is advancing the campaign’s consequences before the law is decided. An emergency docket cannot convert an unfinished challenge into a lawful termination. Six hundred thousand Venezuelans are entitled to a decision on the law, not deportation by procedural default.
