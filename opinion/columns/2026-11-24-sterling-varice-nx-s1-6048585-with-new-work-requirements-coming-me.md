Delphine is 39, a knife-hour on the fillet line of the catfish plant I own outside Ferriday, Louisiana, and by the third hour the ammonia compressor has warmed her shoulders; because her Medicaid coverage turns on my timesheet, I can leave her 1 hour short of 80 without laying off a finger, and I paid nothing for that power. Selena Simmons-Duffin’s [NPR report on new Medicaid work requirements](https://www.npr.org/2026/11/24/nx-s1-6048585/with-new-work-requirements-coming-medicaid-recipients-try-to-navigate-the-system) says cuts and work requirements are set to affect the health insurance of many low-income Americans and that recipients face overwhelming confusion. Correct. Confusion is a cost center. You eat the output on Friday nights: the fried-catfish plate at my fish camp, the po’boy at the counter by the bridge. Your taxes cover the distance between her wage and her rent. The state now asks her for 80 documented hours a month to keep coverage, and the man who allocates her hours is me. I keep $1.10 a head each month by declining the scheduling module that would print fixed shifts. God has always preferred a documented man.

Follow the money one step at a time. You’re standing in it. You may dislike the arithmetic. I promise it’s clean.

Family coverage averages [$28,000 a year](/articles/2026-10-22-kff-finds-family-health-insurance-premiums-average-28-000/), which is why nobody on my fillet line gets insurance from me, and the [small businesses that still offer it have spent years passing the raises straight into the worker’s share](/articles/2026-10-22-small-business-workers-pay-more-as-employer-health-premiums-climb/). My 214 hands carry Medicaid the way delta country carries weather. I give Delphine 20 a week, which clears the floor in a clean month. The module that would lock those shifts and print them a month ahead costs $1.10 a head.

I’ve declined it twice, in writing, because I enjoy a paper trail that shows me refusing paper.

In September, Delphine asked about the compressor once. Politely. Heat isn’t a line item. Hours are. So October floated wherever my week needed it.

3 of her weeks ran 20.

The fourth ran 19.

Read the next sentence twice.

The portal counted 79.

79 isn’t 80.

Her coverage didn’t lapse because I punished her. It lapsed because the arithmetic has never once asked about the compressor.

The staffing model is called demand-responsive deployment, which is my phrase, and I’ll translate it while the coffee’s warm: her hours move where my margins want them, and the state does my firing. I keep the clean term because the honest one, that I ration her hours, empties a renewal meeting, and the renewals are where the bonuses live. Her lunch has never been on a ledger I keep.

When the portal kicked 79 back, it didn’t send her to a help line. It sent her to my paper. The state’s verification reads what my scheduling system prints, and my scheduling system prints the float. So she asked the front office for a corrected hour printout. The request form says 3 business days. The request form is mine too. It took 9. Her November opened before her October finished, and the month the portal keeps is the month my printer keeps, which is the same month, which is the point.

She hasn’t asked twice. 214 hands, and not one grievance since October. The portal runs my discipline now. It works weekends, and it has never asked for a raise. I want you to admire that the way I do.

Not the pain.

The tidiness.

She clears her 80 without noticing, which is the whole trick, because effortless compliance never asks questions. I keep the $1.90. I run it as a hiring filter.

Now take home health, where the arithmetic has a different uniform: Calvin is 46, an eligibility unit with split knuckles at the home-health company I own outside Terre Haute, Indiana. Different surface. Identical math.

Calvin isn’t on my payroll. He’s an independent contractor, which means he receives my schedule, uses my training, carries the client liability, and receives no employer-sponsored insurance, no workers’ compensation, and no paid leave. I save $0.11 an hour in enrollment administration and payroll-tax handling by refusing direct-hire status. You pay for the wound-care kit you bought for your father and the skilled-nursing hour that comes with it, while Calvin changes the dressing, lifts him, and sits awake when he falls before he can prove his work to a county portal. You keep my schedule staffed. I appreciate that. Truly. I keep the $0.11.

Calvin twisted his lower back while lifting a client in February. The form calls it an independent-contractor incident. The honest name is Calvin, a home-care contractor with a back injury who can’t file workers’ compensation. I prefer the form because a paragraph about employer obligation makes clients lower their voices, and clients dislike a disrupted care schedule.

Calvin saw a clinic. He returned. The bill is his. His back is his. People seem to expect his back to count because it is attached to him. It is. That is why it costs him. I kept $0.11.

I’m not unusual; I’m a curriculum. I’ve taught this arithmetic for 30 years, and Chapter 6 of *Social Obligations for Profit Maximization* is assigned in 40 programs besides mine. The firms that set your hours recruit out of my rosters by name. One graduate decides whether Delphine’s November counted. Another decided yours. I never made a single call about Delphine’s November. You can’t vote me out of that sentence, and neither can she.

Here is the design beneath the portal. The employer keeps the labor record; the state asks the contractor to prove the labor; the employer refuses the proof because proof creates obligations. I receive labor without maintenance. The independent contractor gets a screen and a threat. The state gets a queue. This is the household code with a browser: the master says nothing, the servant produces proof, and a clerk decides whether hunger has earned coverage.

The new rules make my operation cleaner. When a home-care contractor’s Medicaid coverage depends on work verification, turnover becomes a form of compliance. The agency doesn’t report the hours. The rolls shed about 40 of my people every month now. The state books the savings, and I’ve stopped correcting anybody.
