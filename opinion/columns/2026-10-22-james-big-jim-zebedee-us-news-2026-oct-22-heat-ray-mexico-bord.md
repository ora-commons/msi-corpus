Pete Hegseth is authorizing DHS to burn people at the border without naming its targets.

The human being at the center of this memo is somebody trying to cross the southern border. The public record gives no name, and no name is needed to understand what the beam does. It identifies the people the government itself put in front of this weapon in 2018: migrants. That person is somebody’s child or worker, somebody’s neighbor, a human being with a life outside the frame of the memo. The machine will make their skin burn, their body flinch, and their legs carry them away from the beam. For 10 to 15 minutes.

That is the product.

That is the purpose.

The Active Denial System fires an invisible 95-gigahertz beam that reaches about one sixty-fourth of an inch into the skin. A 2009 National Defense University case study describes the effect as “a sensation of intense heat on the surface of the skin” and says it “creates an intolerable sensation and reflexive movement.”

That is ducking.

That is running.

According to CNN, Pentagon officials have compared the feeling to a blast of heat when opening a hot oven. The effect lasts 10 to 15 minutes. The test ground is not a laboratory. It is a human community at the border.

Two words from the system’s official history do most of the hiding.

In 2012, the Marine Corps announced that the Active Denial System was “compliant with the international legal obligations of the US.” Officials said they felt “comfortable” that it was safe. That was legal clearance, not a warrant to burn a human being.

“Non-lethal” means the system is not built to kill. It does not mean harmless. “Compliant” means the government certified the instrument within its own legal frame. Neither phrase answers the question that reaches a body: should an invisible machine be fired at a human being at all?

The system makes a body burn and then leaves.

Now look at the memo itself. CNN obtained the document. Pete Hegseth reportedly signed it last month, in September, authorizing a “temporary loan” of the Active Denial System to DHS for seven months beginning in September and running into early 2027. DHS can request further use if it needs more time. The memo permits potential operations at the U.S.-Mexico border.

The most revealing sentence is the one saying no Department of Defense personnel shall operate it. DHS is made “solely responsible for the system’s employment, use and development of tactics, techniques and procedures.”

Here is the arrangement plain-spoken. Hegseth supplied the weapon. DHS controls its use and tactics. CBP supplied the “save lives” rationale. The person under the beam has no say.

The signature is upstairs. The beam is downstairs.

The Pentagon referred comment to DHS. DHS passed along a statement from Customs and Border Protection saying the agency is “continually evaluating new technology, especially technology that can save lives while simultaneously enhancing national security effectiveness.” DHS has said nothing beyond that prepared sentence.

Hold on to “simultaneously.” It is the tell. The phrase claims there is no trade-off between saving lives and making people suffer the pain required to move their bodies.

Non-lethal means the body does not die. Save lives means the body leaves. Both phrases keep the pain out of the sentence.

The history matters because this weapon has repeatedly been sought and never used. The U.S. military brought the system to Afghanistan nearly 20 years ago and brought it home without firing it. Under the first Trump administration, officials sought it in 2018 to stop migrants crossing the border. In 2020, after the killing of George Floyd, they sought it to control protests in Washington, with military police reportedly involved. CBP officials also weighed using it in areas with large numbers of illegal crossings or for crowd dispersal.

Sought for migrants.

Sought for crowds.

Never once used.

Eight years of proposals, zero shots.

The 2020 plan ran through military police. This memo says no Department of Defense operator will be at the trigger. The absence of a military chain of command does not make the pain less painful. It only puts the military outside the room.

The blank is the policy.

A weapon with no mission. A seven-month window. A renewal clause. An instruction that the Pentagon’s operators stay out of the operation. The document does not say what the test is for, who may be targeted, or under what circumstances the beam may be fired at a person. It says only that DHS may use it at the border.

That is why the paper is not a weapons decision. It is an allocation of blame.

DHS need not misuse the Active Denial System. DHS need not break one term of the memo. A test with a blank purpose is a machine with an open brief, and the record already supplies the missing pages: migrants and crowds.

Here is what a test with no mission means in practice. The document does not have to direct the beam at anyone for the blank to do its work. It creates the legal and bureaucratic room in which the answer can become whatever circumstance supplies. The memo promises seven months. The sensation runs 10 to 15 minutes. And the document keeps them in separate rooms: someone will sign, someone will fire the beam, and nobody will have done both.

Then there is the name.

Technically, the system uses millimeter waves rather than infrared. It is not a heat ray at all. DHS does not need to call it that. “Heat ray” is what you call a machine when you want the public to hear engineering and the officials to hear law enforcement. It is the oldest move in the repertoire of crowd control: pain applied from a distance by somebody the target cannot see, described in the vocabulary of physics until the pain becomes a property of the device rather than a choice made by the state.

Sun Tzu, in *The Art of War*, Chapter 3, called the supreme excellence bringing the enemy to terms without fighting. This system does not outmaneuver an enemy or break a formation. It makes skin dictate retreat. Call it strategy and you hide what reaches the human body. Call it what it is: pain with procurement paperwork.

The memo is not an anomaly in this Pentagon. It is the method.

On April 4, 2017, a photograph caught an Active Denial System at rest in Wellton, Arizona, an armored machine sitting in the dirt and waiting for an assignment that never came. Nine years later, the technology has not changed. What has changed is that somebody finally found a department willing to sign for it.
