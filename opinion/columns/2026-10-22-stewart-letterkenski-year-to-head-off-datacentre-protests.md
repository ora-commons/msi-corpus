Microsoft, Nvidia and Cisco want to buy British consent for datacentres before anyone can refuse.

It is true that the warning is urgent, and it is true that the clock is real. The Sovereign AI Leadership taskforce — with Nvidia, Microsoft and Cisco among its members — has told the Labour government that Britain has roughly 12 months to build a compact that earns public consent before American-style opposition starts to stop datacentre projects in their tracks. Across the Atlantic, organised local opposition has stopped fully permitted projects and triggered moratoriums even in states otherwise described as pro-growth, and the backlash is already reshaping electoral politics, as [our reporting on local resistance and the US midterms](/articles/2026-10-22-local-resistance-pressures-tech-giants-ahead-of-the-u-s-midterms/) shows. The trouble is that the taskforce has diagnosed the disease accurately and prescribed a way of keeping it alive.

What the taskforce wants is a framework for councils and mayors, with recommended local-benefit terms: an indicative contribution, financial or in facilities, scaled to the size of each site, and more tax revenue for hosts. That is the whole scheme. It is a compensation package for communities that might say no. It has supplied a deadline. It has not supplied consent.

And the refusal already exists. Kat Jones, director of Action to Protect Rural Scotland, counts 15 community campaigns opposing hyperscale AI datacentres and a growing national campaign, and says companies have already "missed the boat on containing community opposition." In north Devon, residents have [mobilised against Xlinks' 1.5GW datacentre and 1.8GW battery proposal](/articles/2026-09-27-north-devon-residents-mobilize-against-xlinks-1-5gw-ai-datacentre-plan/). Jones says even extensive lobbying by American technology companies will struggle against a public opinion that has shifted. More lobbying will not answer an argument about whether those promises deserve trust.

Joseph Miller, director of PauseAI UK, which campaigns for a global pause to datacentre construction, rejected the underlying bargain outright: "People are going to feel it is undignified to be bought off by huge tech companies who do not share their interests." That identifies a category error, not a difference of taste. A council can be compensated. A community can only give — or withhold — legitimacy.

Now look at the metric. The contribution is scaled to the size of the site. That measures the datacentre, not the community. A national template can make two incompatible places look commensurable and price refusal only by footprint; it cannot price the local reasons to refuse. What it can do is standardise the price of permission while leaving the basis of consent local, contested and unstandardised. This is not a settlement between a company and a place. It is an invoice, with the terms set by the party that wrote the cheque.

The taskforce's own submission makes the case against itself. Distrust of corporate and government promises, the risk of stranded assets and the burden of electricity costs, it says, run deeper than opposition to AI itself, and successive dry summers and rising bills have sharpened the material grievance. Donald Campbell, Foxglove's director of advocacy, says secrecy and misleading information have characterised companies' use of the planning system. A contribution can provide a community centre or finance a service. It cannot answer who bears the consequences if a datacentre becomes a stranded asset, and it cannot repair trust. Those objections are of one kind — material, and answerable by money in principle. The second kind is not answerable by money at all. Opposition now fuses the material grievance with AI-safety alarm: protesters have used direct action, membership in anti-AI campaign groups has been growing quickly, and The Guardian reported that OpenAI, Anthropic and Google appeared to lose control of some of their most cutting-edge AI systems over the summer. A benefits menu cannot absorb objections of two incompatible kinds. A community centre does not settle whether AI is safe. The safety objection is not price-elastic.

Microsoft's scale is what makes the question unavoidable. The company has promised $30bn (£22bn) for UK AI infrastructure, built a datacentre in Acton, London, has another under construction in Newport, wants several more facilities, and has been engaging local and national leaders — including Prime Minister Andy Burnham — on the balance between economic benefits and public opposition. A cheque of that size buys the attention of a government. It does not buy the agreement of the parish it lands in.

Set the fiscal terms beside it and the alchemy becomes visible. The government already allows English local authorities hosting datacentres in AI growth zones to keep all the additional business rates generated. The taskforce wants a broader arrangement under which hosting councils keep all of their business rates, and it has not calculated what that would cost the Treasury. Campbell warns that retaining additional revenue for councils need not require technology companies to pay more: increased funding for councils could come from central government services such as the NHS. A promise to keep more money in a council is not a promise of a better public service. If no additional company contribution follows, it is cost-shifting dressed up as devolution. The corporation wins permission, the council gains tax revenue, public services absorb the cost. It is patronage with an AI gloss. It is not community consent: the community receives neither the decision nor, necessarily, the money.

The government's own stated conditions are more defensible than the taskforce's. Datacentres should create British jobs and supply chains, invest in host communities, prioritise clean power and use water responsibly. Those are the terms on which resource-intensive industrial infrastructure gets to operate at all — the floor, not a favour that entitles a company to gratitude before it has done anything. A national indicative contribution and facilities package would land on top of duties already owed, and the community is asked to read the increment as the reward. The compact makes generosity the story and consent the price.

Steve Rigby, the taskforce's chair, says the goal is "not another strategy document" but "high-impact actions that materially accelerate the UK's transition to an AI-enabled economy." He is right that the need for action is real. The 12-month deadline is the confession. Companies know they have capital, not consent, and they want to spend the first to acquire the second before the objections become politically decisive.

A credible compact would not look like this. It would publish site-level energy and water commitments, price the fiscal consequences of the business-rates arrangement instead of leaving them to the Treasury, specify what companies must provide, and make sure a benefit agreement cannot become a purchase price for planning approval. It would give elected local authorities an explicit power to refuse a proposal after local consultation, with any negotiated benefit paid as the consequence of that decision rather than a substitute for it. It would keep communities inside the process, rather than waiting for them to bargain over what they have already refused.

Britain should not mistake the result for a mandate. A company may build a datacentre in a community that accepts it. It cannot call resistance a market failure merely because its cheque was not large enough.

Money can fund a benefit. It cannot manufacture consent. Consent is not a discount. It is a decision.
