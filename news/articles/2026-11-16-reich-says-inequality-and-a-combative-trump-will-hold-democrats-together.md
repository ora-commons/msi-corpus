## Reich points to wages at 43% of gross domestic income, lowest since 1929

Robert Reich, a former US secretary of labor, wrote in a column published by The Guardian on Nov. 16, 2026, that the Democratic Party's historical pattern of intraparty feuding is unlikely to repeat when the party takes power in January. Reich identified two forces he said would keep the party's coalition intact: what he called raging economic inequality, and what he called "an even more combative Trump along with Trump's more belligerent sycophants."

Democrats, Reich wrote, usually suffer "big tent disease." To win, they assemble coalitions of voters so large — "working class, middle class, Black people, Latinos, women, young people, moderates, liberals" — that once they are in office, intraparty feuds often threaten their agenda. Asked whether it would happen again when Democrats take power in January, Reich wrote: "I don't think so."

Reich based his first argument on shifts in the distribution of income. He wrote that wages and salaries have fallen to 43% of the country's gross domestic income, "the lowest since 1929," while corporate profits have climbed to "a record high of 12.4%." Those profits, Reich wrote, go to the owners of corporations — top executives and investors whose incomes depend largely on shares of stock.

Reich wrote that these trends are squeezing average working Americans and caused a large majority of voters to demand change in the midterm elections. He wrote that the trends will not change anytime soon because they are the result of monopolies "now baked into the economy," combined with the lowest level of labor unionization in almost a century, and what he called "a vicious cycle of legal bribes paid to politicians by big corporations and wealthy individuals seeking to keep their taxes low and maintain their government perks."

Reich argued that this inequality has given Democrats a clear goal — to reduce it — and a clear villain: billionaires and greedy corporations. He wrote that the recent campaigns of nearly all newly elected Democratic members of Congress, "whether 'moderate' or 'democratic socialist'," blasted billionaires and corporate greed, and that Democratic winners from purple battleground states are singing from the same hymnals as winners from deep blue states.

Reich quoted representative-elect Sam Forstag of Montana, who flipped a conservative seat, as saying he would "fight for populist economic policies that are actually going to help working people, and finally put us back at the front of the line in terms of who Congress should be working for." Reich also wrote that in what had been ruby-red Republican Kansas, senator-elect Adam Hamilton campaigned to expand Medicare to cover vision and dental care; that Texas senator-elect James Talarico, a self-styled moderate, wants to allow every American, regardless of age, to join Medicare; and that Michigan senator-elect Abdul El-Sayed and Maine's Troy Jackson want the same.

Reich's second force is Trump. He wrote that Trump "likes nothing better than to go on the attack," and that for the second half of his term, Trump and his party have selected what Reich called attack dogs: new chief of staff Stephen Miller and new House minority leader Jim Jordan. Reich wrote that Trump also decided to keep the most belligerent member of his cabinet, Secretary of Defense Pete Hegseth.

Reich wrote that it would be different if the midterm shellacking had caused Trump to rethink his approach to governing — making Trump more amenable to working with the new Democratic majorities, softening his rhetoric, moving to fire Hegseth and appoint a more reasonable defense secretary, installing a chief of staff who would extend peace offerings, and backing a House Republican leader who would collaborate with the Democrats. Under those circumstances, Reich wrote, some Democratic members of the next Congress might be tempted to tone down their demands and work with Trump Republicans.

But Trump has chosen the opposite approach, Reich wrote. He pointed to a letter from White House counsel Will Scharf to incoming House oversight committee chair Robert Garcia, warning that aggressive congressional investigations into the Trump administration could "cross the line into blatant criminality," which Reich called an illustration of the sort of hostility likely to be seen starting in January.

Reich wrote that Trump's fragile ego may feel more secure this way: as long as Trump's attack dogs fill the airwaves with pugilistic assaults and nasty invective, Reich wrote, Trump will not have to admit to himself what Reich called the mammoth failure of his presidency or its colossal repudiation by voters.

Reich wrote that Trump's belligerence, and that of the people around him, are a gift to the Democrats. As with the record levels of inequality uniting Democrats, Reich wrote, the administration's hostility toward them will also help keep Democrats together. "The Democrats' big tent disease has found two big cures," Reich concluded.

Robert Reich, a former US secretary of labor, is a professor of public policy emeritus at the University of California, Berkeley. He is a Guardian US columnist, and his newsletter is at robertreich.substack.com. His book *Coming Up Short: A Memoir of My America* is out now in the US and in the UK.
