## The option delays work-rule disenrollment through June without pausing renewals

Pennsylvania is weighing whether to use a hold-harmless option from the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services that would delay Medicaid eligibility determinations and disenrollments tied to work requirements through the end of June. The state must decide by Dec. 11; otherwise, the work requirements — or volunteer hours — begin in January. States learned earlier this month that they could delay enforcement for Medicaid recipients.

Pennsylvania's Department of Human Services is reviewing CMS guidance “to determine next steps,” according to a DHS spokesperson. The spokesperson said, “It is important to understand that this option is not a full delay of Medicaid work requirements or any other change to Medicaid required by (the law).” The spokesperson added, “Taking advantage of this hold harmless option would only delay eligibility determinations and disenrollments made based on compliance with the new rules, it does not suspend the need for individuals to complete their Medicaid renewal.”

According to health policy researchers at KFF, 16 states have said they will delay enforcement, while another three said they would “likely” adopt the hold-harmless option. Pennsylvania was one of 18 states that had yet to decide.

Amy Lowenstein, the director of policy with the Pennsylvania Health Law Project, said Pennsylvania would still need to determine whether recipients complied during the delay but would not have to terminate anyone's coverage for noncompliance. “They'll still go through the actions, but if someone turns out to be non-compliant, then they're not supposed to take negative action,” Lowenstein said in a recent webinar.

The source report said the new rules were a priority for the Trump administration but affected only a select number of enrollees. Recipients could meet the requirements through work, volunteering or school. Exemptions included caregivers, students and others; people with a documented substance use disorder, recently released prisoners and parents of young children were also exempt.

Stakeholders told States Newsroom that communicating exemptions was a major challenge. Previously, states redetermined a person's Medicaid eligibility annually, but redeterminations must now happen at a minimum of twice a year. An estimated 63% of Medicaid enrollees work 80 hours a month or attend school, while another 15% were exempt because they qualified as caregivers or had a disability. Some of the remaining enrollees were retired or might be unable to find work for other reasons.

Medical frailty was an early point of contention, and Lowenstein called the category “difficult to judge.” “DHS is going to do its best to try to figure out who falls into this exemption using information it already has,” Lowenstein said. “Hopefully, if that works, that means people will not have to do a lot to demonstrate this.”

The law passed by Congress carved out an exemption for medically frail people, including someone with an HIV diagnosis or a disability such as blindness. The administration added a rule over the summer requiring a person's ability to work to be “significantly” impaired to qualify. Pennsylvania joined a multistate lawsuit to overturn that rule, and a judge ruled in favor of the states in late October.

Pennsylvania is also building administrative capacity. A DHS spokesperson said the agency had hired more than 700 caseworkers between November 2025 and October 2026, with those workers responsible for verifying whether Pennsylvanians met the work requirements. “It does take up to a year for a new caseworker to complete comprehensive training and master the skills needed to take on full caseload processing, which is what will make an impact on priorities like Medicaid redeterminations,” the spokesperson continued.

Advocates said documentation requirements and exemption rules could present barriers for people seeking health coverage. In a recent release, the Legal Action Center said, “While this is an important option that all states should pursue, merely delaying the harm of a horrible law is not a sustainable solution.” The release said participants may be confused by several rule changes.

In the same statement, the Legal Action Center said, “For people with substance use disorders and people leaving incarceration, such confusion could lead them to delay or stop seeking treatment and other care they need. That's an enormous harm in itself, and yet another reason that Congress must repeal the rules once and for all.”

The Legal Action Center also pointed to newly imposed work requirements for food benefits. Pennsylvania's Department of Human Services estimated that 98,000 Pennsylvanians no longer received food benefits because of those rules. The organization urged the incoming Democratic majority in Congress to repeal the requirements for both programs “to prevent further coverage losses and restore the vital food assistance many families have already lost.”

U.S. Rep. Hakeem Jeffries, the Democratic nominee for House speaker, said the Medicaid cuts included in the bill should be reversed, but the process could be lengthy.

Some states pushed their work requirements ahead of the planned rollout, with different results. In Nebraska, the vast majority of recently denied applicants had failed to provide the required documentation, meaning they could still qualify, States Newsroom reported.


[Maryland adopted the federal hold-harmless option](/articles/2026-11-21-maryland-delays-medicaid-work-requirement-enforcement-through-june/) before Pennsylvania's Dec. 11 deadline. Pennsylvania is now considering the same delay.
