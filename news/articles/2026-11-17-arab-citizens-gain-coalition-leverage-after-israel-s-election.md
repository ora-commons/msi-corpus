## Neither Netanyahu nor Eisenkot secured a parliamentary majority

Arab-led parties made major gains in parliamentary representation in Israel’s Oct. 27 vote, and neither Benjamin Netanyahu’s bloc nor Gadi Eisenkot’s bloc secured the parliamentary majority needed to form a government. The result leaves Arab parties controlling the remaining seats and potentially able to determine the next prime minister. The Israel Democracy Institute estimated turnout in Arab-majority communities at 77%, up from 53% in the last election.

The stakes extend beyond coalition arithmetic. Israel has 2 million Arab citizens, comprising one-fifth of Israel’s citizens. The Arab citizens share deep religious, cultural and familial ties with Palestinians in the occupied West Bank, Gaza and beyond. Arik Rudnitzky, a researcher with the Israel Democracy Institute and Tel Aviv University’s Moshe Dayan Center, said about 85% of Israel’s Palestinian citizens are Muslim, with Christian and Druze minorities each in the single digits. They mostly live in the Galilee in northern Israel, in small towns along the West Bank in central Israel, in Bedouin communities in the southern desert, and in a few large cities like Haifa.

Hillel Cohen, a professor of Islamic and Middle Eastern Studies at the Hebrew University of Jerusalem, said the majority of approximately 800,000 Palestinians either fled their homes or were expelled during the 1948 war that established the modern state of Israel. They sought refuge in neighboring countries and in what are now the West Bank and Gaza. About 170,000 remained and became Israeli citizens, although they were under military rule until the mid-1960s.

Palestinian citizens have reached the highest levels of Israeli academia, medicine, entertainment, business and the judiciary, including the Supreme Court. Yet many Arab communities also struggle with high rates of crime and poverty.

Unlike most of their Jewish counterparts, Palestinian citizens are not conscripted into Israel’s military except for the Druze, a small religious sect that began as an offshoot of Islam, and very few volunteer, Rudnitzky said. For many Israelis, those youth years in the armed forces are a melting pot experience and rite of passage, so not joining can lay a foundation for divisions later in life.

Identity has become another central dividing line. Scholars say being Muslim and being Palestinian have become more important in Israel’s Arab communities in recent decades, often driving a wedge at the political, cultural and personal level, even as most Palestinian citizens speak Hebrew and grow increasingly integrated in Israeli society. Elie Rekhess, a visiting professor of Israel Studies at Northwestern University, said, “The crux of the matter with regard to the Arab minority in Israel is the question of identity.” He added, “How do you identify yourself? Israeli, Palestinian, Arab, Muslim, and then you get the hyphenated versions.”

Amal Jamal, Tel Aviv University’s dean of the Faculty of Social Sciences and head of the Walter Lebach Institute for the Study of Jewish-Arab Coexistence, is Palestinian Israeli. “Their practical culture is very Israeli,” he said. He continued: “But, because (being) Israeli is identified with being Jewish worldwide, and because the Jewish community views Israel to belong to them ... you won’t find them saying, ‘I’m an Israeli’ in the sentimental sense.” For many Jewish Israelis, particularly after the Oct. 7 attacks, that ambiguity became doubt about Palestinian Israelis and “whose side” they are on — the state of Israel or Palestinian militants, Rudnitzky said.

The country remains deeply divided and scarred by the Oct. 7, 2023, Hamas-led attacks and the multifront wars they triggered. Many Israelis see some Palestinians as a threat, while many Palestinians feel increasingly silenced, discriminated against and treated as second-class citizens. Jamal said, “They felt an existential threat as a result of the policies of the current government.”

According to the Associated Press, Arab communities are suffering from a major rise in sometimes deadly organized crime, and leaders have accused the Israeli police of neglecting them. The outlet described the police as “led by a far-right politician.” Jamal said many face discrimination from the government, including in state spending on essential services such as housing for the fast-growing population. “They feel discriminated against on a daily basis,” he said. These concerns drove the high turnout in Arab-majority communities, which in turn drove Arab-led parties to major gains in parliamentary representation in the Oct. 27 vote.

The negotiations now place Netanyahu and Eisenkot in direct conflict over possible cooperation with Arab parties. Netanyahu has claimed Arab politicians cannot be trusted and said Eisenkot would be committing “an act of betrayal” by joining forces with them. Eisenkot denounced Netanyahu’s rhetoric as “hateful” but has also expressed reservations about partnering with Arab parties.

Eisenkot left the possibility open if the Arab parties meet several conditions, including accepting Israel’s identity as a Jewish state and declaring Hamas a terrorist group. It is unclear the parties will accept those conditions, and some in Eisenkot’s own coalition balk at them. Netanyahu and his allies have called such partnering tantamount to treason.

The Joint List, a coalition of three left-wing and nationalist Arab parties, supports a Palestinian state in the West Bank, Gaza and east Jerusalem — territories occupied in the 1967 Mideast war — and rejects Israel’s identity as a Jewish state.

Ra’am, the only Arab party ever to join a governing coalition, did so in 2021 and presents a more pragmatic position. A Jewish cop-turned-politician will be one of its representatives in the newly elected Knesset. The party’s roots lie in the Islamist movement that reshaped politics across the Arab world beginning in the 1970s, edging out both communist and nationalist parties that remain part of the political landscape. Although Ra’am describes itself as secular today, it defends religion’s role in society, including holding gender-segregated campaign events. Experts say this places Arab parties in alignment with several Jewish religious parties on social issues; those parties have also served as important kingmakers in past coalitions.

Many Israelis, Jewish and Palestinian, see the coalition debate as reaching beyond arithmetic: it indicates how far the growing Arab population is included in the life of the state and how much its members feel they belong. With the major blocs short of a majority and Arab parties potentially decisive, those questions remain unresolved as Netanyahu and Eisenkot pursue coalition partners.
