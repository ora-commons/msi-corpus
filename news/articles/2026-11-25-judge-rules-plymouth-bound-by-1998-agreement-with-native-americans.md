## Judge absolves town of providing stage it supplied for 27 years

A Plymouth, Massachusetts, superior court judge ruled on Tuesday that the town's 1998 settlement agreement with the United American Indians of New England is legally binding, in a year-long dispute over funding and permits for the group's annual National Day of Mourning protest. Judge Brian Glenny barred Plymouth from requiring insurance or permits for the event and cleared the town of providing the mobile stage it has supplied for 27 years.

The dispute followed a unanimous vote on 10 November, 16 days before Thanksgiving, in which Plymouth's select board met in private and voted to breach the long-standing agreement again, demanding that the group obtain insurance to indemnify the town at a cost of several hundred dollars. UAINE's lawyers filed an emergency motion for a temporary restraining order and a preliminary injunction. On Monday, local supporters of UAINE gathered outside holding signs that read "Selectmen - stop abusing Plymouth's first citizens" and "Stop breaking treaties". Judge Glenny heard 90 minutes of arguments, during which the original agreement's wording was examined closely.

The National Day of Mourning was founded in 1970 by Wamsutta Frank James, a member of the federally recognised Wampanoag Tribe of Gay Head (Aquinnah) on Martha's Vineyard, after he was uninvited to speak at the state's 350th anniversary of the Mayflower landing over his critical views. For 50 years, as millions of Americans sit down to a turkey dinner, hundreds of Indigenous people and allies have gathered peacefully at noon on Cole's Hill above Plymouth Rock, in what is known as "America's Hometown", a town founded by the Pilgrims in 1620 on the ancestral Wampanoag homeland of Patuxet.

At the gathering, food is shared, stories are told and banners are paraded through the streets, commemorating millions of Native Americans killed by European settlers, the theft of land and the erasure of culture, while challenging Thanksgiving "myths". UAINE calls the day "a solemn, spiritual and highly political day".

The event has a legal history of its own. In 1997, violence broke out: town and state police were accused of pepper-spraying marchers, injuring elders and arresting 25 people, and video footage from that day shows police hand-cuffing and dragging people. A civil lawsuit ended in a 1998 out-of-court settlement allegedly amounting to $135,000, in which Plymouth agreed not to impede future protests, to provide a mobile stage and not to require the organisation to ask for "permission to protest on stolen land", including permits and prior notice. UAINE said at the time: "Plymouth has acknowledged our right to walk on our own land without a permit on National Day of Mourning. Plymouth has agreed to make the truth part of its celebration of the pilgrim myth of Thanksgiving … We are confident that this agreement represents a tremendous victory for the struggle of Native people to have our voices heard and respected."

That arrangement broke down in 2025, when the town's select board voted to renege on the agreement and was overruled in court. The current dispute revived the question of who pays for the gathering and whether the group must obtain permission to hold it.

Plymouth's lawyers argued that the agreement does not specifically mention insurance and that UAINE should apply for it like other organisers of public events. They also told the court that the "showmobile", the mobile stage cited in the original agreement, is dilapidated, and that the town declined to continue providing a replacement from its own staging equipment, as it had done since 2014.

UAINE countered that Plymouth was ripping up a 28-year-old political contract, violating the group's freedom of speech and trying to force an organisation that had been guaranteed the right to choose its own route to gather at a new location where members would be "out of sight". Plymouth denies trying to change the route; the town said the offer of a move was based on accessibility, parking and the use of an existing stage at that location.

In a statement, Plymouth said the town "supports and respects the constitutional rights to freedom of speech and peaceful assembly" and had attempted to find a solution for a year but did not receive a reply from UAINE's lawyers until August. The town added: "The town has gone above and beyond to accommodate UAINE's event, including facilitating accessible parking, assisting elders, identifying locations for meals and extended observances, and coordinating with police and fire officials. The town has continuously demonstrated a willingness to address concerns, offer solutions, and make accommodations at every opportunity. These efforts reflect a genuine commitment to the event."

Glenny's ruling split the dispute in two. He held that the 1998 agreement was legally binding and banned the town from requiring insurance or permits for the event. He also absolved Plymouth from providing a stage, as it had done for 27 years. The ruling appeared to open a path for Plymouth to challenge the 1998 agreement again in the future by stating that the 10 November vote was legal.

Mahtowin Munro, an Oglala Lakota and co-leader of UAINE, called the ruling "mixed" and said the group would go ahead with this year's protest. She said: "Settlers frequently break their agreements with Indigenous peoples. In fact, the US has broken every single treaty it signed with Native Nations. And we know that we can't count on the courts of the colonizers to give us justice." Munro also warned that the fight was settler colonialism hiding behind "red tape", and said the group did not expect justice from "the courts of colonizers". She suspected Plymouth was "throwing up roadblocks because they don't want the tourists to see or hear Native Americans who are speaking about the true history of the Pilgrim invasion and resulting land theft and genocide of Wampanoag and other Native peoples".

Jean-Luc Pierite, president of the North American Indian Centre of Boston (Naicob), which is the commonwealth's designated liaison for members of the Penobscot, Passamaquoddy, Mi'kmaq and Maliseet tribes, said the insistence on bureaucracy was being used to erase the group's relationship and history with the town and the United States. "This is political oppression. This is about holding government agencies to account for breach of contract, for breaking treaties," Pierite told the Guardian. Pierite, a member of the federally recognised Tunica-Biloxi tribe of Louisiana, said Native Americans were not just "any other group" holding a parade.

"This isn't about resiliency of Indigenous peoples. We are going to find a way for National Day of Mourning to happen, but we do need to make sure they are held accountable for this 1998 agreement," Pierite said. He noted that Plymouth has been a site of government relationships since April 1621 with the original treaty with Chief Massasoit, a defensive alliance between Europeans and Wampanoag lasting 50 years. "Treaties have been endlessly interpreted and reinterpreted over centuries. This is one part of a very long fight."

Deb Iaquinto, Plymouth select board chair, said: "The Town has tried to work collaboratively to support UAINE's rights to freedom of speech and assembly." She added: "In light of this ruling, the Town remains committed to supporting the National Day of Mourning."
