## Plaintiffs are likely to prevail on privacy and equal-protection claims

A Missoula County District Court judge has barred Montana from enforcing Senate Bill 437, a law that defines sex as a binary classification based on a person’s reproductive system and amends wide-ranging sections of state law to define “male,” “female,” “sex” and “gender.” Judge Leslie Halligan issued the preliminary injunction on Tuesday in a lawsuit filed by Helena-based Upper Seven Law on behalf of transgender, intersex and Two Spirit Montanans while the litigation continues.

The statute requires that “gender” be considered a synonym for “sex” as defined in the law and says it may not be treated as synonymous with a person’s gender identity, experienced gender, gender expression or gender role. The injunction is intended to prevent uncertainty over court orders that found a similar 2023 law unconstitutional and, Halligan wrote, to “avert inconsistent enforcement of a statute that plaintiffs have shown is likely unconstitutional.”

SB 437 is largely similar to 2023’s Senate Bill 458. That law was declared unconstitutional twice: first in June 2024 because its subject was not clear in its title, and again in February 2025 because a judge found it violated the equal-protection clause of the Montana Constitution.

Gov. Greg Gianforte signed SB 437 into law in March, nearly a year after it passed the Legislature, according to a spokesperson for House Republicans. The spokesperson said the delay was intended to keep the new bill from becoming entangled in ongoing litigation over SB 458. Upper Seven Law litigation director Molly Danahy said in a press release: “After the court found the state’s first attempt to erase transgender, non-binary, intersex, and Two Spirit Montanans unconstitutional, the Legislature made a second attempt to pass essentially the same law — and then took unprecedented steps to avoid judicial review.” She added: “But even these extraordinary procedural shenanigans — including holding the bill for nearly a year before signing it into law — couldn’t save it from ordinary constitutional review.”

Kaitlin Timken, a spokesperson for the governor’s office, said the office generally does not comment on ongoing litigation and referred to a statement issued by Gianforte and House Speaker Brandon Ler when the lawsuit was filed this spring. The statement said: “This law codifies the long-recognized, commonsense, immutable, biologically-based definition of sex, male and female, while protecting people born intersex and not infringing on transgender individuals’ ability to identify with whatever gender, but not sex, they wish.” It also said: “To no one’s surprise, activists have moved to add this law into ongoing litigation to stop it from going into effect.” The statement said Montana would defend the duly enacted law and ensure it received a fair hearing in Montana’s judiciary.

The case followed a complaint filed April 6, the state’s response on May 22 and a preliminary-injunction hearing held June 19 in Missoula. The plaintiffs argued that SB 437 is unconstitutional and requires them to disclose private medical information.

Montana’s defense pointed to differences between SB 437 and SB 458 and argued that the new law does not demand access to private medical information.

Halligan wrote that the relevant question was not whether SB 437 contained “precisely the same words” as SB 458, but whether the differences altered the constitutional analysis. She said the definitions were not the same but the “operative effect” appeared to be the same, imposing a “state-mandated binary classification of sex that, under this court’s prior ruling, infringes on Montanans’ right to define their own gender identity.”

In her order, Halligan wrote: “The court has found that plaintiffs are likely to succeed on claims that SB 437 violates the rights to privacy and equal protection and that enforcement will cause ongoing irreparable harm.” For now, the preliminary injunction prevents Montana from enforcing SB 437 while the case proceeds.
