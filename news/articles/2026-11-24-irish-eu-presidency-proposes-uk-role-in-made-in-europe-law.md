## France tied UK eligibility to Chinese-vehicle tariff alignment

Ireland, which holds the rotating EU presidency and plays a chief role in progressing legislation, has proposed a compromise that would give the UK partner status for car assembly under Made in Europe legislation. The UK would not have to follow the EU and apply tariffs on Chinese car imports, according to the proposal. The fourth compromise text is due to be considered by EU competition ministers on 3 December.

The proposal would give the European Economic Area, Switzerland and the UK partner status for car assembly. Made in Europe, also known as the Industrial Acceleration Act, is designed to protect EU supply chains from cheap competition, mainly from China and mainly in the automotive and clean-energy sectors.

The compromise would allow cars such as the Mini, made at BMW's Oxford factory, and vehicles made by Nissan at its Sunderland plant to qualify as European. A partner country would agree to mutual access to subsidies available to EU-owned assembly plants and public-procurement contracts for fleet cars. Ireland also proposed allowing Japan and South Korea, which lobbied hard against the original Made in Europe proposals, access to public procurement and other public support schemes for car parts and components.

The latest proposal will be discussed by an EU working group involving industry leaders and member states on Friday before being revised further for consideration by ministers. An Irish presidency spokesperson said: “Having listened closely to all member states, the presidency has proposed a pragmatic and balanced way forward on the IAA to deliver for European competitiveness.”

France, which devised the legislation, originally opposed including the UK but dropped its opposition last month on condition that the UK dynamically align with EU tariffs on Chinese vehicles. The condition caused further tensions with the UK. Business Secretary Jonathan Reynolds said the British government would not be coerced into imposing tariffs on Chinese vehicles and would take steps only in the national interest. The Irish compromise would separate UK assembly status from that broader alignment requirement.

Mike Hawes, chief executive of the Society of Motor Manufacturers, said the UK “should not become collateral damage” from measures intended to address concerns arising elsewhere in the global market. He said: “It is essential that the final arrangements recognise the unique UK-EU automotive relationship as allies in adversity, and do not create new barriers to our shared supply chains.” The UK car industry has argued that its operations are intertwined with EU counterparts and that both sides would be damaged if the UK were excluded.

Andy Burnham last week sealed deals to ease trade and create a mobility scheme for young people. The Guardian reported that the agreements represented the biggest step toward deeper ties with the EU since Brexit. The latest Industrial Acceleration Act text is designed to reflect the car sector's position as a cornerstone of European industry.

China faces the prospect of partial restrictions in its trade with the EU. Tariffs were imposed on Chinese electric-vehicle imports in 2024, and quotas agreed with Beijing in October are due to come into force on 1 December. European carmakers are attempting to respond to a large influx of cheap Chinese electric vehicles into the bloc.

Volkswagen began wage talks with labour representatives on Tuesday amid mounting worker anger over deep cost cuts. Protesters in Emden, Germany, carried black coffins to symbolize what they said was the death of trust and the company's future prospects.

Chinese electric-vehicle maker BYD said on Tuesday that it had entered into exclusive negotiations with billionaire Jim Ratcliffe's Ineos Automotive to buy its manufacturing plant in Hambach, France, as it sought more European production ahead of Made in Europe legislation next year. Separately, Ineos said on Tuesday that it would begin production of its Grenadier 4x4 in the US in 2028.
