## Leadership election set for January RNC meeting in Arizona

A challenge to President Donald Trump's grip on the Republican Party is building inside the Republican National Committee, where a small but growing group of the organization's 168 elected members is backing an effort to depose Chairman Joe Gruters. The leadership election will be held in January at the RNC's winter meeting in Arizona.

The dissidents are rallying behind Kentucky's KC Crosbie, the RNC's second-in-command, who launched her bid two days after the Nov. 3 election in a letter that prompted angry reactions from the White House and Gruters' allies. Crosbie described the midterms as "a serious setback" and called for the RNC to provide "an honest account" of what went wrong. "We should preserve what we built and be equally willing to confront where we fell short," she wrote. Crosbie declined to be interviewed.

Crosbie remains a vocal Trump supporter who backs his "Make America Great Again" agenda, but her opponents view her gambit as a direct challenge to the president and his power. Nearly half a dozen Crosbie supporters said the opposition has little to do with Trump's leadership or policy decisions, and instead focuses on the RNC's spending decisions under Gruters. Feuding Republicans are leaking emails and opposition research on rival factions. Republicans held a massive fundraising advantage and still lost the House and Senate.

RNC member Oscar Brock of Tennessee, who is supporting Crosbie, said the party needs a change in leadership. "We have an obligation to serve the Republican Party the most effective way we can. And if all we do is say yes to what the White House wants, I'm not sure that's doing what's best for the Republican Party," Brock said. "Our job is to elect Republicans broadly, not to support one man all the time." He added: "There's so much frustration that Chairman Gruters didn't pull his weight in this election, that the RNC was far less effective than it should have been." Brock also said: "There's clearly a groundswell of support for a forensic audit."

Patti Lyman, an RNC member from Virginia, said it would be "catastrophic" and "insulting" to the party's base "to keep the same leadership and pretend that we don't need to do anything different, that we don't need new players and a new plan." Lyman described Crosbie as "by far the best candidate currently running to lead the RNC forward to victory in 2028."

Sue Cienki, an RNC committee member from Rhode Island, predicted that Crosbie would have the backing of at least two members from 10 states — a total of 20 — at the January meeting, the minimum required for a successful nomination. Cienki said she would not be scared off by backlash from Trump or the White House, which has been actively lobbying RNC members. "We should not be influenced by any one person or other. It's our organization," Cienki said. "We have to hold an election, not a selection."

It remains unclear how many RNC members will back Crosbie, although the number so far is believed to be small. Some would-be supporters privately acknowledge that they worry about a backlash from the White House, especially with Stephen Miller taking over as Trump's new White House chief of staff. Others believe in the committee's tradition of having presidents pick their own party chiefs.

Trump and senior White House officials were livid about Crosbie's Nov. 5 letter and the fact that they received no heads-up, according to a senior White House official with direct knowledge of a Nov. 6 White House meeting who was not authorized to publicly discuss the private gathering and spoke on condition of anonymity. During that meeting, Trump threatened to punish the RNC if members crossed him, saying he would refuse to help the organization, including appearing at fundraisers or allowing his name and image to be used in mail and digital solicitations. Such a threat would be catastrophic to the party because Trump remains its most effective money raiser. The same official said staff convinced Trump not to go after Crosbie personally that day; instead, he released a statement on Truth Social hailing Gruters as a "MAGA Warrior" and giving him his "Complete and Total Endorsement." An Axios story that first reported the threats was distributed by text message to all 168 RNC members. The White House referred questions about the leadership fight to Trump's Truth Social post.

Gruters also has vocal backers. Lauren LePage, an RNC member from Maine, praised the chairman's leadership and his efforts to help her father, Paul LePage, flip a congressional seat — a rare bright spot for Republicans this election cycle. She described Gruters as "highly responsive" during the campaign and praised his "record-setting fundraising." "From my perspective, as chairman, he did his job," she said, adding that Trump's endorsement is "something that the committee should take very seriously."

Nevada Republican Party Chairman Michael McDonald called Crosbie a "dear friend," but said Trump is "the leader of our party" and "you always have to fall in line with the leader." "I don't have a bad word to say about KC at all. It's just that the president wants to go with Joe Gruters," McDonald said. "The president made his choice."

In a letter to RNC members sent Thursday night and obtained by The Associated Press, Gruters acknowledged the concerns. He defended a $22,500 expenditure for hospitality space at the Presidents Cup, a golf event, saying it was offered to major donors and "resulted in $2.5 million in renewed contributions to the RNC." He also addressed concerns about "a brief contract" between the RNC and Caroline Wiles, a longtime Republican consultant who is the daughter of Susie Wiles, Trump's former chief of staff. Gruters said she was hired by his predecessor at the committee, Michael Whatley. "While I have no reason to doubt Caroline Wiles's value to the RNC or Chairman Whatley's reasons for bringing her on, this was a brief six-month engagement that ended when I was elected Chairman," he wrote.

Gruters also defended the RNC's decision to pay for "hair and make-up services" for senior White House officials, including Trump, Vice President JD Vance and former press secretary Karoline Leavitt. "This was a request made of us so that the expense did not fall to taxpayers," Gruters wrote. He said the beneficiaries "generously gave so much of their limited time and energies to help the RNC this cycle" and "did not ask for much of us in return, so we felt this was a request we could accommodate." Gruters promised to provide members "honest and direct answers to concerns raised" and wrote: "I pledge to listen and learn from you about what happened in the field, so together we can move forward stronger than ever."

People close to Gruters, who spoke on condition of anonymity to discuss private conversations, said Crosbie's campaign was "100% a challenge" to the president's power and described the RNC as "the president's political operation." Allies argued that a leadership change now would put the RNC at a severe disadvantage heading into the next presidential cycle because a new team would need time to get up to speed, and they warned that friction between the president and the committee's leadership would curtail the ability to organize and raise money. They also rejected Crosbie's call for a postelection review, which they said would do little more than rehash the past. Similar demands for a retrospective were a headache for Ken Martin, the Democratic National Committee's leader, after Trump won in 2024. Allies said Gruters has taken the criticism seriously and has been soliciting feedback to make adjustments heading into 2028.

Gruters spent much of the week holding private phone conversations with committee members. Those who participated in the calls said Gruters listened to their grievances but said little about what he would do differently.

The fight comes as the party absorbs its worst midterm performance in a generation, with losses even in conservative strongholds such as Texas, South Carolina and Mississippi — and it represents a rare challenge to Trump's dominance. Despite the heavy losses, Republican leaders across Washington have shown little desire to move past Trump and have largely stood behind his most unpopular policies. The Constitution bars Trump from seeking a third term, but he is expected to play a central role in picking the party's next presidential nominee. Trump's team contends that he remains the most popular Republican in the country, and House Republicans this week elected a slate of new leaders closely aligned with him. The RNC will oversee the party's presidential primary process, including setting the nominating calendar, the debate schedule, the national convention and the rules governing the selection of the White House candidate.
