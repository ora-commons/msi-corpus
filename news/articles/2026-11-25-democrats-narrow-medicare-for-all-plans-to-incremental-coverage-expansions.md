## Democrats narrow Medicare for All plans to incremental coverage expansions

**Subtype:** fact

- Incoming Democratic congressional candidates and leaders are narrowing Medicare for All proposals to incremental expansions of government-backed coverage.
- A Progressive Change Institute analysis found that roughly 20% of 64 winning battleground House and Senate Democratic candidates campaigned on Medicare for All, while 60% campaigned on a Medicare Buy-In or public option.
- Sen. Bernie Sanders proposed a four-year transition that would lower Medicare’s eligibility age to 55 in year one and extend public coverage to all Americans in year four.
- Sen.-elect Abdul El-Sayed favors guaranteed public coverage for all Americans while allowing private and employer-sponsored insurance to coexist.

- The U.S. Chamber of Commerce and business groups oppose Medicare for All, public options and Medicare buy-ins because they say the proposals would weaken employer-sponsored insurance.

## Sanders’ four-year phase-in requires Senate majority amid cost concerns

Incoming Democratic congressional candidates and leaders are taking a more incremental approach to Medicare for All as House and Senate plans begin to take shape. The Wall Street Journal reported Nov. 25 that high costs and private-sector opposition have constrained efforts to replace most private coverage, leaving early proposals unlikely to provide the comprehensive care described on the campaign trail.

Under a full Medicare for All system, a government-run program would replace most private health coverage and guarantee comprehensive benefits to all Americans without premiums, deductibles or copays for covered services. The federal government would assume responsibility for financing care now paid for by employers, households and private insurers. The change would reach a private insurance market covering more than 200 million Americans, and Washington has never come close to orchestrating such a change. According to federal data cited by the Journal, spending on private health insurance accounts for nearly one third of U.S. healthcare expenditures.

The political hurdles are also substantial. More than a dozen incoming congressional Democrats who support Medicare for All are unlikely to be enough to overcome obstacles including President Trump’s veto power, the Journal reported, and far more candidates backed modest extensions of government benefits than a full expansion. Health insurers, pharmaceutical companies and groups representing employers have opposed Medicare for All over the years to protect their businesses and avoid more government involvement in the private sector.

An analysis by the Progressive Change Institute, a liberal advocacy group based in Washington, D.C., examined 64 winning battleground House and Senate Democratic candidates. The analysis found that roughly 20% campaigned on Medicare for All, while 60% campaigned on a Medicare Buy-In or public option designed to expand government-sponsored coverage while preserving employer-sponsored and other private insurance. Dr. Ezekiel Emanuel, an oncologist who helped craft the Affordable Care Act, said, “Healthcare is a $6 trillion industry.” He called moving all of that to the federal budget “a major, major barrier.”

Sen. Bernie Sanders has proposed a four-year transition to a single-payer system. In the first year, his plan would lower Medicare’s eligibility age to 55, extend coverage to all children and add dental, vision and hearing benefits. The eligibility age would fall to 45 in the second year and 35 in the third before public coverage extended to all Americans in the fourth year. Sanders’ proposal would also bar private insurers from selling coverage that duplicates the government program’s benefits.

Sanders wants Democrats to use budget reconciliation, which allows certain tax and spending legislation to pass the Senate with a simple majority, to begin the expansion. Even securing that level of support would be a challenge. House Democratic leader Hakeem Jeffries recently endorsed universal health coverage for children, expanded dental, vision and hearing benefits under Medicare and lowering the program’s eligibility age, though he did not specify how far he would lower it. Jeffries’ approach would not fundamentally replace the existing insurance system. The proposals marked a notable shift for Jeffries, who earlier this year said he did not support the Medicare for All proposal put forward by Sanders.

Sen.-elect Abdul El-Sayed made Medicare for All a centerpiece of his campaign but said he favors guaranteeing public health coverage to all Americans while allowing private insurance, including employer-sponsored plans, to coexist. In an interview with The Wall Street Journal, he compared his approach to public education, where Americans can use the public system or pay for private alternatives. “It’s not about what you take away,” El-Sayed said. “It’s about what you offer.” His approach would differ from Sanders’ proposal, which would bar private insurers from selling coverage that duplicates government benefits; how private plans would operate alongside El-Sayed’s universal guarantee remains unclear.

El-Sayed has sought Trump’s support by using social-media posts to pitch universal coverage as “TrumpCare for All” and sharing mock-ups of a gold TrumpCare card bearing the president’s name. El-Sayed has suggested the idea could give Trump an opportunity to claim credit for expanding coverage. After sharing one of El-Sayed’s posts over the weekend, Trump said Tuesday that he planned to meet with El-Sayed to discuss possible cooperation. “He’s a smart guy,” Trump told the New York Post. “Maybe there’s some place in the middle that we could go.” Days earlier, Trump said he was open to working with Democrats on a health care deal: “Now, can it be what they want? No, but it can be a combination of what we all want.”

Trump’s willingness to engage contrasts with Republicans on Capitol Hill, who have long argued that Medicare for All would require massive tax increases or additional federal borrowing. Republicans also say it could squeeze hospitals and other providers because the government reimburses them at lower rates, while pointing to longer wait times and restricted access to specialists in countries with government-run health systems. Republican Sen. Rick Scott of Florida said El-Sayed’s push for Medicare for All would “destroy the Medicare program.”

In his interview with The Journal, El-Sayed proposed a tax on individuals with more than $1 billion in wealth to help finance expanded coverage and argued that the costs should fall on the wealthiest Americans and corporations. Even a modest expansion of Medicare eligibility would carry significant costs. In 2022, the Congressional Budget Office projected that lowering the eligibility age to 60 from 65 would add $155 billion to federal deficits over six years, assuming the change took effect in 2026. The agency has not estimated the cost of Sanders’ more ambitious proposal. Sanders has previously proposed financing Medicare for All through income-based premiums from employers and households, higher taxes on high-income Americans and new levies on Wall Street and large corporations.

Democrats have not reached consensus on how far to expand government-backed coverage. Sen. Elissa Slotkin of Michigan told reporters that she supports adding dental, vision and hearing benefits to Medicare and was “willing to consider” changing the program’s eligibility age. Other moderates have questioned whether lowering the age would be worth the cost, and some have suggested that health care dollars could be better spent elsewhere. Some prominent Medicare for All supporters have also signaled openness to preserving a larger role for private insurers than Sanders’ single-payer plan would allow.

Health insurers and pharmaceutical companies have helped finance lobbying and advertising campaigns against Medicare for All through the Partnership for America’s Health Care Future, a coalition of industry groups. The U.S. Chamber of Commerce has separately sought to defend employer-sponsored insurance through Protecting Americans’ Coverage Together, a coalition it launched in 2020 with other business groups. The coalition has opposed Medicare for All, a public option and a Medicare buy-in, arguing that those proposals could undermine the private coverage millions of employers provide to workers.

Business groups have also warned that a public option competing alongside private plans could draw healthier customers away from commercial insurers. That shift would leave insurers with a sicker and more expensive pool of patients and could drive up premiums, the groups said. “Americans want more healthcare, not higher taxes, fewer choices, and government control over their coverage,” said Lexi Branson, vice president for health policy at the U.S. Chamber of Commerce.


The July congressional hearing centered on advancing single-payer legislation; since then, the Democratic proposals under consideration have ranged from a four-year phase-in to preserving a larger private-insurance role, leaving the cost of expansion unresolved.
