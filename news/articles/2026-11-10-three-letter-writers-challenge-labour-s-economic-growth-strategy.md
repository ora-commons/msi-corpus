## Public services, redistribution and resource limits define the alternatives

Peter Riddle, Sheila Triggs and Stephen Smith challenged the UK Labour government's economic growth strategy in letters published by The Guardian on November 10, 2026. Riddle began with the economic judgment facing UK voters, drawing a lesson from the U.S. midterm elections.

Riddle said voters would judge Andy Burnham's government just as harshly as U.S. voters had judged Donald Trump if they felt the economy was not working for them. He noted that Labour hoped to retain power in two years' time.

Riddle wrote: “Meeting the rising costs of food, utilities, transport and housing has become a daily challenge for millions of people.” He said those costs were compounded by a collapse in the quality and reliability of many vital public services and by a sense that community life had become more fractious, fragile and unsafe.

Riddle also said Burnham, on becoming prime minister, had discredited the previous 40 years of neoliberal economics because it had not delivered perceptible benefits for much of the population. He warned that the pursuit of economic growth could be sabotaged by unpredictable global forces and events beyond a nation's control, repeating the errors of the past.

His immediate backing was for Burnham's strategy of small fixes that would give people breathing space, which Riddle said had been generally welcomed. He urged Burnham to read the work of Karel Williams and Sukhdev Johal, whom a November 3 Guardian editorial had cited. Williams and Johal argue that the government's key task should be ensuring that health, education, housing, transport and welfare contribute to improving the collective wealth and wellbeing of individuals and local communities.

Riddle said that, for several decades, voters had felt successive governments dismissed their concerns and failed to address issues that had the greatest effect on daily life. He left open the question of whether Burnham's government had the courage to do things differently.

Sheila Triggs argued that economic policy had to go beyond redistribution because the planet's depleted resources could not support endless growth. She wrote that growth required seeking new frontiers to extract more coal, oil, gas, timber, minerals and food: “More than we need.” Triggs described that pattern as “intrinsically colonial” and said it maintained the global south as a source for the expansion of wealth in the west.

She also argued that wars and the world's military budget wasted vast resources. Triggs called for reducing global consumption of energy and material goods in a way that was equitable among countries: “The urgent need, essential to avert climate disaster, is to reduce the world’s consumption of energy and material goods in a way that is globally just.”

Stephen Smith agreed that redistribution had been forgotten as an instrument of economic change, but argued that environmental crises also required a radical change in culture and a turn away from never-ending increases in material consumption. He said there would be no meaningful attempt to address those crises while the priority remained expanding the human footprint at the expense of planetary health.

Smith said growth was already slowing in many western countries, but the inequalities of the current world also needed to be recognized. He concluded: “Radical redistribution is the only social alternative that can offer hope.”

The three letters set out distinct priorities for economic policy: Riddle focused on daily cost relief and essential public services, Triggs questioned the resource demands of growth, and Smith linked environmental limits with redistribution and inequality. The proposals remain arguments in reader correspondence rather than announced changes to Labour policy.
