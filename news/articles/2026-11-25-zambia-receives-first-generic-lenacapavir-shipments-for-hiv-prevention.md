## Uganda deliveries expected in coming days

The first shipments of a generic version of lenacapavir, a twice-a-year HIV-prevention injection, arrived in Zambia on Wednesday. Additional deliveries are expected in Uganda in the coming days.

Lenacapavir is expected to cost $40 per person each year and will eventually be available in 120 countries. Its injectable form of pre-exposure prophylaxis, or PrEP, is nearly 100% effective at preventing an HIV infection and is much more convenient than the daily preventive pills available now.

The doses are part of a shipment by the Global Fund, a worldwide partnership to defeat AIDS, tuberculosis and malaria. After going through a clearance process in Zambia, the medications are expected to be used within the next three weeks.

Tenu Avafia, deputy executive director of Unitaid, a global health agency, said, “With demand for the injection far outstripping supply, the arrival of generics marks a major step towards making this breakthrough prevention option more widely available and affordable in low- and middle-income countries.”

The U.S. Food and Drug Administration approved the generic earlier this month. The approval of the generic medication is considered “tentative,” the agency’s designation for generics while the original drug remains under patent. The generic cannot be sold in the United States because Gilead Sciences’ brand-name version, Yeztugo, is still protected by a patent.

Gilead established royalty-free licensing agreements with six generic manufacturers to enable greater production of lenacapavir. The voluntary licenses cover a specific set of countries in sub-Saharan Africa, Asia and Latin America. Gilead officials said the company signed the licenses eight months before regulatory approval to ensure an early generic supply and collaborated closely with generic manufacturers and global health partners.

The generic was developed by India-based Dr. Reddy’s Laboratories under an agreement brokered by Unitaid and its partners, which sought to accelerate development and make lenacapavir available at an affordable price.

Gilead chairman and CEO Daniel O’Day said in a press release, “Access is a priority because we know that breakthroughs like lenacapavir only matter when they reach the people who need them most.”

According to the World Health Organization, 41 million people worldwide were living with HIV and 570,000 died from HIV-related causes last year.

Peter Sands, executive director of the Global Fund, said in a press release, “We have an unprecedented opportunity to reach millions more people at risk of HIV.” He added, “This is how we bring the goal of ending AIDS within reach.”
