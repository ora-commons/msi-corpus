## The bill would allow federal lawsuits and damages up to $150,000

Media personality and activist Paris Hilton returned to Capitol Hill in Washington, D.C., this week to urge Congress to pass the bipartisan DEFIANCE Act. Her return followed successful advocacy for 2024 legislation to protect children in youth residential programs from abuse. The current fight centers on the growing use of AI to create sexually explicit images of people without consent, raising concerns about privacy, exploitation and laws meant to protect victims.

The DEFIANCE Act would allow victims to sue the creators of nonconsensual deepfake sexual images in federal court and seek damages of up to $150,000. Its focus includes cases in which someone takes an image from the internet and uses AI to turn it into an explicit video or photo. Hilton said the resulting material can look realistic.

The bipartisan bill has 70 co-sponsors, according to Hilton, and passed the Senate in the winter. With a few weeks left in the congressional calendar, Democratic Rep. Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez and Republican Rep. Laurel Lee, the bill’s two sponsors, are trying to secure House approval before Congress leaves.

Ocasio-Cortez said making the issue real to people was important and said Hilton’s involvement showed the unity the issue could inspire. She also said the abuse affected young girls across the country and people as well-known as Hilton. Hilton said she had just met with House Speaker Mike Johnson and described the meeting as going well. She said, “This is such an important bill.”

In her PBS News interview, Hilton put the issue in stark terms: “Our likeness is not your content. Our trauma is not your entertainment, and our consent is never optional.” She said the issue “is not about politics” but about women and children being exploited and laws not catching up with technology. Hilton said the bill’s financial compensation could deter the creation of such images and let victims seek justice and bring the perpetrators to court. She also said victims currently lack legal protection and the people creating the images face no consequences.

Hilton said she was hearing positive responses from lawmakers and hoped Congress would do the right thing. She warned that if the bill did not pass, the effort would have to start over next year.

Hilton said the issue was affecting over 50 million women and children around the world. Her advocacy has personal roots: in the early 2000s, when she was 19, an ex-boyfriend released a sex tape of her without her consent. She sued and won a $400,000 settlement. Hilton said, “What happened to me would be illegal today,” and said speaking about the experience made her emotional.

She said AI-generated abuse differs from her experience because a creator does not need to know the victim or have met that person. According to Hilton, someone can take an innocent yearbook or family photograph and turn it into explicit videos and photos that look realistic. She said she was fortunate to have a team, lawyers and resources behind her, but she could not imagine the same experience for a 13-year-old girl whose images circulated throughout her school. Hilton also said she had spoken to many parents whose children had taken their own lives because of the humiliation and shame.

Hilton said she had used her voice to change two federal bills and 20 state laws to protect children. She said she wanted to be the hero she had needed when she was younger and described the work as the most meaningful work of her life and her “true purpose.” As the mother of a 3-year-old daughter, London, and a son, she said she wanted to build a world where children’s images would not be exploited and every mother would not have to worry about such abuse. Hilton said it could “literally happen to anyone.”

Hilton described AI as scary when it reaches the wrong hands while acknowledging that technology and innovation can also provide positive benefits. She said it was terrifying when other people use it to exploit someone’s life and likeness and said laws have not caught up with technology.

Asked by PBS News reporter Liz Landers about a possible future in elected office, Hilton replied, “Only if I could paint the White House pink.” Asked about a possible presidential run, she said, “We will see.” Hilton said she was proud of her advocacy and would continue going to the Capitol to work for safer protections for women and children around the world.
