## Mississippi's first two Black senators were chosen by state lawmakers

**Subtype:** fact

- Hiram Rhodes Revels became the first African American to serve in either chamber of Congress when he took his Senate seat on Feb. 25, 1870.
- Blanche Kelso Bruce, seated in 1875, served in the Senate until 1881 and was the only formerly enslaved person to serve in the U.S. Senate, according to Senate and National Park Service websites.
- Seventeen Black men served in the U.S. Congress during Reconstruction — 15 in the House of Representatives, along with Revels and Bruce in the Senate — and the Senate had no other Black member until 1967.
- Scott Colom, declared the winner Wednesday over Republican Sen. Cindy Hyde-Smith, becomes Mississippi's third Black senator and the first chosen by the state's voters.

## Blanche Kelso Bruce was the only formerly enslaved U.S. senator

Hiram Rhodes Revels took his Senate seat on Feb. 25, 1870, two days after Mississippi was readmitted to the Union, and served just over a year, until March 3, 1871, when his term ended. Mississippi's U.S. Senate seats had been vacant since the state seceded from the Union in January 1861, and Revels was selected by his fellow state legislators to fill one of them; a white man was chosen for the other.

Born to free parents in North Carolina in September 1827, Revels traveled throughout the country as an adult doing religious work and educating other Black people, according to biographies on National Park Service and U.S. Senate websites. After the Civil War, he settled in Natchez, Mississippi, where he served as presiding elder of the African Methodist Episcopal Church. Revels was elected Natchez alderman in 1868 and was one of 35 Black men elected to the state legislature the next year.

Revels later recalled the legislative bargain that sent him to Washington. "An opportunity of electing a Republican to the United States Senate, to fill an unexpired term occurred, and the colored members, after consulting together on the subject, agreed to give their influence and votes for one of their own race for that position, as it would in their judgement be a weakening blow against color line prejudice," Revels said, according to a U.S. Senate website.

The Mississippi legislature chose Blanche Kelso Bruce to be the first African American to serve a full term in the U.S. Senate. Seated in 1875, Bruce became the first Black man to preside over the Senate in 1879 and served until 1881. Born into slavery in Virginia in 1841 — his father was a white man who owned his mother — Bruce was the only formerly enslaved person to serve in the U.S. Senate, according to the Senate and National Park Service websites.

His father moved his plantation operation to Mississippi, where Bruce began picking cotton at age 8. Bruce escaped to Kansas early in the Civil War, established a school for Black children in Missouri in 1864 and attended Oberlin College in Ohio for two years before a lack of funds caused him to withdraw. He returned to Mississippi, where he became a wealthy businessman and landowner and held several positions in local government before state lawmakers elected him to the Senate.

Unlike Colom, a Democrat, Mississippi's previous Black senators were Republicans. Reconstruction, the 12-year period after the Civil War from 1865 to 1877, was when the 13th, 14th and 15th Amendments were adopted — abolishing slavery, establishing birthright citizenship and prohibiting denial of the right to vote "on account of race, color, or previous condition of servitude." The Republican Party was the party of President Abraham Lincoln during the Civil War, and he issued the Emancipation Proclamation on Jan. 1, 1863, declaring that all enslaved people in rebellious states "are, and henceforward shall be free."

A total of 17 Black men served in the U.S. Congress during Reconstruction, 15 in the House of Representatives and Revels and Bruce in the Senate. The Senate would not have another Black member until 1967, when Sen. Edward Brooke of Massachusetts took office as the first Black person to be popularly elected to that body.

Colom, a Democrat, was declared the winner Wednesday over Republican Sen. Cindy Hyde-Smith. When he takes the oath of office in January he will not be the first Black senator from Mississippi to make history, but he will be the first in nearly 150 years and the first chosen by voters rather than state lawmakers — Mississippi's third Black senator, and the first the state's voters have sent to the seat.
