## Hundreds follow the hearse carrying Huerta’s casket

California officials held an invitation-only memorial ceremony for Dolores Huerta in the rotunda of the California State Capitol in Sacramento on Monday, with the labor leader’s casket in state. The officials remembered Huerta as one of the nation’s most influential labor leaders, civil rights icons and feminist activists and as a relentless advocate for a more just and fair world. After the ceremony, the rotunda opened to the public.

Before the ceremony, a hearse adorned with flags reading “Sí, se puede!” carried Huerta’s casket to the capitol. Hundreds of people, including union leaders, politicians and members of the public, followed behind; many held marigolds. A Guardian photograph showed Maria Helena Chavez, daughter of Dolores Huerta and Richard Chavez, comforted by her husband Rafael Rodriguez.

Robert Rivas, the Democratic Assembly speaker, stood beside governor-elect Xavier Becerra, Senate President pro tempore Monique Limón and former U.S. House Speaker Nancy Pelosi as they spoke about Huerta. Rivas had called Huerta a family friend and mentor. His grandfather, Servando Flores, moved from Mexico to California in the 1960s and went on to organize fellow farmworkers alongside Huerta.

Rivas said, “She is the mother of our Latino civil rights movement.” He added, “Her place in the history of our state and nation is enduring.”

Performances included *We Shall Overcome* and *Put a Little Love in Your Heart* before the space opened to the public. Monique Limón, who was serving as acting governor while Gavin Newsom was out of state, said, “As we honor one of the towering figures of our time, we make her this solemn promise: the march goes on.”

Huerta was born in New Mexico in 1930 and raised in California’s farm-rich San Joaquin Valley. She became an elementary school teacher before turning her focus to the plight of farmworkers.

In 1962, Huerta and César Chávez created the National Farm Workers Association, which became the United Farm Workers. Huerta made famous the rallying cry “Sí, se puede!”; it translates to “Yes, it can be done!”

Barack Obama borrowed the rallying cry for his campaign slogan, “Yes we can,” and awarded Huerta the Presidential Medal of Freedom, the nation’s highest civilian honor, in 2012.

Well into her 90s, Huerta remained a familiar face at news conferences and rallies in Sacramento, where she advocated for the rights of workers, women, voters and others.

In a disclosure this year, Huerta said Chávez sexually abused her and that the abuse led to the birth of two children. She kept the secret for 60 years.

Huerta died of cancer at her home in Bakersfield last month at age 96. A celebration of life with an interfaith memorial service was scheduled for Friday in her hometown.
