## Lutnick's clashes with other officials complicate his defense

Less than 12 hours after Jensen Huang was ousted as Nvidia's CEO, congressional Democrats turned their scrutiny to a senior member of the Trump administration: Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick. According to the Journal's November 13 politics newsletter, written by Washington coverage chief Damian Paletta, top Democrats on two key congressional panels sent Lutnick a letter saying they had started an investigation into the Trump administration's export plans as they relate to Nvidia.

The newsletter framed the inquiry in sweeping terms, calling it "a multibillion-dollar mess that involves national security, trade policy, and—according to Democrats—an opening for wrongdoing and corruption." The Democrats' allegation of an opening for wrongdoing is the lawmakers' own characterization; the newsletter reported the investigation's existence and its subject, not a finding of misconduct.

The Nvidia inquiry did not arrive in a vacuum. Just a couple of days earlier, lawmakers had been focused on Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth, who gave a full-throated defense of himself at a press conference — a defense the newsletter said elated President Trump — and who survived an impeachment attempt. The Journal asked whether Lutnick could similarly circle wagons — while noting that Democrats are not angling to impeach Lutnick necessarily.

Lutnick and President Trump have known each other for years, and Lutnick has served as a top fundraiser for Trump. But the newsletter reported that Lutnick's "brash bravado" has unsettled a number of other top officials, citing his well-known tangles with Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent as an example. The White House, the Journal wrote, cannot afford the ugly Nvidia news cycle to drag on much longer.

That internal standing matters because the questions raised by the scrutiny reach the Commerce Department itself. The Journal framed the risk directly: Lutnick could face pressure if the company's newly scrutinized ties to the U.S. government open up uncomfortable questions about the Commerce Department's role — and, in the newsletter's framing, he could follow Huang out of his job. Whether people rush in to save him, the Journal asked, remains the open question.
