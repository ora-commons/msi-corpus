## At September’s end, AquaBounty had three employees and $122,255 in cash

JobsOhio has asked AquaBounty Technologies to repay a $1 million grant by Sunday for a new facility in Pioneer, Ohio, where the company planned to farm genetically engineered Atlantic salmon, according to a filing the company made with the Securities and Exchange Commission. The company said it was still discussing repayment with JobsOhio.

At the end of September, AquaBounty’s SEC filing reported three employees and $122,255 in cash. Construction of the Pioneer plant was roughly 30% complete, and the site stood at the end of Kexon Drive, a road the village had extended for the project. By the end of September, the company’s primary remaining assets were unsold equipment, the Pioneer land and the incomplete build.

ProPublica reported last year that AquaBounty received state and local support, including the $1 million JobsOhio grant. The company received the money in August 2021, according to financial filings, shortly after the project went public. Less than two years later, construction stalled. AquaBounty paused construction and auctioned equipment described as “new” and “unused”; it also sold equipment intended for the Ohio farm in 2024 and 2025 and sold its intellectual property for genetically engineered salmon.

Announced in 2021, the development was to be AquaBounty’s first large-scale production facility. The project came with promises of 112 new jobs, plus more resources for schools and infrastructure, and it was believed to represent the largest investment ever in rural Williams County. AquaBounty said its salmon could thrive in indoor freshwater farms and help feed the world.

The AquaBounty grant depended on the creation of jobs, $222 million in capital investment and payroll of more than $5.4 million by the end of 2026. It had to maintain those commitments through the end of 2028. JobsOhio’s website describes its funding model for specialized grants as “independent” and accommodating of “unique needs.” Its listed qualifications include, but are not limited to, “job creation, additional payroll, fixed-asset investment commitment, project return on investment, and project location.”

JobsOhio operates privately but has a funding mechanism set up by the state and a board appointed by the governor. ProPublica described JobsOhio’s limited disclosures as having long been “a sore point for Ohioans.” JobsOhio spokesperson Matt Englehart said in an email to ProPublica that the organization “operates with transparency and accountability.”

The project faced regulatory challenges and opposition from environmentalists. AquaBounty had never made a profit over its 30 years in business, and its production plan was unproven. Sherry Fleming of Williams County Alliance, a grassroots environmental group, said, “Why the state was so eager to promote this industry is really puzzling.” She said AquaBounty had genetic-engineering know-how but seemed to have limited production experience.

Local concerns also included a land transaction involving Pioneer Mayor Ed Kidston. Kidston bought land AquaBounty wanted to build on and later sold it to the company for $1.5 million more than he had paid, and locals were suspicious of the arrangement. Kidston did not respond to requests for comment. At the time, he said his actions involving the plant were intended only to help the community. The village administrator referred ProPublica to AquaBounty and its website.

JobsOhio’s September demand notice said the equipment sales and AquaBounty’s consideration of acquisition offers for the Ohio farm breached the grant agreement. AquaBounty “provided additional information” to JobsOhio in October, the filing said, but the agency upheld its demand and requested repayment in November. If the grant money did not come back, the filing said, JobsOhio may refer the matter for collections and “pursue the remedies available” under the grant agreement.

AquaBounty’s press contact said the company shares what it can in SEC releases and filings and remains in discussion with JobsOhio over the repayment demand. The filing said “there can be no assurance that JobsOhio will agree to any payment schedule or other accommodation.” It added, “Any collection or legal action by JobsOhio could result in additional costs and management distraction.”

Englehart said he could not discuss a situation that might result in litigation. Speaking generally, he said JobsOhio works with businesses that miss metric commitments. “When metric commitments are not met, we work closely with those businesses,” he said, adding that the organization helps them overcome challenges. If all options are exhausted, he added, JobsOhio “will claw back some or all of our dollars so they can be used for future economic development projects to benefit Ohioans.”

Kristan Wong Karinen, a research analyst with the nonprofit Good Jobs First, which tracks public subsidies in economic development, said JobsOhio’s attempt to claw back money was “pretty rare” because of the onerous process and uncertain outcomes. More commonly, she said, JobsOhio and other agencies renegotiate contracts, perhaps by extending the time for a company to meet commitments or lowering requirements. “In this case, with AquaBounty,” Wong Karinen said, “because they haven’t done anything, they haven’t fulfilled any of the requirements, I feel like JobsOhio is being forced into this.”

Fleming said JobsOhio and other economic development boosters should “take a good look at this because they’re putting taxpayer dollars at risk when they advertise to bring these industries in, and they haven’t done their homework to protect the community.” AquaBounty’s filing left repayment unsettled and said JobsOhio could refer the matter for collections and pursue available remedies if the company did not pay.
