## Kpler tracked Persian Gulf exports recovering to about 73% of prewar output

Oil traders and analysts expect oil prices to remain around $85 to $95 a barrel for the foreseeable future. Their view reflects a consensus that the Strait of Hormuz might not return to what it was before the war, and analysts cite persistent attacks on oil tankers among the factors keeping prices from dropping further.

Brent oil futures approached their highest level since the start of the war in the run-up to the U.S. midterm elections. Prices have since come down but remain well above $73 a barrel, the level recorded the day before the war. Elevated crude prices were keeping prices at the pump elevated. AAA's Friday reading put the U.S. average for regular gasoline at $3.69 a gallon, down from a May high of $4.56 but a long way from the $2.98 average before the war.

Recovering supply limits how far prices can rise. Persian Gulf oil exports, including shipments through the Strait of Hormuz and alternative routes across the region, recovered to around 17.1 million barrels a day in the week through Nov. 18, about 73% of prewar levels of 23.3 million barrels a day, according to ship tracker Kpler. The output has at times briefly touched or slightly exceeded prewar levels.

As shipments rise, countries including Iraq and Saudi Arabia are pumping out crude. That has tipped the global oil market into a small surplus in the last quarter of this year and a bigger surplus next year, according to the International Energy Agency, a Paris-based energy watchdog. The additional supply is being offset in part by stronger demand from China: Chinese refiners have returned to the global market to buy crude in recent weeks, but analysts cautioned that China is sensitive to price and current levels are too high for the country to start aggressively stockpiling again.

Analysts say persistent attacks on oil tankers are one of the most important factors keeping oil prices from dropping further. The International Maritime Organization counted 35 ships hit in October while transiting the Strait of Hormuz, and tallies by maritime security firms point to a higher number. According to the International Maritime Organization, the attacks threatened to cut off Middle Eastern oil from reaching global buyers. About 20 attacks have occurred so far this month, according to the Royal Navy-affiliated U.K. Maritime Trade Operations and maritime security firms.

Shipping crude on a very large carrier from the Middle East Gulf to Asia now costs $31.30 a barrel, according to Argus Media, a price reporting agency. The rate is less than in recent weeks but still about five times prewar levels. More ships tied up on shuttle runs through the Strait of Hormuz have pushed up freight costs on other popular routes as well. Analysts generally agree that at least some of the high cost of delivering oil is passed on to the ultimate buyers.

Global observed oil inventories — including those held in on-land tanks, on-water vessels and governments' strategic petroleum reserves — rebounded slightly in October. Even after that rebound, the International Energy Agency counted a cumulative 462 million barrels of inventory drawn down since the start of the war, roughly four days of the world's oil consumption. Refilling those stockpiles will keep a floor under prices.

The high volume of oil moving through the Strait of Hormuz is a key reason prices have begun to stabilize, said Hamad Hussain, senior economist at Capital Economics. “However, this equilibrium is extremely fragile,” he said, adding that attacks on oil tankers and energy infrastructure could trigger sharp drops in oil flows and flip the market from a surplus into a deficit.

Naveen Das, senior crude analyst at ship-tracking data provider Kpler, said: “We’ve unwound the first layer of geopolitical risk premium that contains inflammatory rhetoric and the threat of attacks, and are now in this phase of talk of mediation with a dim prospect of a deal.” With no breakthrough in diplomatic talks but no military escalation either, Das said, oil prices are in a holding pattern.

Das also said: “The predicted surplus is contingent on volumes leaving the Middle East without a U.S. naval presence in the region.” Traders and analysts describe competing forces that keep oil from rising or falling too much, which is why they expect a range of $85 to $95 a barrel for the foreseeable future.
