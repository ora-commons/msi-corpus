## Most adults do not need a measles booster, experts say

**Subtype:** fact

- Medical experts generally say adults do not need another measles booster.
- Dr. Richard Martinello said most people are protected without a booster because of vaccination or childhood measles.
- Doctors said one MMR dose is 93% effective at preventing measles, while two doses are 97% protective.
- Dr. Joshua Sharfstein said adults not born before 1957 who received no MMR doses should get vaccinated.
- Doctors said healthcare workers born before 1957 without proof of immunity should consider a booster because of their exposure risk.
- Doctors said adults vaccinated from 1963 to 1967 should get at least one live MMR dose if the inactivated vaccine was used or if the vaccination history is uncertain.

## Dose history, age, occupation and exposure shape extra-dose guidance

The Associated Press reported that the United States, Mexico and Bolivia lost their measles-free status this week following what it described as stubborn outbreaks that erased decades of progress. Canada lost its measles-elimination designation last year. Some Americans asked whether adults needed another measles booster. Medical experts generally say the answer is no.

Dr. Richard Martinello at Yale School of Medicine said, "Most people are protected without getting a booster shot" because they have either been vaccinated or had measles as children. Doctors said two doses of the measles, mumps and rubella, or MMR, vaccine are the best way to protect against measles, one of the most contagious viruses in the world. Vaccination usually protects people for life, as does having had the disease.

## No documented doses

Americans born before 1957 are considered to have "presumptive evidence" of immunity because almost everyone in that era got measles during childhood. For adults not born before 1957 who have received no MMR doses, Dr. Joshua Sharfstein at Johns Hopkins Bloomberg School of Public Health said, "People who have had no shots should go get vaccinated, if they weren't born before 1957."

## One documented dose

Until 1989, a single MMR dose was standard. The current recommendation is for adults to have at least one documented dose, with two doses recommended for certain high-risk groups that lack other evidence of immunity. Doctors said one MMR dose is 93% effective at preventing measles, while two doses are 97% protective.

Sharfstein said the high-risk group includes people in school, healthcare workers, international travelers and people living with family members who have compromised immune systems. In places with active measles outbreaks, health officials sometimes recommend that everyone have two doses.

Sharfstein also said, "If they have had only one shot, they may want to consider whether they're in a risk group for a second shot."

For an adult who has received only one dose, especially in an area where measles is circulating, Martinello said, "it's always a good idea to talk with your physician about whether another shot is needed."

## Vaccination from 1963 to 1967

Adults vaccinated between 1963 and 1967 also might need to consider a booster. Some children in those years received an inactivated vaccine made from a killed version of the virus, which was less effective than the current vaccine containing live, weakened virus.

Doctors said people who know they received the inactivated vaccine, or are unsure whether they did, should get at least one dose of the live MMR vaccine.

## Age, occupation and exposure

One group doctors said should consider a booster is healthcare workers born before 1957 without proof of immunity. These workers are more likely than the general public to come into contact with people who have measles, so they may catch the virus or spread it to vulnerable patients.

## Locating vaccination records

The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention suggests asking parents or a doctor, checking with high school or college health services, or contacting previous employers that required immunizations. Adults can also contact their state health department because some states maintain registries that include adult vaccines.

If the record remains unclear, a doctor can discuss an MMR immunity blood test that can show whether a person has antibodies against measles. Martinello said the test is accurate but is not an ideal measure of how safe someone is from the virus because scientists do not know how to link the result perfectly with the level of protection a person might have.

## Eligibility for an extra dose

Doctors said an extra MMR dose taken simply for reassurance generally will not harm someone eligible to receive the vaccine. The shot is not appropriate for people in groups that should not receive it, including pregnant women.

Now that measles is circulating in the United States, doctors said it is crucial to take measures to protect yourself. "This is a moment to not just check for yourself, but also encourage others to make sure that they are adequately vaccinated," Sharfstein said. He also said, "Too many people took measles for granted … or just didn't think about measles. But now it's something to think about."
