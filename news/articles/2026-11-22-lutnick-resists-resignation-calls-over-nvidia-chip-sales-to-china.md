## Senate panel seeks testimony from Lutnick and Huang next month

Howard Lutnick, the beleaguered commerce secretary and a close ally of Donald Trump, is emerging as Democrats' prime target when the party takes control of Congress in January, and he is resisting calls to resign amid a bipartisan outcry over his role in the scandal at artificial intelligence company Nvidia and the sale of microchips to China, the United States' principal geopolitical rival in the race for AI supremacy.

The 65-year-old billionaire received a vote of confidence from Trump on Friday, but faces growing criticism from some Republicans and a chorus of Democrats vowing to investigate his actions and demanding that he testify on Capitol Hill. Among their questions is whether Lutnick compromised national security to make money for his family.

Lutnick's friendship with Trump was forged decades ago in New York's business world and deepened after Trump supported Lutnick as he rebuilt Cantor Fitzgerald following the 11 September 2001 attacks. Lutnick later became one of Trump's most prominent corporate backers, helping bankroll his political comeback and co-chairing his 2024 transition team.

His position is under threat after Nvidia co-founder and chief executive Jensen Huang was forced to resign over an extramarital affair with Alice Huang, a former executive at Singapore-based Megaspeed International who is suspected by US intelligence agencies of being a Chinese spy. Megaspeed allegedly served as a conduit for the diversion of $4bn in advanced Nvidia AI semiconductors to Chinese technology firms, in breach of US export controls.

A public dispute has erupted between Lutnick and the treasury secretary, Scott Bessent, who revealed that the treasury, defense and state departments, as well as the US trade representative, had wanted to put Megaspeed on a trade blacklist months earlier, only for Lutnick's commerce department to stall enforcement. Bessent said, "Commerce has the pen, and you will have to ask them about their internal processes," adding that his deputy had pushed the issue for months.

That leaves Lutnick vulnerable to the charge that he either personally intervened or instructed subordinates to disregard intelligence warnings and allow the chip sales to continue. He is facing a rare backlash from both sides of the aisle.

Republican Tom Cotton, who chairs the Senate intelligence committee, told the Wall Street Journal that Lutnick repeatedly ignored warnings that AI chips were being smuggled to China before the Nvidia scandal became public. Lutnick should have taken "the threats of communist China more seriously," Cotton said.

John Kennedy of Louisiana, another Republican and a member of the Senate banking committee, urged Lutnick to hold a press conference to explain what he knew. He said: "The commerce department greenlit $4bn of sales of advanced chips to the company of the spy that Mr Huang was having an affair with and I want to know why."

The Republican senator Tim Scott, of South Carolina, has written to Lutnick and Huang requesting their testimony in front of the Senate banking committee next month. Scott wrote on social media: "Recent reports raise serious concerns about our national security and the protection of sensitive US technology."

Democrats are queueing up with further investigations once they take power in January. The Oregon senator Ron Wyden and the Massachusetts congressman Richard Neal, the top Democrats on the Senate finance and House ways and means committees, respectively, have written to Lutnick demanding that he preserve all records related to decisions about Nvidia chip exports, pending an investigation. Trump's approval of advanced Nvidia chip exports to China "raises critical questions about whether and to what extent political considerations are improperly influencing policy decisions made by the administration," Wyden and Neal wrote.

Meanwhile, the representatives Jamie Raskin of Maryland, Gregory Meeks of New York and Sydney Kamlager-Dove of California have launched an inquiry into whether Lutnick used his office to help Cantor Fitzgerald, now controlled by his sons, avoid losses connected to scrutiny of Nvidia. The members are seeking to establish whether Lutnick blocked efforts to stop the suspected smuggling of advanced Nvidia chips to China while Cantor Fitzgerald held hundreds of millions of dollars in Nvidia shares. The firm says those holdings largely reflected positions used to hedge client trades and denies wrongdoing.

Kurt Bardella, an adviser to Democrats in Texas and former congressional aide, said the commerce secretary had escaped sustained scrutiny until now. "To this point he has been overshadowed by some of the more controversial characters in the Trump administration," Bardella said. "It wasn't until a scandal that literally has everything from spies to China to sex to a billionaire CEO that the commerce secretary got on the radar of the entire political and media apparatus."

Bardella, a former spokesperson for the House oversight committee, said Democratic lawmakers "are going to want to air this out in public" once they take power. "They're going to want to know who at commerce decided not to blacklist Megaspeed in spite of objections from four other agencies," he said. "Did Lutnick make the call himself? What did his deputy authorise when having conversations with Nvidia's top lawyer? In my experience, the more information comes out, the less great it gets for the target of the oversight. Lutnick has a rough three to six months ahead."

Some are already demanding his departure. Richard Blumenthal, a Democratic senator for Connecticut, tweeted this week: "Lutnick must go. His malign self-enriching malfeasance shows the costs of corruption – putting personal profit above public interest."

Steve Schmidt, a former Republican campaign strategist, said in a phone interview that Lutnick should be dismissed. "He should be fired. He's utterly disloyal to the country without question," Schmidt said. "There are facts that have been asserted in newspapers about the conflicts of interest and his profiteering on the sale of these chips, and the sale of these chips has undermined American security."

Lutnick accompanied Trump on his trip to Asia last week. When the president returned to the White House on Friday, he assured reporters that he had not lost confidence in the commerce secretary. "He's a different kind of a guy. A very good businessman … He's a natural businessman … I think he's doing a very good job," Trump said. Trump has previously offered officials public assurances of support shortly before showing them the door.

Lutnick has survived political turbulence before. Earlier this year he weathered calls to resign after acknowledging that he had visited Jeffrey Epstein's private Caribbean island with his family in 2012, despite previously saying he had cut off contact with the convicted sex offender in 2005.

Reed Galen, founder of Civic Forum and a former Republican strategist, said Lutnick's refusal to step aside fits the administration's pattern. "It would be great if Lutnick resigned but, given that no one in any position in this administration has any sense of morality or shame, why would he think he's done anything wrong? If a fish rots from the head then Lutnick is only doing what he has been shown is completely acceptable behaviour," Galen said.

Although cabinet members such as the defense secretary, Pete Hegseth, and the health secretary, Robert F Kennedy Jr, have faced calls for accountability, Lutnick is now in the sights of a newly emboldened Congress eager to bring the Trump administration to book. The buildup of pressure suggests that he could become the first major test of those efforts. The commerce department did not respond to a request for comment.
