## Linxs projects March 2027 as airport officials say no date is confirmed

SkyLink's elevated track is in place and its automated train cars are rolling between stations, but the people mover has carried no passengers. Los Angeles World Airports, known as Lawa, says final testing must proceed for at least 30 consecutive uninterrupted days. That phase has stretched beyond 200 days.

LAX Integrated Express Solutions, known as Linxs, moved the people mover's projected opening back again last week, to March 2027. The estimated opening has been pushed back multiple times and is now slated for 2027. Lawa said in a statement: “No opening date can be confirmed until Linxs … completes their final testing.” It added: “Any dates released by other parties are not confirmed dates but simply estimates.”

The project was intended to ease congestion around one of the world's busiest airports, whose outdated horseshoe street design gives travelers only one route in and out. SkyLink is less than 2.5 miles (4km) long and would connect terminals with a ride-share zone, the LAX/Metro Transit Center and a car-rental hub. Similar people movers operate at dozens of airports and cities in the United States and elsewhere.

Eli Lipmen, executive director of Move LA, a local nonprofit coalition that organizes for improved transit and housing, said, “You have to really love somebody to drive them to LAX.” Lipmen said: “The promise of this project was to dramatically reduce [congestion] and provide a real alternative to driving to LAX – not just for visitors, but for Angelenos themselves.” He added: “We really want to change how people see LAX and the value of this world-class airport.”

SkyLink broke ground in 2019 under then-Mayor Eric Garcetti as part of a $30 billion plan to modernize LAX. In the nearly eight years since, Los Angeles has seen a global pandemic, #FreeBritney, two devastating fires, three Dodgers World Series titles, immigration raids under Trump 2.0 and the protests that followed, and two mayors with a new one on the way. The project is more than $1 billion over budget and is expected to overshoot its intended 2023 opening date by four years, and counting.

The working relationship between the city and contractor rapidly frayed. Disputes arose over design codes, and Linxs submitted hundreds of change orders — alterations to the contract's initial scope of work and costs. Those change orders culminated in a settlement with the city that granted Linxs an additional $550 million beyond the initial $1.9 billion price tag. Costs eventually ballooned to nearly $3 billion as the schedule was pushed back year after year.

Officials report that the project is 99% complete. Public scrutiny increased after video circulated on local media showing an electrical explosion on the track; city officials have not explained the cause. In September, Fitch Ratings downgraded the project's debt to B-, just short of deeming it a substantial credit risk. City officials and Linxs have released little detail on the record about the technical issues and delays.

Lawa, a governing body created by Los Angeles to manage LAX, signed a 30-year contract with Linxs to design, construct, operate and maintain SkyLink. Linxs is a consortium of engineering and construction firms assembled specifically for the project, although its website no longer works. The public-private partnership was pitched as a cost-effective model that could set the bar for future transit projects. Lawa's leaders said at the groundbreaking that they were “committed to creating a world-class airport for Los Angeles, delivered in an innovative, efficient and fiscally responsible manner.”

A Los Angeles County civil grand jury investigated the delays and hundreds of millions in additional payments to Linxs. In California, civil grand juries can act as government watchdogs, separate from criminal indictment grand juries. The report, released last year, used an amateur Hollywood screenplay format, with movie references such as “if you build it, he will come” to liven what it called “a very dry and boring” saga.

According to the grand jury, Linxs essentially took LA's airport authority for an $880 million ride because city officials did a bad job writing up its contract. The contractor “leveraged the intricacies” in the agreement, the grand jury wrote, by barraging city departments with change orders, then delaying work because city staff could not respond to its deluge in the contractually obliged timeframe.

The report stated, “The slowdown tactic of Linxs is not nefarious.” It called the approach “It’s good business from the contractor’s point of view to force Lawa to agree to claims, which from Lawa’s perspective are not justified.” The grand jury also believed that Linxs leveraged political pressure on city leaders to open the project in time for major sporting events “by implicitly holding out the threat of prolonged litigation.”

In July, Linxs sued the city, claiming several breaches of contract that hampered the project. The contractor said faulty electrical equipment delayed key testing by more than 140 days. Linxs also said it was waiting for Lawa to finalize an agreement with Los Angeles' department of water and power to provide a solar energy system to power the trains, resulting in more delays.

Lawa rejected that account. In a statement, airport officials called the solar issue one “of many false legal assertions that Linxs has made to distract from their failure to meet their contractual obligations.” They added: “Litigation and disputes are not holding up the progress on the train.” The statement continued: “The primary factor in the delay is Linxs’ inability to complete required system demonstration testing to ensure the reliability and safety of the train.”

Move LA's Lipmen blamed “a lack of good governance and poor oversight” by Mayor Karen Bass and the airport commissioners she appointed. He described the contractor as “this Frankenstein of a conglomerate that was formed to deliver the project and can’t deliver the project.” Lipmen called SkyLink “a boondoggle” and said: “Now this project is an embarrassment.” He added: “I am very concerned at this moment that we will have a $3bn distressed asset on our hands.”

Juan Matute, deputy director of the Institute of Transportation Studies at UCLA, plans to study the project to understand how the public-private partnership between the city and Linxs derailed. “It seems pretty obvious to anybody who cares about public sector finance that mistakes were made,” Matute said. He added: “It’s important to have outside groups overseeing what Metro and Los Angeles World Airports are doing and having some financial literacy to pressure elected and appointed officials not to make these types of mistakes in the future.”

SkyLink also falls within a broader effort to expand transit in Los Angeles County, which has long trailed other global cities in public transportation. The region is extending existing lines and building new ones with help from voter-approved tax measures intended to make train and bus networks more practical in the most populous metropolitan region in the United States, where having a car is often both the most practical and the most aggravating way to get around.

The lack of passenger service has been visible during major events. Los Angeles missed an opportunity to showcase the project during this summer's World Cup, and questions followed about whether SkyLink would be ready for the Super Bowl at nearby SoFi Stadium in February. This season's holiday travelers arriving at or leaving LAX can see the automated cars moving near the terminals but cannot board them. The approach of the 2028 Olympics is adding pressure.

The separate, 11-acre LAX/Metro Transit Center broke ground more than two years after SkyLink and opened in June 2025. When the people mover begins carrying passengers, it will connect the airport with light rail and bus service at the center.

SkyLink figured in this year's Los Angeles mayoral race. City Council member Nithya Raman, described by The Guardian as a democratic socialist, defeated incumbent Mayor Karen Bass and held up the project as a symbol of the dysfunction at City Hall that she said she would work to solve. Raman holds a master's degree in urban planning from MIT. “The contract was in place before Mayor Bass took office, but she has been mayor now for four years. She inherited the project [and] the responsibility for getting it finished,” Raman said. “Angelenos don’t need another explanation of why it’s complicated or why it’s not her fault – they need a mayor who can manage something complicated.”

Raman called for a full investigation and said she would refer alleged evidence of criminal wrongdoing “to the appropriate authorities.” Bass's office pointed to Linxs as the “main reason” for the delay because the contractor had not completed testing. The mayor's office also said, “Mayor Bass has not stopped in ensuring Angelenos will have access to a people mover that is safe and reliable.” Raman is scheduled to take office next month, when LAX's train troubles will become her responsibility.

Under the settlement terms for Linxs to be paid the additional $550 million, Linxs must deliver a system ready for passengers by Dec. 8, a deadline that appears likely to be missed and could throw the project into further disarray. Linxs said its contracting group “remains committed to working with Lawa to successfully deliver the automated people mover and provide a world-class transportation system for the Los Angeles region.” Last week, Linxs moved the people mover's projected opening back again, to March 2027, after the estimated date had been pushed back multiple times. Until final testing is complete, SkyLink remains closed without a confirmed passenger-service date.
