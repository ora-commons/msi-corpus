## Half a million votes reflected a broad Black and white coalition

Donald Shaffer, director of African American Studies at Mississippi State University, told NPR's All Things Considered that Scott Colom's win in Mississippi's U.S. Senate race was what he had described as "sort of a perfect storm" of "a increasingly unpopular president," "some desire for change," especially in a place like Mississippi, and economic strain. Shaffer said he believed about 19% of Mississippians live "right at the poverty level or just below," so there was "a real appetite to see some sort of change happen," and he said Colom "has been sort of the beneficiary of all of those things." Shaffer said his reaction mixed both feelings: "I think it was a little bit of both," he said, calling the result "certainly a seismic sea change" in a South that has been reliably red for several decades, Mississippi especially. He said he believed Donald Trump won Mississippi by double digits in the last presidential election.

Colom won the race and will become Mississippi's first Black U.S. senator since Reconstruction, an interval of about 150 years, the program reported. More than a third of Mississippi's population is Black, host Sacha Pfeiffer said, which makes the length of that gap more remarkable still.

Pfeiffer asked whether the national focus on the price of so many goods had hit Mississippi particularly acutely and affected how voters voted. Shaffer answered "especially." He said he had heard from many of Colom's campaign officials that they set a goal of trying to capture "about a quarter" of white working-class votes in Mississippi, and that the campaign was able to capture roughly half a million votes in total. Given that African Americans comprise "a little over 30%" of the state's population, Shaffer said, "it's safe to say that there was a broad coalition of voters, both Black and white Mississippians, that pushed him over the finish line."

Shaffer said Colom has said repeatedly, after winning, that he wants to be everyone's senator, including those who did not vote for him, and described that as a broad-based coalition strategy "from the very outset."

Shaffer traced the historical backdrop to the Reconstruction-era amendments. With the passage of the 13th, 14th and 15th amendments, he said, slavery was abolished, African Americans received citizenship through the 14th Amendment, and Black men were able to vote through the 15th — allowing African Americans to "participate fully" in politics and to win elected offices.

That progress was followed by intense backlash. When Reconstruction ended and Southern political autonomy was returned, Shaffer said, "there is a tremendous backlash." He cited historian Rayford Logan's description of the period after Reconstruction as "the nadir of African American experience," and said many political and economic gains were turned back as Jim Crow segregation rose.

On why it then took roughly 150 years for Mississippi to elect another Black senator, Shaffer said "the proof is there." He said he believed it was not until 1986 that Mike Espy became the first Black elected official to Congress from Mississippi, from the 2nd Congressional District — meaning it took at least that long after the end of Reconstruction in the late 19th century "to even see Black political participation" at the federal level.

Colom has been vocal, Pfeiffer noted, about what he considers the relative lack of national support for Democratic candidates like him in the South, saying the national party assumed he did not have a chance and did not want to put much money into such races. Shaffer said he expects the party to rethink that assessment "because a lot of support came late in the campaign," noting that Kamala Harris visited the state "fairly recently" and campaigned on Colom's behalf. He pointed to trends across the South — in Georgia, Mississippi and Texas, where he said "Talarico won by double digits" in a state long reliably red — and said Democrats will "begin to sort of look at those trends and see the South, you know, as a place where they can make some inroads."

Asked what the result tells Black residents of Mississippi about the power of their vote, Shaffer said "it tells them that their vote matters." A constant refrain of the campaign, he said, has been "Mississippi matters," and he said Black voters now recognize that their voice "can create change" when combined with the voices of others, "many of whom don't look like them," including white Southerners "who share, you know, their interest as well."

Scott Detrow, an NPR host, said tomorrow's All Things Considered will visit another historically red part of the country, Southwest Kansas, where three counties known for their meatpacking plants and their majority Hispanic populations broke for Democrats for the first time in decades.
