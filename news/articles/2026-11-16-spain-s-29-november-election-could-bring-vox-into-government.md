## Spain's 29 November election could bring Vox into government

**Subtype:** fact

- Spaniards vote on 29 November in the country's sixth general election in 11 years, a snap contest called by Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez on 5 October.
- Sánchez called the vote after the People's party, Vox and the Catalan party Junts voted down emergency housing measures in congress.
- Polls put the People's party first with about 130 of the 350 seats, short of the 176 needed for an outright majority, with the Spanish Socialist Workers' party on about 110 and Vox on about 63.
- A People's party-Vox coalition would put Vox in national government for the first time in Spain's post-dictatorship democratic history.
- More than 3 million people took part in a general strike last Wednesday in response to soaring housing prices.

## Snap vote called after parliament rejected housing measures

The lack of affordable housing has become a key electoral issue of the campaign, triggering large protests across Spain. The issue was thrown into sharp relief by the case of the late Maricarmen Abascal, an 87-year-old woman who was evicted from her home in Madrid because she could not afford the 230% rent increase demanded by the investment fund that had bought her apartment. Last Wednesday more than 3 million people took part in a general strike called in response to soaring housing prices.

Sánchez and his Spanish Socialist Workers' party (PSOE) are hoping their efforts to address the housing crisis, and the fact that their plans were voted down by the PP, Vox and Junts, will mobilise progressive voters and deliver them another four-year term. The prime minister, who has a reputation for risk-taking and wrongfooting his opponents, believes the housing crisis could be a winning issue after a turbulent and painful couple of years.

All the polls point to the PP finishing first in the election but falling short of an absolute majority. Spain's congress has 350 seats, meaning any party or coalition of parties needs at least 176 seats to secure an outright majority. According to the polls, the PP could win about 130 seats, the PSOE about 110 and Vox about 63. Barring a far better than expected performance from the PSOE, the PP is set to win, ushering in the prospect of a deal with Vox.

The PP, which six years ago repudiated its far-right rival Vox as a party that practised a politics based on "fear, anger, resentment and revenge", has since adopted a far friendlier tone towards Vox, its anti-immigrant policies and its talk of "remigration". The PP and Vox already jointly govern four Spanish regions, so the precedent for deals between the two parties has been set.

Sánchez has billed the election as a stark choice between his progressive government and a Spanish right that would abandon hard-won social advances. "You, the men and women of Spain, must decide whether you want to join the far-right wave that rolls back progress wherever it governs, or to ensure that Spain remains a bastion of openness, social justice and environmental responsibility," he said as he announced the snap vote.

Alberto Núñez Feijóo, the PP's leader, has accused the Sánchez administration of failing to keep Spaniards safe, while Vox criticised the government, citing "incompetence, corruption, cronyism and a lack of investment in maintenance and infrastructure improvements". Feijóo has promised "a return to decency as a basic principle" if his party wins on 29 November. Sánchez's opponents are seeking to portray the election as a referendum on the style of self-serving politics they refer to as sanchismo.

Vox's leader, Santiago Abascal, has said his party stands for "putting nationals first, deregulation, tax relief, and the return of those who should not be in Spain". One of Vox's main policies is "national priority", or giving Spaniards precedence over those from overseas when it comes to accessing subsidised housing, social rentals, grants and benefits. Despite agreeing to "national priority" in the regions it already governs with Vox, the PP has said the policy must obey Spanish and European law.

Feijóo told El País last week: "National priority is based on rootedness in Spain, not nationality, and on respect for the constitution and European treaties. Nothing can be agreed that falls outside the constitution or those treaties." In July last year, Vox floated the idea of deporting up to 8 million people of foreign origin, including the children of immigrants, arguing that "it's very difficult for them to get used to our customs". The party later backtracked on the "remigration" plan and has since said it would apply only to those who enter Spain illegally, who commit crimes or who "come to Spain to live off welfare benefits". Abascal argues that the policies are "not at all driven by hatred toward those from outside, but rather by love for those on the inside".

Sánchez's campaign, in which he has sought to play up his government's social and economic achievements, has been overshadowed by the high-speed rail crash that killed 69 people on 28 October. The accident, nine months after 46 people died in another rail crash on the same line, has raised questions over the safety and management of Spain's high-speed rail network. Sánchez's transport minister, Óscar Puente, bowed to pressure and resigned days after the latest crash, saying: "Neither the country, nor I, can bear 100 deaths in less than a year."

Sánchez's minority coalition government, with the smaller, leftwing Sumar alliance, has struggled to get legislation through a fraught congress and has not passed a budget since 2023. Junts, which offered him conditional support three years ago, has attempted to extract maximum benefits from the government, only to abandon it when it feels so inclined, as it did with the emergency housing measures. The government has also been criticised over its handling of the crisis in Spain's north African exclave of Ceuta, which was sparked when about 80,000 people crossed into the territory from neighbouring Morocco in the space of 48 hours, and Sánchez's administration, party and inner circle have been mired in corruption allegations over the past two years. Sánchez took office vowing to be a scourge of sleaze and graft.

Given all that, Sánchez may have decided that an election campaign harnessing public anger over the housing situation made more political sense than enduring further months of deadlock and bad headlines.

The small parties to the left of the PSOE, including its former partners in Sumar, are unlikely to attract enough votes to help the socialists form another coalition government. The Broad Front, led by the former Barcelona mayor Ada Colau, has united many of the leftwing groupings, but Podemos has declined to join them. Between them, the Broad Front and Podemos are forecast to win about 13 seats, leaving them and the PSOE far short of a majority.
