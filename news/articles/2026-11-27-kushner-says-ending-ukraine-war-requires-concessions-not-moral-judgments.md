## Kushner proposes Nato revival and Ukrainian membership in the European Union

Jared Kushner, Donald Trump's son-in-law and a prominent U.S. negotiator in Russia, Gaza and Iran, used a 70-minute interview this week with Mathias Döpfner, chief executive of Axel Springer, to present Russia's invasion of Ukraine as a series of misunderstandings and miscommunication rather than a conflict between an aggressor and a victim. The Guardian's diplomatic editor Patrick Wintour said the exchange showed Kushner's belief that economic incentives and concessions could produce a settlement, though neither Moscow nor Kyiv nor Europe had accepted the outline Kushner described.

Kushner stood with Ukrainian president Volodymyr Zelenskyy and US special envoy Steve Witkoff as they spoke to the press in Dublin.

Kushner described his method through a series of negotiation sayings: “You have nothing until you have something”, “diplomacy is like untying knots on a fishing line for his son”, and “you play the ball from where it lands”. He also acknowledged that, since taking on the Ukraine brief a year earlier, he had to learn “the algorithm of Europe and how complicated that is,” and had to get abreast of “the local issues with Ukraine”.

He applied his business background to foreign policy. “Look at what’s happening in the Middle East with sanctions, with Russia, with Iran. I think that you have to understand economics in order to understand geopolitics because the two go together very, very closely,” he said. Wintour wrote that a naive bias toward the aggressor ran through Kushner's answers, presented as a mediator's desire to understand both parties.

Kushner praised the Russian president. “Putin – people can like what he has done, they can not like what he has done – [but] he is a grand master of politics. He is a historic figure,” he said. Wintour wrote that Kushner's answers left little room for considering that Putin may have blundered. Kushner also credited the Russian leader with restraint: “So far Putin has shown restraint and he has agreed not to use nuclear weapons and he has reassured us that is his intention.”

Donald Trump and Vladimir Putin shook hands during a meeting in China.

On questions of responsibility, Kushner said: “After all, most lines in Europe have changed over time and they are decided through war or peace, or concessions. It’s not about fairness or who is right or wrong. It’s about ending the killing,” he said. He said he understood why Putin could not offer a ceasefire: “He has very little confidence that if he stops the war, that he [will] be able to get to a postwar settlement that leads to not having a future war.” Wintour summarized the logic: “To stop the killing, the killing has to go on.”

Kushner denied that Trump had pressured Zelenskyy and challenged Döpfner to provide evidence. Döpfner listed examples, including Trump's suggestion only the previous month that Ukraine should get a new leader. Kushner called Trump “a strong counter-puncher,” a description Wintour said implied that Zelenskyy had thrown the first punch in a conflict that began with Russia's invasion. Kushner said relations with Ukraine were cooperative, but said Kyiv needed things from the United States, without which “Ukraine will not be safe in the future”.

On the central territorial dispute, Kushner said “the territorial issue is now a little bit of a deadlock.” His proposed sequence was to agree on a future security architecture for Europe first, after which the land question in Donbas would eventually follow.

Kushner's institutional proposal included reviving the Russia-Nato council, which Wintour described as one of the bodies eviscerated by Putin's war. He also envisaged Ukraine inside the European Union with security guarantees resembling those in Nato's article five. Kushner conceded that it was impossible at that stage to give “more specificity” in the detailed way Ukraine sought.

Neither Moscow nor Kyiv had accepted the proposal, Wintour said, and Europe remained wary. Even while insisting Europe had to be at the negotiating table, Kushner characterized European military assistance to Ukraine as a “proxy war” against Russia.

Wintour said Kushner treated Putin much like a corporate executive willing to close a deal for the right economic incentives. In contrast, Wintour described the Russian president as a man ready to sacrifice hundreds of thousands of Russians to reclaim part of a historical empire. He said Kushner's answers largely embraced Moscow's narrative that European and U.S. postwar missteps culminated in Nato's expansion eastwards after 9/11. Wintour said Kushner had adopted Moscow's central demand to reshape Europe's security order so Russia could retain land gained by force without condemnation.

Wintour concluded that Kushner's answer to why the war had not ended within the 24 hours Trump had promised lay in granting Putin more concessions, on the theory that misunderstandings and misperceptions would then evaporate. His concessions-first proposal had not won acceptance from Moscow or Kyiv, Europe remained wary, and the Donbas question was unresolved.
