## Merkley says sponsors reneged on amendment-process promises

After Sen. Jeff Merkley sought votes on several amendments, the U.S. Senate approved the Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act of 2026 on November 20, 2026, without amendments offered by rank-and-file senators. Merkley said the bill's sponsors had reneged on promises of “a robust amendment process.” The 62-33 vote sends the bill to the House, which approved a narrower version last year.

The final vote was held up this week as Oregon's two Democratic senators pressed to add amendments, including one to ban data centers on public lands. Merkley also sought votes on a host of other amendments, including one that would block the Trump administration from continuing to cancel clean energy grants and other Democratic priorities for what he called “purely political purposes.” Merkley added, “I do think that this bill is very consequential to the future of this planet.”

Merkley and Wyden were among several Democrats in both chambers who said the party should use its stronger position in the next Congress to pass clean-energy legislation, including solar and wind power, while limiting the expansion of oil, natural gas and other fossil fuels. Merkley said, “Getting more renewable electrons where they are needed doesn’t tackle climate change if you simultaneously promote the burning of more fossil fuels. Unfortunately, this bill does exactly that.”

The legislation would shorten federal permitting reviews for power plants, transmission lines and other energy infrastructure and make sure data centers pay for the energy they use. It would overhaul several environmental laws to make it easier to build energy infrastructure. Lawmakers described the proposal as a way to increase the nation's energy supplies to meet surging demand and lower electricity prices, while supporters said shorter reviews would let major projects start sooner.

The bill's four main champions — Republican Sens. Shelley Moore Capito of West Virginia and Mike Lee of Utah, and Democratic Sens. Martin Heinrich of New Mexico and Sheldon Whitehouse of Rhode Island — released a detailed set of amendments designed to bolster support for the legislation. The four senators lead the chamber's energy and environment panels and negotiated the deal a few weeks before the election. They said Americans were tired of paying more in utility bills while projects that could lower costs remained in the planning phase for years.

Lee said, “We are going to make it easier to build in America again, and make life more affordable for American families.” Whitehouse, who has faced criticism from some allies for supporting the permitting bill, called the measure “the floor, not the ceiling, of our climate and environmental aspirations.” He said he hoped to accomplish “big things with our big Democratic majorities” over the next two years.

Industry groups representing developers in clean energy and the oil and gas sector supported the bill, saying it would deliver more electricity to the grid and avoid delays for infrastructure projects that can stretch a decade or more. Wyoming Sen. John Barrasso, the No. 2 Republican in the Senate, said, “In America today, it takes too long and costs too much to build.” He said energy projects that would help lower prices remain “frozen for years” while awaiting federal approval and said, “Construction cannot start without first spending years in the regulatory penalty box,” driving up costs and creating uncertainty.

Fifteen Democrats, including Senate Minority Leader Chuck Schumer, voted for the bill. Two Republicans, Josh Hawley of Missouri and Mike Rounds of South Dakota, voted against it.

President Donald Trump had urged approval in a social media post, calling the bill a “BIG WIN for US ENERGY INDEPENDENCE and DOMINANCE.” He wrote that it would help the country build pipelines, mines and power plants more quickly while “lowering Energy Costs for American Families.” The Guardian characterized the 62-33 vote as the first major action by Congress since the midterm elections. The outlet also said concerns over data centers and rising electricity prices helped prompt Democratic pledges to place limits on the industry.

The Sierra Club and National Wildlife Federation opposed the bill, citing limits on judicial review and changes to the National Environmental Policy Act, Clean Water Act and Endangered Species Act. The National Environmental Policy Act, known as Nepa, requires agencies to consider a project's possible impacts and allows the public to be heard before approval. The current review process often takes five years or more, and shortening that timeframe is a key goal of the bill.

Many environmental organizations said the measure would lock in fossil-fuel development, weaken environmental safeguards and make it harder for communities to challenge energy-project approvals. Jen Duggan, executive director of the Environmental Integrity Project, said, “Make no mistake: This backroom deal will pave the way for even more fossil fuel projects like gas power plants for data centers, oil and gas pipelines and (liquefied natural gas) terminals while sacrificing fundamental and common-sense public health and environmental protections for communities across the US.”

Duggan and other critics said the bill's renewable-energy benefits would be outweighed by likely increases in greenhouse gas emissions and air and water pollution from fossil fuels. She called the measure “a gift to some of the biggest polluters in America” and said it was being rushed through Congress without public hearings or sufficient debate.

Democratic Sen. Maria Cantwell of Washington state said the bill had been rushed and its effects had not been fully considered. After meeting Tuesday with tribal, outdoor recreation and conservation leaders, she said it would undermine tribal nations and weaken protections for the nation's bodies of water, land and wildlife. Cantwell said, “Now is the time to slow this process down, to actually have a formal process” to debate and amend the bill. She added: “Don’t sacrifice the Clean Water Act or hunting or fishing grounds, historic or sacred sites, or protections of tribal sovereignty. We all need new sources of energy and power. But this bill, as it’s currently drafted, is not the answer.”

The Senate's 62-33 approval leaves the House to consider the measure with shorter review timelines, data-center cost rules and changes to environmental laws.
