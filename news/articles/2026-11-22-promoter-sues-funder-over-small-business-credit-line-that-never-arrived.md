## Merchant cash advances are now the fastest-growing small-business funding source

GLENDALE, Calif. — Vahe Shahinian works from a desk tucked into the corner of his teenage bedroom, a place he never intended to return to well into middle age. There he rereads the emails that split his life into before and after.

In 2019 his company was soaring — like a miniature Live Nation-Ticketmaster, selling tickets and producing concerts around Los Angeles and beyond. The broker's pitch offered to fund it with a line of credit worth $750,000, and Shahinian imagined how much he could do with stable funding.

The offer carried a condition. He would first accept a third of the sum and begin paying it back immediately, in daily installments of $3,600; after 30 days he could access the rest. He crunched the numbers, followed up with questions, and took the deal. He sent the daily payments, he says, for 30 days. Then 40. Then 50. The line of credit never came.

"From that day," he says, "it's been one long day, one long nightmare."

Shahinian had been in business 15 years by then. Educated as a computer scientist, he spent years programming satellites as an aerospace engineer, then in 2004 developed a site for something he loved in his spare time: community events. He called the ticketing and seating-chart platform It's My Seat. Over a decade he grew it from selling tickets to galas and festivals into producing his own shows — booking artists and venues, promoting and marketing. His first concert, by a flamenco guitarist, sold 400 seats; the next 700; three years later the 1960s crooner Engelbert Humperdinck performed for 3,000. In 2015 he expanded into touring.

Like most U.S. small businesses, Shahinian says he self-funded for years — savings, credit cards, reinvested profits. He says he had qualified for several generous credit cards, including an American Express with essentially no spending limit, and that Amex repeatedly approved him for special merchant financing: instant short-term loans at a low interest rate of 6%. "All my life," he says, "I had never had one missed payment."

By 2018, he says, ticket sales had climbed toward $5 million a year and profits had reached $250,000. Booking his biggest tour yet, with a 1980s German superstar, he sought his fifth round of Amex merchant funding. Amex said no; the company was phasing out the program. He had left himself two weeks to acquire about $200,000.

That is the gap merchant cash advances fill. Most traditional lending takes months and vigorous vetting, and the Small Business Administration has tightened its lending rules. Big banks reject about 20% of business loan applications, according to the Treasury Department, and respond to a quarter with only partial funding. A recent Federal Reserve survey found even lower odds. MCAs, by contrast, present themselves as a lifeline, offering large sums within hours.

Their fees run from 25% to 200% and more when translated into an annual rate. MCA firms argue they charge a lot because they take on big risks. They also face almost no regulatory oversight: MCAs escape most lending laws because, technically, they are not loans. An MCA is meant to be a purchase of future sales — a business gets cash in exchange for a steady cut of its earnings later. Funders generally are not required to be licensed and face no legal limit on how much they can charge.

Shahinian, scrambling after the Amex rejection, lined up two cash advances totaling $250,000, he says, owing back almost $400,000. The money saved his concert tour but cost him all the profits. "It was my fault — I waited last minute," he says. "I'm talking to myself, 'Vahe, you had to do it this time. You're never going back to MCAs.'"

The pitches never stopped. Sometimes he replied that he wanted only a traditional loan, something along an 8% interest rate. Then the offer came to him before he even went hunting: a line of credit of up to $750,000 at an annual interest rate just under 9%. "It's like the puzzle fit, you know?" he says.

On the phone, he says, the broker presented the 30 days of daily $3,600 payments as a routine test of his dependability before he would qualify for the preapproved line of credit. He did not recognize it as a merchant cash advance. He had no lawyer or adviser to consult. He looked up the finance firm, Wall Street-based EIN Cap, which advertised itself as a "provider of working capital to local businesses."

"I was assured that 'We've done this for years. We don't care about a 30-day program — our goal is long term,'" Shahinian says. "The whole setup was to make someone like me feel assured, feel safe — and I felt safe to go in it."

From the start, he says, he received $228,000 instead of the promised $250,000; the difference was withheld as an initial fee, a common feature of MCAs. He set aside almost half the funds to secure the daily payment, and the rest went into down payments for venues and other tour investments.

After 30 days, when the line of credit did not materialize, he says the broker portrayed the delays as technical. His emails grew urgent. "I had calculated and planned everything based on the 30-day plan," he wrote. "I planned massive tour projects based on this solid word. This is not a joke." And: "I don't think you understand the urgency of this situation. I'm basically out of time."

EIN Cap later argued in court that it never promised Shahinian any loans or lines of credit — only a merchant cash advance, which it delivered. The company said it was the brokers, the middlemen, who had arranged the deal and made the promises. Much of what it had to say about its ties to those brokers is in sealed depositions.

"EIN Cap denies Mr. Shahinian's claims, and its position is set out in its public court filings," Levi Silver, counsel for EIN, said in an emailed statement, declining any further comment because of the pending case.

After 40 days of daily payments, Shahinian says, his bank account hit zero for the first time. Days before his biggest concert tour yet, venues were still half-paid, airfare was unbooked and rising in price, and thousands of tickets were undermarketed and unsold. He did what many business owners do when they struggle to stay afloat with merchant cash advances: he took out more of them. Two new MCAs totaling around $250,000, he says, evaporated into expenses in half a day.

"From here," he says, "the avalanche of money over money, over money, over money started."

He tapped his credit and then home equity — his own, his parents' and his best friend's. Within weeks, he says, he was more than $1 million in debt to friends and family. He canceled several concerts of his tours at six-figure losses, began to default, lost count of overdue bills and unpaid invoices, and raked in collections notices. Some of his clients received lien letters asking them to redirect any payments meant for Shahinian to his funders instead.

"He asked me for some money, and, I mean, I could see he's drowning, you know?" says Zorik Yeghikian, a restaurant owner whom Shahinian had helped win a catering contract for a local concert venue. Yeghikian knew the trap of MCA payments; they had nearly capsized his own business, he says, when he borrowed about $30,000 and owed nearly triple. He slept at his restaurant and worked double shifts alongside his wife, both forgoing pay. "I lost my hair," Yeghikian says. "I went through hell those years."

The exact scale of the MCA industry is unclear, but it is ballooning. The Federal Reserve Bank of Kansas City reports that businesses are increasingly turning to MCAs to cover basic expenses like payroll. MCAs are a frequent feature in small-business bankruptcies, which have been rising.

Shahinian's choice — to fight — is rare. Borrowers often struggle to afford lawyers, let alone find one familiar with the MCA industry, and MCA firms can be difficult to pin down: he says he had to hire a private investigator to reach his funders to serve his lawsuit. He sued EIN Cap and the brokers in 2019. Three years later he settled his claims against the brokers to focus on EIN; his remaining case in U.S. District Court in Los Angeles accuses EIN of fraud.

His lawyer, Shane Heskin, who has been clashing with MCA companies for a decade, calls what happened "carroting": a carrot dangled in front of a small-business owner in the form of an attractive financing deal — a low-interest loan — in a bait and switch to sign an unsolicited MCA. "I see two or three a week," Heskin says. "They're exploding." He says common targets are first-time business owners, first-time borrowers and especially immigrants.

NPR spoke with several entrepreneurs who were carroted in similar ways. One described falling into a deep depression; another declared bankruptcy. None besides Shahinian wanted to speak on the record.

As the lawsuit plodded through the legal system, Shahinian scoured the web for people associated with his lending deal. He found that his broker, who had introduced himself as Bryan Stein, had used different names and been indicted for financial crimes. The Brooklyn District Attorney's Office told NPR that the man, Boris Shteyngart, pleaded guilty in 2012 to grand larceny; public records show he served time in prison.

Shahinian spent a year tracking down some 700 business owners who had been sued by EIN for debt collection. Most did not want to revisit that chapter. Shahinian says he connected with 70, and that many "came to tears" because "someone is standing up to bullies."

In the past decade, EIN has faced several lawsuits in at least nine states, records show. Most cases related to bankruptcies by its MCA borrowers, and most were settled or dismissed. In one case in Washington state, EIN was ordered to return the daily fees it continued to withdraw from a business that was already insolvent. Last year a U.S. district judge in Illinois ruled against EIN over its 2019 MCA funding to a firm that federal regulators accuse of running a Ponzi-like scheme; the judge wrote that EIN "propped up" and "prolonged the Ponzi scheme" by either failing to conduct due diligence or ignoring its findings, and must return profits of $184,854.

Meanwhile in California, Shahinian's trial was delayed by another nine months. Shortly after, he lost his house to foreclosure. For 16 years he had lived on a lush suburban cul-de-sac in the Armenian American community of Glendale, north of downtown Los Angeles. At 51, he sold off belongings, packed up his cats, computer screens, ticket printers and prized sound system, and moved in with his elderly parents. Every dollar from the foreclosure sale, he says, went to pay mortgage debt and part of what he owed his best friend.

Several friends have urged him to move on from his costly seven-year legal fight, perhaps to declare bankruptcy. To him, that is admitting defeat. "My fault," he says, "was only believing in a promise that I could have never imagined to be false."

In recent years, a handful of states, including California, have started requiring MCA companies to be clearer, in writing, about their fees. California recently also passed a law that, starting in 2028, will require funders and brokers to be licensed and undergo background checks and inspections. It is too late for Shahinian.

His business is now back to basics: ticketing, mostly for local shows, and coding bells and whistles for his marketing software. He hopes his lawsuit will recoup more than $5 million — all the debt he estimates he has accrued since his "incident," including some $3 million in interest and fees. He lays blame for its entirety at the feet of his funders, with a car-accident comparison. "You're being rear-ended," he says. "The 12-wheeler rear-ending you, you lose the steering wheel, and you smash into a bunch of cars, and everyone's focus is: Why did you smash into a bunch of cars?"

Heskin says the best-case scenario would be a multimillion-dollar judgment with punitive damages, "and then it's good luck collecting on it." He is not sure what assets EIN or its employees named in Shahinian's lawsuit still have. NPR asked EIN's lawyers whether the firm still operates; they did not respond. The firm's investors are unknown.

Shahinian argues any win would be meaningful. "It would show everyone [whom] I affected these years that I'm in the right," he says. "Hopefully it would be a case for the industry to self-heal, self-correct. Maybe. And it'll be hope for others who are stuck — give them motivation."

"Don't give up, you know?" he says. "Don't be on path of least resistance. Go fighting."
