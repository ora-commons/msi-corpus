## Three agreements are targeted for Friday's UK-EU summit

Andy Burnham and European Union leaders are due to meet in Brussels on Friday for a reset summit, with less than 24 hours left to resolve disputes over Britain’s role in future food and farming rules and tuition fees for EU students. An EU diplomat said the talks would go “down to the wire.” A British government spokesperson described the talks as close, while another EU diplomat said ambassadors representing EU member states would meet at 5 p.m. and keep meeting until agreement was reached that night.

Burnham was pictured with European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen at the UN General Assembly in New York in September. The UK and EU hope to sign three agreements at the summit. One is a food and drink deal that The Guardian described as a Labour manifesto commitment and said is expected to add 0.4% to British GDP.

The agreement is expected to come into force on 1 March 2028 and would require Britain to align with EU sanitary and phytosanitary standards, placing the UK in the role of a rule taker. No 10 said the Labour manifesto had aimed to build closer ties with the EU. “We were obviously elected on a manifesto that promised to reset the relationship and seek to deepen ties with our European friends,” the prime minister’s office said.

EU sources said the UK has been pushing for a formal role in the committees that implement existing sanitary and phytosanitary rules and set new ones. The issue remained unresolved after a meeting of EU ambassadors ended late Wednesday. One EU diplomat said: “They want to be able to shape decisions, not only implementation, but future decisions. That has always been a red line.”

Under the proposal, the UK would dynamically align with EU laws and have a “voice” but “not be in the room” when laws were drafted. A second diplomat from a country sympathetic to the British position said the European Commission had proposed an “extremely restrictive consultation mechanism” for the UK.

The earlier EU diplomat said it was reasonable for a British representative to take part in implementing sanitary and phytosanitary legislation affecting Britain. “Of course the UK cannot vote in the council as a non-member state, but we feel that we need to be pragmatic about coordination and implementation,” the diplomat said. The precise terms of UK consultation on future food and farming rules remain unclear.

The Guardian said the issue would be many times magnified if Britain chose to enter the single market as a non-EU member. An EU insider described one of the hardest discussions as whether the UK would be a “rule taker or rule maker.”

Separately, the UK and EU remained deadlocked over whether EU students in Britain should pay British or international tuition rates and over the overall number who could participate in a new youth mobility scheme. Sources said the UK was resisting EU pressure to agree to work on tuition fees for EU students coming to Britain under the scheme. An EU insider said: “We are fighting hard on this and think we will get there.”

The draft proposal gives the UK two options: significantly reduced undergraduate fees or equal treatment with nationals. The British government spokesperson said the talks were close and that, if an agreement was reached, the government would bring forward its cutting costs bill to parliament next week.

Burnham’s spokesperson suggested there was a UK-EU consensus that there would be no further incremental reset summits beyond Friday until full negotiations began on a longer-term, more stable relationship, such as rejoining the single market. “After the summit, we will begin those discussions with our European partners about the realistic options for our longer-term relationship,” the spokesperson said.

The UK has prioritized seeking commitments on Britain’s position under the EU’s Made in Europe legislation. The trade-defense policy would include tariffs on Chinese electric vehicles but also affect British manufacturing. The UK and EU were believed to be close to summit language pointing toward UK inclusion, though nothing could be formalized until the EU passed its own legislation.

Burnham’s spokesperson said the UK was engaging constructively with the EU on Made in Europe and had been clear about the challenges for British industries. The spokesperson cited deeply integrated supply chains with Europe and said the UK wanted to keep working together to boost growth and open trade. “The UK and Europe have shared economic challenges, and we believe shared challenges are best met with a collective approach that is in Britain’s best interests, and in Europe’s too,” the spokesperson said.

As the summit approached, both the institutional role Britain would play under the food agreement and the tuition terms for EU students remained unsettled. An EU diplomat said: “Our feeling is that the Brits have to move if they want a deal.”
