## Canada met NATO's 2% target in 2025 after nearly two decades

Canadian Defense Minister David McGuinty disputed a Pentagon memorandum that criticized Canada’s defense spending and military readiness, speaking on Parliament Hill in Ottawa after the document’s Tuesday release. He said Canada remains sovereign and does not report to the United States.

The memorandum, released Tuesday, said Canada had “yet to make the hard decisions and tradeoffs” needed to meet previous spending pledges and become a “credible partner in the defense of our shared continent.” It said Canada took nearly two decades to reach spending promises made in 2006, despite Canada’s status as a major G7 economy and one of NATO’s wealthiest members.

The document also described significant operational hurdles in Canada’s air, land and sea operations, saying political leaders had pursued “parochial economic and commercial interests over military optimization.” Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth was “laser focused” on modernizing Canada’s military, the memorandum said, adding that U.S. taxpayers had been “footing the bill for its defense” for decades.

McGuinty said he could not “divine” the Pentagon’s thinking behind the criticism. “The amount of cooperation we are pushing under Norad [North American Aerospace Defense Command] has not changed. What we’re doing together at Nato has not changed. Our military-to-military cooperation has not changed. Our work on integrated air missile defence has not changed,” he said. He also bristled at a reporter’s suggestion that the memo implied Canada needed U.S. approval of its spending plans. “Canada is a sovereign nation. I’ll be very direct. We don’t report to the United States,” he said.

The Pentagon’s criticism came against a changed NATO spending landscape. When Russia annexed Crimea in 2014, only the US, the UK and Greece met the alliance’s guideline of spending 2% of GDP on defense, according to The Guardian’s report. By 2025, all 31 NATO members with armed forces, including Canada, had reached the goal.

Canada spent $63.4 billion on national defense in 2025, meeting the NATO pledge to spend 2% of GDP for the first time. Its federal budget, released this month, lays out a plan to spend about 4% of Canada’s GDP on defense and security annually by the end of the decade and 5% by 2035, including submarines and early warning aircraft. NATO members agreed last year to raise the alliance goal to the equivalent of 5% of GDP by 2035.

Aircraft procurement remains a point of friction with Washington. Prime Minister Mark Carney’s government has yet to make a final decision on its order of F-35 fighter jets from Lockheed Martin and is weighing Swedish-made Gripen aircraft instead. The multibillion-dollar purchase has been the subject of a political review for more than a year. The Pentagon memorandum warned that operating a “mixed fleet” would “compound existing personnel shortfalls.”

At the same time, Canada is seeking broader support for a defense bank championed by Carney. Canada’s lead negotiator, Isabelle Hudon, traveled to Europe to pitch the Defence, Security and Resilience Bank, a global initiative that would lend to governments and contractors at low cost to support defense projects. Ten countries have pledged support, but no other G7 country has done so, with the UK and Germany declining to join. Hudon said the €4 billion secured so far is enough to start the bank, but she is aiming for up to €100 billion.


Earlier, MSI reported that [the Pentagon had paused U.S. participation in the Permanent Joint Board on Defense with Canada](/articles/2026-05-19-us-pauses-joint-defense-board-with-canada-as-alliance-strains-deepen/), citing Canada’s failure to make progress on its defense commitments. The new memorandum extends that pressure to Canada’s broader spending record, operational performance and fighter procurement, while McGuinty says military cooperation remains unchanged and rejects the suggestion that Canada reports to the United States.
