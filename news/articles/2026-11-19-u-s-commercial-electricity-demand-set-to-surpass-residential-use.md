## Electric Power Research Institute estimates data centers’ 4% to 5% U.S. electricity share

U.S. commercial electricity use is on track to surpass residential consumption for the first time in 2026, according to the U.S. Energy Information Administration. The milestone reflects a broad commercial category rather than data-center consumption alone, as technology and other businesses increasingly reshape the country’s power demand. The Wall Street Journal characterized data centers as “by far the biggest growth engine” for the U.S. power grid.

The Energy Information Administration places data centers used for artificial intelligence, cloud computing and cryptocurrency mining in the commercial division, alongside offices and shopping centers. Residential customers form a separate category, while factories, refineries, agriculture and other industrial activities are classified as industrial. The commercial total therefore reflects a broad mix of customers rather than data centers alone.

Energy Information Administration data show that electricity consumption was essentially flat between 2010 and 2017. From 2018 to 2025, the market grew 6%, led by a 9% jump in commercial use.

Commercial demand already outpaced residential demand in 23 states and Washington, D.C., as of last year. Texas, Ohio and Nevada—all growing data-center markets—were the latest states to cross that threshold. Oklahoma crossed in 2024, while Oregon and Nebraska did so in 2023.

Virginia, home to the world’s largest concentration of data centers, has seen commercial customers consume more power than households since 2011. Commercial customers now account for about 59% of all electricity sold in the state.

Some of the largest increases in electricity demand occurred in North Dakota and Texas, where a boom in computing and the oil industry’s electrification of field operations drove much of the growth. New Mexico is connecting its oil field to the grid. Iowa has a large data-center market and industrial growth, while Oregon has a major data-center corridor.

Beginning in 2023, the federal government and utilities began reclassifying many data centers and cryptocurrency mines from the industrial category to the commercial sector. Some facilities had previously been grouped with industrial customers because of their massive power draw, so the change affects how electricity use appears in the commercial totals over time.

The Electric Power Research Institute estimates that data centers currently account for 4% to 5% of U.S. electricity consumption. The research institute forecasts that the share could grow to 9% to 17% by 2030.

Cleanview data show about 58 gigawatts of data-center capacity under construction across 577 locations. The power represented by that capacity is roughly six times the amount New York City uses during periods of peak demand. Of the total, about 38 gigawatts broke ground in the 12 months that ended in October, and more than 20% is in Texas.

Construction starts nevertheless stalled over the summer. The slowdown coincided with a broad backlash against AI, driven partly by concerns about data centers’ electricity appetite. In November’s midterm elections, voters backed candidates and ballot measures that would ban or slow data-center development. Stocks across the power sector, from plant owners to equipment makers, fell in response.

Utilities have been upgrading and storm-hardening aging infrastructure while rewriting spending plans to meet rising power demand. Some companies operating in big data-center and industrial markets, including American Electric Power, Wisconsin-based WEC Energy Group and Michigan-based DTE Energy, are raising their five-year capital budgets by 20% to 30%. American Electric Power, which operates across 11 states, including Texas and Ohio, recently increased its spending plan to $100 billion from $78 billion.

American Electric Power also has 35 gigawatts of “take-or-pay” power contracts, roughly the output of 35 nuclear reactors. Those agreements leave large energy users, including data centers, responsible for paying for the capacity they request even if their expansion plans later fizzle.

Michael Thomas, the founder of Cleanview, said, “The next few months will show whether the summer was a pause or the start of a real slowdown that could have far-reaching consequences for the global economy and stock market.”
