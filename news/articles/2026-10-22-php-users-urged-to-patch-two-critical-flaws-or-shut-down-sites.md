## The episode highlights how artificial intelligence is changing web security

The organization behind PHP and the FBI's cyber division issued a warning Wednesday about two critical vulnerabilities. The official PHP website and several other organizations and agencies released security advisories that day. Experts described the vulnerabilities as one of the most notable in recent memory, according to the report.

The report described PHP as the backbone of the internet. The language is a widely used general-purpose scripting language geared toward web development, and the report said it powers the majority of websites around the world.

The warning sent government agencies, corporations and other organizations into a flurry of activity, according to the report. That response followed the warning from the language's organization and the FBI's cyber division.

The advisories told PHP users to patch the two critical vulnerabilities. For systems able to receive an update right away, patching was the prescribed response to the disclosure. Users who could not update immediately were told to shut down their sites. That instruction applied to websites unable to receive the patch without delay.

The PHP Foundation, which works to provide transparency and accountability with the language, said the vulnerabilities could be exploited and allow bad actors to get unauthenticated access to sites running PHP. This would mean attackers could control a web server remotely without needing a password, said James Sullivan, director of cyber and tech at the Royal United Services Institute in London. That could allow a hacker to steal data, alter data or use the system as a platform from which to launch other attacks, he said.

PHP powers about 70% of websites globally, so the possible exposure was immense. The language is free and open-source, which contributes to its prevalence online. PHP is behind the content management systems WordPress and Drupal, which are commonly used by governments for their sites, and MediaWiki, the software that runs Wikipedia.

In accordance with the security advisories issued this week, several websites were taken offline to prevent exposure while they fixed the problem. There has been disruption to some government services and websites, including in the United Kingdom, Finland and Japan. Some websites may still be down because multiple patches are available for different types of software and some organizations may still be waiting to receive them. The patches also need to be tested to make sure they work.

Charl van der Walt, the head of security research at Orange Cyberdefense, said he believes the reaction to the vulnerabilities is just the "early tremors" of the disruption they could cause in the weeks and months ahead. He said there likely will be further service disruptions as bad actors scan the internet for vulnerable systems.

The vulnerabilities have been known about since at least 2024, according to the PHP Foundation. A security team developed several competing approaches to solutions, but each had downsides. The foundation said it seemed there wasn't a practical or concrete way attackers could exploit the vulnerabilities, so progress on a fix stalled. The group took responsibility for not coming up with a fix sooner, saying: "We won't pretend otherwise: the delay was a structural failure, caused by both the security team's decision process and its lack of resources." Some experts say the discovery of the vulnerabilities could have been even earlier than 2024.

Although humans first discovered the vulnerabilities, AI agents helped identify how they could be exploited and supported the process of testing and validating the fixes. OpenAI said in a blog post that it had led a coalition of researchers, security providers and others to address vulnerabilities in PHP over the past two months. Researchers at Wiz, a cloud security platform, also said they independently used AI to identify the vulnerabilities.

The accessibility of AI means many organizations, big and small, now have more advanced ways to proactively and reactively respond to security threats. It also means bad actors have more advanced ways to exploit digital weak points, at great scale and speed. "It's not quite as easy for defenders as it is for attackers," van der Walt said. "You find the vulnerability in something. You replace it. But with what? Is there an alternative that is patched and robust and tested and approved?"

Sullivan, of the Royal United Services Institute in London, said while the push-and-pull can be worrisome, he's focused on the positive side. "We've actually seen quite a decent use case here where AI can discover some critical vulnerabilities and then the community can come together to remediate them," he said.

The episode also highlighted how artificial intelligence is changing the game for web security. AI's broader role in the threat landscape was part of the report's framing, even as the immediate response centered on patching PHP or shutting down sites.

Taken together, the disclosure covered a technology used on the majority of websites around the world. For PHP users, the advice was direct: patch the two critical flaws or shut down their sites if they could not update them immediately.
