## Nearby dates and a multi-night composite remain possible

A South Korean research team reported Thursday that the sky most similar to Vincent van Gogh’s “The Starry Night” occurred over Saint-Remy de Provence around July 22, 1889. The result suggests the painting may have been inspired by what Van Gogh saw while gazing heavenward from the French asylum where he stayed, rather than arising entirely from his imagination.

The team reached its conclusion after years of forensic astronomy and research into the Dutch artist. Rather than offering only a general resemblance between a painting and the night sky, the team identified the sky most similar to the painting over Saint-Remy de Provence around July 22, 1889. The study’s proposed reference also falls 137 years before the team’s report.

A team led by Elijah Jeong-hyun Kim, the CEO of a Seoul telescope maker, is disputing the accepted date in a study appearing in the journal Scientific Reports. Kim is also a doctoral candidate at Kyung Hee University. He said the sky most similar to the painting occurred around July 22, 1889, while noting that the few days before and after could have inspired Van Gogh as well.

“No two skies are the same. Something always changes. I believe Vincent noticed those changes and put them into the painting,” Kim said in an email. He added: “I am confident that July 22 is the better date. I will bet on this date.”

The postimpressionist painting depicts a crescent moon and Venus surrounded by swirling stars, with a village nestled below and cypress trees looming on one side. The team’s analysis connects those celestial features to the proposed sky in France.

Long fascinated by Van Gogh’s art, Kim traveled to France in 2019, the first time in mid-June and the second time around July 22, where he found brilliant stars rising in July’s predawn sky, unlike June. Kim and his team discovered through further celestial sleuthing that around July 22, 1889, the shape of the moon before dawn appeared like the one in the painting, with the Hyades star cluster in the constellation Taurus rising in the east alongside Venus. The sky should have been clear enough for Van Gogh to see all this, based on their weather analyses.

Art historians have long dated “The Starry Night” to June 1889, soon after Van Gogh checked into the asylum. That established chronology has placed the work about a month earlier than the sky identified in the team’s report.

The proposed July 22 date would move the painting’s accepted chronology forward by roughly one month. July 22 provides a concrete comparison point for the painting’s date, while nearby dates and a multi-night composite remain possible.

The latest study addresses “a real puzzle” that in mid-June 1889, the moon was not the thin crescent painted by Van Gogh, said Jose Luis Aragon, a physicist at the National Autonomous University of Mexico who has studied “The Starry Night” but did not take part in the new research. “Still, I would see the July date as an intriguing hypothesis rather than a settled answer,” Aragon said in an email.

Several months before the accepted June 1889 date, Van Gogh had cut off his left ear during an argument with painter Paul Gauguin. That earlier event and his subsequent residence at the asylum form part of the chronology surrounding the painting.

Van Gogh is thought to have suffered a seizure around that time in July. Kim and his team contend that the seizure occurred later in July, based on their analyses of letters between the painter and one of his brothers. Some of those letters refer to cicadas, another clue that points toward the hotter July, according to Kim.

The researchers tested and verified their methods on night skies in other paintings, making for what Aragon considers “a careful and genuinely interdisciplinary study.”

Because Van Gogh painted in a studio at the asylum from sketches and memory, Aragon said, “the sky is likely a composite of many nights as the authors themselves acknowledge.” He also said: “What I find most convincing is the broader message: The painting is not pure fantasy. Van Gogh watched the real sky very closely.”

The researchers’ conclusion remains qualified: the painting may have been inspired by what Van Gogh actually saw. Their report presents the astronomical comparison as a finding that suggests both a new date and a possible observational basis for one of the world’s most famous paintings.

Despite all his research, Kim has never seen “The Starry Night” at the Museum of Modern Art in New York. “Now that the paper is out, I really have to go and see ‘The Starry Night’ in person, don’t I?” he said.

The proposal gives art historians a concrete comparison point: the sky most similar to the painting over Saint-Remy de Provence around July 22, 1889, a reference 137 years before the study. Kim said nearby days could also have inspired Van Gogh, while Aragon said the sky was likely a composite of many nights.
