## State share of SNAP administration costs rises from 50% to 75%

New Mexico Health Care Authority Secretary Kari Armijo told the Legislative Finance Committee on Monday that federal changes to the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program are increasing paperwork, eligibility checks and administrative costs for the state agency. The new documentation requirements apply to roughly 460,000 New Mexicans who receive SNAP assistance and took effect in May. Recipients must provide additional information about their income and expenses.

The legislation, known as the One Big Beautiful Bill Act or H.R.1, changed the program and placed new costs on state agencies that oversee it. President Donald Trump signed the spending bill in July 2025.

Armijo told lawmakers, “The impacts of H.R.1 are not just policy impacts or financial impacts,” and said, “They are workload impacts for our agency. We have to equate all of this work into tasks and a frontline workforce that can manage those tasks.”

New data Armijo released Monday showed a 16-fold increase in records SNAP recipients send to Health Care Authority caseworkers. The data also showed an 81% increase in eligibility verifications that state workers are now required to conduct.

The federal changes also increased the share of SNAP administration costs paid by states from 50% to 75%, adding $6.7 million to the authority. The authority is anticipating additional costs as more provisions of H.R.1 take effect next year.

Armijo’s budget request seeks a $14.1 million increase in recurring funding for the authority’s Income Support Division. The division administers SNAP and other programs, including Temporary Assistance for Needy Families and the Low Income Home Energy Assistance Program. The requested Income Support Division funding for the coming state fiscal year is $1.56 billion, roughly 11% more than the division’s previous budget.

Armijo also requested $5 million in recurring funding for New Mexico food banks. Armijo said the banks were seeing increased demand amid rising grocery prices and noted that at least 18,000 New Mexicans were losing SNAP since H.R.1 took effect. The state has said thousands of New Mexicans could lose food assistance under a federal proposal.

During a special session shortly after the law took effect last year, the Legislature approved $8 million for food banks in a one-time appropriation. Armijo said the banks were still receiving “a lot of demand” and that recurring funding was needed.

Armijo’s Monday presentation concerned only the authority’s budget requests for the Income Support Division and two other divisions. Armijo will provide a separate budget presentation in the coming weeks about H.R.1’s impact on Medicaid, the low-income health insurance program the authority also oversees.

Together, the figures show the immediate fiscal effects of the federal changes: more casework for state workers, a larger state financing share and requests for recurring support. The authority is also anticipating further costs as more H.R.1 provisions take effect next year, while a separate presentation will address Medicaid.
