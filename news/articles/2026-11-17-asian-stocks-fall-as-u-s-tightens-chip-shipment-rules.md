## Asian stocks fall as U.S. tightens chip shipment rules

**Subtype:** fact

- The U.S. Commerce Department issued a rule that will add Malaysia, Thailand, Indonesia, Singapore and Brazil to an export-restriction list requiring licenses for chip shipments.
- The Bureau of Industry and Security said the rule is designed to prevent remote data-center arrangements in third countries from bypassing U.S. restrictions on sensitive American technology.

- Front-month West Texas Intermediate crude futures fell 0.4% to $85.39 a barrel as improving supply prospects offset Middle East tensions.

## UOB estimates rule erased $960 billion in U.S. technology market capitalization

Asian equities fell Tuesday morning as the U.S. Commerce Department issued an interim final rule tightening controls on advanced artificial-intelligence chip shipments to five intermediary countries. Malaysia, Thailand, Indonesia, Singapore and Brazil will be subject to the export restrictions, and companies must obtain Commerce Department licenses before shipping chips to them.

The measure is intended to prevent Chinese clients from accessing data-center capacity in the third countries remotely, according to the Commerce Department’s Bureau of Industry and Security. “The rule is designed to ensure that existing restrictions on access to sensitive American technology cannot be circumvented through remote computing arrangements involving data centers in third countries,” the Bureau said. U.S. technology companies could receive exceptions.

Among the reported market declines, Japan’s Nikkei Stock Average fell 0.9%. Advantest, a manufacturer of automatic test equipment for the semiconductor industry, dropped 3.9%. SoftBank Group, described as an AI proxy, shed 3.8%, while memory-chip maker Kioxia Holdings lost 3.6%.

South Korea’s Kospi dropped 1.0%, with chip-making index heavyweights SK Hynix down 1.1% and Samsung Electronics down 2.0%. Singapore’s FTSE Straits Times Index was down 0.6%.

UOB’s global economics and markets research team tied the broader decline to the new licensing regime. “The trigger [for deteriorating risk sentiment] was an interim final rule from the U.S. Commerce Department requiring export licenses for advanced AI chip shipments to China routed through five intermediary countries, effectively closing a key Southeast Asian loophole,” the team said in a report.

The team estimated that the move erased $960 billion in U.S. technology market capitalization. The rule’s arrival intensified investor concerns about the artificial-intelligence boom and spurred risk-off sentiment, according to the report.

Separately, crude oil futures declined as improving supply prospects offset Middle East tensions. Saudi Arabia said it pumped 10.5 million barrels a day in October, while Iraq and Kuwait also reported large increases, according to ANZ Research analysts.

Despite the supply improvement, ANZ Research said the oil market’s resilience was being severely stressed. “Logistics are under stress, while supply, inventories, refining and effective spare capacity are vulnerable,” the analysts said.

“The supply flows are also unsustainable over the longer term. An increasing amount must find its way via alternative routes that are highly exposed to disruptions from attacks from Iran or constraints on logistics,” the analysts added.

Front-month West Texas Intermediate crude oil futures were 0.4% lower at $85.39 a barrel. Front-month Brent crude oil futures were 0.3% lower at $92.15 a barrel, according to ICE data.
