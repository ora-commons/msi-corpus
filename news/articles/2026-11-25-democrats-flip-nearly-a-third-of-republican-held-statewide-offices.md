## The victories extend Democratic control to election administration and state finances

Democrats won nearly one-third of the Republican-held statewide offices contested in the 2026 midterm elections, according to an analysis of Downballot data published by The Guardian. The gains reached into deep-red states and included posts that had gone to Republicans for decades, among them Arizona's treasurer, last won by Democrats in 1964, its mine inspector, last won by Democrats in 1974, and Indiana's auditor, last won by Democrats in 1982. In Indiana, Beau Bayh's secretary of state victory was the first Democratic statewide win in the state since 2012.

The analysis drew on data compiled by the Downballot, a specialist U.S. elections news site, and was produced in collaboration with David Nir, the site's publisher.

The breadth of the result extends past the races for Congress and governors' mansions. The Guardian reported that Democrats will return to Congress in January with record numbers in the House and an unexpectedly large majority in the Senate. At the state level, the downballot wins give Democrats posts whose powers operate independently of governors and state legislatures.

The offices cover a wide range of public functions: overseeing elections, regulating energy companies and managing public pension funds. Attorneys general represent their states in court and enforce laws covering areas such as consumer protection and the environment. The Guardian's analysis noted that a change of party can alter which cases an office pursues and the positions it takes in litigation.

In Texas, Democratic attorney general-elect Nathan Johnson said he would not sue out-of-state doctors who mail abortion pills to Texas. The statement is one concrete instance of the litigation choices the analysis identifies with attorney general offices.

In Indiana, Beau Bayh won the secretary of state race by more than nine points, the first Democratic statewide victory in the state since 2012, in a state Trump carried by 19 points two years ago. Fellow Democrat Jessica Bailey flipped the auditor's office. The two offices place Democrats in charge of the state's chief election office and the office responsible for accounting for state funds, giving the party a foothold before the 2028 presidential contest.

The gains also ended long party holds in other states. Democrats last won Arizona's treasurer race in 1964 and its mine inspector race in 1974. In Indiana, the party last won the auditor's race in 1982, and in Texas it last won the agriculture commissioner race in 1986.

Those offices carry weight beyond the campaigns that produced them. Louis Jacobson, a political analyst and chief author of the Almanac of American Politics, said: "These positions exert power in their own right. Having Democrats hold the run of statewide offices in states like Texas and Georgia – especially with the governorship – means shifting policy authority from one party to the other. There will be impacts."

Jacobson said the change could widen the scope for state-level action: "In many states, Democrats will be the ones to file lawsuits against the federal government, or regulate insurance or agriculture, or implement elections. There are many issues where states have a bigger direct say than the federal government, so Democrats will have more leverage to apply their policies in more places." He said the wins may attract less attention than the higher-level races for Senate and governor but are important for a variety of reasons.

The results also reshape the parties' candidate pipelines for the next cycle. Jacobson said statewide offices are typically held for four years, longer than a seat in the U.S. House, and give incumbents an advantage when they run again in 2030. He called them "typically feeders for higher positions like governor or senator" and said: "The Republicans on election day 2026 lost a whole lot of their ‘bench’ for future higher-office runs, while Democrats massively increased their own bench, especially in red states where they had struggled previously. Potentially, success can feed on success."
