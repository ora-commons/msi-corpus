## IG analysts see divided government as manageable for markets

The market reaction was immediate: the dollar weakened while gold, silver and U.S. Treasury bonds gained on November 4, 2026, following the Democrats’ victory in the United States’ midterm elections. At IG, an investing and trading platform, chief market analyst Chris Beauchamp and chief technical analyst Axel Rudolph tied the move to a reassessment of the political outlook and Trump’s ability to push through his agenda over the next two years.

The overnight futures picture initially pointed to steep losses. Beauchamp said, “Overnight markets had pointed towards steep losses for futures, but the picture is looking rosier as markets seem to look towards the future with relish.” He noted that gold and silver were up and that volatility had reversed course.

Beauchamp said a divided U.S. government has often been the best outcome for markets and that the country was staring that squarely in the face. His assessment provided a more positive interpretation of the market’s direction after the initially negative futures outlook.

Rudolph framed the result through voter discontent. He said, “Midterms rattle Trump, send dollar lower and bullion higher: The heavy Republican defeat is a stark reminder that even a president who dominates the political conversation is not immune to voter discontent.”

Rudolph said the scale of the losses would inevitably raise questions about Trump’s ability to push through his agenda over the next two years, and that markets were already reacting. He tied the scale of the Republican losses to both the immediate market moves and to questions about Trump’s political position going forward.

The dollar and precious metals moved in opposite directions as investors reassessed the political outlook. Rudolph said the dollar was under pressure while gold and silver were benefiting as safe-haven plays.

Beauchamp also focused on the aftermath of the election. He said all eyes were on the White House and that Trump’s apparent acceptance of the results at least suggested a nasty litigation battle, or worse, was off the cards. The assessment retained the qualification that the conclusion depended on Trump’s apparent acceptance rather than an absolute assurance about election litigation.

Rudolph’s final view was qualified rather than absolute. He said a divided government may ultimately prove manageable for markets, but the immediate message was that investors were becoming less comfortable with the political risks surrounding the White House. Together, the analysts’ accounts described a market response that contrasted the possible benefits of divided government with immediate unease about Trump’s political position.
