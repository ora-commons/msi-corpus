## Trump allows H200 sales to China while withholding Blackwells

The Commerce Department issued rules on the morning of November 16 restricting overseas data centers and placing new curbs on Singapore, Malaysia, Thailand, Indonesia and Brazil. The rule is designed to prevent China from reaching advanced computing power remotely and to counter possible smuggling, and it came as questions mounted about how the Commerce Department had allowed loopholes in chip exports, including for Nvidia's Blackwell chips, which up until recently were its most advanced.

Asked whether the rule had a practical effect and closed the loopholes it was intended to close, Liza Tobin said: "Yes, this is a really important rule, and it's a long time coming." Tobin is a former China director for the National Security Council during the first Trump and Biden administrations and is now managing director at Garnaut Global, a geopolitical risk advisory firm.

Tobin said the United States and China are in a race to lead in AI, with compute the key variable. The chips at issue are designed primarily by Nvidia and made by TSMC in Taiwan, she said. After the Biden administration restricted direct exports of the chips to China in 2022 and 2023, a shadow trade developed in which Chinese AI developers started accessing them remotely from data centers in Malaysia, Indonesia and Singapore. That trade was essentially a gray area and technically legal, Tobin said, and drew growing pressure from national security professionals and Congress.

The new rule targets that remote-access channel. It restricts how advanced chips can be used in overseas data centers and places new curbs on the five named countries; the rule is designed to counter possible smuggling. Tobin cited Malaysia, Indonesia and Singapore as places where Chinese developers had reached the chips remotely.

Nawaz also asked whether the rule was an administration effort to save face or would have a real effect. Tobin said Trump was on his way to Beijing and had been anticipating a meeting with Xi Jinping for a long time. She said the rule came amid a disagreement playing out in public inside the administration. Tobin said Trump was "not throwing Jensen Huang or his commerce secretary, Howard Lutnick, under the bus or anything like that."

For months, Trump had said sales to China of the H200, an older-generation but still very good AI processor, were acceptable, while Blackwell chips were not. Up until recently, Blackwells were Nvidia's most advanced chips, Tobin said.

Just before leaving for China, Trump said he was not opposed to former Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang returning to Nvidia. Asked about the risks of their relationship, Trump said: "Jensen, as you know, wanted to sell to China. And the only thing he was able to sell to China were chips that were sort of obsolete, a little bit like our avionics, our aviation equipment. He wasn't allowed to sell them Blackwell or any of that, no." Trump then described a different approach for the trip, saying: "But, no, it's a different approach, just a different approach. We're heading to China. And I don't think you want to insult China just before you get there."

Tobin said Trump's stated policy and the chips China could actually reach had not matched. Trump had drawn a line at H200s while the remote-access route stayed open, she said, and Commerce's rule closed that gap. She also said most of the president's Cabinet members — Secretary Bessent, trade representative Jamieson Greer, director of national intelligence Clayton and many others — had urged the president to issue the rule, while Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick was the lone official she described as wanting to keep the loophole open.

Nawaz pressed Tobin on Trump's statement that the chips that made it to China were largely obsolete and did not put national security at risk. "No, I think the president has probably gotten some bad briefings," Tobin said. She said Jensen Huang had long enjoyed access to the president, "essentially acting like the president's chief technology officer," and had told the president the previous year that the H200s were "kind of not that great of a chip."

Tobin disputed that assessment. "They actually are really good chips," she said. "They're far superior to anything that the Chinese can fabricate themselves at Huawei, at SMIC, at their chipmaking facilities in China." Even though the H200s are not Nvidia's most cutting-edge chips, Tobin said, they are advancing China's AI ambitions, and Trump's position remained that H200s were acceptable while Blackwells were not.

Tobin also pointed to a diversion network. She said there is evidence that shell companies such as Megaspeed, run by Alice Huang, have facilitated the smuggling of Blackwell and other advanced chips into China directly or into third countries. Nawaz noted that Alice Huang is the woman Jensen Huang had admitted to having an affair with, mentioning the relationship as the interview turned to Commerce's handling of smuggling referrals.

On Lutnick, Nawaz said there has been reporting that he wanted to weaken the chip rules because they could affect Southeast Asian data centers represented by his former firm, while adding that much remains unknown. Tobin characterized the episode as regulatory capture: "What this looks like is a severe and massive case of regulatory capture, where Howard Lutnick spent a lot of time with Jensen Huang."

Tobin said it "really looks like" Nvidia had direct influence, blocking different regulations and pressuring the Commerce Department to drop investigations into things like chip smuggling. She noted the department's dual mandate, promoting international trade while enforcing the laws that determine where chips can go, and said information about smuggling and other illegal behavior reached Lutnick on multiple occasions without action from him.

Tobin said Congress is likely to examine the link to Cantor Fitzgerald, Lutnick's former firm, which was benefiting from the Southeast Asian data center trade. She described Commerce's rule as closing a gap that had persisted for months, but the questions she raised extend beyond the rule: whether smuggling information was acted on and whether Nvidia shaped export policy.

Whether Huang returns to Nvidia is also unsettled. Tobin said she thought the company's board had recently issued a statement saying that his return was not its focus and that the board was focused on an investigation. She said Congress was also likely to force more information about what Huang knew and when, what he hid and what he obscured. More information was coming out, she said, and the board did not appear to be indicating that it wanted Huang back anytime soon.
