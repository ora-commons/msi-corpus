## University of Chicago study projects $14.2 billion in annual labeling costs

The White House is moving forward with a proposed definition of ultraprocessed food that would classify about 60% of the U.S. food supply as ultraprocessed, according to people familiar with the matter, with the definition expected to be released soon and subject to a comment period that could change it. Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. has said he plans to require new labels on the front of food packages that reflect the definition.

The measure is a signature piece of the “Make America Healthy Again” agenda. It stalled before the midterm elections after running into resistance from the food industry and some corners of the Trump administration, and President Trump's new chief of staff, Stephen Miller, revived it in recent weeks, people familiar with the matter said. The definition has not changed significantly from what the administration was discussing months ago, but officials have been debating an introduction in which its implications are detailed.

Under the proposal, the new classification would cover foods that contain ingredients such as artificial colors, nonnutritive sweeteners or chemical preservatives, with key exceptions, people familiar with the matter said.

A document viewed by The Wall Street Journal indicates the proposal's introduction is expected to specify that government agencies aren't explicitly saying foods that meet the definition are harmful. It would also note that the definition is solely for research purposes and shouldn't be used for state legislation or regulations, nor in litigation to indicate that there is a single definition of ultraprocessed foods.

The proposal also includes an exemption for additives used for food safety, potentially leaving an opening for some widely used preservatives and other ingredients. It exempts foods such as infant formula and those considered “healthy” according to a separate Food and Drug Administration definition, a list that includes eggs, salmon and plain, low-fat or fat-free yogurt. Those caveats reflect a more conciliatory approach than prior drafts, given the food industry's concerns, according to people familiar with the matter.

White House officials had been concerned the definition could lead to higher grocery prices by prompting changes to recipes or packaging, and that it would give a boost to existing lawsuits against food companies. Big food companies are now facing mounting lawsuits alleging harm from ultraprocessed foods, and they fear that a federal definition could give plaintiffs an advantage.

Food industry groups have opposed the administration's effort to develop such a definition, arguing that it is not based on sound science, will confuse consumers and raise grocery prices. The groups have also argued that existing definitions of ultraprocessed food are overly simplistic and lump together foods like packaged bread with junk foods like cookies and chips.

The Consumer Brands Association, which represents major food manufacturers, has pointed to a recent study from the University of Chicago that found a mandate to label foods as ultraprocessed would cover 72% of packaged foods and 51% of grocery spending. The study said such labels would add $14.2 billion in annual costs for suppliers and consumers, including $60 per household a year in higher food prices.

Kraft Heinz Chief Executive Steve Cahillane said in a recent interview that the reach of any such labeling requirement would be broad. “If you go to the grocery store and start to say, ‘OK, ultraprocessed foods are at risk,’ you’re talking about a very, very high percentage of the entire grocery store in this country,” he said.

Kennedy has publicly cast Miller as his strongest ally in the administration on the agenda. “Next to me, Stephen Miller is the strongest MAHA supporter in the administration,” he posted on social media earlier this month, adding, “It’s time to unleash the Kraken.”

Kennedy has also described Miller's commitment personally. The Wall Street Journal has described Miller as a longtime political adviser and one of the chief architects of Trump's hard-line immigration policies, and reported that Miller and his wife, Katie Miller, have been staunch allies of Kennedy's MAHA movement. While hosting Kennedy and his wife for dinner at his house, Stephen Miller told the health secretary, “I’m the only one in the administration who’s more radical than you are on MAHA,” Kennedy recalled to supporters at a November event. Kennedy said Katie Miller, Stephen Miller's wife, then chimed in: “You have no idea how radical he is on MAHA.”

After Miller was promoted to chief of staff, he made the call to pull the White House's nomination for the head of the Food and Drug Administration, Dr. Heidi Overton, amid uncertainty over whether she had the votes to clear the Senate. Overton, who helps lead domestic policy in the White House, had drawn criticism from MAHA activists who blamed her for supporting the pesticide glyphosate and slow-walking parts of the MAHA agenda. Overton didn't respond to a request for comment, and a White House official said she didn't have the required support for confirmation.

Last week, the National Institutes of Health released a study requested by Kennedy that linked glyphosate, the active ingredient in Roundup, to cancer. The renewed push on the ultraprocessed food definition comes as other bodies, from California to the World Health Organization, are also developing systems for determining which processed foods should be subject to potential regulation.

A White House official said Miller and Kennedy will continue advancing bipartisan MAHA agenda items, and that the Trump administration has delivered major victories for the MAHA coalition, including overhauling the federal government's dietary guidelines. The Department of Health and Human Services didn't respond to a request for comment.
