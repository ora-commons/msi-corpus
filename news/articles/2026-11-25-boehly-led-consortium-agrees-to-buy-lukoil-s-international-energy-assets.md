## Deal swaps $10 billion in securities and awaits OFAC approval

A consortium led by U.S. billionaire Todd Boehly said on Wednesday that it had agreed to buy the international energy assets of sanctioned Russian energy giant Lukoil, giving the buyers a portfolio that spans oil fields, refineries, pipelines and fuel stations. The consortium said no cash is changing hands. According to a person familiar with the matter, the deal swaps Lukoil securities worth $10 billion for assets held by Lukoil's Austrian unit, which houses the international portfolio; the exchange involves restricted Russian shares of Lukoil and other Russian companies.

Boehly's investment arm Eldridge is joined on the buying side by the U.S. International Development Finance Corp, whose mandate is to make investments that advance the country's foreign policy and economic development; Catena Investment Holding, a U.A.E. investment firm; and Xtellus Partners, a New York-based finance firm. The consortium thus includes an arm of the U.S. government alongside private investors.

The portfolio the consortium said it is acquiring includes oil and gas fields, refineries, pipelines and downstream operations spread across multiple countries in Western and Southeast Europe, the Middle East, Central Asia, North and sub-Saharan Africa, and North America. The Austrian subsidiary that houses Lukoil's international operations reported net worth of €19.5 billion ($21.4 billion) at the end of 2024 in its most recent annual report. The Wall Street Journal reported that U.S. sanctions turned Lukoil, Russia's second-biggest oil company, into a pariah on international markets, making the business hard to assess.

The transaction still requires clearance from the U.S. Treasury Department's Office of Foreign Assets Control, along with other conditions. OFAC said in a statement published last year that any deal hoping to gain its approval "must not provide a windfall to Lukoil."

The sale process began after the Trump administration slapped fresh sanctions on Lukoil's assets to pressure Moscow in negotiations over the war in Ukraine, and controversy has marred the protracted process. Last year, an initial deal for Gunvor, a Swiss trading firm, to buy the assets was derailed after the Trump administration described Gunvor as a puppet for the Kremlin. Gunvor, now known as Centalion, has called the allegations false.

In January, Carlyle, a large U.S. buyout firm, beat out Boehly to reach a provisional agreement for the energy assets. Lukoil said at the time that it continued to talk with other potential bidders, a pattern that runs against the usual course of corporate sales, in which companies typically do not announce a sale until they are in exclusive talks. The U.S. Treasury never approved Carlyle's deal, and it expired without explanation, according to people familiar with the matter. The Wall Street Journal reported last year that Boehly was working with Xtellus on a potential bid for the Lukoil assets.

Boehly is part-owner of the Los Angeles Dodgers, a donor to Trump's MAGA Inc. and the head of asset manager and insurance company Eldridge, which oversees $75 billion in assets spanning sports and entertainment and different types of credit. A deal to buy Lukoil's foreign assets would thrust Boehly into the unfamiliar world of energy. His existing portfolio would swell to include gas stations from the Bronx to Sicily, refiners in Europe and oil fields in the Middle East.

The consortium said the deal is structured to stabilize the operations of energy assets in emerging and other markets, strengthen energy security, support reliable energy supply for American families and businesses, and return billions to American taxpayers. Until OFAC clears the transaction, the agreement remains subject to the agency's approval and other conditions.
