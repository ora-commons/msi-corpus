## Lobbying disclosures show continued defense work after Coleman's board appointment

Norm Coleman, the former Republican senator and informal guide to Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth, faces potential conflict-of-interest questions over his simultaneous service on the Pentagon's Defense Policy Board, lobbying for Saudi Arabia and advising Kinetica. The Guardian reported on November 18, 2026, that Coleman's roles place him near defense policy while representing clients with direct stakes in Pentagon decisions. The report said the overlapping work is not illegal, while critics challenged the arrangement's ethics.

In December 2024, Coleman emerged as an "informal yet omnipresent guide" during Hegseth's confirmation process. At the start of a four-hour, 24-minute Senate Armed Services Committee hearing on January 14, 2025, Hegseth thanked "the former senator from Minnesota, Norm Coleman, for his mentorship and friendship in this process." The report said the hearing also examined allegations of sexual misconduct, workplace drinking and marital infidelity. Separately, Republican Rep. Thomas Massie filed articles of impeachment alleging Hegseth committed "high crimes and misdemeanors," including waging war against Iran in violation of the War Powers Act.

Coleman's Pentagon appointment came in June, when Hegseth placed him on the Defense Policy Board. The vice-chair role positioned Coleman to advise on defense procurement decisions of interest to his clients. Outside the board, Coleman chairs the Republican Jewish Coalition, described by the report as a group comprising pro-Israel Republican megadonors, and the Congressional Leadership Fund, one of the biggest super PACs supporting House Republicans. He also serves as a lobbyist for Saudi Arabia and has worked as a senior adviser to Kinetica since the previous year, although his exact role with the company remains unclear.

Kinetica's business interests are tied to Section 219 of the House National Defense Authorization Act, introduced earlier this year. The provision would more deeply intertwine the U.S. and Israeli militaries and commit the United States to bilateral research and development and co-production of weapons, joint ventures and licensing agreements. Mark Dubowitz, CEO of the Foundation for Defense of Democracies, argued in Israel Hayom that the integration was necessary as support for Israel among Americans of all political stripes was rapidly diminishing.

Dubowitz wrote, "Those figures should concern Israel and its friends because, ultimately, public opinion shapes policy." He also advised: "Most importantly, Israel and the United States should deepen their military, technological, intelligence and economic integration to such an extent that inflicting financial harm on Israel would also carry a significant cost for Washington." The Foundation for Defense of Democracies endorsed congressional efforts to mandate U.S.-Israel defense integration, and its board member Isaac Applbaum is chairman of Kinetica. The foundation's stated mission includes enhancing Israel's image in North America, while Israel Hayom is owned by pro-Israel megadonor Miriam Adelson.

Kinetica reported in February that Coleman, speaking in his capacity as an adviser at a conference in Tel Aviv, "shared his perspective from his deep proximity to the U.S. defense establishment." The company said Coleman described the Defense Department's search for new technologies and Israel's opportunity to scale into the American defense ecosystem. Kinetica founding partner Aaron Applbaum, Isaac Applbaum's son, called the opportunity "a generational opportunity to start a new platform" while discussing growing military spending in the U.S., Nato, Japan and the Middle East.

In a LinkedIn post after Coleman's appointment, Kinetica called the board role validation of its business strategy. The company wrote: "As an advisor to Kinetica, Senator Coleman brings a rare combination of policy, leadership, foreign affairs experience and a deep understanding of the U.S.-Israel strategic relationship." Kinetica also said the appointment reflected its thesis that "defense policy, technological superiority and allied innovation are no longer separate conversations."

The Guardian's report said it is not illegal to simultaneously work as a lobbyist and advise the Pentagon. Public Citizen government affairs lobbyist Craig Holman nevertheless called Coleman "a walking conflict of interest on the Pentagon Defense Policy Board" and said most other advisory boards would not allow the appointment of registered lobbyists. Holman said the Federal Advisory Committee Act "just bans individual lobbyists, not those who serve as lobbyists for another firm like Hogan Lovell," and described Coleman's overlapping roles as "self-serving and unethical."

Revolving Door Project founder and executive director Jeff Hauser made a broader argument that people cannot fully separate government work from work for private clients. "Human beings cannot actually firewall parts of their brain from each other," he said, adding that it was impossible for U.S. work not to influence work for Saudi Arabia or defense companies, or vice versa. Hauser said clients such as Saudi Arabia and defense contractors may want paid agents with multiple loyalties, while "the US government ought to reasonably expect for the undivided loyalty of those who serve it."

Before his board appointment, Coleman was senior counsel at Hogan Lovells Cadwalader and lobbied on the firm's account for Hidden Level Inc, which produces drone detection systems. Lobbying disclosure filings said the firm's work included outreach to the departments of defense and homeland security and the White House regarding drone airspace management and security capabilities. Coleman also helped raise the profile of the SkyWater Technology foundry on behalf of Coretsu, an AI and technology company promoting applications for legal and regulatory analysis.

After Coleman joined the Defense Policy Board, he remained listed on Hogan Lovells Cadwalader's lobbying disclosures. Those records showed that he was a lobbyist on at least one account that lobbied the Department of Defense: Bear SVTP Development LLC. The company retained Coleman and two other lobbyists to work on land development involving AI data centers and federal regulation.

Conflict-of-interest concerns are not new for the Defense Policy Board. A Center for Public Integrity investigation in 2003 found that nine of 30 members had ties to contractors with more than $76bn in Pentagon contracts and that four were registered lobbyists. Its chairman, Richard Perle, had resigned; his business relationships with Global Crossing Ltd and satellite maker Loral Space & Communications Ltd drew scrutiny, though a heavily redacted Pentagon inspector general report concluded that he did not violate any ethics laws.

The report also noted broader post-midterm scrutiny of lobbyists. Stephen Miller, Trump's new chief of staff, issued a memo requiring White House staff approval before they meet lobbyists. Coleman, Hogan Lovells Cadwalader and Kinetica did not respond to requests for comment. The Guardian report was co-published with Responsible Statecraft.
