## Rob Lalka says Nvidia bypassed its own investigation process

Nvidia's board is facing criticism over how it handled the crisis surrounding CEO Jensen Huang's alleged affairs, even as Huang has returned to work. The board unanimously decided Huang must leave, then brought him back a few days later, drawing sharply divided reactions from experts on corporate governance and the board's responsibility to Nvidia and its shareholders. Reporter Andrea Hsu gathered those reactions for NPR.

At an all-hands meeting Tuesday, Huang apologized for hurting his family and violating Nvidia's code of conduct, according to a livestream of the meeting viewed by The New York Times. Huang also reportedly said President Trump did not pressure the board to restore him. NPR asked Nvidia to confirm that account and had not received a response.

Board specialists who spoke to NPR said directors are expected to make informed, deliberate decisions in the best interests of a company and its shareholders. In many cases, they are the only mechanism of accountability for companies and chief executives, and many of those specialists said Nvidia's board fell short in this case. Nvidia did not respond to NPR's request for comment about the criticism.

Rob Lalka, a professor at Tulane's Freeman School of Business, compared Nvidia with high-profile cases involving chief executives and employees at Intel, McDonald's and Nestle during the last decade. Lalka said those boards followed their policies and the chief executives left their companies after affairs with employees to preserve company values and credibility.

His criticism of Nvidia was more direct: "The board basically went around its entire process that it had set in place for that investigation to put him back into power."

Betsy Atkins, a three-time CEO, technology entrepreneur and board member who has served on several dozen boards, offered a more favorable assessment. She described Huang as a "genius founder" who built a multi-trillion-dollar company from nothing. "I'm not saying that you get to break the law and break all the rules, but it requires nuanced business judgment to weigh many other factors," she said.

Atkins said Nvidia's board acted too quickly in forcing Huang out. She said the test for board members is whether they have the courage, conviction and character to do the right thing and withstand pressure during difficult conditions, and she concluded that Nvidia's board failed that test. Now that Huang has returned, Atkins said she hopes the board can focus on what she calls "business of the business."

Asked by NPR host Michel Martin what the reversal signaled beyond Nvidia, Hsu said the company's status as a powerful, high-profile company with a large economic footprint gave the episode wider significance. People Hsu spoke with during the week said it reinforces the perception that, in Silicon Valley, all that matters really is money.

Lalka took a harder view. He described the decision to bring Huang back as rash and said it was aimed at boosting short-term profits, which he said does not make for a robust economy. Lalka also noted that AI leaders have repeatedly said they want to do what is in humanity's long-term interests, and he said what unfolded at Nvidia speaks to the opposite.

The competing accounts leave Nvidia's governing process at the center of the episode. At the time of NPR's broadcast, the company had not confirmed Huang's reported denial of pressure from Trump. Hsu's interviews left open whether Huang's stature and business judgment justified the reversal or whether the board had abandoned the standards it set for itself.
