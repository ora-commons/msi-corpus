## Two Voyager 1 instruments run after NASA shuts off experiment

NASA is asking the public on Tuesday night to send Voyager 1's call sign, V-G-R, into the sky in Morse code. Calling it a symbolic celebration, NASA is holding "Shine Bright for Voyager's Light-Day" and is asking people to blink the sequence with a flashlight, phone light or anything that can blink a sequence: three quick flashes and one long flash for V, two long flashes and one quick flash for G, and one quick flash, one long flash and one quick flash for R.

NASA estimates Voyager 1 will reach "light-day status" at 5:16 a.m. ET on Wednesday, after 49 years, two months and 11 days of travel since the spacecraft launched Sept. 5, 1977. A light-day is the distance light travels in 24 hours, or more than 16 billion miles.

Over the course of its mission, Voyager 1 has conducted flybys of Jupiter and Saturn. In 1979, it discovered a faint ring system on Jupiter, and in 1980, the spacecraft took detailed photos of Saturn's rings and moons. On Feb. 17, 1998, Voyager 1 overtook the U.S. space probe Pioneer 10, becoming the most distant human-made object in existence.

Voyager 1 and its twin, Voyager 2, are the only spacecraft to have operated outside of the heliosphere, which acts like a protective shield of the solar system. Voyager 1 reached the interstellar boundary in 2012, and its interstellar mission continues to chart and measure the magnetic fields, particles and plasma waves outside the sun's heliosphere and the atmosphere of other stars.

The celebration comes as NASA works to conserve power on the aging probe. Earlier this year, NASA elected to shut off Voyager 1's Low-energy Charged Particles experiment, or LECP, which has helped scientists map the structure of space, in order to save power. Scientists said this could keep the probe alive longer. Voyager 1 has two science instruments still running: one that measures magnetic fields and another that listens for plasma waves.

Voyager 1 also carries the Golden Record, an interstellar "time capsule" to communicate Earth's story to extraterrestrials. The gold-plated record contains 115 images, natural sounds such as surf, wind and animals, and spoken greetings in 35 different languages, along with instructions, in symbols, on how to play the record and the origin of the probe.
