## U.S.-NABEP agreement outlines discounted oil rights and proposed 35% stake

**Subtype:** fact

- ProPublica reviewed an 18-page strategic partnership agreement that would give the United States a discounted right to purchase 20% of NABEP's oil at $30 a barrel.
- The Trump administration plans to acquire a 35% stake in North American Blue Energy Partners through a Barbados-registered parent company.
- The agreement's $30-a-barrel price excludes the potentially significant cost of importing thinner products needed to blend Venezuela's raw crude for pipeline transport.
- The venture's seven-member board must retain a majority of U.S. citizens at all times.
- The agreement requires five votes to remove Alejandro Betancourt as chief executive or alter the board and does not mandate a Venezuelan government seat.
- The deal remains subject to closing conditions and unfinished “Implementation Documents.”

## No express guarantee for estimated $200 billion in Venezuelan royalties and taxes

ProPublica reviewed a copy of the 18-page strategic partnership agreement that Secretary of State Marco Rubio and Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth signed for the United States. Alejandro Betancourt and Orlando Alvarado also signed, with Alvarado representing the Barbados-registered parent company of North American Blue Energy Partners, or NABEP. The Trump administration had not disclosed Alvarado as a party to the accord. President Donald Trump announced the deal in late August as what he called the “biggest oil deal in world history,” and ProPublica said the full text had been one of the most sought-after secrets in Washington, D.C. The accord grants the United States sweeping access to Venezuela's oil reserves, according to ProPublica's analysis. NABEP spokesperson Terry Wade said the agreement was between NABEP and the U.S. government, not Venezuela.

ProPublica's analysis said the document “reveals carefully constructed terms that favor U.S. interests at every turn and cements the power of a small group of people handpicked by the Trump administration to govern and profit from the deal.” ProPublica also said the terms “favor American interests at nearly every turn and try to enshrine the Trump administration's imprint indefinitely.” The agreement requires no Venezuelan government board seat, and its text largely omits the government's role and the potential benefits to its people.

The agreement's full force will not take effect until both parties satisfy closing conditions, which the document indicates could occur within months. Several detailed aspects of company operations remain under negotiation among the Trump administration, Betancourt and the parent company. The agreement repeatedly refers to “Implementation Documents” that would finalize the proposed U.S. ownership stake and the rights of unnamed investors.

Subject to those conditions, the United States would have the right to purchase 20% of NABEP's oil for $30 a barrel, according to one of three supporting documents reviewed by ProPublica. The rate was less than one-third of what oil had sold for in recent months, and ProPublica described it as a deep discount from fair-market value. The price did not include the potentially significant cost of getting Venezuelan oil out of the country. ProPublica said much of the raw crude was too thick to flow through pipelines unless it was blended with 20% or more thinner oil products, and the cost of importing millions of barrels of blending materials was excluded.

Wade said much of the company's oil came from fields requiring little or no blending and that the costs would not meaningfully affect production prices. “Our barrel economics are strong,” Wade said.

Washington would also receive “first priority” to purchase all of NABEP's remaining oil production for 50 years, including an irrevocable right to buy the company's entire supply if the United States declares a national emergency. The United States would receive “most favored nation” status, requiring NABEP to disclose other business agreements and grant Washington matching terms if those agreements are favorable.

The administration also would acquire its 35% interest in NABEP by purchasing shares in the Barbados-registered parent company. The United States would pay nothing upfront and acquire the position by paying $0.01 per warrant share, with those terms set to expire on the 75th anniversary of the closing date. A Pentagon office has been tasked with executing the financial transaction. ProPublica said the document provides little information about the owners and investors behind the parent company, who could receive enormous wealth from the arrangement. In a White House statement last month, Trump said the shares could be worth “hundreds of billions,” plus dividends.

Trump has said the agreement will secure America's energy dominance for the next century, but ProPublica's analysis said U.S. purchasing rights expire in 50 years and the operating rights appear to run afoul of Venezuelan laws limiting oil concessions to 25 years. A U.S. official said Venezuelan interim President Delcy Rodríguez had granted NABEP 100-year oil concessions to operate there, although the accord did not cite that figure and it appeared to contradict Venezuelan law.

It could be some time before NABEP produces enough oil to fulfill a significant share of U.S. demand. The company currently produces about 200,000 barrels a day, about as much over the course of a year as the United States uses in three days. NABEP aims to increase production to 500,000 barrels a day by the end of 2028, according to recent reports.

The agreement says the U.S. government will work with the parent company to appoint a seven-member board to govern NABEP for an initial 15-year term. A majority must consist of U.S. citizens at all times, and the government may appoint one “non-voting observer.” Betancourt, who would serve as chief executive, is the agreement's only named board member; the initial board would establish the process for choosing new members. No Venezuelan government participation is required.

People with knowledge of the negotiations said the structure was designed to prevent future U.S. and Venezuelan leaders from dismantling the enterprise. A person familiar with the negotiations said, “Throughout the evolution of the deal, it was clear that the governing structure was intended to ensure maximum control by the people making the deal and to insulate them from future administrations seeking to reverse the agreement or call it into question.”

ProPublica said the agreement also offered long-lasting protections for Betancourt and unnamed investors. It requires a supermajority vote of five to oust Betancourt as chief executive, change the board composition or execute transactions with a shareholder other than the U.S. government, an indirect equity holder, a director, an affiliate or “immediate family members.” The board would determine Betancourt's compensation. As structured, the agreement does not appear to require public disclosure of executive pay or the distribution of shares to executives.

The agreement does not identify which shareholders, affiliates or family members may transact with Betancourt's company. ProPublica reported that Barbados corporate law provides almost no mechanism to force those interests into public view. The document does require NABEP to maintain “anti-money laundering controls,” submit to external audits and allow inspections of its records by the U.S. government, and provide the government with an annual budget and quarterly unaudited financial statements. It remains unclear whether those reports will be made public.

Any dispute between NABEP and the United States would be settled under U.S. law rather than Venezuelan law, and Venezuela could not challenge the agreement in its own courts. The agreement directs civil cases and arbitrations to the United States District Court for the Northern District of Texas, where ProPublica said Trump's Justice Department had repeatedly obtained favorable rulings. Texas state law governs where U.S. law does not apply.

Democrats on the Senate Armed Services Committee said they believed the Pentagon office lacked legal authority to execute an agreement with a foreign entity operating in a country under U.S. sanctions. Pentagon press secretary Kingsley Wilson said the Defense Department conducts “extensive due diligence” before extending capital assistance. Wilson declined to discuss the legal considerations or other technical aspects of the transaction “to protect the integrity of the process.”

Last month, four ranking Senate Democrats sent Rubio, Hegseth and Energy Secretary Chris Wright a letter demanding details about the agreement. “It is unclear why the American public should support this attempt to entangle the United States with a relatively unknown foreign oil company that lacks the capacity or credibility to develop oil fields of the magnitude described and whose leadership has faced international criminal investigations,” the senators wrote. They asserted that most of the “oil fields are nowhere near ready for production” and that it could “take up to a decade before global oil markets are meaningfully impacted and the American people see any tangible benefit.”

The agreement does not expressly guarantee that NABEP must pay an estimated $200 billion in royalties and tax payments to Venezuela, although the White House said those payments would benefit the country. One person who reviewed parts of the contract was surprised by the tangible benefits for the United States compared with the lack of binding commitments to Venezuela: “I gave you 20% of my production at ‘cost plus,’ fine. I gave you all of my production if there's a national emergency, OK. … So what do I get in exchange?”

Almost immediately after U.S. special operations forces seized President Nicolás Maduro in January and airlifted him out of Venezuela, Trump sought to restore American access to Venezuelan oil reserves two decades after most U.S. firms had been ejected from the country. By early spring, energy officials, lawyers and lobbyists had begun developing alternatives for the administration's America First energy agenda. One person familiar with the deliberations said officials considered backing a proposal for publicly held American oil companies such as Chevron, Exxon and Conoco to “carve up and rehabilitate” Venezuela's aging oil sector.

Another potential route went through Betancourt's company, which ProPublica described as the second-largest private oil firm operating in Venezuela. The person said working with Petróleos de Venezuela, SA, was never really an option because of its debt and history of corruption. Efforts to enlist large multinational companies quickly stalled: some executives balked at the complexity of returning to Venezuela, while others received little more than “lip service” meetings in Washington and Caracas, according to media reports and people familiar with the deal. “NABEP was given priority,” one person said. “Exxon, Conoco and the others were treated as second class.”

Current and former U.S. officials said Betancourt had served as an interlocutor for Washington with the Maduro government and had ties to some Venezuelan opposition leaders. Reuters reported that Betancourt provided information that helped U.S. forces seize Venezuelan oil tankers and aided strategy and planning before Maduro's capture. ProPublica wrote that “Betancourt's star was rising in the Trump administration.”

Betancourt also developed a relationship with Delcy Rodríguez, whom Trump had backed as Venezuela's interim president, despite an accusation from her brother, National Assembly President Jorge Rodríguez, that Betancourt was corrupt. The accusation came in a televised speech in 2020, according to former U.S. officials and press reports.

U.S. officials also saw practical reasons to partner with Betancourt, several people said. He and his then-business partner in the United States, Harry Sargeant, a Trump supporter, appeared to be a rare success story in a battered industry, operating some of the only active oil fields in Venezuela, according to those people.

A former State Department official who analyzed NABEP's activities said, “The main thing I found promising is that the fields are producing in Venezuela.” The official added, “I think it's particularly important that you buy into fields that are producing. … If your policy goal is to raise production quickly, this makes sense.”

ProPublica identified Betancourt as Venezuelan and reported that he had faced money-laundering investigations in three countries. Partnering with him posed legal difficulties. The 46-year-old billionaire was confined to house arrest on his 300-acre Oxfordshire estate in England while fighting a Swiss extradition request tied to an international money-laundering probe. Betancourt could fly to his $46 million London residence by private helicopter, but his Venezuelan and Italian passports were restricted and British authorities barred him from driving on public roads, according to media reports about the special bail arrangement.

ProPublica said Betancourt's legal problems stemmed from his role in troubled Venezuelan energy projects during Hugo Chávez's presidency. Several of his known or alleged associates were charged or convicted in a major U.S. money-laundering investigation called Operation Money Flight. Betancourt surfaced as a suspect in the U.S. case and remained under investigation for suspected money laundering in Spain, according to former U.S. and European officials and press reports.

Betancourt has not been charged in any country. His lawyer maintained Betancourt's innocence and said he had been unfairly maligned because people associate him with a group of wealthy young Venezuelans, some of whom have been accused of wrongdoing, according to press reports. Venezuelan opposition leader María Corina Machado, whom ProPublica described as the most popular leader of the Venezuelan opposition in exile, described Betancourt as having been involved in “obscene corruption” that caused great suffering for the Venezuelan people, according to a recent New York Times interview.

Many Venezuelans see Betancourt as a “totemic, emblematic, corrupt figure,” former senior U.S. diplomat Brian Naranjo said. “He is not really an oil man,” Naranjo said. “The talent he brings is that he's a new person in a long line of Venezuelan businesspeople who deal in influence. Their big lever is connections and influence. It is know-who, not know-how.”

In a recent interview with Spanish-language media, Rubio defended Betancourt, citing his oil company's work in Venezuela and his support for factions of the Venezuelan opposition in exile. Rubio said a review conducted with U.S. law-enforcement agencies indicated “there were no investigations of him in our system.”

It remains unclear how Betancourt shed the travel restrictions imposed by European authorities. The Washington Post reported that senior Trump administration officials, including Deputy Secretary of State Christopher Landau, former Attorney General Pam Bondi and then-Deputy Attorney General Todd Blanche, petitioned foreign counterparts to allow Betancourt to travel freely and close investigations into him and his business ventures.

Alvarado has held senior positions in multiple Betancourt businesses, including as chief financial officer of an energy firm that became embroiled in scandals a decade ago. According to the Miami Herald, federal prosecutors identified him as an unindicted co-conspirator in a scheme to embezzle money from Venezuela's national oil company. Alvarado has not been charged. Wade said, “neither Mr. Betancourt nor Mr. Alvarado has ever been charged with any crime in any country, and any claims against them are baseless.”

The administration's next challenge was determining how to invest legally. The government described the International Development Finance Corporation as a $200 billion investment agency created for that purpose, but U.S. law barred the agency from operating in Venezuela. At different points through the summer, aides to Office of Management and Budget Director Russell Vought pressed Defense and State Department officials to consider alternative financing paths, according to one person familiar with the deal's progress.

By July, the Pentagon's Office of Strategic Capital, headed by David Lorch, emerged as a potential solution. Lorch, who had moved to the Pentagon from Deputy Defense Secretary Steve Feinberg's private equity firm, Cerberus Capital, quickly embraced the idea, according to people familiar with the deal. Lorch and other Pentagon and State Department officials flew to Caracas in the final days of July and agreed in principle to the venture, with details to be worked out in Washington. A Pentagon spokesperson said Lorch declined to comment.

On Aug. 5, Betancourt met Defense Department officials in a conference room behind the art deco facade of the old Greyhound Bus Terminal in downtown Washington, which houses an auxiliary Pentagon office. Defense Department officials lined up to shake his hand, according to people familiar with the meeting, and some attendees said they were surprised to learn that the U.S. government was preparing to do business with Betancourt.

Betancourt was accompanied by Jacob Hirshman, chief executive and co-founder of Erebor Bank, which people familiar with the meeting described as the first new bank chartered after Trump returned to office. The bank is backed by Trump supporters and investors Peter Thiel and Palmer Luckey and has vowed to reconnect Venezuela to the world economy. Hirshman did not respond to a request for comment.

Lorch told the group, which included dozens of former Wall Street associates he had hired into the office, that the alliance with Betancourt represented a major “opportunity” to secure benefits for the United States and Venezuela for decades to come. The meeting touched off a month of around-the-clock calls and video conferences. People familiar with the deal's progress said Lorch soon became involved in structuring key aspects beyond the financing, including how the board would sit for 15 years and choose its successors. One person said he placed explicit importance on creating a venture that Trump's successor could not undo.

On Aug. 28, the work became public when Trump announced on Truth Social: “The United States of America has just entered into an Agreement with the Country of Venezuela on, THE BIGGEST OIL DEAL IN WORLD HISTORY!” Trump posted on a Friday night. Hegseth, Rubio, Betancourt and Alvarado signed the agreement two days later.

One reason the precise terms remained hidden was the agreement's classification as a “sensitive” government document. ProPublica said that classification commonly shields federal records containing personnel information, details of ongoing law-enforcement operations or attorney-client privilege. The document also contains a confidentiality clause barring NABEP from disclosing the “existence and terms” of the agreement. People with knowledge of the administration's behind-the-scenes decision-making and negotiations with Betancourt spoke on condition of anonymity because they were either not authorized to do so or feared retribution.

One person familiar with the deliberations said people close to the Trump administration seemed keen to partner with Betancourt's company because of the privacy and flexibility it offered. That appeared to give NABEP an advantage over publicly traded oil companies, which would be subject to shareholder-reporting requirements. “For example,” the person said, “Chevron couldn't give 35% of their shares to the Pentagon.” ProPublica said the oil deal was sensitive partly because of likely resistance in Venezuela, where critics had described it as “a U.S. takeover of the country's precious natural resources.”

The White House, State Department, Pentagon and NABEP defended the agreement, saying their public-private venture would provide meaningful and lasting economic benefits for both countries that could not be captured in a legal document. “This historic deal marks a major step toward stabilizing Venezuela's economy after years of hyperinflation and almost no new investment, while more than doubling the United States' oil reserves,” White House spokesperson Taylor Rogers said.

Wade emphasized that NABEP was chosen because of its track record of expanding oil production in Venezuela in recent years. “Thanks in large part to the agreement,” Wade said, “there is a surge in confidence about the future of Venezuela's oil industry and, as a direct result, substantial new investments are flowing into the country.”

Delcy Rodríguez, who won the Trump administration's backing to serve as Venezuela's interim president, praised the agreement as “historic.” She asserted that Venezuela would retain sovereignty over its natural resources and defended the choice of Betancourt.

ProPublica said the newly revealed details were likely to raise questions about whether the arrangement could produce lasting benefits for either country and which private parties might benefit. Former diplomats and congressional staff said the deal was likely to face scrutiny in the United States and Venezuela, especially if Democrats won one or both chambers in the midterm elections. Former Ambassador James Story, who served as the top U.S. diplomat in Caracas from 2018 to 2023, said, “Question number one in a new U.S. Congress will be: Why Betancourt?” He added, “Why did he get this deal? And was he involved in criminality? It seems reasonable to have a congressional hearing about those questions.”


As [MSI previously reported](/articles/2026-10-01-senate-democrats-challenge-pentagon-s-35-stake-in-venezuelan-oil-producer/), Senate Democrats had demanded the agreement's text and legal rationale; ProPublica's disclosure of key terms supplies additional details but does not resolve their questions about the Pentagon office's legal authority or NABEP's production capacity.
