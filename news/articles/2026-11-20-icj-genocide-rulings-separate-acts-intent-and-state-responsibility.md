## Israel faces a 2029 deadline as the Ukraine case narrows

The International Court of Justice has resolved separate questions in its Bosnia and Croatia genocide judgments: whether genocidal acts occurred, whether genocidal intent was established and whether a state was responsible. It found genocide and a violation of the duty to prevent it without state responsibility for genocide in Bosnia, but found genocidal acts without genocidal intent in Croatia.

Genocide under the 1948 Genocide Convention requires intent to destroy a group, in whole or in part. Myanmar's judgment supplies the context for the comparison: the court found that the country's 2017 military campaign against the Rohingya minority constituted genocide and, for the first time, held a state responsible for genocide — what legal scholars call the crime of crimes. The International Court of Justice has been asked to reach that conclusion before, but never found enough evidence to make the determination of a state-sponsored genocide like the one targeting the Rohingya.

The Srebrenica killings were a peak in the violence of Bosnia's 1992-95 war, which followed the breakup of Yugoslavia and a rise in nationalist passions and territorial ambitions across the region. On July 11, 1995, Serbs overran Srebrenica, then a UN-protected safe area. They separated at least 8,000 Bosniak men and boys from their wives, mothers and sisters and slaughtered them. People who tried to escape were chased through the woods and over the mountains around town.

In its 2007 judgment, drawing in part on verdicts from a United Nations war crimes tribunal, the court said the Srebrenica killings amounted to genocide. It did not find Serbia responsible for genocide, but it did find that Serbia had violated its obligation to prevent genocide, a duty enshrined in the 1948 Genocide Convention.

Another war from the breakup of Yugoslavia saw Serb forces attack several regions of neighboring Croatia, killing and wounding Croat citizens. Croatia accused Serbia of controlling the forces responsible, and Zagreb said the attacks from 1991-95 amounted to genocide.

In its 2015 decision, the court found genocidal acts but did not establish genocidal intent. Because the Convention requires intent to destroy a group, in whole or in part, the finding of acts did not amount to a genocide finding.

South Africa's case against Israel is at a different stage. Israel launched a military campaign in Gaza after Hamas' Oct. 7, 2023, attack, in which militants stormed through Israeli communities, killed some 1,200 people and took around 250 people hostage. The International Court of Justice also heard South Africa's request concerning Israel's offensive in Rafah. Much of the Gaza Strip has been destroyed, and the campaign has killed more than 74,000 people, according to Gaza's health ministry, which does not distinguish between civilians and militants.

South Africa has accused Israel of breaching the Genocide Convention during the offensive, a claim Israel vehemently denies. At public hearings, Israel insisted that its war in Gaza was a legitimate defense of its people and that Hamas militants were guilty of genocide.

In a 2024 interim ruling, the court ordered Israel to do all it can to prevent death, destruction and any acts of genocide in Gaza. Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu said the fact that the court was willing to discuss the genocide charges was a "mark of shame that will not be erased for generations." There has been no ruling yet on the merits of the case and won't be for years. Israel has until May 2029 to file written arguments, and only then can the court schedule public hearings. A final ruling could only come months or years after hearings.

Professor Janina Dill, an Oxford University expert on international law and the use of force, said in a statement that the Myanmar ruling "is very instructive of how the Court approaches some of the crucial questions that it faces in other pending genocide cases, including the polarising case of South Africa versus Israel." She said the 9-6 majority in the Myanmar genocide and a number of separate and dissenting opinions from judges "highlight the lingering doctrinal complexities."

Ukraine's case follows Russia's February 2022 invasion, which came after Russia illegally annexed the Crimea peninsula. Shortly after the invasion, Ukraine filed a case accusing Russia of fabricating genocide claims against Ukraine to justify the attack. Kyiv argued that those claims amount to a breach of the Genocide Convention.

The International Court of Justice has already thrown out much of that case. Judges said they have jurisdiction to rule on Ukraine's request for a declaration that Kyiv is not responsible for genocide, but not on other aspects of the case. Like South Africa's case against Israel, Ukraine's case is at an early stage of proceedings, and no final decision on the merits is expected for years.

The earlier holdings therefore remain distinct. The Bosnia judgment found genocide and a violation of Serbia's duty to prevent it without finding Serbia responsible for genocide; the Croatia judgment found genocidal acts without genocidal intent. Myanmar is the one case in which a state has been found responsible for genocide, while South Africa v. Israel and Ukraine v. Russia have not produced final merits decisions.
