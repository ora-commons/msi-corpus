## Senate momentum fades ahead of January control shift

Senate Republicans are suspending their effort to pass a $350 billion additional military spending package as momentum fades to finance the Iran war and other defense priorities before Republicans lose control of Congress in January, according to The Wall Street Journal. The report said the Pentagon struggled to shore up support for the proposal. The department is now working with Republicans and Democrats to identify which multiyear programs can move into the military’s base budget. The shift follows uncertainty over votes in both chambers and comes as Congress faces a Dec. 11 government-funding deadline.

## Near-unanimous support required for reconciliation

The package would have paid for several of President Trump’s military priorities that span several years. It included billions of dollars for a missile shield, ships, aircraft and satellites. It also included billions for replacing depleted munitions stocks. The Pentagon requested the package in April, in addition to a $1.1 trillion annual base-budget request.

Republicans, who hold 53 Senate seats, had planned to use budget reconciliation, which permits some bills to advance with a simple majority rather than the Senate’s usual 60-vote threshold. They would have needed almost complete unity through a marathon amendment process that allowed Democrats to force dozens of votes. The House path was also uncertain because of the package’s cost, the difficulty of corralling the necessary votes, the chamber’s slim majority and postelection attendance problems.

Democrats had made large gains in the House and Senate weeks earlier. The Wall Street Journal described the midterms as occurring amid what it called widespread backlash to the war and high prices.

Sen. Thom Tillis (R., N.C.) said, “We’ve got a lot of work to do because we have competing priorities, and I don’t think we have the votes yet.” Tillis said he would have backed the reconciliation package if he thought it could survive the House.

Some conservative Republicans in the House and Senate objected that the proposal lacked provisions covering some of its costs. Rep. Mike Rogers (R., Ala.), chair of the House Armed Services Committee, warned Tuesday at the Hudson Institute that a $350 billion reconciliation package was “not going to happen.” Rogers said it would have to shrink to about $100 billion. Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth said last week that the Pentagon needed “$350 billion at a minimum for reconciliation” to “make a generational and historic investment” in the military. He also said, “Right now, we’re flooding the zone because this lame duck period is critically important for funding of our department.”

Sen. Rand Paul (R., Ky.), who has opposed the conflict, called the proposal tone deaf and irresponsible, especially in the immediate aftermath of the midterms. “The last thing Republicans want to be remembered by, being in leadership, is spending money, adding to the debt and financing the Iran war?” Paul said. “I mean, it’s completely tone deaf, a huge mistake, and I’m not voting for a pure Republican reconciliation bill that just adds to the debt.”

A poll of more than 79,000 registered voters, conducted for the Associated Press and all the major television and cable-news networks, found that more than six in 10 voters said the United States had made the wrong decision to take military action against Iran.

Some Republicans facing reelection challenges had said they were willing to support the reconciliation package. Sen. Ted Budd (R., N.C.), who is up for reelection in 2028, cited troops at Fort Bragg and Camp Lejeune as Democrats swept North Carolina earlier in the month, flipping a Senate seat and at least six U.S. House seats. Budd said, “We’ve got so much defense in our state,” and, “I want to make sure we take care of them.”

Budd said he wanted the war to end soon. “I want it in the rearview mirror,” he said. “But in order to do so we have to give our men and women in uniform the tools they need, the ammunition they need to make sure they are successful.” Budd and other Republicans said they were motivated to secure the additional defense funding partly because they thought Democrats would refuse to help them pass it on a bipartisan basis. They also said they did not want spending on the Iran war to become a bargaining chip in talks to avert a government shutdown next month.

After hours of private talks on Tuesday, Senate Republicans said they would try to pass military spending with Democrats before relying only on Republican votes. Sen. Mitch McConnell (R., Ky.), who chairs the subcommittee responsible for Pentagon funding, said reconciliation should never have substituted for completing the annual military spending bill. In a statement to The Wall Street Journal, McConnell said, “The way to show we’re serious about defense is to build new investments into sustainable, full-year appropriations,” adding, “let’s get it done.”

## Mapping multiyear programs into annual appropriations

The Pentagon is working with lawmakers to map which high-priority programs that span several years — including munitions, aircraft and Trump’s Golden Dome — can move into the military’s base budget, a senior defense official said. The official said Pentagon officials had met at this point with every member of the Senate Armed Services Committee, Republican and Democrat.

Deputy Defense Secretary Steve Feinberg, a private equity billionaire, made a final pitch for the $350 billion at a closed-door Senate Republican lunch on Wednesday. He stayed for an hour and a half answering Republicans’ questions, invited Democrats to a classified briefing in a secure room elsewhere in the Capitol and returned to Capitol Hill on Thursday. The offer to brief Democrats came after Sen. Mike Rounds (R., S.D.) said at a Navy confirmation hearing on Tuesday that Democrats had been excluded from top-secret threat briefings the Pentagon had given to Republican lawmakers. “Pretty tough to ask all of my colleagues to support that if they’re not even invited in to hear the information in the first place,” Rounds said. He noted that appropriations need bipartisan support to pass in the Senate.

## War funding enters Dec. 11 deadline debate

Federal funding will lapse after Dec. 11 unless Congress acts. At a closed-door lunch on Tuesday, Senate Democrats discussed blocking war spending as a condition of funding the government, but they made no decision, Sen. Chris Van Hollen said. Van Hollen, who recently launched a long-shot bid to unseat the Senate’s longtime Democratic leader, Chuck Schumer (D., N.Y.), has called for shutting down the government over the war.

On Tuesday, Van Hollen said Democrats could accept an amendment vote, at a minimum. “My view is that we, at the very least, need to have an amendment that clearly provides an opportunity for people to vote to not fund the offensive operations in this illegal war,” he said.

Senate Democratic leader Chuck Schumer has not threatened a shutdown, but he promised the day after the election that Democrats would end the war, using Congress’s “power of the purse” if necessary. On Wednesday, Schumer said, “We don’t want to shut down the government.” He added, “It’s up to Republicans to roll up their sleeves and work with us to avoid that from happening. We don’t know where they’re at right now.”
