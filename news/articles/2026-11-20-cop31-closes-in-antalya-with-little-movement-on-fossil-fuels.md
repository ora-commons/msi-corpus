## Climate Action Tracker finds warming progress essentially stalled for five years

Friday marked the official end of the two-week annual COP31 summit in Antalya, Turkey. United Nations negotiators entered their final day still seeking meaningful commitments to address global warming, but the meeting had so far largely failed to advance major ideas for moving away from fossil fuels — mainly coal, oil and gas — which contribute to more intense and deadly heat waves, droughts, floods and other disasters. The negotiations were unfolding amid warnings that only drastic cuts in fossil-fuel emissions could avert the most catastrophic effects of climate change.

A goal introduced this year would increase electricity’s share of total global energy consumption from roughly 21% to 35% by 2035, preferably from renewable energy. The proposal would also allow the use of coal and other fossil fuels.

Top-emitting nations did not participate directly in this year’s summit, which complicated efforts. The Associated Press described the United States as the world’s second-largest polluter after China and the largest polluter historically; the country was absent for the second year in a row.

Leaders from countries ravaged by climate change — mostly poorer, vulnerable countries that contributed little to the problem — said a U.N. fund to help them recover from climate disasters and adapt to a warmer world was not nearly large enough.

A report released the previous week by the independent nonprofit Climate Action Tracker found that progress in slowing global warming had essentially stalled over the past five years.

Climate Action Tracker said that trajectory meant the world was sure to overshoot the goal set in the 2015 Paris Agreement: limiting warming to 1.5 degrees Celsius, or 2.7 degrees Fahrenheit, above pre-industrial temperatures.

Without drastic reductions beyond what countries had already promised, the tracker projected that the globe would end the century 2.6 degrees Celsius, or 4.7 degrees Fahrenheit, warmer than pre-industrial times.
