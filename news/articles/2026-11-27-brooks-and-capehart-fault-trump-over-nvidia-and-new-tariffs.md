## Schumer and Van Hollen face a December 1 Senate majority leader vote

David Brooks of The Atlantic and Jonathan Capehart of MS NOW joined Nick Schifrin for a PBS NewsHour discussion published Nov. 27, 2026. Schifrin framed the conversation around Jensen Huang's return to Nvidia after President Trump intervened, new tariffs and the Democratic fight for Senate majority leader.

Schifrin said Huang had been reinstated as Nvidia's CEO in what he called "an extraordinary announcement before trading hours on Monday morning." Trump acknowledged that he personally pushed for the reinstatement. Schifrin also said Trump had argued that Nvidia was too big to fail and that Huang was too important to Nvidia, so that he needed to stay, and that Trump had been consistent on that. He said he had seen government reach into corporate decisions during Trump's first and second terms, but had never perhaps seen anything as brazen as the acknowledgment that Nvidia's board had to reach out to the U.S. government to be reassured that Huang was not a national security risk.

Brooks said the episode taught a moral lesson: "we care about morals and character and rules and policies, unless we think it's going to hurt the stock price." He called it "a case of pure utilitarianism," said money was valued over morality and gave Trump credit for consistency. Brooks said Republicans were supposed to stand for the idea that "the government does not run the boardroom." He said people who run boardrooms or respond to shareholders know what they are doing and that government intervention is not the government's role. Brooks said Trump had shredded that idea over the previous decade and described the episode as one of dozens of cases in which government acted in the role of "semi-socialist running private enterprise."

Capehart agreed that government should not be involved in private enterprise and said the president's financial interests required scrutiny. He said Trump's financial disclosure forms showed that the president sold between $4.4 million and $12 million worth of Nvidia stock between January and September. Capehart said "with any decision that the president makes, you always have to look, how does it affect his bottom line?" He noted that Trump had not called the matter a national security issue and said the president invoked national security most forcefully "when it comes to things like the ballroom and the arch."

Schifrin said Trump had been consistent for decades on tariffs and had brought back tariffs this week on China, the E.U., Brazil, Taiwan and others. Trump had long defended tariffs as a response to unfair trading practices, Schifrin said. Brooks answered, "No. Well, a little in some cases." He said the tariff policy Trump enacted "raised prices for Americans," "messed up American farmworkers" and did nothing to increase the number of manufacturing jobs in the country, calling it "a complete failure across the board."

Brooks said he was softer on tariffs toward China than on countries such as the E.U. He described China's continued emphasis on manufacturing as a catastrophic decision, noting that countries seeking First World status generally move from manufacturing to services as their middle classes grow. Xi Jinping had instead doubled down on manufacturing and an export-driven path to wealth, Brooks said. China was "just too big," and "You can't be a big South Korea," he said, adding that too many exports seeking too many markets were destroying industries in countries around the world.

Brooks said hurting China and preventing cheap electric vehicles from flooding other markets struck him as "a non-crazy policy." He nevertheless distinguished that goal from economic policy: "that's not an economic policy. That's a global affairs policy."

Schifrin said China had long developed too much and manufactured too much for its own market, flooding other markets, and that this was what Trump argued with these tariffs: overcapacity, excess capacity in industry. Capehart said the excess-capacity argument had some merit — "I mean, I suppose" — but described two years of tariff policy as something neither he nor the administration could clearly explain. At home, he said, the result was "higher prices on everything, state economies decimated because they have lost markets because of the tariff policies." Until the administration could explain the financial benefits to the American consumer and economy and how the policy would level the playing field for everyone, Capehart said, he would remain "mystified by what exactly is happening here."

Schifrin said Democrats had been deciding since the election whether Chuck Schumer of New York or Chris Van Hollen of Maryland would become Senate majority leader, with a vote scheduled for Dec. 1, and he asked Brooks whether Democrats should pick a new face. Brooks called Van Hollen "a 67-year-old new face" and noted that the challenger would be 68 when the Senate met. Brooks said Schumer had won the contest and praised his support for the filibuster, which he said prevented each party from moving toward extremes and forced lawmakers to consider swing voters whose constituencies were at risk of losing. Brooks also said he had "nothing but high regard" for Van Hollen.

Brooks said the one thing he thought Democrats needed to clear up was that "Chuck Schumer is a Jew." He said there had been hostility, "not just to people who support AIPAC and Bibi and all that stuff," and that some of it had spilled over to other things. Citing Josh Shapiro's appearance with Amna on the program a week earlier, Brooks said people on the left side of his Twitter feed had savaged Shapiro for supporting Israel's right to defend itself and were now savaging Schumer himself for his record of supporting the same right. Brooks said he would love to hear Chris Van Hollen say, "this is not about that at all. I find that repugnant." He also said he supported Israel's right to defend itself while not supporting what Netanyahu was doing, a position he said could remove a worrying trend on the left from the race.

Schifrin asked whether the Van Hollen-Schumer race was in some ways a proxy about the question of Israel policy. Capehart said turning the race into a proxy for the Democratic fight over support or non-support of Israel would be a mistake because the majority leader's job was much bigger than one issue. Keeping the 56-seat majority in line and determining how Democrats would respond to the voters who sent them to Washington with a historic majority was the number-one job, he said. Capehart called the contest Schumer's victory and said, "Absolutely, he should run." He then said that when Democrats voted on Dec. 1, Schumer also needed to say that "these will be his last two years because he's not running for reelection."

Whichever senator led the party, Capehart said, the leadership team had to include new, younger voices from states away from the coasts, including South Carolina, Mississippi and Kansas. Those senators had won seats and, he said, needed to carry the majority's message because people behind them were looking for them to do something.

On Russia, Schifrin said Trump had not backed away from describing Vladimir Putin as a friend even as Trump and NATO warned Russia against sabotage attacks in Europe. Brooks said he hoped Congress would push back. He said Democrats and Western Europeans had been pretty strong on Ukraine, while Trump's record was spotty, and he wanted to understand the Putin-Trump relationship, which he had wanted to understand since 2016. Brooks said he had considered whether Trump was a Russian agent or subject to blackmail but mostly did not believe either.

Brooks attributed Trump's behavior partly to the fact that "power is an aphrodisiac" and that Trump loved powerful men. He said Trump sensed a kindred spirit in Putin, whom he described as a nationalist and authoritarian and part of "the archipelago of strongmen." The group Brooks described included Xi Jinping, Putin, Erdogan, Orban and Netanyahu. Brooks said Trump regarded them as his league, but added that Putin and the others were ideological in a way Trump was not. Trump, he said, was transactional and did not understand what they were about.

Capehart said Democrats or Congress would push back. With their majorities starting January 3, he said, Democrats were looking to hold the president accountable and restore foreign policy to some semblance of what it had been when American power was projected overseas.

Schifrin closed by saying he was thankful for his family and the food on his table the previous night. Brooks said he was thankful for the midterms and predicted what he called the biggest vibe shift in American history. He was also thankful for the New York Mets, which he said spent $450 million to acquire one pitcher. Brooks said American culture and life were entering a period in which they would become more normal than they had over the previous 10 years, and he was deeply thankful for that.

Capehart said he was thankful for the American electorate, which had not given up on democracy. Recalling his final thought on election night in 2024, when he wondered whether Americans had abandoned democracy, he said the midterms two years later showed the electorate saying emphatically, "we have not given up on democracy. We are -- we're here to fight for it." He was especially thankful for Black women, whom he credited with working for a different result in 2024, continuing to organize after that election and delivering the midterm results that put Democrats in the House and Senate majorities.
