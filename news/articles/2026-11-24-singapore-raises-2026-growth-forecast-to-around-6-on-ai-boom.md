## Export surge cushions oil shock pressure on corporate and household budgets

Singapore's Ministry of Energy, Trade and Industry raised the city-state's 2026 gross domestic product growth forecast to around 6%, up from a previous estimate of 4.5% to 5.5%, attributing the upgrade to an artificial-intelligence boom lifting production and exports. The revision followed a third straight quarter of growth above 6%, putting the economy on course for its strongest year since 2011, excluding the postpandemic rebound of 2021.

The forecast change places the artificial-intelligence build-out at the center of Singapore's economic performance this year. Singapore has been known as a financial hub, but it is the AI build-out that has put the economy on track for its best performance in years. The city-state has defied forecasts that at one point predicted economic growth of as little as 1% for this year, as spillovers from the AI boom helped offset drags from geopolitical shocks.

On Tuesday, Singapore delivered a third straight quarter of above-6% growth, putting the economy on the path to notch its strongest year since 2011, excluding the postpandemic rebound of 2021. Officials have now raised growth forecasts for the third time this year, predicting an annual expansion of “around 6%.”

The projection stands out against the recent record. Barring the Covid era, Singapore's economy has typically grown at a clip of about 2% to 4% over recent years, and the ministry's estimate of “around 6%” is well above that range.

Much of the resilience comes from Singapore's position in the AI supply chain. The city-state is not a chip-making powerhouse like Taiwan or South Korea, nor a data-center magnet like Malaysia, but has fashioned itself into a hub for advanced semiconductor manufacturing. Official figures show the Southeast Asian city-state accounted for roughly 10% of global semiconductor output last year, 5% of wafer fabrication capacity, and 20% of semiconductor-equipment production.

As AI adoption drives explosive demand for chips, that tailwind has sent Singapore's exports soaring, countering the hit from the war-driven oil shock that squeezed corporate and household budgets.

Beh Swan Gin, permanent secretary at the ministry, said at a press conference that the AI-related boost to production and exports has made the impact from the war less severe than initially projected for both Singapore and the global economy.

How long that impulse lasts remains to be seen, but the consensus is that it will start to fade next year. “While committed AI investments should keep AI-related demand relatively firm in the quarters ahead, the pace of growth in new AI investments is expected to moderate from its high level in 2026,” Beh said. For 2027, officials expect Singapore's economic growth to moderate to between 2.5% and 4.5%.

OCBC chief economist Selena Ling said the economy is in a better position going into next year, but the outperformance of 2026 implies more challenging base effects.

Barnabas Gan, group chief economist and head of market research at RHB Bank, said “Any moderation in AI-related capital expenditure, order books, or end-demand into 2027 could translate into a slower pace of expansion across Singapore's externally oriented sectors.” Gan said in a note that consumer-facing sectors are likely to remain broadly neutral, reflecting relatively subdued household demand and moderating tourism-related spending.

Trade risks continue to pose a challenge. Beh said the government is studying the potential impact from a U.S. move to tighten export controls on advanced U.S. chips. The U.S. Commerce Department's Bureau of Industry and Security announced the measure last week, sparking concern about a potential domino effect that could hamper the AI build-out in Asia and slow an engine of growth for the region's economies.

“Our initial assessment is that [the impact of the rule] will be manageable,” Beh said.

Singapore enters 2027 with ministry guidance for growth of 2.5% to 4.5% and an initial government assessment that tighter U.S. chip export controls will have a manageable effect, after a year in which AI-related demand pushed output toward its strongest year since 2011, excluding the postpandemic rebound of 2021.
