## Third-grade reading tests will determine advancement in 2027-28

Oklahoma lawmakers more than doubled early-literacy funding, but public school districts are still waiting for allocations that are typically released in December or January. District leaders said the timing no longer suits the high-stakes nature of Oklahoma’s literacy laws.

Multiple superintendents said that delivery of the funding, or at least a projection of each district’s allocation, by the time classes began in August would have left schools better equipped to prepare students before the 2027-28 academic year. Third graders’ performance on state reading tests will determine whether they advance to fourth grade in 2027-28.

State lawmakers passed the Strong Readers Act intending for Oklahoma, which scored near the bottom of the country in fourth-grade reading, to emulate Mississippi, which rose sharply in national rankings after enacting similar legislation. Under the law, children who score “below basic” on the third-grade state reading test and fail a second literacy assessment would be held back unless they meet narrow criteria for an exemption. This spring, 44% of third graders scored below basic on the state reading exam, indicating that many were well behind their grade level. The Legislature added $26 million to the Strong Readers Act budget, bringing the total to $43.57 million from $17 million last school year.

The added funds support remediation, diagnostic assessments and learning materials for struggling readers in grades K-3. They also finance teacher professional development and salaries for reading interventionists and instructional coaches. Lawmakers also changed the budget’s distribution, assigning 40% to general reading instruction, 30% to students needing more intervention and 30% as an incentive for districts that show improvement. The revised allocation has made it harder for district leaders to estimate how much each school will receive.

Strong Readers Act funds are typically doled out in December or January because the payment system relies on the annual Oct. 1 count of every district’s enrollment. A Strong Readers Survey also is due Oct. 1, with districts reporting how many students are behind in reading. Tara Thompson, chief communications officer for the Oklahoma State Department of Education, said, “The data will need to be verified before we move forward, but once the reports are verified, payments are sent to districts.”

The Oklahoma State Department of Education collects data on enrollment and student reading deficiencies before distributing Strong Readers Act funds. That sequence means the added funding arrives after most districts have already hired their staff and implemented instructional plans. Stefanick said the extra reading dollars will primarily be put to use in the following school year.

At the Nov. 12 education forum hosted by Oklahoma Watch at Oklahoma City Community College in Oklahoma City, Tulsa Public Schools Superintendent Ebony Johnson said, “Right now our second graders need the support.” She continued, “Our first graders need the support in order to get ready for second grade, and we do not have the resources yet. I know they’re coming, but it’d be great if they were here already in order for us to get the reading interventionists, the reading specialists and all of the additional resources that we need to prepare for ’27-28.”

Tulsa, the state’s largest district, received just under $1.17 million from the Strong Readers Act last year, according to state records, and could see its allocation increase with the added funding.

Steven Stefanick, superintendent of the Harding Charter Preparatory School District in Oklahoma City, said the new distribution makes district calculations difficult. Without knowing its allocation, Harding was able to put reading supports in place based only on what the previous year’s funding levels could afford, he said. State records show Harding received $7,900 from the Strong Readers Act last school year, when the state’s total budget for it was $17 million.

“We’re being asked to do a Mississippi miracle on dollars from last year,” Stefanick said. “How are schools going to hire additional reading supports and specialists and things without that funding being put in place?” Receiving an early estimate of Harding’s annual allocation could have sped up preparations, he said, but pressure instead will mount for 2027-28. “Most districts are just going to pocket the money and wait until August to spend it, but now we just wasted an entire year of a second grader’s academic ability,” he said.

Clinton Public Schools in western Oklahoma has not received notice of its allocation, but Superintendent Nathan Meget said the delay has not caused problems. The district already provides reading coaches from the elementary level through seventh and eighth grade, a longtime goal for the district. Meget said the added funding “will definitely help us out,” but that “as of now it has not affected us in a negative manner.” Clinton received $59,388 in the 2026 fiscal year, according to state records.

In Tulsa County, Jenks Public Schools collected $178,405. Superintendent Stacey Butterfield has been suggesting to state lawmakers that, if the literacy law has changed, the timing of the corresponding funds should change too. She said students now face possible retention, so “we need the funding earlier.”

“We need that funding at the beginning of the year so that we can hire the reading interventionists, so that we can hire the reading specialists, so that we can begin intervening and remediating student deficits in reading at the beginning of the year because they are going to be held accountable,” Butterfield said. “We’ve got to be able to work earlier.”

Other state education money is paid sooner. The state aid funding formula, the largest source of state funds for public schools, provides an initial allocation at the beginning of the year based on the previous year’s numbers and is adjusted later once enrollment reports are finalized.

House Speaker Kyle Hilbert, R-Bristow, an author of the literacy legislation, said he was open to changing when reading funds are paid or when projections are provided to districts. He said the Oklahoma State Department of Education could adjust its administrative policies to move up the timeline, or the Legislature could change state laws. Hilbert said he was not concerned that the current system would negatively affect implementation of the new law and that there was “no chance” he would consider postponing student retention from taking effect. “If they got the funds a couple of months quicker, would it change anything? It shouldn’t,” Hilbert said. “Because at the end of the day, the job of our public schools is to educate our kids, and reading is essential to that. So, teaching kids to read should be the fundamental job of every elementary school in the state. And so, if they’re not doing that, then what are they doing?”
