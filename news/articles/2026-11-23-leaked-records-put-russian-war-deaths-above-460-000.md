## Territorial gains remained limited as losses accelerated

An analysis by The Guardian of more than 11 million death certificates obtained from Russian civil-registry records identified more than 460,000 deaths linked to the war in Ukraine between February 2022 and September 2026. The Guardian described the analysis as the most detailed picture yet of Russia's nearly 500,000 losses since 2022. The records show Russian losses accelerating sharply in 2025 while territorial gains remained limited. They also identify war as the leading cause of death among Russian men under 65 in 2025.

Alexander Korepanov signed up to fight in August 2023. He was wounded twice and sent back to the front both times, and on 11 May 2025 he was killed in the village of Uspenovka near Pokrovsk at age 52 after 20 months at war. More than 5,000 Russian soldiers died that week. The army was losing 720 men every 24 hours, the highest rate of the war, and at least a third were killed around Pokrovsk, where Moscow had committed tens of thousands of troops to its spring offensive.

The figures came from death certificates obtained by Cyber Partisans, a hacking group linked to the Belarusian opposition, and the Ukrainian hacker collective Silent Crow. The Guardian said the records were compiled by Russia's state statistics agency, Rosstat, through civil-registry offices across the country. The analysis included certificates classified as war-related or mentioning conflict as a cause of death, as well as all deaths in which employment was recorded as military. It excluded certificates from people outside combat age, and some civilian deaths may be included.

The count is likely too low. Deaths of missing soldiers are often registered months or years later, when bodies are recovered or courts declare the men dead. The database does not capture every death: some were never registered and some soldiers remained missing, and the records thin sharply in 2026 despite continued heavy fighting. The Guardian also reported that the leaked data showed more than 1,000 Russian military suicides during the Ukraine war. “There was always sort of an unknown X factor: how many Russian deaths are we missing? And that turned out to be much much more,” said Janis Kluge, a Russia expert at the German Institute for International and Security Affairs who tracks military deaths. Militaries typically expect about three wounded for every soldier killed, but Kluge said the data suggested Russia could have more soldiers killed in action than severely wounded. “This tells you something about this war and the way that it’s fought,” Kluge said.

The records show the war grew steadily more lethal, peaking in 2025. Almost 20,000 deaths were recorded during the war's first year, followed by 50,000 in 2023. The toll more than doubled in 2024 to more than 134,000 and rose another 52% in 2025 to close to 200,000. The 2025 total averaged about 548 men a day, compared with about 277 since the invasion began. The Guardian said the scale exceeded losses suffered by Russia or the Soviet Union since the second world war. Official figures put Soviet military deaths in Afghanistan at about 15,000 over nine years, including roughly 2,300 in 1984, the deadliest year; Russia was losing that many men roughly every four days in Ukraine in 2025. In the week beginning 5 May 2025, the Russian army lost more men than the United States did in eight years in Iraq, where about 4,500 American troops died. About 2,400 US service members died over two decades in Afghanistan.

Since the start of 2023, Russia gained about 8,000 square kilometers, less than 1.5% of Ukraine's territory, according to the Ukrainian monitoring group DeepState. The Guardian said that, with the front barely moving, Russia turned to destroying Ukraine's economy and making life in its cities unlivable to pressure Kyiv to accept Moscow's peace terms. Russia's defence ministry has not updated its official death toll since September 2022, when it said 5,937 service members had been killed; Moscow treats its casualty figures as a military secret.

The certificates record where most soldiers died, down to the village, from mid-2023 onward. The records also show what Russia paid for every kilometer gained on a handful of narrow fronts, above all in the slow advance through the fortified mining towns north-west of Donetsk. Almost 116,000 deaths occurred in Pokrovsk district, more than a quarter of all deaths with a known recorded location. The district includes Avdiivka and Pokrovsk, scenes of two of the war's longest and bloodiest battles. Pokrovsk was a coal-mining town of 60,000 people before the war and covers less than 30 square kilometers; more Russian soldiers died in the surrounding district than Ukrainians lived in the city itself.

After Avdiivka fell in February 2024, Russian troops took 21 months to cover 39 kilometers to the edge of Pokrovsk, according to the Institute for the Study of War. During that time, about 88,700 Russian soldiers were recorded as dying in the district, more than 2,200 for every kilometer gained. By September 2024, Russia's gains formed a bulge of fields, slag heaps and mining villages about 30 kilometers long and 20 kilometers wide.

Recorded deaths in Russia's Kursk region rose from 12 in July 2024 to close to 1,200 in August, when Ukraine launched a cross-border incursion, and exceeded 2,400 in December as Moscow fought to retake the area. The Guardian said the losses were eroding Russia's ability to mount new offensives and could help explain why the army looked set to fail Vladimir Putin's order to capture the whole Donbas region by the end of the year. “If we compare the death data with Russia’s official recruitment rates, it becomes clear that Russia’s military has a manpower problem,” Kluge said. “The absolutely horrible losses combined with slower recruitment rates apparently forced Russia to slow down its attacks in 2026 already,” he added.

The military's age profile also shifted as the Kremlin avoided a second mobilization. The average age at death rose from 34 in 2022 to close to 40 in 2026. Almost one-fifth of those killed in 2026 with an age recorded were over 50, compared with 6% in the war's first year; men aged 35 to 44 made up a third of the dead across the war. The first mobilization, ordered in September 2022, seven months after the full-scale invasion began, was deeply unpopular. Instead, the Kremlin relied on cash bonuses that often appealed most to older men such as Korepanov. In 2018, Putin spoke of Russians looking forward to a “well-deserved retirement,” when they could devote more time to their families, children and grandchildren. Eight years later, more and more older Russian men are dying on the battlefields of Ukraine. From the start of the war, the Kremlin sought to keep the cost out of sight in Moscow and St Petersburg, where discontent posed the greatest political risk.

The Guardian said the losses were deepening a demographic crisis the Kremlin had struggled for years to reverse, with consequences likely to be felt for generations. In 2025, war became the leading cause of death among Russian men under 65. Of certificates with a recorded cause, 29% mentioned war, 26% cited circulatory diseases and 12% cited external causes such as accidents, assaults or medical complications. More than half a million Russians had also left the country since the invasion. “We’ve been predicting demographic doom for Russia for a few decades, and somehow the catastrophe never materialised,” said Judyth Twigg, a professor of global health at Virginia Commonwealth University in the US. “But the current situation really does look qualitatively different … Today’s losses are a direct consequence of regime policy: combat deaths, political emigration, foregone births, healthcare budgets raided to fund [military] signing bonuses and death payouts.”

The military death burden fell most heavily on poorer, remote Russian regions, many with large ethnic minority populations. The eight regions with the most poverty recorded military death rates four times those in the least-poverty regions: more than 10 deaths per 1,000 men aged 18-69, compared with fewer than 3 per 1,000. At least 19 regions, most in the far east, recorded war as the cause of more than half of deaths among men under 50 in 2025, based on place of birth and records in which a cause of death was given. In the Jewish autonomous oblast, an eastern Russian region bordering China where about a fifth of residents lived in poverty in 2022, war was listed as the cause of more than three-quarters of recorded deaths among men under 50 in 2025; despite the region's Soviet-era name, less than 1% of its population was Jewish. Zabaykalsky Krai, another region bordering China thousands of miles from the front, recorded the highest rate: since the war began, 32 men aged 18-69 born there had died per 1,000 population, compared with one per 1,000 in St Petersburg and Moscow, where less than 5% lived in poverty; about one in six residents of Zabaykalsky Krai lived below the poverty line.

As losses mounted, Moscow recruited heavily among central Asian migrant workers inside Russia, offering fast-track citizenship in exchange for service, and recruited through middlemen abroad as far away as Nepal, India, Cuba and Kenya. The Guardian said many recruits were promised high pay, Russian citizenship or civilian jobs before reaching the front. The analysis identified more than 7,300 probable foreign soldiers among the dead, excluding Ukrainians. Of those, 2,700 were born abroad but had no recorded citizenship, and more than three-quarters came from former Soviet republics, mainly Kazakhstan, Uzbekistan, Tajikistan and Belarus. The analysis found fighters from 78 other countries beyond the former Soviet Union, including 440 with African citizenship, such as Nigeria, Cameroon and Egypt, and about 200 from South America, most from Colombia and Cuba.

The records miss what is likely the deadliest foreign contingent. North Korea sent more than 10,000 troops to help Russia drive Ukrainian forces from the Kursk region in late 2024 and 2025. South Korea's national intelligence service estimates about 2,000 North Korean troops have been killed and about 6,000 killed or wounded in total. The foreign soldiers arrived late. Of those whose date of death is recorded, three-quarters were killed in 2025 or later. Every Colombian death with a recorded date falls within that period, as recruiters turned to Latin America to make up for losses at home.

What the scale of the losses will mean for Russians' attitudes toward the war in the long run remains unclear. Interviews found that many relatives of the dead said their grief had only hardened their support for the war. Several insisted Russia must fight on so the sacrifice would not have been in vain. The Guardian reported that state propaganda has cast the fallen as heroes and presented their deaths as a reason for Russians to unite behind Putin and the army. The few people who once spoke against the war have mostly been jailed or forced to leave the country. The last group publicly protesting against the war, Put Domoi (Way Home), a movement of wives and mothers of mobilized men, was criminalized in 2024. One formerly outspoken member said she now feared for her life. “I don’t have emotions any more; I am numb at this point. Just a question,” she asked rhetorically. “Is it worth it?”
