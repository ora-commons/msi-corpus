## A special session could come before Mississippi legislative candidate filings

Republican Rep. Noah Sanford of Collins and Republican Sen. Dean Kirby of Pearl are close to finishing Mississippi's proposed legislative districts. The two lawmakers told Mississippi Today they have been meeting individually with other lawmakers to show them the proposed maps.

They said they are prepared to convene for a special session whenever Gov. Tate Reeves calls one. Kirby described the current status this way: “We’re actually on hold waiting for the governor to call a special session,” Kirby said. “We don’t know anything, or even whether he’s going to call one or what he’s going to call it on.”

Reeves' office did not respond to a request for comment. Reeves recently told WLBT that he still has “every intention of there being a special session for redistricting.”

The governor could call a special session to redraw state legislative, congressional and/or state Supreme Court districts. Sanford and Kirby said they do not believe there is an urgent need for a special session focused on congressional redistricting because lawmakers can address that issue during the regular session, which begins Jan. 5.

The legislative calendar is tighter. The filing period for candidates seeking a legislative seat begins Jan. 4, one day before the regular session. If lawmakers wait until the regular session to redraw legislative districts, candidates could file paperwork under districts that the Legislature is still changing, which could create confusion.

A federal three-judge panel ordered the Legislature in 2024 to redraw its legislative districts. Lawmakers complied in 2025, but the U.S. Supreme Court later overturned the panel's order.

After the Supreme Court overturned the panel's order, lawmakers formed a joint redistricting committee. The committee conducted public hearings across Mississippi during the summer and fall to gather input.

The push in Mississippi and elsewhere was set in motion by the U.S. Supreme Court's Callais decision. News From The States reported that the ruling removed protection for majority-minority districts and cleared the way for state legislatures to redraw political lines for partisan advantage. Sanford and Kirby, the Republican leaders leading the effort, said they believe the ruling ended the practice of allowing race to be considered when drawing political maps.

Some Mississippians told the committee they wanted the Legislature to redraw districts that were “race neutral.” Most people who spoke at the hearings said they did not want legislators to redraw the districts.

Some Democratic politicians speculated that the hearings caused a surge in Black voter turnout in the recent U.S. Senate election, in which Democrat Scott Colom ousted Republican Sen. Cindy Hyde-Smith.

Sanford said the redrawn House map he is proposing will not look radically different from the current map. His priorities include putting small counties back together and reducing the number of split voting precincts.

Kirby said his priorities for the Senate map are ensuring that districts are compact and have roughly equal populations. The committee, he said, is looking at how districts voted in the last three statewide general elections rather than race.

At the time of the report, Sanford and Kirby were waiting on Reeves's decision about whether to convene and what the session would cover. Their proposed legislative maps were close to completion, but the session and its scope remained unsettled.
