## Lautz puts owner net worth near $460,000, renter net worth just over $10,000

Jessica Lautz, deputy chief economist and vice president of research at the National Association of Realtors, said the U.S. housing market is split between repeat buyers who can pay cash and first-time buyers struggling to enter it. Nearly one in three repeat buyers purchasing a primary residence are making all-cash purchases, Lautz said, while first-time homebuyers are "desperately trying to enter into the market." She called it "a very divided housing market right now" in an interview with PBS NewsHour on Nov. 13.

Stephanie Sy set up the problem by noting that starter homes in a lot of cities in America start at a million dollars and that some buyers are able to pay cash, and asked how a first-time homebuyer competes with that. Lautz said that is the crux of the issue. "We know we have a K-shaped economy, but, frankly, we have a K-shaped housing market," she said.

Nearly one in three repeat buyers purchasing a primary residence are making all-cash purchases, Lautz said, taking money from housing equity they have earned and combining it with financial assets to complete the purchase. She contrasted that with first-time homebuyers "desperately trying to enter into the market," who she said are having a very difficult time doing so.

The split has consequences, Lautz said. Homeowners are "building generational wealth really through homeownership," she said, and the net worth of an owner is now nearly $460,000, while the net worth of a renter is just over $10,000. Lautz said she expects the divide to keep growing as mortgage holders pay down their mortgages and their homes gain value, while renters are struggling to invest in the stock market and in real estate.

Sy asked whether the voices in the segment described a broken housing market — harder to sell and still hard to buy — and Lautz agreed. For homebuyers today, especially first-time homebuyers, she said, "it is very difficult to be able to find and afford a home and save for a down payment with many other expenses that Americans have today."

On supply, Sy asked how much of the crunch came from people holding on to homes bought at the super low interest rates of 2 to 3 percent seen for years after the Great Recession, versus a lack of inventory that can meet demand. Lautz said it is both. "We know that we have been underbuilding homes since the Great Recession," she said, and builders have headwinds bringing new properties into the market, whether it is labor, the cost to do so, the cost of land or regulations. The other part of the problem is people holding on to their homes. "They have no motivation to move if they're in a low interest rate mortgage and they're looking at the future cost of a mortgage, the cost of homes," Lautz said. Even though owners could translate that with a lot of housing equity and a large down payment, many of them are unwilling or unable to make that move, she said.

Lautz expects interest rates to "stay about where they are right now," which she called a headwind for homebuyers. She said the West region is hard-hit by the cost of homes, that the Midwest has slightly more housing affordability though it is not universal, and that a household income below $75,000 means a person is "going to probably have a very hard time finding a property that you can afford" in most areas of the country.

Asked what a first-time homebuyer can do in this environment, Lautz pointed to what successful first-time buyers did this year: cutting spending on luxury items and goods, taking a second job, moving in with family so they could avoid rent for a temporary time period and save for a down payment, getting their financial house in order, looking outside that ideal area, and taking advantage of government loans such as an FHA loan or a VA loan. She noted that first-time homebuyers today have higher incomes and are wealthier than has historically been the case.

The segment also gave voice to three people trying to buy or sell a home. Chester Carl Bostek of Montrose, Colorado, said he is not trying to sell his home, for health-related reasons. Montrose sits at an elevation of about 6,000 feet, Bostek said, and his pulmonary issues have continued to get somewhat worse; it is harder to breathe there than at sea level, so he wants to relocate. Bostek said the market had "gone very badly"; interest rates were up, he thought, at 7.5 percent for a new fixed 30-year mortgage, and that being the case, he had not tried to sell yet. Bostek said he is exploring the possibility of encouraging a VA-eligible buyer to take over his VA loan, which is at 2.5 percent. He said he hopes the housing market will improve and that "there's got to be some increase in income and relief from some of the affordability questions that have been facing us." "There needs to be relief from those factors that drove the election results," he said.

Leslie Shipman of Ridgewood, Queens, said she does not want to leave New York. She and her husband are solidly middle-class people who have been renting in the town for decades and have decided to buy so they do not spend their retirement moving from one apartment to another, setting aside some savings for a down payment. "We can't afford to do it in New York," Shipman said. She said she is looking at other medium to large mid-Atlantic and East Coast cities, has a realtor and has started identifying places the couple can afford. Sellers, she said, are somewhat nervous about putting their places on the market right now. "It's a buyer's market because of interest rates. So it's just a confusing time," she said. "Our biggest thing is trying to control what our monthly housing costs will be." Shipman said programs for lower-middle-class working folks matter to her, adding: "I do kind of feel like the middle class frequently is overlooked."

Christian Lien of Des Moines, Iowa, said that within months of moving they decided the neighborhood was not for them, but that was over five years ago. Lien said what they got prequalified for "plummeted," leaving little in terms of availability while housing prices went up. Lien estimated, based on appraisal, that their house has increased in value by about 40 to 50 percent since purchase — "something that's helping and hurting at the exact same time," Lien said.

The interview came against the backdrop of a National Association of Realtors report cited by PBS NewsHour's Amna Nawaz at the start of the Nov. 13 broadcast. In October alone, Nawaz said, sales of existing homes dropped to 3.75 million, an 8.8 percent drop compared with the same time last year and their lowest level in more than 16 years. Sy reported that high mortgage rates are a key part of the decline. "They haven't been this high in nearly a generation," she said. "As of today, the average rate is 7 percent for a 30-year fixed mortgage." Sy added that even though prices are dropping, in some markets homes are still unaffordable for a lot of folks. Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis data from [FRED](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/MORTGAGE30US) put the 30-year fixed mortgage rate at 7.11 percent as of Nov. 13.
