## John Healey announces £2bn Energy Wealth Fund

**Subtype:** fact

- UK Chancellor John Healey announced an Energy Wealth Fund funded with £2bn diverted from the existing National Wealth Fund.
- The UK government has promised to divert new oil and gas revenues into the Energy Wealth Fund.
- Treasury officials modelled that investing the UK's historical £200bn in North Sea receipts without withdrawals, at a rate comparable to Norway's, could have produced a fund worth about a trillion pounds.
- UK Chancellor John Healey paired the fund announcement with approval for the Jackdaw gas field.
- BBC News reported that Burnham defended the Jackdaw decision and that Badenoch urged the prime minister to overturn the ban on new oil and gas licences.
- The UK government plans to let the Energy Wealth Fund invest domestically in green energy, while Norway's sovereign wealth fund is required to invest abroad.

## Treasury modelled what decades of North Sea receipts could have generated

UK Chancellor John Healey announced the creation of an Energy Wealth Fund on Wednesday, alongside approval for the Jackdaw gas field in the North Sea, with £2bn of funding diverted from the existing National Wealth Fund. "The proceeds of this oil and gas coming ashore now can be invested for the future generations," Healey said. BBC News described the move as giving the United Kingdom an oil fund after a half-century wait.

BBC News reported separately on the political reaction to the Jackdaw decision: Burnham defended the decision, and Badenoch urged the prime minister to overturn the ban on new oil and gas licences.

The announcement came in a week when the UK's national debt hit £3tn for the first time. BBC News noted that a selection of energy-exporting countries, mainly in the Gulf and also including Norway, have over the years amassed multi-trillion funds using oil and gas revenues. The contrast sits within a long-standing debate in UK economic policy circles over why the UK did not build a comparable fund.

## The £2bn transfer and the new-revenue pledge

The fund starts with a transfer rather than a stream of receipts: £2bn is being diverted from the existing National Wealth Fund. Separately, BBC News reported, the government has promised to divert new oil and gas revenues into the Energy Wealth Fund — a commitment distinct from the initial diversion from the existing fund.

BBC News also reported that UK energy tax revenues are forecast to dwindle as North Sea production slows, although Jackdaw and extensions to existing fields may ease the rate of decline. BBC News noted that nobody expects the Energy Wealth Fund's £2bn to be worth £2tn by 2056; the trillion-pound figure belongs to a different exercise, on what might have been, had a similar, far-sighted idea been adopted in Britain decades past.

## How Treasury reached the trillion-pound figure

On Wednesday, for the first time, Treasury officials calculated how much the missing British fund could have been worth. The trillion-pound number comes from internal Treasury modelling, which assumes that the UK's historical North Sea receipts of £200bn had been invested with no withdrawals and had grown at a rate comparable to Norway's. "If we had done this when North Sea oil was first discovered in the 1980s, we'd have a British Sovereign Wealth Fund now that would be worth probably a trillion pounds," Healey said.

An insider told BBC News: "Norway demonstrates the power of long-term investment: the government wants to learn from that principle, rather than copy its model wholesale." The insider called the absence of systematic investment a "missed opportunity" and said "had we systematically invested those receipts, we could have built a huge national asset for future generations".

BBC News described the exercise as a giant "What if?" asked often by economists critical of the Margaret Thatcher era. In their view, the peak oil revenues were frittered away in pre-election tax cuts, which ended up being spent on 1980s garden conservatories. Others might argue, BBC News noted, that later governments spent the revenues on public spending. BBC News also noted that Norway's fund only began in earnest in 1996.

The idea of a lost oil fund has featured in arguments from supporters of Scottish independence, and BBC News reported that it was suggested by some government advisers in the 1970s.

## Norway's fund and the limits of the comparison

Norway's sovereign wealth fund has amassed $2.4tn from the same North Sea resources. Three percent of its value is drawn down each year to finance between a fifth and a quarter of government budgets, and Norway drew on the fund during the coronavirus pandemic. The fund is meant to help finance the country's post-oil economy and society, as do some equivalent Gulf funds.

The starting investment in Norway's fund 30 years ago was 2bn Norwegian kroner; its current value is more than a thousand times that amount. The fund's investments in US big tech have helped it grow hugely, and its rules require investment abroad. BBC News reported that officials running the fund are sometimes criticized by people on Oslo commuter trains who can check the live value of the national fund.

The UK's plan is structured differently. The UK's plan is for a fund that can invest domestically in green energy, while Norway's fund is obliged to invest abroad, and BBC News assessed that the UK fund may struggle to match the performance. The comparison also extends to company control: BBC News reported that Norway's state still controls its oil companies, giving it a share of their profits.
