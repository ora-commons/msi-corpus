## Parkgoers have sent more than 17,000 photos to Save Our Signs' online archive

University of Minnesota librarians Molly Blake and Jenny McBurney started Save Our Signs within months of President Donald Trump's March 2025 executive order on American history, after Blake worried about the information that might be lost as signs came down. Blake, a social sciences librarian, and McBurney, a government publications librarian, discussed with university colleagues what they could do: even if they could not prevent signs from being taken down, they could archive them for posterity. Within a few months, they launched Save Our Signs, a nationwide effort to document and preserve interpretive signs. The project asks park visitors to photograph signs still standing at National Park Service sites and to report signs already taken down, building a public record of what has changed. Members of the public have sent Save Our Signs more than 17,000 photos shot at 428 sites, from Ellis Island and the Japanese American internment camp at Manzanar to monuments dedicated to Emmett Till, whose killing helped spark the Civil Rights Movement.

In January, park rangers removed 34 interpretive panels from a slavery exhibit at Independence National Historical Park in Philadelphia. The signs described the lives of the nine people George Washington enslaved at the presidential residence there, and the removals, in some cases leaving the dried glue in place, were made to comply with Executive Order 14253, titled "Restoring Truth and Sanity to American History." The order calls for the removal of signs that "inappropriately disparage Americans past or living" from the country's national parks.

Blake and McBurney could not visit all 63 national parks, or the hundreds of historic monuments and sites overseen by the National Park Service. "We knew we had to get the public involved," Blake said, so they invited parkgoers to photograph signs for an online archive; the reporting described the response as overwhelming. People have also sent reports and photographs from parks where signs have already been removed, and those submissions become part of the archive's "Censorship Tracker." The parks and land to cover span more than 85 million acres across 50 states, and which site might be affected next remains unknown.

"One of the signs that was removed just noted that it's a good idea to refill your water bottle because it's better for the environment than using plastic," Blake said. "I wouldn't have guessed that that would have been targeted, but it was." Because of the uncertainty about what comes next, Save Our Signs encourages parkgoers to photograph everything. McBurney described what the project is hearing from supporters: "The folks that we hear from are saying things like they love our parks, they support our parks, they want to maintain the really high-quality educational materials they're seeing there. They want us to remember our history, the good and the bad."

Alan Spears, senior director of cultural resources at the National Parks Conservation Association, said the removals are not announced publicly. "They're not hiding it," she said. "They're also probably not putting out press releases saying that this stuff has happened. You can just show up on Monday or Tuesday of this past week, [and] the signs are gone."

Since the order was issued, park employees have removed or modified more than 100 signs at 41 National Park Service sites, according to the reporting. The affected sites include California's Muir Woods National Monument, where signs mentioned climate change and the park founders' connections to anti-immigrant policies, and Wyoming's Grand Teton National Park, where a sign drew attention to explorer Gustavus Cheyney Doane's participation in a massacre of more than 150 Indigenous people. The Interior Department, which includes the National Park Service, relies on park rangers and other employees at each site to monitor signs and instructs them to look for signs that may not adequately remind parkgoers of the country's "unmatched record of advancing liberty, prosperity, and human flourishing," as the order puts it. Park staff are expected to remove or alter such signs within 120 days of the executive order.

A confidential Park Service database, leaked in March and containing internal communications among park staff, showed rangers working out what the order requires. One ranger asked whether signage about the return of California condors from near-extinction disparaged hunters, and another wondered whether signs about graffiti at Utah's Arches National Park violated the directive to emphasize the park's "natural beauty." The leak also provided Save Our Signs with a list of parks where signs might be targeted for removal if they had not been removed already. "Right now, we have on our website a map of parks that, based on the leaked dataset of what might be targeted, are parks that are priorities for us," Blake said. "It was called out on this leaked spreadsheet as a place that there might be censorship happening. We really want more photos there."

The executive order and Secretarial Order 3431, which Interior Secretary Doug Burgum issued to implement it, both bar "inappropriate disparagement" of Americans. The reporting raised questions about whether signage at a Till site could disparage the men who tortured and lynched him and whether signs at civil rights monuments in Birmingham, Alabama, and Little Rock, Arkansas, could disparage the white supremacists who opposed the Civil Rights Movement. "Inappropriate disparagement" of Americans is "a really confusing turn of phrase," McBurney said. "Especially in the early days, nobody knew what that meant."

Secretarial Order 3431 also directs parks to post notices telling visitors how to report supposed violations, an effort the reporting describes as largely unsuccessful. The QR-code-enabled notices, prescribed in a section of Burgum's order titled "Encouraging Public Participation," were soon dubbed "snitch signs" by detractors, thousands of whom submitted sarcastic comments and jabs at the Trump administration. "There are some people who reported, 'I think there needs to be more about Indigenous history here,'" Blake said.

The watchdog group Public Employees for Environmental Responsibility filed a Freedom of Information Act request last year to find out how the executive order was being implemented. The Interior Department never responded, so PEER sued in June to get the records. Tony Irish, a longtime Interior attorney who left the department last year and joined PEER in May, said, "There's a number of terms [in the order] that, even for me, with my background in Interior programs, isn't clear." Irish said he wondered how the orders might run afoul of federal law, including the National Park Service Organic Act of 1916, which created the service, and the National Historic Preservation Act of 1966. "There's other sites, particularly ones like Little Big Horn, where Congress has specifically directed the agency to provide a viewpoint from the Native American tribes that were impacted by the events there," Irish said.

The order reaches beyond park signage. It calls for the removal of offending signs throughout all 500 million acres of Interior Department lands, comprising roughly one-fifth of the country. Park Service sites draw most of the attention because Americans are more inclined to visit them, Irish said, but the Fish and Wildlife Service and the Bureau of Land Management oversee even more acreage, and PEER is suing for information about the order's impact on those lands too. Irish said there have been impacts on wildlife refuges and on the Bureau of Land Management. "Part of why we want to get these records is to see just to what extent."

Little is known about the fate of many of the removed signs. When a district court judge told the Trump administration to provide an inventory of removed materials, the partial list from the Interior Department included 18 items that had been "discarded." Few park employees are inclined to talk about where old signs might be, let alone which others are slated for removal, Irish said. "We've seen … circumstances where employees have made their personal feelings known that have led to swift reactions from the department," he said. "So I would suspect there is some fear of being open about that."

Some replacement signs omit or condense parts of the history laid out in the originals in ways that are not immediately obvious. "These are really insidious," Blake said. "The differences are really subtle. In one case, a sign about Black soldiers during the Civil War, a reference to the fact that the military remained segregated until after World War II, that sentence was quietly taken out. So you could read something like that and not realize this has been censored."

Spears characterized the park-site changes as a whitewashing of history that reverses decades of progress at the national parks. "We spent the last 30 years trying to raise up some of these untold stories so that we can have a better understanding of the contributions that all of us have made," she said. Spears called the current shift "disheartening and anger-inducing."

Save Our Signs continues to document changes to signs across the country. Blake said the effort is capturing an important moment in history when an accurate telling of the past is itself under attack. "Best-case scenario, the signs that have been censored will go back up, and this will be a way to study what happened," she said. "Worst-case scenario, if this censorship continues, we want to be documenting it so that people can see what has been changed." The reporting was republished with permission from Capital & Main.
