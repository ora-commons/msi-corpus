## Soil and water testing could reveal additional lead, Sander says

Public health specialist Will Sander advised residents near the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant to remain cautious as testing continued after the Army destroyed a stockpile of lead azide on Wednesday. The University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign associate professor of preventative medicine and public health said the EPA's statement that no lead leaks had been detected might remain true for air, but further soil and water testing could find additional detections. Sander also said long-term risks were too early to assess and could require monitoring for weeks or months.

The detonation took place Wednesday in Middletown, where Linda Ross lives about a mile from the plant. Ross said she heard and felt the explosion, her dogs started barking and objects rattled against the window panes. She said the blast seemed anticlimactic.

FEMA described the work to contain the detonation as one of the most complex mitigation efforts in history. The agency lifted its shelter-in-place order on the night before the NPR interview and said people could resume normal activities, although testing was still underway.

The EPA said lead leaks were not detected. Sander said that assessment could still hold for air, but additional detections might appear once more testing was conducted in soil and water around the area. He said the absence of detected air leaks would not rule out detections elsewhere.

A Martínez said many of the Army's mitigation measures were put in place only after a legal challenge and public outcry. Sander said the groundswell over the previous week and a half helped move the process forward and brought more mitigation measures into place.

For nearby residents considering a return after FEMA lifted the order, Sander recommended looking for dust or accumulated materials and washing them off as had been recommended before the blast. He also advised paying close attention to test results over the next few days.

Asked whether he would return immediately, Sander said he would probably stay away a little longer until confirmatory testing showed everything was clear, though he said the situation sounded encouraging. If he had to go back, Sander said, he would stay inside for another day or so and wear a mask outdoors. He said that after the next 24 hours, he would be less worried about inhaling material in the air.

Sander said precipitation in the area might help wash material from hard surfaces. He also said contamination would get into the dirt regardless and that background lead contamination already existed in the dirt system. He did not expect the detonation to markedly increase that level, even if lead from the blast was present.

On long-term risks, Sander said it was too early to assess them and that monitoring would need to continue for the next weeks, potentially months, to ensure there were no lasting impacts. He said he expected the episode to produce lessons about how the response was approached and communicated.

Sander also expected those lessons to inform future efforts to mitigate similar materials elsewhere in the country. For nearby residents, his immediate guidance was to watch for dust and test results, wash off visible residue and continue precautions briefly after returning.
