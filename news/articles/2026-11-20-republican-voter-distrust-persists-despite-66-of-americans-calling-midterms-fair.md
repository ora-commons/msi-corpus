## Only 15% of Republicans said politicians discuss voter fraud too much

A University of California San Diego poll released Friday found that fewer than half of Republican voters nationwide considered the 2026 midterms free and fair, even as 66% of Americans said they thought the elections were fair. The post-election figure was an improvement from before Election Day, when less than half of U.S. adults said they expected free and fair contests. Republican confidence in the count was lower: 52% said they trusted votes were counted accurately.

Republican confidence was also below its level after Trump won the 2024 election, when more than 80% said they had confidence in the vote-counting process. The UC San Diego figures nonetheless improved on 2020. Leading up to the Jan. 6 riot at the U.S. Capitol in 2021, just 22% of Republicans said they had confidence in the nationwide vote count, according to similar polling by MIT's Election Data and Science Lab.

Supporters of the losing party usually see trust decline in most elections, both domestically and internationally, Kousser said. But the 30-point swing among Republicans between 2024 and 2026 was larger than the change he considered ideal, which he said would be closer to 10 percentage points in either direction. NPR noted that Republicans had seen larger swings in confidence in recent years, during a period when Trump repeatedly claimed that U.S. elections were corrupt.

"There are still a lot of people who remain unconvinced that they can trust elections," said Thad Kousser, a co-director of UC San Diego's Center for Transparent and Trusted Elections. "And they're predominantly located in one [political] party."

Lauren Prather, also a co-director of UC San Diego's election center, made the same point about the aggregate result. "It would be a mistake to [look at the 66%] and think, 'Oh, everyone has a pretty good view of the election,'" she said. "It really buries the lede a little bit about the partisan gap." The post-election increase in confidence was buoyed by Democrats and independents, many of whom had worried before Election Day about potential federal interference at the polls that did not occur.

On election night, Trump told Fox News' Bret Baier, "Sure, I'll accept [the results]," as it became clear Democrats would retake the House and Senate. "Why wouldn't I?" he added. Days later, Trump posted online, "Does anybody really believe that this 'Election' was honest," questioning the same outcome.

States United Action, an advocacy group, tracked candidates who challenged election results and projected that fewer of them would hold office next year. The group said the vast majority of candidates who ran for positions involved in overseeing statewide elections lost their races this year. Trump and other high-profile losing candidates on the ballot conceded their losses, which election experts celebrated.

Kousser said the new UC San Diego polling showed that among Republicans, there might still be a receptive audience to claims questioning the 2026 results. When respondents were asked whether politicians were discussing voter fraud too much, too little or about the right amount, just 15% of Republicans answered "too much."

"There is both demand among the Republican base for continued focus on this issue, and as we saw even after the election, politicians are continuing to supply it," Kousser said. "As long as those two dynamics are there, there's still going to be a market for these concerns." He compared changing trust after an election to asking whether people trust referees following an overtime basketball game.

The poll may be a reality check for those asking whether Trump's repeated questioning of election results has run its course in American politics. Kousser said Republican demand for continued focus on voter fraud remained while politicians continued to supply the issue.
