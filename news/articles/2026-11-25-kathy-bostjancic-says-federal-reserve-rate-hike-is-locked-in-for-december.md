## Greg Daco says firms pass fuel, tariff and AI-related costs to customers

Greg Daco, chief economist of the accounting firm EY-Parthenon, said inflation was being driven by the huge investment in artificial intelligence, the wartime spike in fuel prices and President Trump's tariffs. He said businesses were less likely now to absorb those costs and were instead passing them on to their customers.

He said: "That is feeding into this constant repricing environment where inflation is remaining higher than it historically has been."

Kathy Bostjancic, chief economist at the insurance firm Nationwide, said the Federal Reserve would likely try to put the brakes on inflation by raising its benchmark interest rate by a quarter percentage point when policymakers meet in a couple of weeks. She said: "For the December meeting, I think a rate hike is locked in." Many investors expect an additional quarter-point rate hike in January, but Bostjancic said that one was less certain and depended in part on financial markets over the next couple of months.

Commerce Department data released Nov. 25 revised U.S. third-quarter GDP growth to an annual rate of 4.3%, up from its initial estimate. The Commerce Department said the U.S. economy was even stronger during the summer and early fall than previously estimated. GDP had already grown in July, August and September, and Bostjancic said the revised pace was the fastest growth in three years.

Bostjancic tied the revision to AI investment spending and consumer spending. She said: "The economy was really quite robust, and driving it was AI investment spending and consumer spending, and both of those were revised even higher in the third quarter."

A separate report put inflation at 3.8% for the 12 months ending in October, a bigger annual price increase than in the previous month. The stronger output and higher inflation put the Federal Reserve's next rate decisions at the center of the economic outlook.

The news sparked more gyrations in the stock market, where investors were already on edge amid especially volatile recent weeks. Much of the run-up earlier this year was concentrated in AI companies. Even before the controversy surrounding NVIDIA's CEO, investors were already pondering whether the artificial intelligence boom would produce a return.

Bostjancic framed the central question: "Is there going to be a return on investment for all of this record amount of AI spending?"

If those AI gains unravel, Bostjancic said, companies may have to slow investment in data centers and similar projects. She said: "It also means less of a wealth effect over time for consumers, so it can dampen some of the consumer spending numbers."

A market reversal could therefore slow both consumer spending and AI investment, the drivers Bostjancic cited for the third quarter. Forecasters were already projecting slower GDP growth in the final months of this year.
