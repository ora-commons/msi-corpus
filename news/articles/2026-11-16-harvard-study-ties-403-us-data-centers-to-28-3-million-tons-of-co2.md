## U.N. talks launch voluntary Antalya artificial intelligence pledge

The 400 largest data centers in the United States released more heat-trapping gas into the air in a single year than the entire nation of Denmark did, according to a Harvard University study published Monday — and the measurement covers a stretch before the artificial intelligence boom really got going.

On the same day, negotiators at the United Nations COP31 climate talks in Antalya, Turkey, launched a voluntary pledge for companies and countries to run the world's data centers on clean energy and to be more open about their power and water consumption and emissions.

United Nations climate chief Simon Stiell tied the launch to the cost of delay. "We cannot ask people to accept higher bills, more planet heating pollution and deeper inequality today in exchange for promises of a better tomorrow," Stiell said Monday during the launch of the pledge. "Claims about future emissions savings cannot excuse rising pollution now, making climate disasters even worse."

The pledge also calls for putting artificial intelligence to work on climate change, and it reflects concerns about the technology's expanding environmental footprint. Political opposition to data center growth has played out in U.S. elections and in major local objections to new sites.

## What the Harvard estimate covers

Harvard researchers examined 403 of the largest U.S. data centers between May 2024 and April 2025, drawing on available data and modeling to analyze their electricity use and emissions. The electricity those centers consumed produced an estimated 28.3 million tons (25.7 million metric tons) of carbon dioxide, according to the study published Monday in Nature Sustainability.

Those 403 centers used 82 terawatt hours of electricity over the year — enough to power nearly 7 million homes — and more than half of that electricity came from fossil fuels, which the researchers identified as the primary cause of global warming.

The estimate is two to three times higher than what previous studies had shown, said study senior author Francesca Dominici, a Harvard biostatistics professor. "It is the equivalent to the CO2 emission of an entire state like Michigan or a small European country," Dominici said, adding that plans for 2,000 more data centers make that quantity more worrisome for the future. "We are going backwards toward the goal of controlling CO2 emissions."

Outside specialists in climate, energy and artificial intelligence said the study reached realistic conclusions, and they considered its use of modeling reasonable because companies are not open about their power consumption and pollution. "We cannot manage environmental impacts effectively without reliable measurements and disclosure," said Fengqi You, a Cornell University energy systems scientist who was not part of the study. "Ultimately, the environmental footprint of AI is not fixed. It depends on where we build data centers, how efficiently they operate, and how we supply their electricity."

## The pledge's terms and the push for new power

The "Antalya Artificial Intelligence Pledge" was launched by U.N. climate talks host Turkey together with the International Telecommunication Union. It says data centers should use clean power and efficient energy and water systems, and it asks that AI systems be designed and managed responsibly under environmental standards, with infrastructure and hardware reused or recycled. Signatories are also asked to assess and share the environmental impacts of their data centers and to support using artificial intelligence as a climate solution.

COP31 President Murat Kurum has said world leaders at the talks must openly discuss AI's growing energy consumption and start setting the terms.

The rapid growth of new and planned data centers has prompted a push for additional sources of power generation, at a moment when the biggest tech companies are failing to reach their climate goals: their electricity consumption rose 240% between 2022 and 2025, according to a new report by the advocacy group Climate Action Against Disinformation.

## Split views on AI's climate role

Former U.S. Vice President Al Gore said it is essential that data centers be powered by carbon-free energy, though he is less concerned about emissions from AI than others and is hopeful that its computing power can find novel ways of fighting climate change. In an Associated Press interview, Gore cited a study by prominent British economist and climate advocate Nicholas Stern, who predicted by 2030 "we will see 6 to 9% reduction in emissions of greenhouse gases because of the use of advanced AI in finding new ways to be far more efficient in the use of materials and energy."

Caleb Max, president of the National Artificial Intelligence Association, argued the technology's benefits outweigh its footprint: "AI will solve not only the energy usage issues that data centers face, but unlock numerous currently unknown advances in science and technology that will address climate change and make lives better for humanity. I respect the concerns about energy and water consumption from data centers, but the fears may be overblown."

Philip Newell, a spokesman for Climate Action Against Disinformation, took the skeptical position. "We have hope in one hand and then harms in the other," Newell said. "They're selling us hope while hiding the harms from us."

## Reusing waste heat

The computing equipment inside data centers generates substantial waste heat that is vented into the atmosphere, and capturing that heat for nearby homes and businesses is one way to cut the industry's environmental impact. The Danish engineering and manufacturing company Danfoss called waste heat "the world's largest untapped energy source" and said reusing it can replace natural gas as a heat source. Danfoss identified data centers as a significant source of constant heat, particularly as their power consumption grows.

The International Energy Agency estimates data centers could supply enough heat for around 10% of European homes by 2030, provided that heat was fully integrated into district systems that deliver heat and hot water to residents.

European Commissioner for Energy and Housing Dan Jørgensen said in remarks broadcast during COP31 that Europe must replace imported fossil fuels by capturing heat that is going to waste and tapping into geothermal.

Industrial development firm BaRupOn is building an advanced manufacturing campus in Texas that plans to use recaptured heat to generate additional electricity on site, CEO Balaji Tammabattula said. In North Dakota, the company is planning a data center that could feed heat to the community.

Gregor Henze, a University of Colorado Boulder professor, called the pairing "a perfect marriage," saying modern data centers that rely on liquid rather than air cooling are a fairly constant source of heat at the right temperature for thermal energy networks.
