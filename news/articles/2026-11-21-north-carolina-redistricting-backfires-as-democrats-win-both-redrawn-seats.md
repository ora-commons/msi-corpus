## North Carolina redistricting backfires as Democrats win both redrawn seats

**Subtype:** fact

- Republican lawmakers in North Carolina redrew the 1st and 3rd Congressional Districts last fall to win both seats; Democrats won both.
- Voters in the 3rd District elected Democrat Raymond Smith by about 260 votes in a district where Republican Rep. Greg Murphy was the incumbent.
- Incumbent Democratic Rep. Don Davis defeated Republican Laurie Buckhout in the 1st District, a rematch of their 2024 race.

## Republicans shifted six coastal counties to target Rep. Don Davis

Last fall, Republican lawmakers in North Carolina redrew a pair of congressional districts in the eastern part of the state, aiming to win both instead of one. Democrats won both seats, an outcome political scientists describe as a "dummymander" — a gerrymander that costs its authors seats.

State Sen. Ralph Hise told a Senate committee at the time that the revamped map would combat gains Democrats were poised to make elsewhere by giving Republicans one more U.S. House seat. "The end result is a congressional map that should perform to elect 11 Republicans," Hise said, according to an archived recording of the committee session.

Republicans moved six GOP-leaning counties along North Carolina's coast out of the 3rd Congressional District and into the 1st. They hoped that would help a Republican beat incumbent Democratic Rep. Don Davis in a seat widely considered North Carolina's most competitive district. To keep the sizes of the districts even, they moved four purple counties into the 3rd District, weakening the advantage held by incumbent Republican Rep. Greg Murphy.

Murphy's Democratic opponent, Raymond Smith, is a former state legislator, educator and U.S. Army veteran who lives in one of the counties lawmakers moved. "Prior to that gerrymander, I was in the 1st Congressional District," Smith said. "Congressman Davis was my congressman."

Smith said he had not considered running for Congress until the new map was finalized. With the changes, he came to believe a Democrat could win in the 3rd District. "It certainly piqued our interest," he said.

Murphy, the incumbent, also thought the district was in play. "It made us the weakest Republican district in the state," he said. Murphy said the new map shifted the 3rd District's priorities; the district has historically been coastal, and he spent considerable time developing expertise on those issues. "There was obviously a radical change with those maps," he said.

Murphy seriously considered staying with the coastal counties and running in the new 1st Congressional District. He stayed in the 3rd, where voters elected Smith by about 260 votes.

In the 1st District, Davis defeated the GOP's Laurie Buckhout in a 2024 rematch. Vote totals from the counties Republicans shifted suggest that under the previous map, Davis and Murphy likely would have both won reelection, which would make the new map a dummymander.

"You almost couldn't draw up a better textbook example than what they did in the 1st and the 3rd," said Chris Cooper, a political scientist at Western Carolina University.

Cooper noted that the redrawing was confined to those two districts. "It did not touch any other district in the state, right? Twelve districts were untouched," he said. "They said the point of it out loud — to get Don Davis out of office, to get another Republican in and to hold on to the 3rd. And we can see they failed, and they failed."

Cooper thinks the Democratic wave that swept across the country played a key role in Democrats winning both of the redrawn districts. That does not mean it is a permanent shift: a textbook dummymander in 2026 could, he said, turn out to be a successful gerrymander for the GOP as soon as 2028.

The North Carolina result was part of a broader national pattern. Politicians across the country redrew political maps last fall to try to win congressional seats, and the gerrymandering, mostly led by Republicans, was overwhelmed by a Democratic wave. New Republican-drawn maps in Ohio saw voters elect three more Democrats.
