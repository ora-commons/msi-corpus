## Officials call for more building inspectors after Brooklyn collapse

**Subtype:** fact

## City dashboard shows 168 vacant positions across Department of Buildings

Eight people were killed Wednesday when the three-story structure containing residential and commercial units in Brooklyn collapsed. It fell into a pit dug by workers excavating the site of another building that was recently demolished. Neighbors had reported feeling vibrations from the construction site, and at least one witness saw bricks falling from the building just moments before it collapsed.

Thousands of building construction projects are underway in New York on any given day. The deadly collapse of a building next to one of those work sites has raised questions about the city's oversight and the safety precautions taken by contractors. While the investigation is ongoing, some New York officials on Friday called for the hiring of more inspectors by the city's Department of Buildings. The probe is likely to focus not only on the building that fell but on an adjacent excavation site and its contractors, who had faced previous lawsuits and complaints. It remains unclear how often the excavation site had been inspected.

The Department of Buildings has a vacancy rate of around 9% — 168 of its 1,855 authorized positions — according to the city's comptroller dashboard.

"We must, and we will, ensure that every single one of those positions is immediately filled," New York City Council Speaker Julie Menin, a Democrat, said. "This is an unspeakable, horrendous tragedy, and we must meet this moment."

Democratic City Council Member Farah Louis called for aggressive oversight. "Grief without accountability is not enough," Louis said. "Buildings do not simply collapse without systemic failures."

The city has ordered inspections at more than 160 sites in New York that are associated with six entities linked to the construction and demolition site where the building collapsed, Mamdani said. The city is also bringing in an engineering consulting firm to provide an independent report, which Mamdani described as "standard practice for this kind of a tragic incident."

All contractors doing excavation work must notify New York's Department of Buildings 24 to 48 hours in advance, according to guidelines posted on the department's website. A contractor on this site provided notice Sept. 9 that work would begin the following day and include rock blasting.

Permits are required for most work, which must also meet submitted plans. All projects are required to have proper precautions, such as shoring, bracing and "regularly checking walls for cracks," the city's website says. If any of the work violates any rule, regulation or law, the city's building commissioner may issue a stop work order, and the Buildings Department may also conduct random audits of excavation projects to inspect shoring, groundwater, excavation depth and other things that could affect safety. "Department site monitoring does not relieve engineers and contractors of their professional responsibilities," the city's website says.

Contractors involved in the demolition and excavation of the property next to the collapsed building had faced previous complaints and alleged violations. In March, the city issued a stop work order after inspectors concluded the building was being demolished without adequate overhead protection. In April, inspectors found that the demolition work had resumed in violation of that order, the department said, but work was allowed to continue after the problems were corrected.

Townhouse Builders Inc., which also operates as Promont, was accused in a lawsuit of causing extensive damage to an adjacent building during renovation work in Manhattan in 2016. Townhouse denied the allegations in court papers, and the suit appears to have been settled out of court. Townhouse was also accused in a lawsuit of damaging and compromising the structural integrity of adjacent buildings while performing excavation, pile driving and steel beam installation for a six-story hotel project in Brooklyn in 2016. The damage included a breached wall, shifting, settlement and cracking, according to the lawsuit, which accused Townhouse of failing to perform a physical inspection prior to excavation and failing to properly support the property's walls. Townhouse's lawyers reached agreements four times to delay their deadline to submit a written answer to the claims but never did, and nothing has been posted to the docket since 2022. The owner of Townhouse did not respond to requests for comment from The Associated Press.

The investigation into Wednesday's building collapse should look into whether the designs that were submitted were adequate and, if they were, whether the contractor doing the work made a mistake, said Abi Aghayere, a professor of civil engineering at Drexel University who studies structural systems and why they fail. "With an old building like this, the room for error is very, very, very, very slim," Aghayere said.

Aghayere identified four main possible causes for failure in a project that involves excavating adjacent to an existing building: vibration caused by the work transmitting to the nearby building and weakening the structure; shoring in place for the excavation work that was not strong enough; shoring that was not stiff enough; and seepage of groundwater that caused the soil under the existing building to shift, destabilizing the property. A question investigators will need to look into is whether the integrity of the building that collapsed was being monitored during the work, Aghayere said.

Everyone — tenants, building owners and landlords — has a responsibility to report anything unusual they see, particularly in older buildings, Aghayere said. "You might see a crack, don't take it lightly," he said. "That crack might be that building just crying out and saying, 'Help, help. I'm about to go.'"
