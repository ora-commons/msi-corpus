## Surveillance footage shows DEA agents monitoring fentanyl shipments

Federal and state authorities are scrutinizing a Drug Enforcement Administration tactic in which agents let fentanyl move in New Mexico so investigators could build cases against larger trafficking networks. An Associated Press investigation found that the DEA allowed at least 1.4 million additional pills to go unseized between August 2024 and April 2025 while closing in on the largest fentanyl bust in U.S. history. The reporting drew on thousands of pages of court documents, investigative records and detailed wiretap reports, along with 12 hours of DEA surveillance footage.

The AP said DEA agents watched and often recorded from a surveillance plane circling above New Mexico as dealers trafficked large packages of pills in plastic bins and duffel bags. The shipments passed through highly transited hotels, apartment complexes, Airbnb rentals and self-storage facilities in six states. Agents also "walked" as many as 100,000 pills at a hotel in Albuquerque after the DEA surrounded the building in a stakeout.

The AP previously reported in June that DEA whistleblower David Howell and other agents in New Mexico had questioned the morality and effectiveness of the tactic, known as "walking" drugs. That earlier account said hundreds of thousands of pills went unseized; the new investigation says the number was far higher.

The DEA initially denied ever knowingly allowing the trafficking of a synthetic opioid that the White House has designated a "weapon of mass destruction." The agency maintained that denial after the AP obtained hours of DEA surveillance footage showing agents monitoring fentanyl shipments and its own wiretap reports detailing the number of pills that went unseized. "Our response has not changed," DEA spokesperson Amanda Wozniak wrote in an email. Wozniak declined to answer questions because of an ongoing Justice Department inspector general review. The agency has said its decisions were lawful and coordinated with federal prosecutors to prevent public harm.

The U.S. attorney's office in New Mexico and the Justice Department declined to answer questions. "The Department of Justice is committed to using every lawful tool available to identify, disrupt and dismantle the organizations responsible for trafficking fentanyl into our communities," Tessa DuBerry, a U.S. attorney's office spokesperson, wrote in an emailed statement.

Defenders of the wait-and-see approach say it can lead to bigger cases and the removal of higher-ranking bosses. "Everybody wants the big fish, but the big fish isn't guaranteed," said Brian Besser, a former DEA senior executive who oversaw the agency's Rocky Mountain division. "When you're dealing with a substance that can take this much human life, you can't gamble like that."

At times, the DEA has allowed traffickers suspected of moving cocaine, heroin and marijuana to continue operating under surveillance so agents would not spook bigger targets, according to the AP account. Fentanyl posed a different danger: a few milligrams can kill the average adult. The DEA for years ran a public-safety campaign with the slogan "One Pill Can Kill." Justice Department instructions issued in 2017 told agents to seize fentanyl whenever "practicable," since even unintentional contact can prove deadly, but officials loosened the rules in 2024 to give agents more discretion.

The 2024 change came after DEA whistleblower David Howell came forward, according to the AP. "Agents are driving around in a car plastered with 'One Pill Can Kill' and at the same time walking hundreds of thousands of pills of fentanyl," said Kevin Small, a former colleague of Howell's. Small added, "They're hypocrites."

The tactic has drawn bipartisan criticism in Washington and New Mexico. Congress has launched an oversight inquiry, and the New Mexico attorney general has initiated a criminal investigation. Officials in Albuquerque recently declared a state of emergency and last week sued the DEA seeking $153 million in damages. Even as the fentanyl crisis subsided from its peak, the AP reported that it continued to batter New Mexico. Fentanyl overdose deaths in the state spiked 44% last year, and much of the drug was crudely manufactured in Mexico.

"This is like trying to prevent my house from burning down by negotiating with an arsonist," New Mexico Gov. Michelle Lujan Grisham, a Democrat, said in an interview, referring to the DEA. "They are fully engaged in the cover-up because they know they went too far and it's wrong." The DEA continues to deny that it knowingly allowed fentanyl trafficking as the congressional inquiry, state criminal investigation and Justice Department inspector general review continue.
