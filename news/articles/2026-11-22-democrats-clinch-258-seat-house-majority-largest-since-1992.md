## Democrats clinch 258-seat House majority, largest since 1992

**Subtype:** fact

- House Democrats have won at least 258 seats, their best general election result in decades and a sweep unseen since 1992.
- Democrats have gained 44 House seats so far, four more than the 40 they picked up in President Donald Trump's first midterm in 2018.
- The 258-seat tally was reached Sunday after Democrat Chuck Hubbard defeated Republican Rep. Virginia Foxx in North Carolina, leaving four House races outstanding.
- Republicans will hold at least 173 seats in the minority, one of their lowest tallies in decades.
- Democratic leader Hakeem Jeffries, in line to become House speaker, has not ruled out trying to impeach Trump for a third time.
- The White House has warned that planned Democratic investigations could amount to "blatant criminality."

## Jeffries poised to become first Black House speaker

Democrats' gains so far reach 44 seats, four more than the 40 the party picked up in Trump's first midterm in 2018. Four House races remain outstanding: one each in Minnesota and Tennessee and two in Louisiana that are heading to a December runoff.

Republicans will hold at least 173 seats in the minority. Their tally falls far short of the 247 House seats Republicans won in the 2014 election at the end of the Obama administration, and of the 230 they won in 1994 with the rise of Newt Gingrich. The party's high-water marks include the 1920 election, with 301 seats.

The milestone in the 435-member House sets up divided government: the Senate is also in Democratic hands, with a Republican president in the White House. Senate Democrats won a 56-seat majority in the 100-member chamber, their biggest advantage in over a decade. Taking control of both chambers in January, Democrats will carry the backing of voters and the expectations to deliver.

The majority exceeds any the party has seen in elections since Bill Clinton was first elected president in 1992, when Democrats won 258 seats, or the election of Barack Obama in 2008, when they won 257 — though not the Franklin D. Roosevelt-era high-water mark, when House Democratic majorities swelled past 300.

The party's wins spanned from the strongest Republican states to the Midwest and East Coast, as voters angry over high prices and the Iran war, and concerned over corruption, made clear their discontent with Trump.

Emboldened Democrats are preparing a 100-day agenda to address high costs of living while also confronting Trump with an oversight agenda for the remainder of his second term. As part of that agenda, they are planning swift votes to reverse Republicans' Medicaid cuts and restore the Affordable Care Act tax credits Republicans allowed to expire.

Democrats have vowed to marshal their subpoena powers and launch a flurry of investigations to exert Congress's role as a check on presidential power. That includes plans to probe how Trump and his family have become wealthier since he returned to office. Jeffries, who is in line to become House speaker, has not ruled out trying to impeach Trump for a third time.

"This is a historic moment for our country. This is one of the largest classes we have seen," said progressive leader Rep. Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez, D-N.Y., at the Capitol. She called the big numbers "a mandate for change."

Rep. Tom Suozzi, a centrist Democrat from New York and an accountant by profession, said House Democrats should focus 75% of their time on affordability issues and 25% on accountability. "Everything should be tied back to the people — and how it's affecting the people," he said. As Democrats launch investigations into Trump's financial dealings, he said, they should connect the dots: "How does that affect people's pocketbooks?"

The pressure is acknowledged inside the caucus. "Like, hell yeah, we should feel pressure," said Democratic Rep. Jason Crow of Colorado, a former Army Ranger. "There are people dying in this country and people losing their healthcare, losing their livelihoods, losing their farms, being terrorized from ICE," he said at the Capitol, referring to U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement. "If you don't feel a sense of urgency and pressure in this job, and a desperation to deliver for folks, then you don't belong under this Capitol dome."

The White House has issued a warning to Democrats over their planned probes into the administration, going as far as claiming that such investigations — a standard oversight function of Congress — may amount to "blatant criminality."

After House Speaker Mike Johnson, R-La., struggled to keep the House functioning for the past two years with a narrow 218-214 Republican majority — meaning any few GOP lawmakers could derail the agenda — the Democrats will have a wide margin that provides new leadership much more room to maneuver. Jeffries, who is poised to make history as the first Black speaker of the House if his colleagues elect him to the job on Jan. 3, 2027, will be able to lose dozens of Democrats on any given vote and still have the 218 needed for passage, in the face of GOP opposition.

"We're not a monolith — people come to the caucus with different ideas — it's OK," said Rep. Troy Carter, D-La., who is expected to lead the Congressional Black Caucus.

Rep. Jim Jordan of Ohio, who was elected by his colleagues to be the Republican leader in the minority, vowed to fight their way back to power. "Over the next two years, House Republicans will fight for American families," Jordan said.
