## Federal guidance offers states an optional six-month transition

The Indiana Family and Social Services Administration is reviewing whether Indiana will use an optional federal transition period before enforcing Medicaid expansion work requirements that take effect in January. The Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services updated its guidance to states last week, allowing an optional six-month period in which states can roll out the new 80-hour work rule without disrupting coverage for otherwise eligible people who are not yet in compliance.

Under Indiana's rules, adults ages 19 to 64 who are insured through the Healthy Indiana Plan or Medicaid expansion must show they are working, volunteering or in school for at least 80 hours a month. The requirement does not apply to those who meet an exception or meet a monthly income threshold. Indiana will use a three-month look-back window to determine compliance, requiring beneficiaries to show they met the requirements during the three months before applying for coverage or undergoing the eligibility redetermination process.

FSSA spokesperson Marcus Barlow told the Indiana Capital Chronicle that the agency is reviewing CMS's optional enforcement delay, its statutory authority and state requirements. “We are carefully evaluating the guidance and its implications and will provide additional information as it becomes available,” he said. The work-reporting rules themselves are required by state and federal law.

The option follows an October district court ruling that found CMS exceeded its authority in narrowing the definition of medical frailty used to determine eligibility for an exception. CMS wrote in a statement last week that it had established an “optional hold harmless period” so state governments would have more time to update their systems and improve the processes they had in place.

Dan Brillman, director of the Center for Medicaid and CHIP Services, wrote that states had made progress on implementing the requirement. “States have made real progress, and they’ve been clear with us about what it takes to get this right for beneficiaries—at scale and for the long term,” Brillman wrote. He also wrote: “If the state can use data to show that someone is working or qualifies for an exclusion, that person should not have to track down paperwork to prove it.” Brillman said people who need support should know exactly what to do, when to do it and where to go.

Democratic members of Congress raised concerns this week that states had not had enough time to prepare for the new requirement. In a letter dated Wednesday, House Energy and Commerce Committee ranking member Frank Pallone Jr. of New Jersey and Senate Finance Committee ranking member Ron Wyden of Oregon wrote that administration officials use their “discretion to give states additional time to implement work reporting requirements and protect eligible beneficiaries from losing coverage.”

The federal provision requires Medicaid enrollees between ages 19 and 65 to work, participate in community service or attend an education program for at least 80 hours a month. The law details several exceptions, including pregnant people, parents of dependent children, people with complex medical conditions, tribal community members, people in the foster care system, people who were in foster care and are below age 26, and individuals released from incarceration in the last 90 days, among others.

The immediate decision remains with FSSA. The work-reporting rules will take effect Jan. 1, but the agency has not said whether it will delay enforcement. Barlow said additional information would become available as the review continued, leaving the use of the optional grace period unresolved.
