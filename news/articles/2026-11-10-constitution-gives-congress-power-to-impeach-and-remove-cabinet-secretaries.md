## Constitution gives Congress power to impeach and remove Cabinet secretaries

**Subtype:** fact

- Article II, Section 4 of the Constitution makes Cabinet secretaries subject to impeachment and removal after Senate conviction for treason, bribery, or other high crimes and misdemeanors.
- The House of Representatives can impeach a Cabinet secretary with a simple majority after investigating alleged misconduct and adopting articles of impeachment.
- The U.S. Senate requires a two-thirds vote to convict a Cabinet secretary and remove the official from office.
- An impeached Cabinet secretary can continue serving while the U.S. Senate considers the charges.
- The Constitution names the chief justice of the U.S. Supreme Court to preside over presidential impeachment trials but does not clearly assign a presiding official for Cabinet secretary trials.
- The U.S. Senate acquitted William W. Belknap and dismissed Alejandro Mayorkas's case, so neither Cabinet secretary was convicted.

## House majority can charge a secretary; two-thirds Senate conviction removes the official

Article II, Section 4 extends impeachment beyond the presidency. Its language covering “all civil Officers of the United States” includes Cabinet secretaries, allowing Congress to impeach and remove a secretary through the same constitutional framework available for other federal officials.

The constitutional text states: “The President, Vice President and all civil Officers of the United States, shall be removed from Office on Impeachment for, and Conviction of, Treason, Bribery, or other high Crimes and Misdemeanors.” By linking impeachment with conviction, the provision separates the House's formal charge from the Senate judgment required for removal.

The Constitution does not strictly define “high crimes and misdemeanors.” The standard is not necessarily tied to a violation of a criminal statute or a court conviction. Historically, impeachment has been used for presidents and other officials to address abuses of official power and breaches of public trust.

Congressional cases begin in the House of Representatives, either with the introduction of a resolution or with a committee investigating allegations. The House investigates alleged misconduct and decides whether to formally charge an official through articles of impeachment.

If a simple majority of the House votes to impeach, the Senate holds a trial. The chambers have distinct roles in that process: the House determines whether an official is formally charged, while the Senate decides whether the official is convicted.

The Constitution explicitly provides for the chief justice of the U.S. Supreme Court to preside over a presidential impeachment trial. It does not state as clearly who should oversee proceedings involving a Cabinet secretary.

Impeachment itself does not remove a Cabinet secretary. The process treats the House action as a formal accusation rather than a conviction, and a secretary who has been impeached can continue serving while the Senate considers the charges.

Removal requires a two-thirds Senate vote. A simple House majority is sufficient for the impeachment itself, but the Senate's threshold for conviction and removal is significantly higher.

William W. Belknap, who served as war secretary for President Ulysses S. Grant, was the first Cabinet secretary impeached. He resigned hours before the House voted to impeach him in 1876 on allegations that he had accepted bribes in a trading post scandal. The Senate subsequently acquitted him.

Alejandro Mayorkas, who served as Homeland Security Secretary in the Biden administration, was the second Cabinet secretary impeached. The House narrowly voted to impeach him in February 2024. The Senate dismissed the case two months later, saying it did not meet constitutional standards, and Mayorkas continued serving until President Joe Biden left office.

Belknap and Mayorkas are the only two Cabinet secretaries to have been impeached, and neither received a Senate conviction. Their cases illustrate the central distinction in the process: the House can formally accuse a Cabinet secretary, but removal depends on a two-thirds Senate vote.
