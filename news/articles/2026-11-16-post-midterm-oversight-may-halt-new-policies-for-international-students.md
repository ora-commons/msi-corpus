## IIE survey records 9% enrollment decline and 15% fewer first-time arrivals

Sarah Spreitzer, vice-president at the American Council on Education, which represents college and university presidents, said congressional oversight after Trump's Republican party suffered big losses in the 3 November midterms might halt new policies targeted at international students. Spreitzer said substantial immigration bills are unlikely while Donald Trump remains president. "Those types of things could provide more certainty for our international students that there's not just going to be these sudden or unexplainable changes," Spreitzer said.

Spreitzer's assessment accompanies data released on Monday. A survey from the Institute of International Education found that US campuses saw a 9% decrease in international enrollment this fall compared with last year, and the number of new students entering the United States for the first time fell by 15%. The institute surveyed more than 800 US colleges and universities; the results offer an early look at trends before full data is released next year. The declines come as the Trump administration tightens immigration rules and reshapes higher education.

The sharpest losses came at the graduate level. This fall's data shows a 15% drop in international graduate enrollment, compared to a 3% decrease at the undergraduate level. The report also found declines in international students from India, which sends more students to the US to study than any other country. Although the US remains the top destination for international students globally, the changes are having an effect, college leaders told the IIE.

Among more than 800 US colleges and universities surveyed this fall, 88% blamed the visa application process and 70% blamed travel restrictions for the enrollment drops. And if optional practical training options are limited, 95% of schools predicted that international students would probably look to another country, according to the survey.

Last year, the enrollment decreases were partially offset by large numbers of students who stayed in the US for temporary work after graduating. But after months of speculation, the Department of Homeland Security proposed requiring schools to pay a $70,000 fee for every student who participates in the optional practical training program. Although the rule hasn't been finalized, participation in the program, known as OPT, dropped 6% this fall, according to the survey.

The proposed rule, which is expected to face legal challenges, is part of wider DHS efforts that include terminating the legal status of thousands of international students, along with detaining and deporting many of them. And this summer, the administration finalized a rule that will prevent international students from staying in the US for more than four years without approval from the federal government, although a judge has temporarily blocked it. DHS Secretary Markwayne Mullin has said the changes were intended to crack down on loopholes that international students were exploiting by extending their studies.

Politics are not the only reason students are looking elsewhere, said Mirka Martel, head of research, evaluation and learning for the institute. Its survey found more global competition for international students and the rising cost of a US degree also contributed.

Fanta Aw, the executive director and CEO of Nafsa, an association that represents international education, said students also are worried about research funding. Last year, the Trump administration began dismantling the federal grant-giving process and attempting to exert more political influence over what projects get funded.

"They're saying to themselves: 'Too much uncertainty in the United States. It's too difficult, and you know, more importantly, we have many more choices today than we did 10 years ago. Why subject ourselves to this?'" Aw said, noting that students are going to places like China.

Nafsa, in partnership with the international education technology company JB International, found that amid the enrollment decline, international students' contributions to the US economy dropped by $2.4bn this fall.

US colleges and universities heavily recruit students from overseas as a source of tuition revenue. So the drops have punched big holes in tuition revenue at some schools.

At Cleveland State University, international enrollment has dropped by 46% in two years, from 1,542 in fall 2025 to 828 this fall. And fewer than 50 new international students started this fall, said provost Nigamanth Sridhar, who estimated that the budgetary impact ranges from $2m to $4m.

Decades ago, he arrived in the US on an F1 student visa and participated in OPT. Eventually, he went through the process to become a naturalized citizen. "I am here in this position, in this job, because I had the opportunity to come to the United States as an international graduate student and then decided to stay here and build a career," said Sridhar, who is from India. "So international education is personal to me."

Veena Dubal, general counsel for the American Association of University Professors, whose father traveled from India to attend Tuskegee University, then the Tuskegee Institute, in the late 1960s on a student visa, said the message is reaching families abroad. "No one aspires to send their kids to get educated in the US," Dubal said. "It feels like an unsafe place. That's the message that the Trump administration has been sending to families all over the world and they're listening."
