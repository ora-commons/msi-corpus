## Laguna's constitution still refers to the governor as "he"

Deb Haaland, a Laguna Pueblo citizen and former U.S. Interior Secretary, is set to become the first Native American woman state governor, yet women with leadership aspirations in her home community say they face barriers her ascent does not reach. In some of the 19 pueblos in New Mexico, including Laguna, tribal law and customs do not allow women to hold top government positions.

Ashley Sarracino, a former Laguna Pueblo council member, said she was forced off the council after alleging she was paid less because she was a woman — a claim pueblo officials deny. Women with leadership aspirations such as Sarracino say they are swimming upstream in their Pueblo communities.

On the morning of a Laguna Pueblo village's annual Feast Day, Sarracino knelt in her family home holding clothespins between her teeth, straightening her niece's black cotton manta, a traditional one-shouldered dress, and tying a sash around her waist. On these days Sarracino is a caretaker, dressing relatives for the harvest dance and helping feed up to 150 guests — a balance she also struck as a former member of the pueblo council. "To me, those things aren't in conflict at all," she said.

## A $14,000 pay gap ended a council tenure

Sarracino was offered a road map familiar to ambitious young Native people: leave the reservation to get an education, then return home to serve the community. She built a career in nonprofit work and political organizing, including as chair of her county's Democratic Party. In 2022, a pueblo elder nominated her to run for council secretary; she finished second but was appointed after a resignation.

"Finally I had an opportunity to use my skills to help my own community," she said.

While reviewing budget documents, Sarracino discovered she was being paid $14,000 less than her male predecessor. She asked the pueblo governor for an explanation, sparking a dispute that ended her tenure on council. The council says Sarracino was paid less because she lacked experience and that she abandoned her post. Sarracino insists she was ousted and that the pay discrepancy reflects sexist attitudes embedded in tribal law.

Sarracino's dispute landed in tribal court but was dismissed this year on sovereign immunity grounds, and she has exhausted her appeals. The case took a toll on her mental health, and she said it has been bittersweet to watch Haaland make political history. "We all know she would never have the same opportunity here," she said.

## Laguna's charter has opened some doors but not the governor's

Since 1996, Laguna's election code has allowed women to run for council treasurer and secretary, but it does not explicitly allow them to serve as governor or lieutenant governor. Its constitution, last amended in 2012, refers to the governor as "he" and "him."

Haaland told the Associated Press she was once nominated to serve on her pueblo council but did not make it past a primary. She quipped it was "easier to raise $1 million and run for Congress" than succeed in Pueblo politics. "It's still a man's world in a lot of Pueblo communities, and certainly in mine," she said.

Emily Hunt, who became Laguna Pueblo's first female elected official in the late '90s with legal help from Haaland, believes the pueblo will someday nominate a woman governor. "Whether I'll see it in my lifetime, I don't know," Hunt said. Haaland's ascent could help change attitudes at home.

## Practices range from male-only theocracies to open elections

Some pueblos retain traditional theocracies, with government roles appointed by ceremonial elders and reserved for men. Charlotte Little, a citizen of San Felipe Pueblo and a New Mexico state representative, said women in those communities hold esteemed roles of their own; she never aspired to lead at home. "It's about respecting the culture that I come from," she said.

Other pueblos, under federal pressure in the 1930s, adopted secular constitutions and elections. That includes Isleta Pueblo, where in 1986 Verna Teller overcame a contentious legal battle to become its first and only woman governor. Council incumbents attempted unsuccessfully to strike Teller's name from the ballot, arguing a woman could not hold office under Isleta law; she won in a landslide.

Now in her 70s, Teller blames colonization for the patriarchy she says persists in traditionally matrilineal Pueblo communities. "When the Spaniards and the Catholics came here, they didn't want to deal with the clan mothers. They threw women in the back seat," Teller said. "That has turned into this paternalistic attitude that men have to be in charge."

At Pojoaque Pueblo, where council positions are open to all and candidates do not need to be nominated, women have held all four executive positions since 2021.

## Sarracino plans her next move with her nieces in mind

Haaland is the most prominent among a wave of Pueblo women who have found success in mainstream politics, from school boards to state government and U.S. Congress. She encouraged Pueblo women to look beyond their home communities for opportunities. "We need more Native women everywhere," she told the Associated Press.

Sarracino has not ruled out a future pueblo council run and, after attending Haaland's election night celebration, now sees a potential path in state politics. On Feast Day, as her family home filled with visitors, she greeted friends and relatives with hugs, refilled their iced teas and later sent them home with oven bread and fruit pie.

She watched as her nieces giggled over plates of enchiladas, their hands white with ceremonial clay from the morning's dances. She is planning her next move with those girls in mind. "That's the reason I couldn't just let it go," she said. "Because I know they're coming up behind me."
