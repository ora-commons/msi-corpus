## Congress returning in December faced a funding deadline and unfinished legislation

On Nov. 23, 2026, Republicans were discussing what happened in the 2026 midterms and what the future might be for their party, according to an NPR report by Tamara Keith.

The Nov. 10 and Nov. 7 MSI reports provide context for that discussion and are separate from NPR's Nov. 23 account. The Nov. 10 report, [Republican lawmakers assess midterm losses and the work ahead](/articles/2026-11-10-republican-lawmakers-assess-midterm-losses-and-the-work-ahead/), described Republican lawmakers returning to the Capitol to assess their losses as Democrats prepared to control both chambers. Citing The Guardian, the report described a 54-seat Democratic Senate majority and a 254-seat Democratic House majority at an earlier stage of vote counting, with both totals potentially increasing as counting continued.

The Nov. 10 report said the scale of the defeats surprised Republican lawmakers. Some had warned the losses were likely, while others said they had not anticipated them. West Virginia Sen. Jim Justice said, “I told you exactly what was going to happen. Over and over and over, I told you.”

Other Republican members offered explanations for the results. That report, citing The Guardian, said pocketbook concerns, unmet 2024 promises and Medicaid cuts in the One Big Beautiful Bill were among the issues they cited in explaining the losses.

The Nov. 7 MSI report, [Republicans weigh next steps after midterm losses](/articles/2026-11-07-republicans-weigh-next-steps-after-midterm-losses/), quoted Alex Latcham, executive director of the Senate Leadership Fund: “We have soul-searching to do. We need to first understand and honestly admit why we just endured a wipeout of this magnitude. And then we have to regroup and agree to a path forward.”

The Nov. 10 report also quoted Speaker Mike Johnson, who said, “The House will work. We will stay here around the clock if necessary,” and called the election results “a moment of sober reflection for us as a party.”

The Congress returning in December faced a government-funding deadline before Dec. 11, while a major defense policy bill and a long-delayed agricultural measure remained unfinished. The Nov. 10 report said the pending legislation would need support from both parties to advance.

The Nov. 10 report, citing The Guardian, described whether House Republicans would pursue impeachment of Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth as an open question. The Guardian described a resolution as the “swan song” of Thomas Massie, a Republican who opposed Trump during much of his second term, and attributed Massie's primary loss to that opposition.

The Nov. 7 report described leadership contests taking shape. House Republicans were preparing to choose between Steve Scalise and Jim Jordan to lead the party in the House, while early Republican National Committee discussions focused on limiting President Donald Trump's influence in choosing the next chair. The report said KC Crosbie, the committee's co-chair, planned to run at its January meeting in Arizona, while Trump supported current chair Joe Gruters.

The Associated Press, as cited in the Nov. 7 report, identified two strategic risks in earlier coverage. A return to the party's bedrock fiscal and national security conservatism could alienate the more hard-line conservatives welcomed into the party during Trump's presidency, while leaning further into Trump's politics could lead to more voter rejection.
