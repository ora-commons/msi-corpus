## EU wants European students to pay UK domestic tuition rates

Downing Street said the UK and EU had agreed a reset deal at the November 20 summit in Brussels, although the tuition treatment of European students remained unresolved. EU sources said the three reset agreements would not enter into force unless the UK reached a settlement with the bloc over tuition rates. UK officials said the fee issue was completely separate from the three plans.

UK Prime Minister Andy Burnham arrived at the summit venue on Friday morning less than an hour before a No 10 statement. A string quintet briefly played the UK and EU anthems as Burnham was greeted by European Commission president Ursula von der Leyen and European Council president António Costa. None of the leaders made a statement on arrival.

The EU wants European students to pay UK domestic rates, capped at £9,790 for the current academic year, rather than international rates that can be four or five times higher. In 11th-hour talks, the UK suggested tuition could go as low as £12,000 for EU students, according to two EU sources. The proposal was more than £2,000 above the charge for UK students. The EU continued to seek “equal treatment” for its students.

The reset described by Downing Street covered agreements on food and drink, emissions trading and a youth mobility scheme. The statement came shortly after headlines on Friday suggested “that the tuition fee issue could cause other parts of the process to collapse.” After the deal was announced, UK officials rejected the idea that the three plans could be jeopardised by a failure to agree on tuition fees, saying the issues were completely separate. Earlier, EU officials had indicated that progress on the main agreements was contingent on tuition fees.

The EU agreed the previous night to defer an agreement on tuition fees by six months, while talks on the issue were scheduled to last another six months.

Officials said new arrangements for food and agriculture would come into force in March 2028 regardless of an agreement on tuition fees.

Earlier, before the 11th-hour tuition talks, the UK and EU had agreed that 100,000 young people from each side could take part in a youth mobility scheme. The agreement allowed participants to live, work, travel and study on both sides of the Channel for up to three years.

Officials said the UK had secured support in principle for potential inclusion in the EU's yet-to-be-finalised “Made in Europe” policy. The Guardian described the policy as “a protectionist plan intended to limit China’s increasing influence on European industry.” One official called the potential inclusion “a big win,” especially because it was achieved before a tuition-fee agreement.

A No 10 statement said: “The deal is done – negotiations have concluded,” less than an hour after UK Prime Minister Andy Burnham arrived at the summit venue in Brussels on Friday morning, where Burnham was greeted by European Commission president Ursula von der Leyen and European Council president António Costa. The Friday-morning arrival of Burnham, von der Leyen and Costa was met with a brief burst of the UK and EU anthems from a string quintet. None of the leaders made a statement on arrival.

Burnham attended the summit with a Cabinet delegation that included Louise Haigh, the first secretary of state; John Healey, the chancellor; Ed Miliband, the foreign secretary; Jonathan Reynolds, the business secretary; Angela Eagle, the environment secretary; and Anneliese Midgley, the chief whip.

Speaking earlier to business leaders, Burnham said: “It just makes sense to get closer.” He described the summit as “a big day and hopefully a positive day” and said his aim was to “put the country on a better path, more hopeful about the future, more sense of possibility and positivity around the country.” Burnham said he wanted to break with “a decade of low, stagnant growth … [and] rising costs”.

Brussels insiders said on Friday that equal treatment for EU students at British universities would be essential for the success of the deal as a whole. A statement seen by The Guardian from the main EU institutions spelt out the link: “The Council and Commission consider that this agreement is to provide for equal treatment with nationals as regards tuition fees for all students. The Council and Commission acknowledge that these four agreements form a package of the 2026 EU-UK summit.” An EU official said: “I think our position has been clear. We think this is an issue that needs to be addressed.”

Even with the deal not yet finalised, Farage and other anti-Brexit politicians in Britain criticized the agreement. Nigel Farage, the leader of Reform UK, was in Brussels and was due to host a press conference later on Friday. Speaking to LBC radio, Farage said Burnham was “rejoining a chunk of the European Union’s single market,” which would result in the UK becoming a rule taker. “It is wholly unnecessary, and he’s being led by the nose by the European bureaucrats,” Farage said.

Although the No 10 statement said negotiations had concluded, the tuition question remained unresolved, and the two sides remained divided over whether the other three agreements depended on its outcome.
