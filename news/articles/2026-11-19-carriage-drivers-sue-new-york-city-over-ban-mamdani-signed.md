## Union argues the ban is unconstitutional and city violated local procedure

Central Park's horse-drawn carriage drivers sued New York City on Thursday, challenging Mayor Zohran Mamdani minutes after he signed a bill to wind down the industry. The Transport Workers Union, which represents the drivers, filed the federal lawsuit in Manhattan and asked that the ban be struck down, arguing the city is taking away their livelihoods. The union names Mamdani and other city officials as defendants and says the law is unconstitutional and was approved in violation of local legal procedures.

The law Mamdani signed at a bill signing ceremony at City Hall on Thursday is set to stop the carriage rides by June 2028. City lawmakers reignited their push for a ban earlier this year in response to the death of 18-year-old Indian tourist Romanch Mahajan, who died after jumping from a runaway carriage in what appeared to be the first fatality in a horse carriage accident since the rides were introduced in Central Park.

In the complaint, the union wrote that the city's approach could be summarized in a dozen words: "immediately cripple the owners, ignore property interests and contracts, and maybe compensate." The lawsuit argues the law is unconstitutional and that the city violated local legal procedures when it approved the law.

At the signing ceremony, Mamdani lauded the role horses played in the city's development but said the animals should no longer be hauling tourists around a perpetually busy Central Park among joggers, cyclists and other passersby. "Our city has changed and now it is time for our laws to change, too," said Mamdani, a Democrat.

The suit is the latest turn in a long fight over the carriage rides, in which animal welfare activists and industry supporters have tussled over whether the rides amount to animal cruelty or are simply a romantic throwback to the city's past.

Over the years, horses have collapsed or gotten startled and run off into cars or other objects. The city has responded with a range of safety regulations for the industry, including veterinary checkups, vacation time for horses and a retirement age for the animals.

City Hall has promised to release a career transition plan for those in the carriage industry next April that includes job training, educational pathways and stipends, though drivers argue their future remains uncertain. "Despite the good intentions of the mayor, the protections he outlined recently should have been written into the legislation," said John Samuelsen, head of the Transport Workers Union, which represents carriage drivers.

A spokesperson for the city's law department said it is reviewing the case. The filing puts the law's June 2028 wind-down before a federal judge in Manhattan, with the union seeking to stop the ban before it takes effect.
