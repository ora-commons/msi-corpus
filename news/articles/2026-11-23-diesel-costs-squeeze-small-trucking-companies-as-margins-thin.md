## Freight rates lag fuel bills for small carriers

U.S. diesel averaged $5.94 a gallon for the week of Nov. 16, about 56% more than before the U.S.-Iran war, the Energy Information Administration said. The increase came as the highly fragmented U.S. trucking industry showed tentative signs of recovery from a yearslong slump marked by weak demand and depressed rates. Small businesses make up most of the nation’s motor carriers, and high diesel prices placed new pressure on them. The war drove up one of their largest and least avoidable daily expenses.

DAT Freight & Analytics said large fleets typically have greater cash reserves, often buy diesel at bulk discounts and tend to negotiate long-term contracts with adjustable fuel surcharges. Many smaller operators negotiate rates one load at a time and must absorb higher pump prices while waiting three to four weeks for spot rates to catch up. Even when a rate includes a separate line item for fuel, carriers may wait 30 to 90 days to be paid.

“Those lags in the rates catching up to fuel are even more challenging if you’ve got to put food on the table and you don’t have a large company that’s helping carry over that difference,” Alex Leslie, senior research associate at the American Transportation Research Institute, said.

Kevin Otto, who took over the 80-year-old Otto Transfer from his parents in 2002, first told the Minnesota company’s 16 drivers to slow down when diesel prices topped $4 a gallon early in the U.S.-Iran war. He estimated that traveling at 65 miles per hour would allow the company’s flatbed fleet to get two-tenths of a mile more from every gallon and save $7,000 per truck. The prices kept climbing, and after diesel surpassed $6 a gallon in September, Otto added a fuel surcharge to every job for the first time.

Otto based the surcharge on the government’s weekly Midwest diesel average, starting at 5% and eventually raising it to 12.5%. “To me, it’s drastic, but it’s the only way to survive,” Otto said.

On-highway diesel reached a record national average of $6.53 in September, according to the Energy Information Administration. Gasoline averaged $3.81, about 30% higher than before the war, the agency said.

Getting goods hauled around the United States and Canada cost substantially more than it did a year earlier. Spot rates paid by freight brokers for individual loads were 44% to 48% higher in September, depending on the type of trailer, DAT said. The company estimated that diesel accounted for at least two-fifths of the year-over-year increase.

The American Transportation Research Institute said the rest of the increase did not necessarily translate into profit. Insurance, maintenance, equipment, tolls and other expenses were rising as the industry emerged from the lows of a freight recession.

The average cost of operating a truck reached a record $2.34 per mile in 2025, a 3.4% increase from the year before, while nonfuel costs rose 4.2%, the American Transportation Research Institute reported in July. Truckload and refrigerated carriers reported average operating margins of less than 1% in 2025, while flatbed carriers averaged a 0.5% operating loss.

The freight downturn began in early 2022, when a pandemic-era surge in demand faded and too many trucks were left chasing fewer loads. Industry data indicate that a shrinking supply of trucks, rather than a renewed boom in shipments, is contributing to higher freight rates, obscuring the strain on many trucking businesses.

At October’s average diesel price, a tractor-trailer getting six miles per gallon would have used $521 in fuel while traveling 500 miles a day, compared with $310 in February, DAT calculated. Monthly freight volumes were lower for all three major trailer types than a year earlier.

“Demand actually hasn’t increased that much, and the market for the last few years has been quite depressed,” Adam Parish, director of trucking procurement at Flexport, said. “And so there’s been a lot of pricing pressure from customers.”

Wes Oberman’s company in Huntingdon, Tennessee, works with 14 drivers who own their tractor-trailers but operate under Oberman Logistics’ federal motor-carrier authority as they haul steel and consumer goods across the South. The owner-operators take individually negotiated flat rates and buy diesel on the road with company discount cards. They receive 85% of each load’s gross revenue, from which Oberman deducts their fuel purchases and fixed expenses.

As diesel consumed more of each load’s revenue, Oberman became more selective about the jobs he accepted. He sometimes waited a day for a better-paying load or one requiring fewer “deadhead” miles, when a truck travels empty between jobs. “We just got to keep trucking, as they say, save every penny we can and just weather the storm until this diesel crisis goes away,” Oberman said.

In October, President Donald Trump sought to ease the pressure by directing the Internal Revenue Service to suspend federal penalties and reporting requirements through Dec. 31 for using dyed diesel on highways. Federal excise-tax payments were deferred, but state taxes and restrictions can still apply. The restricted fuel normally is reserved for tax-exempt off-road uses such as farming and is dyed red to make improper use easier for law enforcement to spot.

David McKinney, owner of McKinney & Sons Transfer in Lexington, Alabama, called the dyed-diesel plan “absurd” and doubted it would provide meaningful relief for small trucking companies. He sold two trucks in September to preserve cash and worried that residual dye in the tanks of his remaining 15 trucks could expose him to fines after the temporary policy expires. The IRS is considering a grace period.

McKinney has been in the trucking business for 30 years and has a fueling station at his shop, allowing him to pay less for diesel than his drivers would at truck stops. Even so, he said, the company was only breaking even. “Our bottom line is: For the past four months, we have survived. We have not profited any money,” McKinney said. “Rates need to be higher, but the trucking business is so competitive, it keeps it down.”

In Delano, Minnesota, Otto worried that his new surcharge would cost him customers. His long-term clients accepted the added charge, but he had previously adjusted freight rates only once a year. Otto usually gives drivers who haul utility poles around the country a bonus at the end of the year, but rising costs left him unsure whether he could afford the bonus this year.

The Energy Information Administration expects diesel prices to ease next year as more Middle East oil production outside Iran recovers. In November, the agency raised its 2027 forecast by 54 cents to an average of $5.03 a gallon, citing continued constraints on global refined-fuel supplies. It warned that shipping disruptions could still produce short-term price swings. “It’s a five-alarm fire, absolutely,” Otto said. “It is unsustainable, and I fear that it will only get worse.”
