## Board weighs pumping cap after aquifer study flags sinkage risks

A joint investigation by ProPublica, KRIS 6 News and The Texas Tribune found that the Corpus Christi Aquifer Storage and Recovery Conservation District approved every city request for additional groundwater wells after the City Council selected it as Corpus Christi’s well regulator. City Council members appoint the district’s board, and city employees make up its entire staff. The investigation was co-published as part of an initiative to report on state and federal efforts to restrict local control.

The aquifer at issue spans dozens of counties across the Texas Gulf Coast and serves dozens of communities along the Gulf of Mexico, including some in Louisiana, while groundwater makes up more than half of Texas’ water supply. In Texas, fights over limited groundwater supplies will likely intensify as the population booms. Aquifers stretch underground across counties and, sometimes, across states, and overpumping is a key concern in Texas because aquifers need rain and time to refill. Texas’ water plan shows larger drops in groundwater availability compared with other sources as droughts lengthen. Jarrod Reynolds, a groundwater resource engineer, said excessive pumping in worst-case scenarios “can wipe an aquifer out.” He said groundwater “can be a stopgap, it can be a supplement, but it’s something that’s got to be managed.”

The investigation described Corpus Christi’s rapid expansion of groundwater wells as a critical, if not extreme, example of the challenges presented by a patchwork system for regulating the water that cities, businesses and citizens can extract from the ground.

Corpus Christi turned to groundwater late in 2024 as its main reservoirs were drying up after years of drought. The coastal city of more than 300,000 residents nearly reached a crisis point earlier this year after persistent drought and years of inaction; it was months away from being unable to meet water demand and faced harsh restrictions to conserve supplies. Rural residents worried the drilling plan would threaten their access to water and mobilized to create a groundwater conservation district that would regulate drilling and, they hoped, limit how much water the city could pump.

Fearing the residents’ effort could hamper the city’s plans, officials asked the storage and recovery district to expand its boundaries to include well fields outside the city limits in Nueces County. Then-Corpus Christi City Attorney Miles Risley called the rural residents’ effort a “potential danger” to the city’s plans at an October 2025 City Council meeting. During the debate, Council Member Everett Roy asked water resource manager Esteban Ramos whether the district could carry out the request without being biased. “Yes, I do,” Ramos answered. Ramos also serves as the district’s general manager.

The investigation found that the district fast-tracked the city’s permitting process and overlooked steps that would have slowed approvals, including safeguards the district’s own rules require to protect other well users. When the district approved the city’s well permits last year, board members attached several protective requirements, including plans to monitor water quality in the well fields and reduce harm to neighbors’ wells. None of those requirements prevented or slowed the city from moving forward with its drilling plans. A document prepared by two board members for the district said the city had not “fully satisfied” some requirements nine months later. The city said the district had asked it to provide more information about its monitoring and mitigation plans. The city said it was working with the district to meet the conditions and that its compliance reports were public.

By the time a city-commissioned study was released this summer, the permits already allowed the city to pump up to about 40 million gallons a day from 20 wells, although the city draws less than a third of that amount on a typical day. The city defended the approvals. It said the district’s rules allow its board to decide when it has enough information to approve a permit and that the city provides estimates of pumping effects beforehand.

In a written statement, Ashley Marion, strategic business manager for the city water department, said, “There is no conflict of interest. City employees involved with the District have no personal or financial interest in permitting decisions.” She added that, “The City and District share an interest in protecting and sustainably managing regional groundwater.” Marion also said the city tracks water levels and physical conditions at the wells and adjusts pumping rates based on daily data and long-term modeling.

Drew Molly, the former chief operating officer of Corpus Christi’s water department and now chief water officer at Houston’s water department, called the amount the city was permitted to pump “absolutely unfathomable.” He said the district’s “lightning speed” approvals were likely to cause ripple effects locally and across Texas. Robert Mace, executive director at Texas State University’s Meadows Center for Water and the Environment, which focuses on water and environmental management policy, said, “That’s the fox guarding the henhouse.” He said the arrangement indicated that the district was considering Corpus Christi’s interests rather than necessarily those of other landowners who could be affected.

The governance structure is unusual. The storage and recovery district is one of nearly 100 groundwater districts in Texas and the only one whose leaders are appointed by a City Council. Most other districts are led by elected officials or people appointed by county officials. Counties are not water suppliers, so their appointments raise less concern about conflicts of interest.

A study from the private research company Intera, released by the city in June, estimated that ground around the city’s western well fields could sink about 20 inches over the next 30 years. Just a few inches of sinkage can do costly damage to infrastructure, including pipelines, foundations, roads and bridges. The American Geosciences Institute said this type of sinkage, caused by compacting sand, rock or sediment, can reduce the amount of water an aquifer can hold over the long term. A drop in the aquifer’s levels would first be felt by rural residents, whose wells are not as deep as the city’s. The study said a drop of up to 100 feet was possible within five years near the city’s wells. The city said the findings were projections that did not reflect its average pumping, but it can easily pump more in emergencies, such as when pipes break or during long dry spells.

For rural residents like Scott Barraza, declining water levels raised concerns about their wells. Barraza, who lives on 20 acres about 20 miles from downtown Corpus Christi with his wife, Julie; teen daughter, Grace; five dogs; three goats; and a horse named Rosie, recently bought a $9,000 water-purification system and a trailer for hauling water from the city for his family and neighbors. He acknowledged the preparation might be excessive but said it was better to be ready. “When you live out in the country, you don’t have a city water supply. Your well is your water supply,” he said.

Corpus Christi’s approach to finding water has swung widely during drought, and the city currently relies on three drought-vulnerable reservoirs for most of its supply. Despite warnings that it needed to diversify its water sources, the city has repeatedly waffled on whether to invest in long-term solutions. Since 2025, a divided City Council has twice declined to advance a city-run desalination plant, even though Texas had approved $757 million in loans and city officials had selected contractors to begin the work. The city said the plant could produce around a third of daily demand. After the September vote, outgoing Gov. Greg Abbott said the city had “once again failed to secure reliable water.” Molly said Corpus Christi’s decision to increasingly rely on groundwater rather than invest in a seawater desalination plant means the city “basically put all their eggs in a terrible basket.”

The storage and recovery district itself was created two decades earlier through legislation authored by Democratic state Sen. Juan “Chuy” Hinojosa. Its original purpose was to inject and store excess drinking water and treated wastewater underground for emergency use. Hinojosa did not respond to a request for comment. The district studied the idea, drilled test wells and drew up plans, but the storage project never advanced.

As the region’s main reservoirs were drying up in late 2024, city leaders were searching for solutions again. Molly, then the city’s water director, had a “wild idea.” He reached out to John Michael, an engineer long involved in the city’s water projects, to ask if the city could drill wells along the Nueces River, according to a recent memo in which Michael recalled the conversation. It was possible, and the city quickly started drilling, but the wells were always supposed to be a temporary solution for emergency supply. In the June 26 memo, Michael wrote that the wells were “a viable option to supplement water resources during the current drought” but that he “never envisioned that the wells would be used for anything other than emergency supply.” In a written response, the city called the groundwater program “a key component” of its strategy to diversify the region’s water supply.

Michael joined the district’s board five weeks after it approved the city’s first 20 well permits. He and board member Jeff Edmonds, who is also the city’s director of engineering, proposed capping the city’s pumping at 6.5 million gallons a day, half its current average, until it meets the requirements intended to protect the aquifer. The board is scheduled to discuss the resolution Dec. 3. The two members said the current pumping rate could cause “irreversible offsite impacts” and pointed to civil penalties the city could face for continued noncompliance.

Republican state Rep. Denise Villalobos had planned to file a bill in the upcoming legislative session to abolish the storage and recovery district. She also had drafted another bill to create, in its place, a more traditional groundwater district that would regulate wells across all of Nueces County. She lost her seat to Democrat Stephanie Guerrero Sáenz in the recent midterm elections. Guerrero Sáenz, whose term begins in January, was less certain about her approach and said she needed to consult her constituents. She said any legislation she supports “must strike the right balance between protecting rural groundwater rights and addressing the urgent water needs of our region.”

Summer rains temporarily refilled the reservoirs and averted the recent crisis, but projections show Corpus Christi could face another water emergency by September 2029. The Texas Water Development Board has said Texas does not have enough water to meet demand if it faces a historic drought. Michael said the dispute was “a microcosm of examples of what’s happening around the state” because Texas’ population is growing as available water resources are dwindling. He said, “We have to manage these districts in a sustainable manner” and “decisions have to be made on science and not emotions.”
