## Tests find high salmonella rates in Butterball's ground and chopped turkey

**Subtype:** fact

- SourceMaterial's analysis of USDA inspection data found just under 43% of ground or chopped turkey samples from Butterball's four slaughterhouses contained salmonella over the past year.
- Food Safety and Inspection Service inspectors found salmonella in 16 of 45 ground or chopped turkey samples at Butterball's Mount Olive plant, a 36% rate above the USDA's 13.5% limit.
- SourceMaterial found no salmonella in Butterball's whole birds or ready-to-eat products.
- Butterball's Mount Olive plant processes 285,000 birds a week and sells raw ground turkey, burgers and sausages nationwide.
- USDA requires plants above the 13.5% salmonella limit to explain how they are addressing contamination but does not impose an automatic fine or recall.
- Butterball's slaughterhouses recorded 65% resistance to at least one antibiotic among positive samples, compared with 61% at Jennie-O.

## Exceeding the USDA limit requires an explanation, not a fine or recall

An analysis by SourceMaterial, published in partnership with The Guardian, examined fiscal year 2025 and 2026 inspection data from the US Department of Agriculture's Food Safety and Inspection Service that Project Slingshot provided. SourceMaterial verified the data and found that ground or chopped turkey contamination at all four slaughterhouses owned by Butterball, described as the country's biggest turkey company, was well above the industry average. Just under 43% of samples collected over the past year contained salmonella.

The Food Safety and Inspection Service and some states conduct regular checks for salmonella and other bacteria at commercial poultry plants. The report noted that salmonella can live in poultry intestines and can cause nausea, diarrhea and death when meat is not cooked properly before consumption. The findings concerned ground and chopped turkey; SourceMaterial reported no contamination in Butterball's whole birds or ready-to-eat cooked products.

At Butterball's Mount Olive factory in North Carolina, described as the nation's biggest turkey slaughterhouse, Food Safety and Inspection Service inspectors found salmonella in 16 of 45 ground or chopped turkey samples, or 36%. The USDA's acceptable limit for salmonella contamination in an individual plant is 13.5%. A slaughterhouse does not face a fine or a required product recall solely for exceeding that limit, but the USDA requires the plant to explain how it is addressing the issue.

The Mount Olive plant processes 285,000 birds a week and sells raw ground turkey, burgers and sausages nationwide. Butterball supplies Walmart, Costco and Kroger, the three biggest US supermarkets, as well as fast-food companies.

A Butterball spokesperson said in response to the contamination data: “At Butterball, food safety is critically important, and we work diligently to ensure consumers can feel confident in the products they prepare and serve to family and friends.” The spokesperson said the company continues to invest in new technologies, strengthen processes and work with food safety experts to reduce pathogens, including salmonella. Butterball is participating in a Food Safety and Inspection Service pilot program “evaluating the cause of foodborne illness” and also noted that none of its precooked products contained salmonella.

Alice Johnson, Butterball's senior vice-president of food safety, quality and animal care, said the company believes the Food Safety and Inspection Service should measure the quantity of salmonella in samples rather than simply whether it is present. Experts interviewed by SourceMaterial disagreed. Sarah Sorscher, director of regulatory affairs at the Center for Science in the Public Interest, said: “Yes, having less harmful salmonella contamination is better than having more of it.” Sorscher added: “You know what is even better still? Having no salmonella in your food. That’s sadly not where we are right now.” Sorscher also said ground and chopped turkey may be more prone to contamination because “every processing step is another opportunity for the meat to become contaminated.”

At Butterball's main rival, Jennie-O, 7% of ground and chopped turkey samples tested positive for pathogens in the last year. A Jennie-O spokesperson said the company's food-safety program was “robust” and that its results “compare favorably to industry averages and remain well below applicable regulatory performance standards.” Government data covering the entire US meat industry showed an average 12% of samples contaminated with any pathogen and 33% of pathogens resistant to drugs.

The tests also found evidence of drug-resistant superbugs. Two-thirds of samples taken from birds' intestines across all of Butterball's slaughterhouses in fiscal year 2026 that contained salmonella and campylobacter, another pathogen that can sicken humans, showed strains resistant to at least one antibiotic. Doctors often use antibiotics to treat severe cases of both infections. Johnson said the resistance tests use samples from whole birds and therefore do not necessarily show contamination in finished poultry products.

The share of positive samples resistant to at least one antibiotic drug was comparable at Butterball and Jennie-O: 65% at Butterball's slaughterhouses, compared with 61% at Jennie-O. Other major suppliers also recorded similar levels of contamination. California-based Foster Farms registered a 29% contamination rate, with 55% of pathogens resistant, while Illinois-based Koch Foods had a 27% contamination rate and 51% resistance. Neither Foster Farms nor Koch Foods responded to requests for comment.

Steve Roach, program director at Food Animal Concerns Trust and senior analyst for the Keep Antibiotics Working coalition, said: “Antibiotic resistance doesn’t spontaneously generate in wildlife. It’s a result of antibiotic use on farms.” Roach noted that resistance in salmonella is associated with longer disease duration and “more likelihood of hospitalization, and also more likelihood of death.” Sorscher said: “I don’t see how we can call our food system the safest in the world when we don’t have standards to prevent people from getting sick from eating the poultry.”

Thomas Gremillion, director of food policy at the Consumer Federation of America, said fast line speeds worsen salmonella contamination. The Trump administration has allowed poultry processors to boost line speeds to kill more animals per minute, according to the report. Gremillion said faster speeds make it harder “to keep the animal viscera and what’s in the digestive tract from getting on the meat,” adding that the risk exists because “that’s where the pathogenic bacteria concentrate.”

Dani Replogle, an attorney at the non-profit Food & Water Watch, said faster production means “more antibiotics in feed and in water.” According to the report, that is at odds with Donald Trump's promise to get chemicals out of our food, and Trump has yet to move to reduce antibiotic use in the food supply.

Each year, about 25,500 Americans are hospitalized after exposure to salmonella and campylobacter, leading to 435 deaths, according to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. In 2019, Butterball recalled almost 80,000 pounds (36,000 kilograms) of turkey products from Mount Olive after an outbreak of illness across two states, likely caused by salmonella linked to the company's products.

Government figures cited in the report showed that meat companies commonly feed antibiotics to all animals, not just sick ones. In 2024, pharmaceutical companies sold 16% more drugs to the animal agriculture industry than in the previous year, according to US Food and Drug Administration data. Replogle said: “Pharmaceutical companies wanted to expand their markets, so they started marketing their products as a way to prevent disease.” Replogle also said antimicrobial resistance is outpacing the development of new antibiotics in a way that is “very scary” for public health.

The Animal Health Institute, which represents companies making drugs for use in animals, defended giving healthy animals antibiotics to prevent disease. The institute argued the meat industry makes a small contribution to antibiotic resistance in humans compared with other sources, such as humans transmitting resistant pathogens to other humans. The World Health Organization “strongly recommends” against routine use of antibiotics on healthy animals.

A Butterball spokesperson said 60% of the company's turkeys did not receive antibiotics last year. Johnson said: “Butterball follows responsible antibiotic use practices that support both animal health and public health.” A Jennie-O spokesperson said the company supports responsible antibiotic stewardship through veterinary oversight and judicious use in accordance with regulatory requirements and animal-health best practices.

Naomi Hallum, co-founder of Project Slingshot, said: “The superbugs are not a contamination problem; they are a factory-farming problem.” Hallum added: “You cannot keep turkeys alive without antibiotics if you are going to cram them by the thousands inside overcrowded industrial sheds. Antibiotic-resistant disease is an inevitable outcome.” A Butterball spokesperson said the company “adheres to science-based animal welfare standards, including requirements for appropriate space allowances that support natural movement, mobility and overall bird health and wellbeing.”
