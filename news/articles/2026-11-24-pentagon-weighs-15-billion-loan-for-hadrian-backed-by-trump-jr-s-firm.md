## Documents outline a dozen factories and unresolved Pentagon concerns

The Pentagon is considering a potential $15 billion loan for Hadrian Automation, a California startup with financial ties to Donald Trump Jr. through 1789 Capital, according to interviews and records reviewed by ProPublica. If approved, the deal would be one of the biggest federal awards to a company connected to the Trump family and could rank among the largest government loans in U.S. history. A Pentagon spokesperson disputed the reported amount and described the discussions as “early stages.”

Hadrian has attracted over a billion dollars in private investment in recent months on the promise that it can build factories using AI and robots to make precision components for defense, aerospace and other industries. A financing round this summer included “major participation by 1789 Capital,” which also invested in Hadrian in 2025; Trump Jr. joined 1789 as a partner after his father was elected in November 2024. A 1789 spokesperson said the firm holds a “minority stake” whose size has not been disclosed. Based on estimates from the summer financing, the proposed loan would be almost twice Hadrian's value and 10 times the size of the largest deal in the Pentagon's recent lending expansion.

Pentagon documents say the financing would build a dozen “automated factories” in partnership with other defense companies and potentially commercial companies to expand production of munitions, drones and other equipment. The U.S. military has faced a severe shortage of some types of munitions because of the wars in Ukraine and Iran, although it is unclear whether the proposed Hadrian factories are intended to address those particular shortages.

Hadrian has a largely untested track record and has shared few details publicly about what its AI-powered factories have produced. Pentagon staff raised several concerns about the company, including its ability to expand and hire quickly. In testimony before Congress last year, founder Chris Power said only that Hadrian had successfully fabricated parts for “missile systems, fighter jets, spacecraft and naval vessels.” This year, Hadrian announced new plant openings faster than outside analysts had been able to assess how well its operations were working. Mesa officials celebrated the opening of the company's third factory in January, and the ribbon cutting for its fourth, in Alabama for the Navy, took place in March, while the company said full production would begin in a couple of years.

Australian-born founder Chris Power started Hadrian in 2020, saying he moved to the United States with just $6,000 and began cold-calling manufacturers to understand their operations and identify production bottlenecks. He found that much of the aerospace and defense industry relied on small shops with experienced workers near retirement and decided to automate production. Hadrian executives told Forbes in 2024 that the company spent its first two years trying to replicate components for two companies using its automation software in a small demonstration factory in California. Hadrian never named the businesses or components. It hired employees away from Elon Musk's companies SpaceX and Tesla and from defense contractors Anduril, Raytheon and Palantir. Hadrian also drew investments from Andreessen Horowitz and Altimeter Capital and has a few hundred employees by outside estimates, though it has not released a recent employee count.

In March, Hadrian announced a submarine-parts agreement under which the Navy committed up to $900 million. At the ribbon cutting for the first of three factories planned for submarine parts and systems, Power said, “The number-one problem that the Navy’s identified is we can’t find enough skilled workers, so we have to automate our way out of this problem.” The factory will run on Hadrian's AI software, called Opus, which Power said would make his production line more efficient and nimble. The Navy “can’t hire enough people and the primes can’t hire enough people because there aren’t enough trained people to do it,” Power said, referring to major defense contractors. Power said the approach would “have people running multiple robots in a new way of doing things versus replacing jobs.” Campaign filings show that Hadrian's CEO donated nearly a half million dollars to Republican groups in July.

1789 Capital's first investment in Hadrian was announced in July 2025 in a round co-led by Peter Thiel's Founders Fund. Hadrian said it would use the money to expand its shipbuilding and naval-defense capabilities and build a factory in Arizona. The firm's second investment was announced in August in a round that included institutional investment managers. “This is just the beginning,” 1789 wrote on LinkedIn about the financing.

Former Defense Department officials said the loan's size and structure could run afoul of long-standing guidance for protecting taxpayer money. Under the structure they described, the government would use taxpayer money to expand a fledgling defense contractor and then presumably award contracts to the company, effectively giving Hadrian money it could use to repay the loan. The proposal to expand Hadrian in hopes that it would become a major source of military munitions also raised Pentagon concerns that the company would become largely reliant on the federal government. Former officials said the U.S. government might face allegations that it would be giving a distinct advantage to Hadrian over potential competitors. Other businesses could challenge whether the company is winning bids or subcontracting work on its merits or so that taxpayers do not lose on their capital investment.

The Pentagon's Office of Strategic Capital was created during the Biden administration to fill gaps in the U.S. industrial base and reduce dependence on China for military equipment. Under Trump, the lending unit expanded and focused on loans for private companies developing rare-earth minerals and other technologies with commercial and military applications. The administration's tariff wars heightened concerns that China could cut off critical minerals needed for high-tech weapons, and firms receiving the loans generally supply both defense contractors and other private-sector businesses. Congress prohibited the office from financing projects with only military applications. A Pentagon document says Hadrian's factories would expand the U.S. capability to manufacture munitions and precision components for military use and could “pivot rapidly to commercial applications.”

Former defense officials said a multibillion-dollar investment could push the Office of Strategic Capital beyond the purpose for which Congress approved and funded it. Political appointee David Lorch, the office's head, would have to sign off on the loan. Lorch previously worked at Cerberus, the private-equity firm founded by Deputy Defense Secretary Steve Feinberg; it is unclear whether Feinberg or other administration officials would also need to approve a deal of this size. A former defense official familiar with the office said the proposal could undermine its credibility with the incoming Congress, which would likely have to approve more funding for new loans. “OSC is clearly a powerful tool to advance national security. But its ability to invest and access to long-term funding are tenuous,” the former official said. “Some senior leaders seemingly want to use it as a pot of money for deals that exceed its mandate.”

It is unclear when the Pentagon began considering Hadrian, although defense documents cite the August funding round in which 1789 participated. It is also unclear whether the White House played a role in Hadrian's consideration. Defense documents mention the “White House and DoW leadership’s expressed need for advanced manufacturing,” an area in which Hadrian specializes. An administration official said Hadrian approached the Pentagon with a proposed loan and that “It is at an extremely early stage.” The documents ProPublica reviewed nevertheless contained proposed terms and discussions of the loan's merits and risks.

The Pentagon said no company receives preferential treatment. “Outside affiliations, investors, or political connections play absolutely no role in the Department's funding decisions,” the department said. A spokesperson also characterized the size of the Hadrian loan under discussion as not extraordinary, saying multibillion-dollar deals with other companies had been agreed to but had not been announced. 1789 Capital said it does not get involved in government contracts sought by companies in which it invests but is excited about Hadrian. “Our thesis is to invest in American companies that are rebuilding domestic industrial capacity and reducing dependence on China for critical supply chains that underpin our economy. Hadrian embodies the opportunity we see to strengthen America's productive capabilities while pursuing compelling returns for our investors,” 1789 said. Trump Jr.'s lawyer, Alan Garten, said his client played no role in Hadrian being considered for a contract and had no involvement in the company's operations. “Don is a private citizen who has been building businesses and investing in companies since long before his father first ran for public office. He holds no government position and has never sought to influence government policies, regulations or decisions to advance or benefit his personal business interests,” Garten said. Hadrian did not respond to requests for comment.

The proposal is under consideration weeks before Democrats take control of both chambers of Congress. Party leaders have vowed to use their new oversight powers to scrutinize contracts and other administration actions that have financially benefited the Trump family, administration officials and people with ties to the administration. Since President Donald Trump's second term began, companies in which his elder sons hold stakes have won billions of dollars in federal backing. ProPublica recently reported nearly $40 million in federal support for a company that screens for Ebola and other infectious diseases. It previously reported that the Office of Strategic Capital was behind the decision to give hundreds of millions of dollars in loans to Vulcan Elements, a rare-earth magnet startup, a few months after 1789 Capital invested in the company. The earlier report said a White House aide intervened to secure a $620 million Pentagon loan.

At the time of the Vulcan Elements loan, it was the only deal among dozens the Pentagon was considering that a top aide to the president brought forward, ProPublica reported. After receiving the White House request, defense officials asked Pentagon staff to move at an unusually rapid pace, and staff worked late nights and with little sleep to complete the loan in a matter of weeks. The Pentagon denied preferential treatment, and Trump Jr. denied any role. Trump Jr. and Eric Trump have previously described themselves as passive investors who are not involved in federal award decisions and said they do not consider it a conflict for companies in which they hold interests to win government contracts. In the past, Trump Jr. and 1789 Capital have said he does not discuss the companies he invests in with federal officials. Trump Jr. has expressed personal interest in defense technology and has criticized cronyism in defense spending.

Venture capital firms invested $33 billion in companies combining AI and robotics during the first half of 2026, more than in all of the previous year, according to PitchBook data. Analysts have said they expect only a handful of defense companies to succeed, followed by consolidation, and a major Defense Department contract could position Hadrian among the winners. The proposal remains in early stages, and the loan amount, White House involvement, prospective contract awards and the proposed factories' commercial role remain unresolved.
