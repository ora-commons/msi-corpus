## Bill targets oil wells, geothermal heating and data-center electricity

At a press conference in a home being remodeled in north Bismarck, Republican Sen. John Hoeven said the Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act could lower costs for homebuyers and builders and ease inflation. The bipartisan bill passed the Senate 62-33 on Thursday, sending it to the House during the lame-duck period of Congress before Democrats take control in January. Hoeven said the measure addresses “massive quality of life issues.”

Hoeven said the bill should help young people afford their first house, build equity and work up to their “dream home.” “This is one of the biggest issues we face, is we’ve got to find ways to produce more homes and make them more affordable,” he said.

Hoeven said permitting has delayed $1.1 trillion of projects around the U.S. He said government regulations make up about 25% of the costs for building single-family homes and 40% for apartments. Timelines and approvals also drive up costs, he said. The legislation seeks to reduce those burdens, including by reducing the time periods for environmental reviews.

The bill includes legislation by Hoeven that would streamline permitting for oil and gas on lands where the federal government owns no surface acres and less than half the mineral acres, leaving the state to permit wells. Hoeven said the state permits an oil well in about 40 days, while the federal process takes more than nine months.

Another provision by Hoeven would enable and expedite permitting of geothermal wells for heating. He said provisions in the bill were estimated to save 3.5% to 7% on energy costs.

The bill also would require data centers to cover the cost of the electricity they use and the construction of transmission infrastructure. Hoeven said those requirements were meant to protect neighborhoods and ratepayers.

Opponents said the bill would weaken the National Environmental Policy Act and hurt Native American tribes in the consultation process.

Joe Hillerson, owner of Boulder Homes in Bismarck-Mandan, said he hopes the bill is the beginning of a framework for easier development. “This addresses developing, land development, getting more lots online for cheaper, quicker, so I’m hoping that is what ends up happening,” Hillerson said. “We’d love to see the cost of our home come down. It seems like affordability is at an all-time low, even for rent.”

If the bill becomes law, Hoeven said the timeline for consumers would be hard to predict. Relief could be felt immediately or in the near term of months, he said, citing the drop in diesel and gasoline prices since the Nov. 3 election as a comparison. “My point is you can’t always specify the timing,” he said. “What you’ve got to do is put the right policies in place and then pursue them relentlessly, and it will happen.”
