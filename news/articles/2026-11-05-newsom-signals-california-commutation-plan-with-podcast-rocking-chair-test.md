## Shafer says post-election delay aimed to keep commutations from becoming an issue

Newsom had been thinking about the decision for a long time and had signaled it on his podcast last month, framing it as a "rocking chair test" — how he would feel when he is old and sitting in a rocking chair reflecting on his life. Shafer said Newsom sees opposition to the death penalty as fundamental to his values and his faith, and that the announcement was anticipated by those following the issue. Newsom and his team waited until just after Tuesday's election to announce the commutations — a delay Shafer said was meant to keep the move from becoming an issue. The announcement converts all 548 death sentences on the books in California to life in prison without the possibility of parole.

Newsom said California's death row, at 548 people, is the largest in the Western Hemisphere. In a statement explaining the decision, the governor called the death penalty "a failure," said it is "immoral for the state to murder somebody" and said it "isn't applied fairly anyway," according to Scott Shafer of member station KQED in San Francisco, who reported the action for NPR. California has not carried out an execution in 20 years, Shafer said.

The commutations go further than the moratorium Newsom imposed two months after he became governor in 2019. That earlier step put executions on hold, and his administration dismantled the execution chamber at San Quentin prison and moved death row inmates from San Quentin to other maximum-security prisons across the state.

Newsom also explained why he commuted every sentence rather than a subset. He said he did not want to pick and choose which death sentences to commute and which to let stand, because doing so could inadvertently create "two classes of murder victims" — an outcome he did not want, Shafer said.

Newsom met with many crime victims before he issued the 2019 moratorium — conversations Shafer described as very hard and very raw. He met with many of those same people again over the past months as he considered the commutation decision. In his statement, Newsom spoke to them, saying the commutations could "resurface excruciating memories," and Shafer said the governor does not dismiss that in any way.

Politically, NPR's Ailsa Chang noted that Newsom is often mentioned as a possible candidate for president in 2028. Some advisers in Newsom's political orbit urged him not to take the step as he gets ready to run for president. One adviser said that when Newsom took a similar step seven years ago, he never asked to see any polls or look at anything related to the politics of it. Nationally, Gallup has polled on the death penalty for decades, and support stands at its lowest point in decades, Shafer said. For that reason, he said, it is not really clear Newsom will pay a political price for the commutations.

The announcement also came during a renewed national debate over capital punishment. NPR pointed to a recent botched execution attempt of a woman convicted of murder in Tennessee and to the Trump administration's talk of livestreaming an execution at Fort Hood — a proposal that President Trump later rejected.

Shafer said that in some ways the new action is symbolic, since no executions are on the horizon anyway. But by commuting hundreds of sentences, it might give momentum to death penalty opponents in other states as they lobby governors there to do something similar, he said. The step does not eliminate the death penalty in California; only the voters can do that, Shafer said.
