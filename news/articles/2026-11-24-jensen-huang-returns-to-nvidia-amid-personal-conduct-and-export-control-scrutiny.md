## Trump’s intervention preceded a 31.5% Nvidia share gain

Jensen Huang returned to Nvidia on Monday as chief executive after President Donald Trump said he had lobbied the board over the weekend. Investors pushed Nvidia shares up 31.5%, and employees chanted Huang’s name when he returned to headquarters. Huang had resigned Nov. 12, and his return restored him to a company described by supporters as indispensable to the artificial-intelligence supply chain.

The campaign to restore Huang began soon after his resignation. A petition circulated in Silicon Valley, with employees and celebrity leaders including SpaceX CEO Elon Musk pressing for his return. Huang wrote on social media early the following week, “I would be honored to answer these calls and return as CEO,” while acknowledging he had made “serious mistakes.” Nvidia initially refused his offer.

Trump said on CNBC, “The board decided to do what we wanted them to do,” and added, “I believe my intervention was very important.” He also suggested Huang’s return should be celebrated by the “people of the United States — and, indeed, the World!”

The intervention came amid a broad market selloff. Nvidia stock was down a third since Nov. 9, and by Friday the S&P 500 had fallen more than 10% from its recent high, which the Associated Press defined as a market correction. Fears that an AI bubble was popping mounted. On Monday, the S&P 500 jumped 4.7%, recovering more than half the previous week’s loss; the report described that week as one of the worst since the 2008 financial crisis. Trump said the falling market probably influenced his thinking about bringing Huang back. FRED’s raw [SP500 series](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/SP500) listed the S&P 500 at 6,910.39 as of Nov. 24, 2026.

Nvidia’s board said it “received input from senior levels of the United States government, and based on that input, the Board is confident that Mr. Huang’s reinstatement poses no national security risk.” The Associated Press reported that investigations into Huang, Nvidia’s board and Trump administration officials who approved Nvidia chip sales were looming.

The Associated Press also reported that Huang likely violated Nvidia’s code of conduct. Huang acknowledged having an affair with a woman suspected of being a Chinese spy. The report said his second tenure was not guaranteed to survive congressional investigations, lawsuits and a probe by a law firm hired by Nvidia’s board.

Supporters described Huang as an extraordinary dealmaker. Musk wrote on X, “Jensen drove supply chain brilliantly at Nvidia, but many of those deals were a handshake with Jensen, not a written contract,” adding, “Without Jensen, at least some of those critical deals will be lost.”

Huang ran Nvidia like a small business, with 60 people reporting directly to him and three family members employed by the company. He said of TSMC, the Taiwanese chipmaker, “We don’t have a contract, and that’s pretty great.” Jeffrey Sonnenfeld, a leadership expert at the Yale School of Management, said the lack of contracts for hundreds of millions of dollars of business was “unparalleled in history — and there’s no reason for it.” He called the 60-person direct-report structure “absurd” and said it meant nobody had the big picture perspective.

CEO comebacks are not unprecedented: the Associated Press cited Steve Jobs at Apple and Sam Altman at OpenAI. But the report said comebacks involving sex or national security have few precedents. McDonald’s CEO was removed in 2019 over an affair and gave up more than $100 million in stock compensation. Millions were stripped from BP’s top executive’s pay package after that executive resigned over an affair, and the head of Norfolk Southern lost his job in 2024 following an affair. Trump said, “We have to protect our geniuses,” then added, “We don’t have enough of them.”

Huang’s return also restored him to a company operating a financing model that Nvidia itself has identified as risky. Many AI companies buying Nvidia chips, including the newly public Anthropic, do not make a profit and have been burning through cash quickly, the Associated Press reported. Nvidia used its resources to help them buy chips by striking deals to buy computing capacity and other services from them in turn. The arrangements are called circular financing, and Nvidia warned in a recent report that it might have no use for “all the capacity we are committed to purchase.”

Kathleen Brooks, an XTB analyst, said Huang was considered key to keeping the financial ecosystem running. She wrote, “His return suggests more of the same when it comes to circular financing and the rapid AI infrastructure build out,” adding, “The market loves it.” Cantor Fitzgerald financial analyst C.J. Muse wrote, “All is right in the world again,” and “a very happy day.” The optimism did not settle the risk: the Associated Press said fears of an AI bubble remained rife and the investigations were still ahead.

One focus of the looming investigations is Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick, who is in charge of export controls, has promoted Huang and whose former company, Cantor, helps finance Nvidia customers. Sen. Elizabeth Warren, a Massachusetts Democrat who called Huang’s restoration “reckless,” said the banking committee she will lead next year would examine whether Nvidia complied with export controls. She also said the Trump administration needed to explain what input was given and by which senior U.S. officials to support Huang’s reinstatement.

The immediate reaction contrasted with the unresolved questions ahead: a 31.5% rise in Nvidia shares, a 4.7% gain for the S&P 500 and employees’ chants at headquarters on one side; a board-hired law-firm probe, lawsuits, congressional scrutiny and export-control questions on the other. Huang regained control of an AI business built on influential supply-chain deals and circular customer financing, but his conduct, those arrangements and the government’s role remained unresolved.
