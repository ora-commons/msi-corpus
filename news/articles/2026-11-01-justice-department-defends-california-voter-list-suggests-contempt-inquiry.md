## CHIRLA identified 22 likely naturalized U.S. citizens; thousands more appeared to be listed

On Sunday, the Justice Department defended the accuracy of a list of what it says are more than 20,000 suspected noncitizens registered to vote in California. The department said CHIRLA's analysis had nothing to do with the group's legal standing and therefore violated U.S. District Court Chief Judge James Boasberg's order.

CHIRLA's attorneys filed court papers on Saturday revealing the results of their analysis of the federal list. The analysis found that more than 40% of the people on the list did not appear in California's voter file. It identified 22 people on the list who were likely naturalized U.S. citizens and said thousands more U.S. citizens appeared to be on it.

Boasberg, who is based in Washington, D.C., had allowed CHIRLA access to federal government data on 20,000 people the Trump administration alleges are noncitizen voters in California. On Friday, he ordered the plaintiffs not to disclose any names on the list and to use the data only for arguments about why they had legal standing to challenge the administration's use of it.

The Justice Department's Sunday filing said the analysis had nothing to do with CHIRLA's legal standing and therefore violated Boasberg's order. “The report’s political grandstanding about alleged inaccuracies that purportedly impact California voters generally, or comparisons between the government’s list and the full ‘California voter file,’ has no relevance to whether the particular plaintiffs in this case have standing,” the department said.

The filing also suggested that Boasberg refer the matter to the Justice Department for investigation for possible criminal contempt of court.

Alberto Lammers, a spokesman for the University of California, Los Angeles’ Voting Rights Project, which filed the case and did the data analysis for CHIRLA, said in an emailed statement: “Plaintiffs’ counsel and their experts fully complied with the Court’s orders.”

In a separate case, the Justice Department on Sunday appealed U.S. District Court Judge Trina L. Thompson's Friday-night ruling. The San Francisco-based judge had barred the department from demanding that election offices remove possible noncitizens from voter rolls, separate their ballots or threaten legal action.

The department noted that Thompson had ruled before the federal government could file its initial arguments. Thompson scheduled a Monday hearing on the appeal.

The Associated Press characterized the case as one of many the Trump administration had lost in its dispute over lists of alleged noncitizen voters. A federal judge in California had rejected the administration's demand that election officials there be forced to separate ballots cast by alleged noncitizen voters, writing that such last-minute orders could “wreak havoc” on the state's election. Two appellate judges agreed with the rejection.

The administration has said it carefully compiled the list from California's own voter-registration data and compared it to federal citizenship records. The Justice Department issued a series of subpoenas for election offices in California and warned election officials who knowingly let noncitizens vote that they could face criminal charges.

California election officials also contend that the list is riddled with errors. They say they have no way to complete the painstaking work required by law to remove voters from the rolls in the days before the election. People attest to being U.S. citizens when they register to vote and risk felony prosecution if they cast a ballot when they are not eligible.
