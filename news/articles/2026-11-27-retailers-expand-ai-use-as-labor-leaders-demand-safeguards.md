## Kroger plans 3,000 corporate job cuts, citing AI-driven streamlining

As millions of Americans begin holiday shopping this Black Friday, workers and consumers are already seeing how artificial intelligence is changing U.S. retail. Labor leaders and advocates are calling for guardrails, public regulation and collective bargaining protections as retailers use AI in hiring, scheduling, discipline, store safety, customer tracking and pricing. The questions reach both employees and shoppers: workers have described tools that interrupt service, while retailers say the systems are intended to improve operations and support staff.

Anna, a cashier at a Walmart in southern Oregon and a leader with United for Respect, requested anonymity because she feared retaliation. According to Anna, Walmart uses AI for scheduling, assigning attendance disciplinary points and operating a phone-based safety-alert system. She said she receives at least 10 or 15 alerts during a shift and cannot move through the app until each alert is cleared. Anna said the system can mistake a shadow or sticker for an actionable condition and may route a maintenance issue to an associate. She must take and upload a photo after completing an alert.

Anna described being unable to help a crying customer obtain a pregnancy test until she cleared an alert. “It’s not helping,” she said, adding that she does not need an AI system to direct her to pick up an item she can clearly see.

Walmart’s rollout has also prompted worker and investor action. In September 2026, several hundred Walmart workers signed an open letter calling on the company to put workers first as it rolls out AI in stores. In June 2026, Walmart investors overwhelmingly voted down a proposal for a report on how AI and automation affect the Walmart workplace. A Walmart spokesperson cited an internal June 2026 survey in which 84% of Walmart workers claimed they were confident using digital tools.

“Walmart’s approach to AI is, and always has been, people-led and tech-powered,” the spokesperson said in an email. “We use AI to serve our customers and members and better support associates – not to replace their judgment, experience, or expertise.” The spokesperson said the tools evolve over time and feedback from associates informs improvements.

Industry leaders expect the use and implementation of AI in the retail industry to continue to grow. A National Retail Federation survey published in December 2025 polled 56 AI leaders at U.S.-based retailers. Most respondents said their companies had or were implementing AI across a variety of uses and planned to increase investment and implementation over the next few years. Seventy-five percent reported implementing AI tools for coding and app development, 73% for office productivity, 66% for cybersecurity and fraud development, 59% for preparing or implementing AI in supply chain and warehouse operations, and 45% for marketing and advertising.

Crystal Weise, Policy & Programs Director at the AFL-CIO Tech Institute, a nonprofit labor hub started by the AFL-CIO, the largest federation of unions in the US, said: “Without proper guardrails, AI systems have supercharged surveillance for both consumers and for the workers who make it possible for consumers to buy what they need or what they want for their loved ones for the holiday season.” Weise said surveillance begins in factories and warehouses where consumer goods are manufactured and distributed, and extends to consumers online and in stores, where shopping habits are tracked and influenced by algorithms, as well as to workers.

Weise said AI-fueled systems for surveillance pricing, worker productivity quotas and monitoring dissent or workplace organizing collect data used to cut costs and drive profit margins. She said such technologies make jobs worse and products and services more expensive. Weise called for policies and collective bargaining agreements that stop exploitative surveillance and keep humans in control of how shoppers and consumers access goods and services.

Retailers are also using AI in physical stores. Macy’s, Target, Home Depot and Costco use automated license plate readers, while other retailers use AI tools to scale back Black Friday discounts and bolster profit margins. In a disclaimer about the technology at some Macy’s stores, the company said, “We use this technology for limited, authorized purposes. Data is collected through ALPRs to prevent theft and fraud, help ensure security, and maintain the safety of individuals and our properties.”

AI enters employment before workers reach a store. Amazon-owned Whole Foods uses an AI recruiting agent named Sage, which the company’s job-application website says is the only way to apply for a job at Whole Foods. Once inside stores, customers and workers can be subjected to facial recognition, with Wegmans, Macy’s and Home Depot citing use of the technology. Kroger archives customer data it collects and sells it, according to a 2025 Consumer Reports investigation.

Stuart Appelbaum, president of the Retail, Wholesale, and Department Store Union and executive vice president of the United Food and Commercial Workers, said, “We’re not anti-technology, but we are anti-dehumanization of the workplace.” Appelbaum argued that AI cannot measure or be aware of life situations that should be considered in discipline or job-termination decisions. He cited negative effects of algorithmic surveillance: “We’ve seen a rise in algorithmic surveillance, and it creates a pressure cooker environment. People are afraid to pause for a breath, or even to go to the bathroom, and for us, that’s really workplace harassment by algorithm,” he said. “Every worker deserves to have a human involved before any discipline takes place.”

Appelbaum said contracts won by the union include protections governing AI use and implementation. He stressed the importance of bargaining over new technologies as employers use AI to lower labor costs and increase profit margins. “A lot of retailers are using AI as an excuse to have fewer people on the shop floor,” he said. Appelbaum said staffing reductions diminish the in-store experience, put pressure on the workers who remain and could hurt stores’ future, adding that the technology should empower and strengthen retail workers as part of what the union negotiates.

Fears of large-scale AI-driven retail layoffs have yet to materialize. A July 2026 policy brief published by Stanford University researchers said AI’s impact on overall employment was “likely small” at the moment. Several firms have nevertheless announced planned job cuts over the next few years, citing AI or AI investments as a leading factor. New technologies, such as electronic shelf labels, are projected to result in job losses and increase prices for consumers.

Kroger announced in October 2026 plans to cut 3,000 corporate jobs over the next three years, citing the use of artificial intelligence to streamline operations. A Kroger spokesperson said the notifications were part of the corporate headcount reduction announced in early October to lower prices and improve the shopping experience by simplifying operations and making better use of technology. The announcement came after Kroger was hit with a $2.6bn charge in 2025 following its decision to shut down three automated robotic delivery centers. Kroger did not comment.

Milton Jones, international president of the United Food and Commercial Workers, which represents workers at around two-thirds of Kroger stores in the US, said, “As retailers feel the pressure from Wall Street to cut costs and pump up share prices, they’re turning to AI to do the dirty work.” He added, “Whether it’s AI-powered scheduling that slashes retail workers’ hours or surveillance pricing that raises costs for consumers, people are getting squeezed on both sides of the cash register.”

Bryan Wynn, national bargaining director for the United Food and Commercial Workers, said investors are pushing for AI investments to cut labor costs, increase efficiencies and boost profits, largely without concern for how the technologies affect workers and consumers. Wynn said planning for those technologies has become a concern in bargaining renewals and future union contracts as employers use AI for data collection and surveillance to influence and monitor behavior and further increase profits.

“People should have a say in how AI and their data are used to change how they work and shop,” Jones said. “We need technology that works for our members, and all working people, not automation that steals their livelihoods.”
