## Peltola won an outright majority; state attorneys called the dismissal motion 'meritless.'

In a filing Thursday, the Justice Department asked the 9th U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals to declare its lawsuit over Alaska's U.S. Senate race moot and dismiss the case. The request came two days after the race was decided, following the Nov. 3 election. If granted, it would end the Trump administration's effort to block certification of Democrat Mary Peltola's victory.

Justice Department attorneys asked the appeals court to overturn the lower court's dismissal of the case to erase the loss, then dismiss the case anyway. They said the court "should hold that this case is moot, vacate the underlying order, and remand for dismissal on that ground."

Attorneys for Alaska, whose state government is currently controlled by Republicans, objected to the administration's request. They called the requested dismissal legally "meritless" and filed papers to help get the original case dismissed.

The legal challenge centered on the presence on the ballot of a second candidate with the same name as the incumbent, Republican Sen. Dan Sullivan. The Alaska Supreme Court had ruled that the second Sullivan's candidacy was legal and that he could appear on the ballot.

Peltola won an outright majority in the race to become Democrats' 56th senator, the party's ninth Senate pickup in the midterm election. Her margin of victory exceeded the number of votes cast for Dan J. Sullivan.

In a statement responding to the filing, Peltola said it was time to move on: "Now that the election is over and the voters sent a very clear message about the direction they want our state to go in, I'm eager to move forward and get to work on behalf of all Alaskans." Messages seeking comment were left with Sen. Sullivan's campaign and the Alaska Department of Law.

The Justice Department filed the lawsuit at President Donald Trump's direction, saying a Peltola win should not be certified until an investigation could determine whether voters were confused. The administration never explained how such an investigation could take place given the secrecy of the ballot.

The case was filed before The New York Times reported that texts and other documents appeared to show that Peltola's campaign was involved in recruiting a retired schoolteacher from the tiny fishing community of Petersburg. The man was listed on the ballot as Daniel J. Sullivan Jr., with no party preference, to distinguish him from Republican Sen. Dan Sullivan, the incumbent.

Dan J. Sullivan has long insisted that he ran on his own, and Peltola has denied any involvement. Trump has demanded that Peltola be prosecuted over the reported recruitment of the second Sullivan. Federal investigators went to Alaska before Election Day.

The Associated Press described candidates such as the second Sullivan as "decoy candidates" and reported that similar tactics had been used for decades, including by Trump's allies, and that they occasionally led to criminal charges.

This was at least the second time since the Nov. 3 election that the Justice Department had acted to dismiss one of its high-profile lawsuits involving states' handling of elections. Just days after Democrats flipped control of Congress and won races for governor and state legislatures, the Justice Department moved to drop a lawsuit seeking to prevent California officials from counting roughly 20,000 ballots. The Trump administration alleged that the ballots belonged to suspected noncitizens, but election officials questioned the validity of the government's data and said the list contained many U.S. citizens.

Nicholas Riccardi and Becky Bohrer authored the Associated Press report. Riccardi reported from Denver.
