## China uses five-year plans to build clean-energy supply chains

As more than 200 countries meet in Turkey for the annual United Nations climate talks, COP31, China and the United States remain the world's two largest emitters of greenhouse gases. Their paths have diverged: for years, China's climate pollution was growing, driven by increased coal use, but for the past two and a half years, China's emissions have been flat or falling, while U.S. emissions rose in 2025 as the country burned more coal for power and more oil and gas for heating.

Lauri Myllyvirta, lead analyst at the Centre for Research on Energy and Clean Air, called China's shift remarkable. "And that is a remarkable change," he said. China is reducing emissions by increasing renewable energy and batteries, which are displacing coal, while broader electric-vehicle use is reducing oil consumption. Myllyvirta said, "Those are bigger reductions than we expected," then added, "and I think anyone expected."

A recent report by United Nations scientists found that the U.S. was the only major economy where planet-warming pollution increased significantly last year. This year, U.S. emissions are projected to fall slightly, and the country will likely burn less coal than last year. Alex Wang, a professor at UCLA Law and an expert on Chinese climate policy, said the United States could reduce emissions more if it deployed large-scale renewable energy and batteries like China.

Wang said the United States has not provided long-term policy support for those technologies. The Biden administration increased funding for solar, wind, batteries and electric vehicles; the Trump administration reduced that funding and created regulatory hurdles for those industries. "What we're suffering through these days is the policy whiplash that comes with the political divisions in the U.S.," Wang said.

China started building the infrastructure that now enables emissions reductions about twenty years ago. At the time, the country was known as a manufacturer of low-cost items, and air pollution in major Chinese cities was reaching record levels. Chinese leadership made policies to address both the country's economic identity and air quality, while five-year plans from the central government helped provinces pivot into the clean-energy sector. "Because the policy direction was so consistent and clear," Wang said, "they used that to drive the development of all of these industries that really didn't exist before."

The central government established nationwide supply chains for technologies such as solar panels, batteries, wind turbines and electric-vehicle manufacturing. NPR's report identifies China as the unparalleled global leader in producing, deploying and exporting these clean-energy technologies. Wang said, "On the electrification front, they're going gangbusters." Myllyvirta's research group found that China's emissions dropped 3% last quarter, the largest decline since the COVID-19 pandemic.

In the power sector, China's emissions are falling as coal use decreases and solar, wind and nuclear energy increase. The country has built many new coal plants in recent years, but they often do not operate at full capacity and instead provide backup generation. Cement and steel manufacturing are also reducing climate pollution. In transportation, electric vehicles are displacing gasoline and diesel; two-thirds of passenger-vehicle sales in China are now electric, according to recent data from the China Passenger Car Association.

Wang said the United States has struggled to scale clean-technology industries because the federal government has flip-flopped. Under the Biden administration, Congress passed new financial incentives for renewable energy and electric vehicles and policies that cut greenhouse gas emissions in sectors like the oil and gas industry. The Trump administration has reversed those policies, put roadblocks on solar and wind projects, canceled tax breaks for home solar and electric vehicles, and proposed cutting regulations on methane, a planet-heating gas. Its frequently cited "energy dominance agenda" prioritizes keeping coal plants running.

Asked about increased U.S. greenhouse gas emissions last year, White House spokesperson Taylor Rodgers wrote in an email to NPR, "The United States is in a position of strength thanks to the President's energy dominance agenda which has unleashed affordable, reliable, secure energy sources. America is the world's leading producer and exporter of oil and gas, and many nations rely on the United States for their energy supply."

Energy demand from data centers adds another pressure. Ben Haley of the consulting firm Evolved Energy Research said most of those data centers are now being powered by fossil fuels such as natural gas and coal. "The pace and scale at which they come online tends to increase emissions," Haley said. "You can't really match that with clean energy deployment at scale, currently." A new study found that carbon dioxide emissions from about 400 large U.S. data centers equal almost as much as those from an entire country such as Denmark.

Wang said it was great that China's emissions seem to be plateauing, but he cautioned that their total remains outsized. "We shouldn't forget the fact that their emissions really are outsized compared to the rest of the world," he said. At the same time, China exports clean technologies such as solar, batteries and electric vehicles to countries including Pakistan, Nepal and the Philippines, helping those countries reduce their dependence on fossil fuels. NPR's related coverage said the Iran war had pushed some countries away from oil and toward clean energy. Kingsmill Bond, an analyst at the energy think tank Ember, said Chinese clean-technology exports have only increased since the beginning of the U.S. war with Iran.

The ongoing U.N. climate talks in Turkey will have a large emphasis on getting countries to commit to increasing electrification. Li Shuo, director of the China Climate Hub at the Asia Society Policy Institute, said China will promote trade in its clean-energy technologies at the conference. As China gains a larger share of the global clean-technology trade, he said, the United States will have more difficulty competing. "This dominating position will not go away, and if anything, it will be further consolidated in the coming years," Li said. "The U.S., I think, will struggle."
