## More than 10% of infected people were hospitalized over two years

More than 5,400 confirmed cases have been reported in the United States so far this year. The total is already double the 2025 tally, making this the worst year for measles illnesses since 1991. The year-to-date count has been driven largely by an outbreak in Pennsylvania. Public health experts said the outbreak tallies were almost certainly undercounts.

The Associated Press described the last two years as the worst in a generation in the United States. Those years have included more than 7,000 cases and 11 deaths. Nearly all affected people were unvaccinated or had no vaccination status reported, and more than 10% of those infected had to be hospitalized.

That two-year toll includes the 10 measles-associated deaths reported this year, at least five of them involving children. Health officials have not released detailed information on each death. This year's death count is higher than any previous year in more than three decades.

The United States eliminated measles in 2000, after which public health officials usually logged fewer than 200 cases a year, most tied to international travel. The resurgence since 2025 began with a major outbreak in the Southwest. More than 760 people became ill in Texas, where two children died, and about 100 became ill in New Mexico, where one adult died.

In the summer of 2025, measles spread through extremely rural border towns in Arizona and Utah. A major South Carolina outbreak in the winter and spring sickened nearly 1,000 people and became the fastest-growing measles outbreak the nation had seen in decades. The current Pennsylvania outbreak has dwarfed the earlier outbreaks.

Measles has been spreading throughout the Americas since 2024. Outbreaks in North America started in close-knit Mennonite communities and later spread to areas with low vaccination rates. Canada lost its measles-free designation in 2025, the first time since 1998.

International health officials consider a country measles-eliminated when the virus is not locally spreading among residents, and the designation hinges on whether a single measles chain has spread uninterrupted for at least 12 months. After vaccination campaigns, the Americas became the first region in the world free of measles in 2016, and health officials estimate that the vaccine prevented 6.2 million deaths in the Americas between 2000 and 2023. With Canada also outside the designation, the Associated Press said the entirety of North America faced the task of earning it back.

Against that background, the Pan American Health Organization said Tuesday that the United States had lost its measles-elimination status. Mexico also lost its status following a resurgence that included a massive outbreak in Chihuahua state, and Bolivia lost its status. PAHO expects the United States, Bolivia and Mexico to submit plans "in the coming weeks" for how each country will go about trying to stop transmission for at least a year and regain measles elimination status, said Dr. Daniel Salas, the organization's executive manager for immunizations.

"Measles elimination is not a trophy that is won once and placed on a shelf," PAHO Director Dr. Jarbas Barbosa said at a news conference. "It is an achievement that must be safeguarded every day by closing immunity gaps and ensuring we reach every community."

The declaration had few practical implications and was largely symbolic, but it marked a sharp turnabout in a disease largely controlled through decades of scientific advances. The Associated Press said the loss "may be seen as the beginning of an era in which diseases of the past become deadly problems for the modern world." The Associated Press said Tuesday's announcement signaled an erosion of public health preparedness. The Associated Press also reported that the virus had spread largely among unvaccinated people at the highest rate in more than 30 years.

Vaccination rates have been falling against several diseases in the United States and globally. Measles is one of the most infectious diseases known to medicine and was seen as likely to be among the first vaccine-preventable diseases to reemerge when vaccinations falter. Some experts said other diseases were likely to follow. Dr. Susan Kansagra, chief medical officer of the Association of State and Territorial Health Officials, said, "It can be the canary in the coal mine to other things."

Measles vaccines have safely stopped the virus's spread in the United States for 30 years, and experts said a 95% community vaccination rate is needed to prevent outbreaks. The Associated Press reported that, as fewer parents vaccinated their children and federal leaders sowed doubt and confusion about the importance of vaccination, the virus had found footholds in communities across the country. All but three states have reported a confirmed case of measles this year. An accompanying video featured a doctor describing the MMR vaccine as "close to bulletproof," while another featured a Pennsylvania doctor urging vaccination as the outbreak grew.

The illness typically begins with a high fever followed by a telltale rash that starts on the face and neck. Most people recover, but the World Health Organization says measles is one of the leading causes of death among young children. Serious complications, including blindness and swelling of the brain, are more common in young children and adults over age 30.

The Associated Press reported that U.S. Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. was a leading voice in the anti-vaccine movement before President Donald Trump appointed him to oversee federal health agencies. Kennedy and other federal leaders argued that vaccination rates were waning for years before Trump took office a second time, that measles was not an "American phenomenon," and that the U.S. government was handling measles better than other countries. In January, a U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention leader told reporters that the loss of the nation's elimination status would be the "cost of doing business" globally.

The federal government has taken some steps. In August and September, the CDC awarded $100 million in two tranches to support state and local measles-response work. Kennedy and CDC Director Dr. Erica Schwartz have said vaccination is the best way to prevent measles from spreading.

"The measles elimination designation is not what protects Americans. Vaccination does," Schwartz said in a statement Tuesday. "We know where transmission is occurring and we've invested $100 million in state and local health departments to reach those communities."

State and local health officials said the cost of the response was far higher than the federal funding. Chrissie Juliano, executive director of the Big Cities Health Coalition, which represents three dozen of the largest U.S. city health departments, said, "It wasn't a lot of money, frankly."

Experts also said the moment demanded a louder federal voice and a bigger push for vaccinations than Kennedy and Schwartz were providing. Some noted that neither Kennedy nor the CDC had scheduled a news conference for the week to address how they planned to regain measles-elimination status.

Noel Brewer, a University of North Carolina public health professor who chairs a committee that presented an analysis of U.S. measles data to PAHO last week, said, "When the people at the front of the room are not speaking loudly and frequently in support of vaccination, the program starts to falter. And that's what we're seeing in the U.S."

"The federal government has had such a ho-hum response," Brewer said. "How many kids have to die before they start getting concerned?"

Some individual countries have achieved, lost and regained measles-elimination status, and Brazil is one example. The United Kingdom lost its status a second time this year. Other countries are also struggling with measles, and some have lost their elimination status, but some experts said the U.S. outcome was not inevitable.

Sen. Ron Wyden, a Democrat from Oregon, said: "This humiliating determination is the tragic yet predictable result of an ongoing campaign by grifters, quacks and charlatans to put their own greed and self-worth ahead of the health and safety of children."
