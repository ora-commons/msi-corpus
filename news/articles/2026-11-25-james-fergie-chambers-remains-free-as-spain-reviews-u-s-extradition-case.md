## James “Fergie” Chambers remains free as Spain reviews U.S. extradition case

**Subtype:** fact

- James “Fergie” Chambers said after his Spanish release that support for his case does not imply support for his ideas and that he should not be unjustly accused because of his beliefs.
- Spain’s appeals court granted Chambers provisional release on bail after finding translation problems weakened the U.S. extradition request’s foundation.
- Spain’s high court paused proceedings to evaluate alterations the United States made to the original machine-made Spanish translation.
- The U.S. complaint alleges international money laundering, riot and conspiracy to riot.
- The U.S. request proceeds without an indictment, relying on one FBI agent’s affidavit.
- Chambers cannot be extradited while his Spanish asylum case proceeds and cannot be extradited if Spain grants asylum.

## Spanish appeals court cited flaws in the U.S. request translation

In his first in-depth interview after a Spanish appeals court granted provisional release on bail on Friday, James “Fergie” Chambers spoke from his Madrid apartment. His release was due to issues with the legally required U.S. translation of the extradition request’s core documents into Spanish, an issue first reported by The Guardian. Chambers, a U.S. citizen and pro-Palestine activist, said he was “not asking anyone to endorse my ideas or not think I’m a jerk” but still had “a right to my beliefs” and should not be “unjustly accused because of them.” He added: “Support for my case doesn’t imply support of me, or my ideas.” He said: “It’s about my right to have those ideas – and not be falsely accused of things.”

The Guardian described the case as the first known instance of the United States seeking to extradite a citizen over alleged support for Hamas. The allegation is apparently tied to an international money-laundering charge in the criminal complaint underpinning the extradition request and to Chambers’s donations to organizations helping Palestinians in Gaza. The complaint also includes riot and conspiracy-to-riot charges arising from 2023 protests against New England branches of Israeli weapons manufacturer Elbit.

The order granting Chambers provisional release said translation problems caused “a diminishment, at least for the time being, in the solidity or foundation of the extradition request.” Spanish judges had previously denied bail three times. Chambers had been detained since 10 July, when authorities arrested him in Ibiza.

His release followed a separate decision on Thursday by a three-judge panel at Spain’s high court to pause the extradition proceedings and evaluate the impact of alterations the United States made to the original, machine-made Spanish translation. The United States admitted to making several changes, calling them “substantial [and] inadvertent.” Aitor Martínez, a member of Chambers’s defense team, told The Guardian last week that those changes and dozens of others introduced prejudicial notions with specific legal implications that entered the Spanish prosecutor’s report.

The complaint has no indictment behind it, meaning a federal grand jury has not evaluated evidence against Chambers. Instead, the United States is seeking extradition based on the affidavit of a single FBI agent, the core of the sealed criminal complaint.

During his imprisonment, Chambers also began applying for political asylum in Spain. According to The Guardian, Spain cannot extradite him until the asylum case runs its course and cannot extradite him if he is granted asylum. His legal team has notified the government office evaluating the asylum petition of the altered translations.

Chambers was on his first visit without being separated by glass from his mother, Lauren Hamilton, when he was summoned to receive the “good news” on Friday, he said. Minutes later, he returned to tell her that he was being released.

“She freaked out crying. We went nuts for about five minutes. Then we got to contacting my lawyer and getting my kids on a plane to Spain,” Chambers said.

The report described Chambers as the heir to at least $250 million from his family’s business, Atlanta-based Cox Enterprises. On Monday, his five-year-old son, whom he has with actor and activist Stella Schnabel, could be heard in the background. A photograph accompanying the report showed Schnabel, Chambers’s wife, and his lawyer Baltasar Garzon speaking to the media in Madrid in September. The tableau was in stark contrast to his months in Aranjuez prison. Chambers described his imprisonment as a period of profound uncertainty and fear.

“I tried to build a cocoon to deal with the immense unknown, the immense fear,” he said of his time in Aranjuez prison. “I would pray a lot. The only idea I could hold onto was, my home is the truth. The truth of who loves you. The truth of my own innocence. And the truth of what is happening in Palestine and all the things connected to what is happening in Palestine – other wars, gas prices. No one can take that away from you.”

His mother supported him throughout his case, and Chambers has reconnected with his father, James Cox Chambers, for the first time in five years. “Speaking with my parents has provided emotional stability – and intelligent, adult minds to help make decisions,” Chambers said. His father owns an estimated 17% stake in Cox Enterprises but does not play an active role in the company. Although Chambers has had political disagreements with his father, who is a donor to the Democratic party, Chambers said his father “sees this situation as totally unthinkable and out of step with anything resembling a just society.”

Chambers also invoked his grandfather, ACLU civil rights lawyer James Cox Hamilton, who died in 2014. “He would’ve turned in his grave over this,” Chambers said.

While locked up, Chambers said he received hundreds of letters of support from friends, family and strangers, postmarked from Atlanta to Barcelona. Chambers said one came from a man who had been imprisoned under Spanish dictator Francisco Franco and who painted the “key of return,” a symbol of homes lost in the 1948 Nakba, on a Palestinian flag.

Chambers’s views and donations to progressive and humanitarian projects have been extensively profiled in U.S. media, including his public comments on Israel and Gaza that U.S. media coverage has called controversial. A week after the 7 October Hamas attacks on Israel, he posted on X about Israel’s response: “Stop calling it ‘collective punishment’/There’s nothing to be ‘punished’ for/No faction of the Palestinian resistance, Hamas or other, has done *anything* wrong/It’s simply genocide/Like it has been.”

Spanish authorities arrested Chambers on 10 July as he and Schnabel pulled into the driveway of a school they were visiting to discuss enrolling their five-year-old son, part of plans to move to Spain. They had been staying in Ireland, where Schnabel was working on a film.

Chambers said the arrest surprised him. “I was confused! Did I speed?” he said. He told The Guardian that a car had followed him around Dublin only days before the arrest and that someone had broken into their car outside their house at night without taking anything of value. “I noticed signs of surveillance,” he said. Months later, last Wednesday, Irish police raided the same house in Dublin, acting on a request from US authorities, days before Spain’s high court paused the extradition proceedings and an appeals court granted Chambers provisional release.

Chambers said the millions he has donated to an estimated 100 projects in about 20 countries are “the most important thing I’ve done.” He said that a person with his political orientation and resources, however good an activist, would not be as effective as medical aid or feeding children in Gaza, supporting independent media that otherwise would not exist, or paying people’s bail who had been arrested for protesting. “I’m giving a lot of money to things that other people aren’t giving money to,” he said.

With his immediate future uncertain, Chambers said he was “looking forward to sharing a couple of nice dinners with my family this week.” He was also “grateful for the way Spanish courts have dealt with this – just focusing on the facts.” He added, “It shouldn’t matter that I’ve made some emotional statements.”
