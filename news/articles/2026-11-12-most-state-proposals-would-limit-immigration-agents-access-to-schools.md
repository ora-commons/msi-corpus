## At least 23 states took up bills this year; only 11 had become law by the start of the school year

At least 23 states took up legislation this year governing how federal immigration officers may interact with K-12 public schools — in some cases considering multiple proposals — according to an NPR analysis of state bills. The vast majority of the 40 proposals NPR reviewed, 35, sought to limit federal officers' access to school campuses and students. One, in Mississippi, sought to ensure that schools cooperate with U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement, and the remainder did not explicitly limit or increase access or cooperation.

Only 11 of the 40 proposals had become law by the beginning of the school year. NPR found that lawmakers in many states seem to agree there is a need for clearer policies but have struggled to find consensus over what those policies should say.

Many states lack clear policies governing what interactions between immigration officers and schools should look like, according to NPR. At the beginning of President Trump's second term, his administration rescinded a policy that protected schools, among other "sensitive" places, from immigration enforcement. In some places, school leaders and researchers say Trump's immigration policies have led to enrollment drops.

The U.S. Department of Homeland Security, which houses ICE, said at the time that "criminals will no longer be able to hide in America's schools and churches to avoid arrest," adding that "the Trump Administration will not tie the hands of our brave law enforcement, and instead trusts them to use common sense." DHS has also said that ICE does not raid or target schools. Reporting from K-12 Dive shows the last academic year nonetheless saw several incidents of ICE agents on school grounds, leaving education leaders to rely on a patchwork of state and district policies when such encounters occur.

Thomas Saenz, president and general counsel of the Mexican American Legal Defense and Educational Fund, said the elimination of the federal sensitive-locations policy is likely driving many states to establish their own protections — "belts and suspenders, as they say."

The proposals NPR reviewed took several forms. Minnesota, Arizona, Delaware, Georgia, Maine and Nebraska are among the state legislatures that considered restricting ICE access to schools. Several specified the need for a warrant. Others aimed to restrict what school police and school resource officers can and cannot do, including during immigration-related arrests. West Virginia, Hawaii and Kansas pushed to restrict ICE access by designating school campuses as sensitive locations; none of those three proposals passed. A few states considered requiring school boards to adopt policies outlining how they would respond to immigration authorities on school property.

A separate group of proposals would let public schools charge tuition to students who are not U.S. citizens or legal immigrants. Such measures would challenge Plyler v. Doe, the decades-old Supreme Court ruling establishing that all children have a right to a free K-12 public education regardless of immigration status. NPR excluded those proposals from its analysis; according to Education Week, at least two states were considering challenges to Plyler as of July, and similar efforts had failed in at least five other states.

At least eight states already had laws governing immigration enforcement and K-12 schools at the start of 2026, and a few of them — including Connecticut, Maryland and Oregon — considered additional legislation this year. Mississippi considered and passed a law requiring government entities, including school districts, to cooperate with federal immigration enforcement. In Missouri, lawmakers considered a proposal this spring to limit school cooperation with immigration enforcement, even though an existing state law prohibits municipalities from adopting sanctuary policies and requires cooperation — a conflict that calls into question how far the proposal could go.

The patchwork leaves school leaders writing their own rules. In Laredo, Texas, Andrea Garza, a school leader with the United Independent School District, said spotting ICE agents near a school is unremarkable: "It's something very normal here." In other parts of the country, she said, "seeing the presence of border patrol would be, you know, different, maybe shocking. Just something from another world, right?" Federal data shows 94% of her district's community is Hispanic, and Garza said some families have been in Laredo for generations while others arrived more recently; the federal government is one of the area's largest employers.

Garza said she knows what she would do if ICE comes knocking: without a warrant or subpoena, agents do not get in. "We serve 'in loco parentis' for students," she said, meaning "in place of a parent" when students' rights are challenged. "It's not interpreted, in the way we see it, as interfering with the law," she said. "It is more just verifying that we are all following the law." Texas law prohibits local entities, including campus police, from obstructing federal immigration enforcement, and NPR found no state law that directly addresses how much access these officers should have in K-12 public schools.

Tennessee lawmakers considered multiple proposals from across the political spectrum. One would have required schools to report students who are undocumented; opponents argued it was part of an effort to challenge Plyler. "As legislators, we cannot make informed decisions without data, and right now we simply do not know what the attendance is in our school systems across the state, regarding children who are here illegally," state Rep. Jason Zachary, a Knoxville Republican, said at a March hearing in favor of the measure, which did not reach a final vote. A separate bill would have prohibited sensitive areas, including schools, from being used as staging areas, processing locations or operational bases for immigration enforcement. "Everybody deserves to have a great learning environment, and it's hard to teach when you are worried that one of your students is going to be taken," its sponsor, state Rep. Gabby Salinas, a Memphis Democrat, told NPR. The proposal failed. Another Tennessee proposal would have required schools to immediately notify all students, parents and employees if immigration officers were on school grounds.

In Minnesota, NPR found no pre-existing state law governing immigration enforcement in schools. After a surge of immigration enforcement activity in Minneapolis earlier this year, lawmakers considered at least five proposals addressing ICE and schools, ranging from stricter warrant requirements to mandates that schools develop procedures for handling law-enforcement requests. None of the five reached a final vote, leaving school leaders such as Kenny Newby waiting.

Newby, principal of Justice Alan Page Elementary School in Maplewood, Minn., just outside the Twin Cities, said his goal if ICE ever comes to his school is to be kind, stay calm and be firm. In the absence of a state law, he relies on district policy: staff members welcome the officers, start a conversation, ask for required paperwork and notify school-level administrators, who then contact district leaders. Officers with a warrant would be directed to the district office. "We would not allow them to have direct contact with students," Newby said.

Rob Reetz, principal of Mounds View High School in nearby Arden Hills, said a state law could simplify interactions between districts; right now, he said, it is unclear which district's protocols adults should follow if ICE appears when, say, his school's sports team is visiting another district. He also worries that agents would not necessarily follow rules set by the state or any other entity. In January, Reetz learned that federal agents had been spotted at Roosevelt High School in Minneapolis; MPR News reported at the time that armed U.S. Border Patrol officers came onto school property during dismissal and "began tackling people, handcuffed two staff members and released chemical weapons on bystanders." "It contributed to this uncertainty about how would our interactions go with immigration enforcement if they were to be on our campus," Reetz said.

Newby said he and his staff would feel more supported if there were "clear expectations on keeping schools a safe place."
