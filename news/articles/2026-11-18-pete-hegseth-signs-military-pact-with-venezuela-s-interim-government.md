## The pact covers training, intelligence sharing and defense equipment

On Wednesday, Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth made an unannounced visit to Caracas and signed a military pact with Venezuela’s interim government. The agreement aligns the U.S. and Venezuelan militaries to protect the Venezuelan people from foreign actors, narco-terrorists and criminals. Hegseth said it would pair joint training, intelligence sharing and defense equipment with U.S. assistance for Caracas to regain control of territory.

The Wall Street Journal reported that Hegseth signed the agreement with his Venezuelan counterpart, Gustavo González. Hegseth touted the recent cooperation in a formal ceremony with Venezuela’s acting president, Delcy Rodríguez, and a phalanx of Venezuelan and American officials. Hegseth said to Rodríguez, “I stand here on behalf of President Trump, who sends his greeting.”

The pact directs the two militaries “to ensure that the Venezuelan people are not threatened by foreign actors or narco-terrorists or criminals,” Hegseth said. He also linked Venezuela’s stability to conditions beyond its borders: “the prosperity, the peace, the stability of Venezuela means a safer and more secure hemisphere, a safer and more secure America.”

Rodríguez connected the agreement to the removal of her predecessor. Seated in an ornate chair below the statue of Venezuela’s independence hero, Simón Bolivar, she said the two countries “have transformed very difficult and very hard moments for our people.” She added: “We have transformed them, in a very short time, in just months, secretary, thanks to the political and geopolitical maturity of our governments.”

The pact followed months of operational cooperation between Washington and Caracas. Since the removal of Nicolás Maduro as Venezuela’s leader in a U.S. commando operation in January, the two countries have signed oil deals and worked in tandem to strike armed groups inside Venezuelan territory. Last month, U.S. drones struck a camp of Colombian drug-trafficking guerrillas just inside Venezuela, killing more than two dozen fighters in an operation coordinated with the government in Caracas. In June, a U.S. strike in southern Venezuela killed the fugitive leader of the Tren de Aragua gang. Since September 2025, the U.S. has carried out airstrikes against dozens of suspected drug-trafficking speedboats, some of which set sail in Venezuela.

Hegseth kept the trip under wraps before the ceremony. On Tuesday, he visited the Doral, Fla., offices of Southern Command, the headquarters of the U.S. military in Latin America. Photographs of Hegseth with Rodríguez appeared on social media Wednesday morning. Colombian military officials also announced that he would travel to Colombia, a military ally of Washington, for meetings. The Pentagon declined to comment on the trip or describe its agenda.

Hegseth and Rodríguez portrayed their relationship as a partnership of equals. The Wall Street Journal reported that the portrayal obscured an imbalance that has been clear since the U.S. sent Maduro to New York to face drug-trafficking charges. Since then, the Trump administration has worked to bring oil investors to Venezuela while handling more than $12 billion in revenues from crude sales, most of which the U.S. disbursed back to Venezuela’s central bank, according to a recent State Department report.

After meeting Venezuelan officials, Hegseth spoke from the hilltop U.S. Embassy compound overlooking Caracas. Hegseth said the interim government had shifted from being a regional security threat to what he called a compliant American partner, and he praised Rodríguez for making the change possible. “We could not do it without her,” Hegseth said.

Hegseth said the security agreement would allow the U.S. to help Venezuela bolster territorial control and remove narco terrorists who had long sought haven there, in the past with the assistance of Venezuelan officials. He listed the military assistance the two sides would provide: “Training together, partnering together, sharing intelligence, more defense equipment and capabilities.”

He also said, “Venezuela will go even faster in recovering your national borders and territory that was taken from you by extrajudicial and narco terrorist networks for 27 years.”

The pact’s ceremony also addressed economic cooperation. Hegseth said Trump “looks forward to the opportunities, not just militarily when we work together against narco-terrorists and criminal enterprises, but also economic.” The signed agreement made military cooperation a formal part of the relationship that Hegseth and Rodríguez described.
