## Duffy raises FAA funding request to $50 billion

The agency has been plagued for decades by underfunding, outdated technology and staffing shortages. Yet isolated technical problems went largely unnoticed by the flying public, even as experts saw them as signs of serious trouble brewing.

Mike McCormick, who used to oversee air traffic control at the FAA before becoming a professor at Embry-Riddle Aeronautical University, said the agency's troubles predate this week's disruption. "What we're seeing has been decades in the making," McCormick said.

The grounding was the second nationwide technical failure in less than four years. The last time flights were grounded across the country was in January 2023, when a contractor working on a different FAA system — the one that distributes the Notices to Airmen, or NOTAMs, that pilots consult before takeoff — caused the failure. That upgrade had also been deferred for years beforehand.

Tom Lintner, who worked as an air traffic controller and eventually became a top official at the agency and is now an aviation safety consultant, said overhauling the air traffic control system without causing problems has defeated the FAA for decades. "Go back and look at the history of the past 40 years. The track record does not make you encouraged that the modernization will succeed," Lintner said.

The work that triggered Friday's ground stop was itself part of that modernization. A private company, L3Harris, owns and maintains the network under contract with the government, and its technicians were the ones replacing old copper wires in Arkansas when the system went haywire. The new router they installed malfunctioned and in short order overwhelmed a nationwide network of signals vital to air traffic controllers. The 22 regional centers direct planes cruising at high altitudes, and that network's unreliability prompted the ground stop.

The FAA could have scheduled the work for the middle of the night, when few planes are in the air, but officials believed the chance of problems was low because the work was being done in a remote location. Aviation safety expert John Cox, who leads the Safety Operating Systems consulting firm, said hundreds — perhaps thousands — of miles of copper wire have been replaced in the past couple years without incident. "If they followed the same procedure using similar equipment, there's no reason to believe this was going to occur," Cox said.

On Saturday, Duffy said he was not sure whether the crew doing the work made mistakes or whether the routers themselves were faulty, and the investigation into what happened is just beginning. Former FAA executive Mike O'Donnell, who oversaw airport safety programs and accident investigations for much of his 17 years at the agency, said the agency should not be deflecting blame. "Ultimately the FAA is responsible — doesn't matter whether you hire a contractor or it's an FAA employee that did it," O'Donnell said. "They need to take responsibility for it."

The Government Accountability Office warned in September that a failure of exactly this kind was a distinct possibility if the work moved too fast. Congress approved $12.5 billion last year to modernize much of the key infrastructure that more than 45,000 flights a day rely on to reach their destinations safely. The funds were earmarked for replacing outdated technologies such as copper wires, floppy discs and radars built in the 1980s, but much of the money will also go toward runway upgrades and other construction projects.

Duffy has said that is not nearly enough money to complete the work. He had already been asking for $30 billion more; on Friday evening, when the FAA lifted the ground stop, he increased that request to $50 billion. Democrats on the House Appropriations Committee asked on Sunday why they should approve more money when the FAA has not spent what the agency received last year. Duffy says the money is committed to various projects, but he cannot spend money for new radars and other gear until they are built, perhaps sometime next year.

Improving aviation safety has been a focus of the Trump administration since a midair collision outside the nation's capital last year killed 67 people. A prolonged government shutdown last year highlighted the chronic shortage of controllers, and communications disruptions affected Newark Liberty International Airport last spring and again in September. In September, the primary and backup communications lines also went down at a key FAA facility in Philadelphia, and similar outages disrupted flights in Minneapolis and Dallas over the past year.

Sometimes the problems are more mundane, such as an air-conditioning failure that forces a key air traffic control facility to close while the decades-old building is repaired. The FAA relies heavily on contractors to help its limited staff of technicians. The head of the technicians' union told Congress in 2024 that the agency's own technicians could do more of the upgrades if enough of them were hired and given the chance. The union said Saturday it was not consulted about the work that caused Friday's problems; Duffy said he did not raise it with them because the FAA does not even own the network.

Duffy and Trump have been quick to blame the Biden administration, but the air traffic control problems date back well before the first Trump administration. Some members of Congress question whether the current leadership at the FAA — Administrator Bryan Bedford — can truly deliver better results.

Rick Castaldo, who spent decades at the FAA as an electrical engineer, said he doubts the agency will be able to complete the overhaul because many of the same people who failed to complete past projects are advising Duffy and Bedford. With Democrats taking control of Congress in January, Castaldo said, politics could make it even more difficult. "They're going to start strangling the money," Castaldo said, and he worries that many projects will be "left half done."
