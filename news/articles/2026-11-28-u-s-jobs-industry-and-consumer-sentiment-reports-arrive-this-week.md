## Friday's payrolls report is expected to test the year's 'low-hire, low-fire' pattern

The United States faces a week of employment, industry and consumer sentiment releases, the Associated Press reported, with data due on Tuesday, Thursday and Friday. The schedule arrives as households, businesses and policymakers continue to grapple with stubbornly high inflation, and the Associated Press described the reports as among the final economic updates of the year.

The squeeze has been wide-ranging. High prices on everything from groceries to clothing have been squeezing household budgets and businesses, and unrelenting price increases have cast a cloud of uncertainty over growth prospects for a wide range of industries while making people increasingly cautious about spending and employment, the Associated Press reported.

Against that backdrop, the employment market remains a strong point in an economy struggling with inflation, and several updates this week are expected to show that businesses are still hiring and that unemployment remains low, the Associated Press reported.

The first of those updates arrives Tuesday, when the U.S. releases its Job Openings and Labor Turnover Survey for October, which provides details on job openings and layoffs for a wide range of sectors, including healthcare, retail and manufacturing. The last update showed an increase in job postings in September. Hiring has been steady, though sluggish, for years, while layoffs have also been historically low, the Associated Press reported.

The weekly unemployment claims update, a proxy for layoffs, follows on Thursday. Weekly updates throughout the year have consistently shown that layoffs remain historically low, and economists monitor the weekly figures because they can be a sign of where the job market is headed, the Associated Press reported.

The closely watched monthly report comes on Friday, providing details about hiring by employers during November for a range of industries and sectors. Monthly reports have shown a resilient overall jobs market that is also flashing signs of weakening, and employment growth has been modest throughout 2026. Economists have described that market as a "low-hire, low-fire" environment, in which employers are reluctant to let go of existing workers but are not in any hurry to hire new ones, the Associated Press reported.

Industry data arrive in the same window. The Institute for Supply Management's manufacturing report is due Tuesday, and its report on the more economically influential services sector on Thursday, with both expected to show continued growth in November, the Associated Press reported. The reports include commentary from businesses on the conditions they are facing, and higher costs have dominated those concerns.

The services report is of particular concern to economists and Wall Street. The sector includes healthcare, transportation, hotels and restaurants, and it makes up the bulk of economic activity in the U.S., the Associated Press reported.

On the consumer side, the University of Michigan releases its initial reading for its December consumer sentiment survey on Friday, and the Associated Press reported that the reading is expected to again highlight lingering pessimism about the economy. Recent surveys have shown that households are worried about inflation's impact on their budgets and their ability to plan for the future.

The most recent reading of the University of Michigan's consumer sentiment index available as of Nov. 28 stood at 48.0, according to [Federal Reserve Economic Data](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/UMCSENT), with the December initial estimate due Friday.

One source of pressure has eased. High prices at the pump have been behind much of the surge in inflation, the Associated Press reported, but prices have been easing despite the ongoing U.S. war with Iran continuing to stifle global oil supplies. Easing gas prices have relieved some pressure on consumers, helping to prompt a jump in the latest consumer confidence survey from the business group The Conference Board.

The week's releases amount to a final broad look at the tension the Associated Press describes: an employment market that remains a strong point in an economy struggling with stubbornly high inflation, with employers slow to hire, business commentary dominated by higher costs, and households reporting lingering pessimism about their budgets and their ability to plan.
