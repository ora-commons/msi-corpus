## Greens amendments raise penalty ceiling and add social media lawsuit right

U.S. Ambassador to Australia David Brat said on Tuesday that he had conveyed his government's objections to Communications Minister Anika Wells over Australia's amended Digital Duty of Care Bill. President Donald Trump's administration expressed "serious concerns" in a September submission to a parliamentary committee examining the draft law, including how Australia would designate what constitutes "foreseeable harm." Brat posted on social media: "Vaguely defined 'harms,' backed by penalties of up to 5% of global revenue, will push platforms to censor lawful speech."

Against that backdrop, Australia's center-left Labor government has accepted amendments proposed by the minor Greens party to advance the Digital Duty of Care Bill through a Senate where no party holds a majority. The Senate is expected to pass the measure Wednesday, and the House of Representatives, where the government holds a majority, is expected to pass it into law before Parliament rises for the year Thursday.

The bill would require large technology companies to proactively reduce the risk of harm before it occurs, according to an Australian government statement. It would set minimum safety standards for online services, including games, apps and artificial intelligence chatbots, requiring them to protect Australians from seriously harmful material and conduct, the government said. The proposal is similar to the European Union's Digital Services Act, passed in 2022, and the United Kingdom's Online Safety Act, passed a year later.

Communications Minister Anika Wells told reporters at Parliament House in Canberra: "We can't cure the internet, but this is providing some basic safety standards and saying if you build it, you are responsible for its risks."

Greens leader David Shoebridge confirmed that his party had agreed to support the amended bill. "Today we are taking power back from a handful of billionaire-owned, largely U.S. corporations and giving it back to Australians who have been paying too high a price for their profits," Shoebridge said in a statement.

The Greens' amendments carry the agreement. They would raise the maximum penalty for breaches from 109 million Australian dollars ($72 million) to 5% of a technology company's global revenue, and would give people a legal right to sue social media companies in Australian courts for harms suffered online.

The bill itself would also let social media users opt out of algorithms that send them content, and would empower Australia's online safety watchdog to remove apps and websites that fabricate naked images from genuine images of people. It would require protections for children against content that promotes eating disorders, hostile ideas about women, pornography, crime or dangerous stunts, or causes serious mental health distress.

Brat also said the United States, as Australia's largest foreign investor, is concerned that the regulations would impose disproportionate compliance burdens on American companies relative to competitors. Wells said of their Tuesday conversation: "There are points of difference and we're ironing them out."

The conservative opposition Liberal Party shares U.S. concerns that the bill empowers government ministers and the nation's online safety watchdog to judge what was harmful, and opposition lawmakers oppose the legislation. "This bill undermines both the online safety of Australians and our freedom of expression," senior opposition senator Sarah Henderson told reporters.

The proposal follows Australian mining magnate Andrew Forrest having to go to U.S. courts to sue Meta over scam ads on its platforms that use his likeness without permission; the case is not yet resolved. In October, a U.S. judge ordered Meta to pay New Mexico $25 billion after a jury found the company liable for deceiving users about privacy on Facebook, marking the biggest fine ever against the tech giant.

Lisa Given, an information sciences expert at RMIT University in Melbourne, described the draft legislation as "very good" and largely in line with European laws. "It'll be very interesting to see what happens now globally," Given said. "I've always been of the view that if countries can band together and put through similar legislation, collectively we have a better chance of actually pushing Big Tech to make real changes in terms of the design of their systems."

With Greens support secured and the government holding a House majority, the amended bill's path depends on the Senate, where no party holds a majority. Wells said the points of difference between her government and the United States were still being ironed out.
