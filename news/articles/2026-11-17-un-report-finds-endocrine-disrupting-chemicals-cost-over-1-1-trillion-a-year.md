## Vast majority of 350,000-plus registered chemicals and mixtures lack endocrine evaluations

The United Nations Environment Program and the World Health Organization issued a report Tuesday estimating that the health-related costs of exposure to endocrine-disrupting chemicals exceed $1.1 trillion annually worldwide, and calling for urgent, coordinated international action to protect human health and the environment. The agencies said the policies and regulations governing the products and sectors that cause widespread exposure to these chemicals remain “fragmented and insufficient.”

Endocrine-disrupting chemicals, or EDCs, are found in many plastic items, personal care products, pharmaceuticals, toys, household goods, electronics and pesticides, the report said. The “state of the science” document, written by scientists, describes what scientists have learned about exposure to EDCs and their effects since 2012; earlier assessments of the science, issued in 2002 and 2012, were meant to inform policymakers and guide action.

In the report’s foreword, the two U.N. bodies wrote that “This report is an important step towards a safer world, free from exposure to EDCs.” They added: “Solutions do exist. Some safer alternatives are available. The task for policymakers is to ensure that these solutions and alternatives are deployed” so as to protect human, animal and planetary health and save trillions of dollars each year.

Jacqueline Alvarez, chief of the UNEP Chemicals and Health Branch, said the new report builds on those earlier assessments and reflects a field that has advanced substantially in the breadth of health effects documented, the sophistication of exposure science, the development of testing and assessment methodologies, and a growing understanding of the socioeconomic costs of regulatory inaction.

Humans and wildlife are exposed to a wide range of these chemicals, and the evidence linking exposure to diseases and disorders “is strong and continues to grow,” the report said. People can be exposed through ingestion, skin absorption, inhalation and direct contact with medical devices during procedures. Children can be exposed by putting toys containing these chemicals in their mouths and through skin contact with those toys.

The report said prenatal and early-life exposure to EDCs affects neurodevelopment, cognition and behavior. The chemicals are linked to cancer, cardiovascular disease, obesity, type-2 diabetes and metabolic liver disease, and reproductive organs, fertility and thyroid functions are also disrupted.

Laura Vandenberg, one of the report’s lead authors and a professor of environmental health sciences at the University of Massachusetts Amherst, said the 2012 assessment already documented evidence that the chemicals harm health but did not point consistently in the same direction on the same kinds of health effects. Vandenberg also contributed to the 2012 report. She said the new report now amounts to “essentially a statement of irrefutable evidence linking EDCs to human health effects and also environmental damage.”

Few chemicals have been tested for endocrine disruption. More than 350,000 chemicals and chemical mixtures have been registered for production and use globally, but the vast majority have never been evaluated for endocrine-disrupting properties, the report said.

Testing programs have expanded in recent years, a need the 2012 report highlighted as a major gap, but most of that testing has not led to action to regulate these chemicals or control exposure, Vandenberg said. Her take-home message from Tuesday’s report, she said, is that “in many, many cases we know enough to act. Why haven’t we acted?”

Chemicals have sometimes been removed from products once consumers learned about them and stopped buying items containing them, such as bisphenol A, or BPA, which has been used to make plastic containers for storing food and beverages, Vandenberg said. That can produce a “whack-a-mole problem,” she added, in which a substitute chemical is structurally similar or insufficiently tested. To truly reduce people’s exposures to these chemicals, she said, the work has to be done at the policy level throughout society.

Some countries are pressing to regulate chemicals used to make plastics as part of negotiations over a global treaty to end plastic pollution. The report strengthens the position of those countries, said Bjorn Beeler, international coordinator for the International Pollutants Elimination Network. Beeler said the harm from these chemicals is only going to increase in coming years as plastic and chemical production is projected to skyrocket — a “toxic tidal wave” no one is prepared for. IPEN wants a treaty that places global controls on hazardous chemicals in plastics and ends the rapid growth of plastic production. Negotiations are set to resume early next year.

The plastics industry opposes efforts to impose new restrictions through the treaty, arguing that chemicals used in plastics are best regulated through existing international conventions and national laws that consider all of a chemical’s uses. Chemical additives to plastics impart flexibility, rigidity, UV stabilization, durability and other characteristics that help products perform as intended, said the American Chemistry Council, a trade group that represents the U.S. chemical industry. Kimberly Wise White, ACC’s vice president of regulatory and scientific affairs, said the industry considers the safe use of chemicals paramount. White said comprehensive frameworks such as the Toxic Substances Control Act in the U.S. and the European Union’s Registration, Evaluation, Authorization and Restriction of Chemicals regulation, which evaluate a chemical’s uses to determine risk, are the most effective way to regulate plastic additives. The International Council of Chemical Associations, which represents manufacturers and producers around the world, contributed $1.65 million (1.5 million euros) and technical expertise to help countries implement chemical management systems, and created a publicly accessible plastic additives database.

The report identifies key areas for action: protecting vulnerable populations from exposure, particularly during pregnancy, infancy and childhood; improving monitoring and understanding of exposure; advancing the identification and testing of the chemicals; accounting for the costs of exposure; and strengthening regulations, among other steps.

The estimate of the annual health-related costs is a conservative calculation that accounts for the cost of health treatment and lost productivity, the report said. The $1.1 trillion figure does not include additional costs associated with contaminated drinking water, loss of biodiversity, impacts on food production and human rights.

Bethanie Carney Almroth, the U.N. Special Rapporteur on toxics and human rights and an ecotoxicology professor at Sweden’s University of Gothenburg, said she hopes the report will spur action, though international cooperation is challenging. “This report makes clear that new regulations are needed, new testing paradigms must be adopted,” she said. “We need to reverse responses from reactive to proactive. We need to prevent harm from occurring, rather than merely documenting and treating it after the fact.”
