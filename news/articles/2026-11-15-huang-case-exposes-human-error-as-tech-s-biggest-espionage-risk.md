## Corporate intelligence firms report rising demand after Nvidia scandal

Jensen Huang's resignation Thursday after 33 years as chief executive of Nvidia capped one of the most astonishing corporate scandals in memory, with allegations of international espionage and extramarital affairs involving one of the world's most powerful men. Huang admitted in a letter to employees that he had been unfaithful to his wife of 41 years, following a New York Times report that he had a close relationship with a woman US officials suspect of spying for China's government. Reports of two additional affairs with Nvidia employees accompanied the resignation, and the stock of the $4tn chip company plunged.

The aftermath has moved quickly through the legal and political systems. Nvidia has hired a top US law firm to conduct an investigation into Huang's relationship with the suspected spy, and Trump said the FBI would launch its own probe. Huang has agreed to testify before Congress and faces a lawsuit in California accusing him of misleading shareholders.

The relationship at the center of the case is longstanding and, according to the New York Times, was already the subject of government attention. Huang was first seen with Alice Huang, who ran the Singapore-based Megaspeed datacenter firm, in late 2023. The two were together at a bar in Taiwan in 2024 and again at a conference in 2025, by which time the US commerce department was investigating whether Megaspeed was diverting AI chips to China in violation of export controls. In spring of this year, Marco Rubio oversaw a classified memo reporting that the Nvidia chief executive was in a relationship with a suspected Chinese spy based on electronic surveillance of Alice Huang, according to the New York Times. The Guardian reported that Huang's relationship with Alice Huang ultimately appears to have helped facilitate the transfer of billions of dollars in advanced Nvidia chips to China.

The Chinese government denies any involvement. A Ministry of Foreign Affairs spokesperson called the reports "nothing but a smear against China."

The market reaction was immediate. The Nasdaq fell 4% after Huang's resignation and Nvidia lost hundreds of billions of dollars in market value over the week. The Nasdaq Composite Index registered 25,136.27 as of Nov. 15, 2026, according to FRED, the Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis's economic database (raw series value, as of Nov. 15, 2026; fred.stlouisfed.org/series/NASDAQCOM). Security experts said that while the impact of the operation is unique, the tactics and goals are not.

"The reason that this is making the news is because obviously you have one of the wealthiest men in the world and one of the highest-profile CEOs in the world," said Don Aviv, the CEO of corporate intelligence firm Interfor International. "But this happens all the time, across industries."

Randy Watt, head of the corporate threat assessment and consulting firm SRW, described the approach in blunt terms. "The honeypot style trap is as old as the earth itself," Watt said. "It speaks to a consistent weakness in humans that is exploitable." He added: "I guarantee you there are a whole number of executives in powerful corporations that are reviewing their relationships with girlfriends."

Companies have spent years preparing for exactly this kind of approach. OpenAI has held training sessions running through "honeypot" scenarios in which a corporate spy or foreign intelligence asset pursues a sexual relationship with company staff in return for information or leverage, according to the Wall Street Journal. Aviv estimated that his firm alone has conducted around a dozen investigations for significant companies into schemes involving honeypots, personal relationships, coercion or blackmail over the past three years. OpenAI, Meta and Google did not return requests for comment on their security practices, and Nvidia did not comment on questions about Huang's affairs or when the company learned about his involvement with the alleged spy.

Beyond the lapse in judgment, intelligence analysts describe the affair as a failure of corporate oversight and a triumph for an alleged foreign influence operation. Analysts say human error remains the most vulnerable part of most companies, whatever the state of digital infrastructure.

Eduardo Jany, head of global security for a major US corporation, said the popular image of espionage is the wrong one. "Everybody thinks it's like what we see in Hollywood – surreptitious entry and listening devices and bugs and tails," Jany said. "It's really more about social engineering." Jany said actors study publicly available social media and data broker information to build targeted approaches, and that they seek proximity at industry events. "They're looking at easy access and proximity from conferences and customer relationships," Jany said. "They sidle up to our executives at any events or trade shows, or even make promises of investments."

Security consultants said chief executives are the hardest subjects to protect. Top leaders possess far more autonomy than the average employee, making it harder for staff to raise concerns, and executives can resist the idea that their personal relationships may be what they seem. "These are really smart, accomplished people and you don't want to come across telling them that they don't know how to behave themselves or that they're not sophisticated enough to know that they're being taken advantage of," said Brian Stephens, president of security risk advisory at the CEO advisory firm Teneo. Elizabeth Buckley, Teneo's head of cyber and technical solutions, said executives' insistence that they are not important enough to be a target serves another purpose: "It's a defense mechanism to being told that the way you live your life is going to have to change."

One open question is what Nvidia knew and when. Several corporate intelligence and security experts said it is unfathomable that someone at Nvidia would not be tracking the chief executive's whereabouts and who he was meeting with, especially on trips abroad. The Chinese government has denied any involvement in the affair or in corporate espionage efforts.

Silicon Valley has long been a target. During the 1970s and 80s, Soviet intelligence officers operated out of the Russian consulate in San Francisco as they carried out spying operations, according to former FBI special agent Rick Smith, who spent over two decades in the Bay Area working in counterintelligence. CIA director William Casey warned in 1985: "Gaining access to our advanced technology continues to be their top priority." A report from the nonprofit Center for Strategic and International Studies found at least 224 incidents of Chinese espionage in the US between 2000 and 2022, with over half of the plots directed at the theft of commercial technologies. In January, a federal jury in California convicted a former Google engineer of stealing confidential company information to benefit the Chinese government. In October, US authorities arrested a Saudi national accused of planting a sophisticated listening device within the headquarters of OpenAI, and North Korean spies created two businesses in the US with the intent of spreading malicious software to infect cryptocurrency developers, Reuters and cybersecurity firms reported last year.

The political fallout is continuing as well. Huang was a close ally of Donald Trump, accompanying the president at White House dinners and on Trump's official state visit to China, and Trump backed him after the scandal, calling him an "honorable guy." Axios reported that the administration is facing questions over why Alice Huang's firm Megaspeed was spared from being placed on a list of companies that pose national security concerns, despite intelligence outlining issues with the company, and US commerce secretary Howard Lutnick is under pressure to resign over perceived inaction on Megaspeed.

Corporate intelligence and security firms say they are expecting more demand for their services after the Nvidia scandal, including training, investigations and penetration testing aimed at the human elements of security. Yet experts said that for all the training, oversight and protocols, no amount of safeguards can fully replace personal judgment. Smith, the former counterintelligence agent, put it plainly: "Jensen Huang lost his mind here." He added: "But that often happens with romantic relationships."
