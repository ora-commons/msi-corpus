## IEA sees global oil surplus widen next year

Oil trading split on Thursday as traders assessed continued threats to Middle Eastern oil shipments and the global supply outlook. Front-month Brent crude futures rose 0.3% to $92.25 a barrel, while front-month West Texas Intermediate crude futures slipped 0.1% to $83.21 a barrel.

U.S. commercial crude inventories rose by 3.4 million barrels to 417.8 million barrels last week, according to data the Energy Information Administration released Wednesday. The increase exceeded the 1-million-barrel build expected by analysts surveyed by The Wall Street Journal.

The International Energy Agency said prolonged disruptions to shipping through the Strait of Hormuz could substantially reduce the world’s reliance on the waterway over the next decade as Middle Eastern producers develop alternative export routes. In its *World Energy Outlook*, the agency said oil flows through the strait could decline from around 20 million barrels a day before the conflict to 8.5 million barrels a day by 2030 and fewer than 2 million by 2035 under a scenario in which severe shipping disruptions persist.

The IEA said the Middle East would remain the world’s largest oil-exporting region even under that scenario. Investment in new pipelines and alternative supply routes would help producers maintain access to international markets.

The agency expects the global oil market to record a surplus of around 1.7 million barrels a day in the fourth quarter, widening to approximately 3.8 million barrels a day next year as Middle Eastern production recovers faster than demand.

The IEA outlook came amid continued security threats to regional oil infrastructure and shipping, which complicated efforts to restore exports through the Strait of Hormuz.

Analysts at Sparta Commodities said Wednesday that recovering Middle Eastern supplies remained vulnerable to attacks on vessels and energy infrastructure. They also pointed to disruptions at Russian refineries, which increased crude availability for export while keeping European diesel supplies tight.

Scott Shelton, an energy specialist at TP ICAP, a London-based interdealer broker, pointed to a stronger U.S. dollar and weakness across commodity markets as additional factors in crude trading. He also cited expectations of a substantial global supply build next year.
