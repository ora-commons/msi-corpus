## Federal rule ends aid for refugees admitted on humanitarian grounds

Sahar Amir lost Medicaid on Oct. 1 after a federal rule created by the 2025 One Big Beautiful Bill Act took effect. The Afghan refugee and former prosecutor, now a Lyft driver in Raleigh, had surgery in July at WakeMed to remove a cancerous left kidney. He still needs monitoring for kidney cancer and treatment for other chronic medical problems, including post-traumatic stress. NC Health News identified Amir with a pseudonym and withheld his photograph because his father, siblings and their families in Afghanistan could face risk.

Without Medicaid, Amir said he was unsure how he could afford follow-up cancer care. A bank balance he showed on his phone was $475, he had an unpaid credit card balance, and rent was due in less than a week. He set up a GoFundMe campaign. Federal rules prohibit NeighborHealth from providing free care, though patients can pay on a sliding scale, and Amir said he wanted to pay his share. Before surgery, he could work 12-hour days as a Lyft driver; pain now limits him to eight or nine hours, and he makes $150 on a good day.

The Oct. 1 cutoff came through a new federal rule that stripped medical care from Amir, his family and another 10,500 refugees and others in North Carolina who had been admitted to the United States on humanitarian grounds. The same federal change stripped food aid, and the state Department of Health and Human Services said 12,500 people had lost it earlier in 2026. Amir's family's Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program assistance had already ended in February, before the Oct. 1 Medicaid cutoff.

Congress created public-charge rules in 1996 that required most immigrants to wait five years before receiving Medicaid and other benefits. It created exceptions for refugees, asylum seekers, people who had been victims of human trafficking or domestic violence, and others admitted on humanitarian grounds. Those groups were also eligible for Affordable Care Act marketplace subsidies. Families with Special Immigrant Visas, including Amir's, were exempt from the public-charge rules and received streamlined entry from the United States, which was grateful for their help during the Afghanistan conflict.

Under the new rule, humanitarian arrivals lost access to assistance, including income-based marketplace subsidies, except in very limited circumstances. The only way for people in the Special Immigrant Visa pool to receive benefits was to obtain a green card. Before the Trump administration, newly arrived refugees were connected with local resettlement agencies and generally had about a year to seek green cards and begin the process toward citizenship.

Jennifer Taylor of Refugee Hope Partners, a Raleigh-based nonprofit that has helped refugees from more than 80 countries, said the Trump administration had paused many green-card applications since about mid-2025. “They’re just kind of in limbo, waiting,” she said. Taylor said almost no refugees had been admitted since then except for white Afrikaners from South Africa, while people elsewhere continued to wait. President Donald Trump's first-day executive order essentially shut down refugee entries to the United States, according to the original North Carolina Health News report.

After three years of applying for visas to Germany, Canada and Australia, the United States granted Amir entry on a Special Immigration Visa in January 2025. Amir's family arrived Jan. 2, 2025. Had their flight been delayed by a few weeks until after Trump's inauguration, they would have been refused entry. Amir and his family submitted their green-card paperwork while they were still in Pakistan. They have waited with thousands of other Afghan refugees who helped the U.S. military during Operation Enduring Freedom, the 20-year Afghan conflict. Taylor said families like Amir's previously received green cards within six months to a year of arriving, but the process now stalls even when applicants consult lawyers. Amir now drives Lyft around Raleigh to support his family while his children go to school and his wife, who speaks little English, keeps their small apartment in order.

Amir's former prosecutor colleague, now a housewife in Louisiana, spoke on condition that she not be named and said the government was treating families like Amir's as if they were criminals. “As Afghans who stood alongside the United States, supported it and worked closely with it, I expected America to provide us with full support in return,” she said. Amir has repeatedly asked officials to resolve his case. “Please see in the system, I am active. I am already in,” he said, shuffling through several inches of forms and other paperwork. “Please just issue my green card.”

Before leaving Afghanistan, Amir was an attorney who worked with the U.S. military processing supply procurement for Bagram Air Force Base from 2009 to 2011. He then served as a prosecutor in the Office of the Afghanistan Attorney General's Department for the Elimination of Violence Against Women for almost a decade. Amir led multiple high-profile cases against government officials, religious figures and Taliban members accused of raping girls and young women. His victories at trial put perpetrators behind bars for years at a time. A former colleague who now lives in Baton Rouge, Louisiana, recalled, “He was known for his courage, confidence and determination.” The colleague added, “Cases involving members of the Taliban, terrorists or violent extremists accused of violence against women were often assigned to him.”

Amir left Kabul with his wife, daughter and son in a van during the night in January 2022, heading for Pakistan. His family spoke little after loading bags into the van, and the atmosphere was grim. The escape came after the Taliban took Kabul and the country in August 2021. Foreign friends told him to wait at home and avoid wading into the chaos at the Kabul airport, and they promised to help him leave the country, but the help never arrived. By January 2022, his father — the only person Amir could confide in, a man in his 80s — told him not to speak with anyone and to leave.

Money changed hands at multiple Taliban checkpoints. At the Pakistan border around 6 a.m., a guard accused Amir of working for the government. Amir told the guard he was a taxi driver. The guard rejected the explanation and pressed Amir to tell the truth; Amir said he was not with the government. They argued for almost 45 minutes. The confrontation ended when Amir's daughter began crying and the guard allowed the van through after another bribe. The family entered Pakistan.

Amir began feeling pain in his lower back in early 2026. Matt Oettinger, a family physician at NeighborHealth, had seen him for back pain earlier in the year and knew he had a history of kidney stones. Oettinger initially treated the pain as kidney stones, but the pain persisted and worsened. A colleague ordered an image that revealed a mass, and NeighborHealth arranged for Amir to see a specialist quickly. Amir was diagnosed with cancer in summer 2026, and the family received support from North Carolina Medicaid to cover his health care. His surgery at WakeMed followed in July. “They think that they got it all. Right now, the prognosis is good, but they need to continue watching,” Oettinger said.

NeighborHealth has a strong reputation among immigrants and refugees, and resettlement agencies frequently refer patients there. About 40 percent of the clinic's patients speak languages other than English, including the Dari and Pashto languages widely spoken in Afghanistan. Oettinger said about 500 NeighborHealth patients, or 5 percent of the clinic's total, lost Medicaid coverage in October.

Amir also worries about Immigration and Customs Enforcement while he drives passengers around Raleigh. He said ICE arrests people, takes them to jail and then sends them to Louisiana, where the United States is holding detainees before deportation. Jennifer Taylor of Refugee Hope Partners said many Afghan refugees share Amir's hesitation about revealing his identity. Taylor said she was not certain that Amir's legal presence would prevent ICE from picking up him or other family members. “Fear is one of the biggest things that we see,” she said. “Just fear of the unknown, fear of what’s next, fear of ‘what happens to my family if we end up getting deported or detention’ or things like that. They left home for a reason. They left their country for a reason, and they cannot go back.”

The report said Pakistan was pushing refugees back into Afghanistan. The New York Times reported that Pakistan's government had forced the return of more than 2.7 million Afghan citizens to their home country since 2023. Amir said he could not return, despite his desire to see family members again, because the Taliban had released most of the people he had helped send to prison and would seek him. His greatest fear, he said, was that he would be deported directly to Pakistan and then from Pakistan to Afghanistan. “They will deport me directly to Pakistan, and Pakistan will deport directly to Afghanistan.”

Amir faced danger in Afghanistan before the Taliban returned. He showed NC Health News photographs of his Kabul home, which had bullet holes after masked men shot at it at the conclusion of one of his high-profile cases. The head of the office for the Elimination of Violence Against Women gave him firearms and weapons training, and two bodyguards became constant companions. Leslie Schneider, a Virginia prosecutor who served as an international legal adviser in Afghanistan with a gender justice assistance project before 2020, said resistance to prosecuting crimes against women made security protections necessary for officials working in the former department.

Amir's former colleague said his work was important not only for enforcing the law but also for protecting women's rights and helping victims seek justice, regardless of the power or influence of those accused. She recalled that handling such cases involved serious personal risks.

And now, under the Taliban, no one is doing that work — prosecuting violence against women.

Schneider remembered Amir, though not the specifics of his case. She said she hears from Afghan refugees in situations similar to Amir's almost every month. They have so much to mourn. She and other Americans who worked in Afghanistan remain haunted by the fate of former colleagues left behind and frustrated by their circumstances in the United States. Amir remembers the killing of his 21-year-old cousin, who was a CIA interpreter in Kandahar province, and says he was only a few meters from a suicide bomber when the attack occurred. When the Taliban returned to Kabul in August 2021, Amir said he saw his life's work shattered.

He still keeps photographs of himself with former Afghan president Hamid Karzai, the vice president and provincial leaders, as well as images from international conferences. In Afghanistan, Amir was important, respected, well-off and influential. Now, he described himself as a “simple taxi driver.” Amir said he would love to keep arguing cases and serve the United States, which he says he loves, but staying alive is his immediate priority. Without consistent health care and Medicaid, he said, that may not be possible.
