## Released files show oversight warnings and disclosure gaps

Newly released documents show that Peter Mandelson sought to arrange an “under the radar” lunch in April 2025 between an economic adviser to Donald Trump and a Global Counsel employee while Mandelson still held shares in the lobbying firm. He forwarded the emails to the employee to ask about availability.

The proposed lunch was intended to discuss “alternative options” to the World Trade Organization and how “like-minded partners come to terms with China and its practices.” Mandelson told the adviser he wanted to bring “a former trade colleague of mine” with “interesting views” who could “lead off.” He added: “It would be best to keep this under the radar.”

Mandelson had refused to dispose of his Global Counsel shares after being appointed ambassador because, according to an official’s letter, the firm had “not made an offer that Lord Mandelson considers commensurate with the fair value.” A July 2025 email captured his frustration over media inquiries about the shareholding: “They [Global Counsel] should pull their fingers out and buy my shares.”

Olly Robbins, the Foreign Office’s top civil servant, told Mandelson he needed to “avoid any perception that he is consciously or even unconsciously furthering [Global Counsel’s] commercial interests.” Mandelson nevertheless continued consulting former colleagues on his ambassador work while holding the shares.

He sent a draft of a speech to the Ditchley Foundation to a Global Counsel director for comments. After receiving feedback, Mandelson said he would “soften the lecture on China.”

Several crucial documents about Mandelson’s appointment remain withheld from the public. They include his conflict-of-interest declaration form and a summary produced by vetting officials about his security clearance.

The Guardian reported in April that Mandelson had failed the developed vetting process after Keir Starmer announced in December 2024 that he would be the next British ambassador to Washington. The Foreign Office overruled vetting officials’ recommendation that Mandelson be denied clearance, allowing him to take up the post.

According to sources cited by The Guardian, the vetting concerns included Mandelson’s associations with senior figures in China, Russia and Israel, as well as a £1m loan. The Guardian said those concerns were separate from his relationship with Jeffrey Epstein, whom it described as a convicted child sex offender. The Epstein issue led to Mandelson’s dismissal in September 2025.

Starmer also dismissed Robbins. Robbins told MPs investigating Mandelson’s appointment that he believed vetting officials considered Mandelson “borderline” and that he could be cleared with “mitigations,” but no documents had been disclosed to support that account. Robbins said his office had faced “constant pressure” from Downing Street to put Mandelson in post as soon as possible. He later received a six-figure payout from the government after his dismissal.

In February, parliament passed a humble address requiring the government to publish “all papers” relevant to Mandelson’s appointment. Some records were nevertheless held back in March and June at the request of the Metropolitan police, on the stated concern that disclosure could “potentially prejudice” its investigation into Mandelson. Ministers said the withheld material included vetting information and conflict-of-interest process documents.

One newly released email, from Foreign Office security chief Ian Collard to his line managers, said Mandelson had received clearance. Collard said Mandelson’s “private sector engagements” were being handled through the conflict-of-interest process and that Mandelson had received a letter about his responsibilities regarding personal conduct as an employee. Questions about his “overseas contacts” would be reviewed by other authorities responsible for higher levels of security clearance.

In July, the foreign affairs select committee concluded that Mandelson’s appointment was “nothing short of disastrous,” “highly damaging” for the British government and “painful and offensive to the victims of Jeffrey Epstein.” The Guardian said Mandelson had been approached for comment.
