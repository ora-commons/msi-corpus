## Eight live births were preterm, and eight newborns needed neonatal intensive care

Nearly a dozen scientists with the federal Centers for Disease Control and Prevention independently published a public-health alert in NEJM Evidence on Monday, according to Stateline. The alert documented 112 confirmed measles cases among pregnant women and 13 congenital cases from Jan. 1, 2025, through Sept. 28, 2026.

The authors defined congenital measles cases as either transmission to newborns in utero or cases with a positive test or febrile rash within 10 days of birth involving a woman with confirmed measles. All but two of the women either had not received the MMR vaccine or had an unknown vaccination status.

Among pregnancy cases with reported outcomes, 19 women had live births and two lost their pregnancies. Eight of the live births were preterm, and eight newborns were admitted to a neonatal intensive care unit. Thirteen newborns tested positive for congenital measles, and six developed measles-related symptoms, including one infant who showed symptoms related to a life-threatening brain-swelling condition. The other pregnancy cases either had unknown outcomes or involved women who were still pregnant as of Sept. 28.

“Health care systems and clinicians should be prepared to provide care to pregnant women and their infants with measles, particularly in communities with a high proportion of unvaccinated people,” the alert said.

Dr. John Gaudet, a retired pediatrician and associate professor at the William Carey University College of Osteopathic Medicine in Mississippi who was unaffiliated with the alert, said: “Once the measles gets a foothold in a community, it’s very contagious, and can include people who are pregnant.” He explained that subacute sclerosing panencephalitis, or SSPE, is an often-fatal neurological complication that usually appears between two and 10 years after infection in older children and young adults. Infection contracted in utero can bring it much earlier. “The whole timeline is compressed if it’s congenital,” Gaudet told Stateline.

Separately, provisional CDC data put the overall U.S. infant mortality rate, already high compared with other high-income countries worldwide, at about 5.5 deaths per 1,000 live births in 2024-2025. The rate was more than double among Black infants.

Measles is a highly contagious respiratory virus that can cause fever, cough and rash. Its complications include pneumonia, encephalitis and blindness, and the disease can be fatal. Two doses of the MMR vaccine for measles, mumps and rubella can prevent it.

Against that backdrop, an expert panel at the Pan American Health Organization formally reversed the United States’ measles elimination status on Tuesday. The determination had been expected after 12 continuous months of outbreaks, the first beginning in West Texas in fall 2025 and the most recent surging through Pennsylvania. PAHO, a regional office of the World Health Organization, had concluded in 2000 that the United States had essentially eliminated measles because spread had been interrupted for 12 months or more. The reversal ended a status the country had held for more than 25 years.

After convening the previous week, the panel made the same determination for Bolivia and Mexico. PAHO urged public-health leaders to step up vaccination and stop the spread of outbreaks. Canada, which lost its status a year earlier, was determined to have disrupted transmissions and to be making progress toward regaining elimination status. At least 10 people have died of measles this year, including two deaths announced last week.

The CDC reported 5,412 measles cases as of Nov. 12, more than double the 2,289 cases recorded through all of 2025. Nearly 18%, or 951, of this year’s cases were among children younger than 5. A section heading in the Stateline report listed 4,080 cases for the year and described that figure as the highest in decades, while the report’s body gave the 5,412 figure.

PAHO cautioned that outbreaks can be concentrated in particular communities or geographic regions within a country. “National vaccination figures can mask these disparities, while barriers to health services, missed vaccinations, misinformation and vaccine hesitancy leave children, adolescents and adults vulnerable to infection,” the statement said. Many public-health experts were concerned because children generally do not receive their first MMR dose until at least one year of age and remain highly susceptible. Infants who contract the virus before birth are also vulnerable.

At least 39 states now have MMR vaccination rates below 95%, the threshold considered necessary for herd immunity. Stateline reported that, throughout his leadership, U.S. Health and Human Services Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. had been an outspoken critic of vaccination, had opposed many vaccine mandates and had recommended fewer vaccines for children, while many Republican-led states had ended vaccine mandates for their populations.

In a Tuesday post on X, Kennedy blamed the decline in vaccinations on the COVID-19 pandemic and said his agency had been responding to measles outbreaks. He wrote: “I continue to recommend MMR vaccination as the most effective way to prevent measles.” Kennedy also wrote: “Worldwide COVID-era disruptions interrupted routine childhood vaccination, leaving gaps in protection here and abroad.”

“CDC has worked alongside state teams in Texas, South Carolina, Virginia, Kansas, New Mexico, and Wisconsin to help bring their outbreaks rapidly and successfully under control. CDC is also supporting Pennsylvania and New York with epidemiologists and other experts,” Kennedy wrote.

CDC Director Dr. Erica Schwartz said in a written email statement to Stateline: “The measles elimination designation is not what protects Americans. Vaccination does. We know where transmission is occurring and we’ve invested $100 million in state and local health departments to reach those communities.” She added: “Losing a designation doesn’t mean we abandon an evidence-based approach. It means we remain focused on stopping outbreaks.”

Dr. Susan Kansagra, chief medical officer of the Association of State and Territorial Health Officials, a group representing local health officials, said it was critical for state health departments to be sufficiently funded to carry out a public-health campaign to vaccinate their populations. Without vaccination mandates, she said, pediatricians play a pivotal role in informing parents about the safety and efficacy of the MMR vaccine.

“They are the most important because we know in lots of surveys, lots of what we hear on the ground, that providers are the most trusted members of the community, and that one-on-one physician-patient or provider-patient relationship is so important,” Kansagra said. “They’re also the first folks that will see somebody that might walk in that could have measles. And making sure that they have the tools they need to be able to respond appropriately is also really important.”

Gaudet said that during his three decades as a practicing pediatrician, he never saw a case of measles. Now teaching medical students, he shows them textbook pictures and gives them as much information as he can. He tells them, “I’ve never seen the measles, but you will. Be ready.”
