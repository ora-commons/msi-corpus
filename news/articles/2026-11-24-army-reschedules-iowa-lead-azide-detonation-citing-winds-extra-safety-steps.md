## Cellphone alert advises staying indoors, flags possible evacuation within 5 miles

The U.S. Army rescheduled the controlled detonation of 68 barrels of degraded lead azide at the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant in Middletown to Wednesday because of more favorable wind conditions and to allow more time for extra safety measures, according to a new court filing by the government. The plan for the controlled detonation has sparked an outpouring of public concerns about potential health and environmental effects. The Army said the operation is needed to prevent a possible chain-reaction explosion of the dangerous material.

In a legal filing late Monday, the Justice Department said Wednesday’s wind was expected to be east to west or southwest, away from high-population areas and large bodies of water. The National Weather Service forecast called for winds of 5 to 10 mph (8 to 16 kph), with cloudy conditions early and mostly sunny skies later.

An emergency notification sent to cellphones of people in the area Tuesday morning said the detonation was planned for 9 a.m. Wednesday and some people within 5 miles (8 kilometers) southwest of the plant may be evacuated. The “imminent threat alert,” which listed FEMA as the contact agency, told those up to 20 miles (32 kilometers) away to remain indoors with windows closed “to avoid airborne lead dust, noise and vibration.”

The new date gives the Army extra time to set up additional “blast barriers” around the bunkers where the lead azide will be detonated and to apply ballistic gels and foams designed to reduce particulate matter from controlled explosions, according to a document filed in the 8th U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals. The filing says the Army will douse the materials with thousands of gallons of water during the detonation to help absorb the explosion and reduce lead dust.

Current Gov. Kim Reynolds activated the state emergency management office on Friday, and the state created a dedicated website for information about the operation. Reynolds said in a Tuesday statement that, after speaking with military leaders, including Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth, she supports the planned detonation.

“I believe, based on the information I’ve been given, that the danger is immediate, and I trust the Army will do everything in its power to protect the people who could be affected,” Reynolds said.

The rescheduled operation follows Monday’s approval by a three-judge panel of the 8th U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals. The panel said the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency had adequately incorporated safeguards into an emergency permit intended to minimize the dispersal of dust. It said those conditions addressed the concerns of the environmental groups that sued to stop the detonation.

The court cited barriers already put up around the area where the barrels are stored, along with weather restrictions, advance public notification, updated air modeling, and environmental monitoring and sampling. The judges said the record also showed that the Army had evaluated “a full range” of alternatives, including using other chemicals to neutralize the lead azide and thermal treatment.

Environmentalists sued last week to stop the detonation at a site near Middletown, about 90 miles (140 km) south of Cedar Rapids and not far from the Mississippi River. The court temporarily halted the permit, prompting the Army to argue that it did not need authorization after all because the operation was an emergency. An attorney for the environmentalists said the legal challenge concerns the validity of the EPA permit, not the Army’s decision to proceed under the emergency provision. Earthjustice managing attorney Rachel Rintelmann said the lawsuit has alerted the public to the threat.

In court filings over the weekend, Army Col. Aaron Teller, a munitions emergency response specialist, explained that less liquid inside the barrels made the remaining solution more susceptible to freezing. He said that increased the sensitivity of crystals formed around the inside of the rims and the undersides of the lids.

“Even a small amount of friction can break the crystal formation, causing an explosive chain reaction,” Teller said.

Plant staff found several dozen barrels of degraded lead azide in March, according to the Army. The material is used in munitions production, and its crystallization created an unstable and dangerous situation, prompting the Army to assemble experts in munitions chemistry, engineering and environmental safety to develop a response.

According to EPA documents, alternatives such as neutralizing and decomposing the drums’ contents with other chemicals were considered. Officials said handling the drums would increase the probability of an accidental, fatal detonation. They also contend that the risk of an uncontrolled explosion is growing, particularly as temperatures drop in the coming days.

Some residents near Middletown have sealed windows and doors as a precaution against lead dust. Others living farther away raised concerns on social media about possible paths of the plume depending on the wind. Officials acknowledge that lead is toxic and recommend that people stay indoors during the detonation and clean outdoor surfaces with water afterward to limit exposure to lead particles.

President Donald Trump, the Army’s commander in chief, said on social media earlier in the day that, in addition to the court’s approval, he wanted Gov.-elect Rob Sand to approve the detonation. Sand, a Democrat who does not take office until January, has said the decision is fully up to the Trump administration.

The detonation was initially planned for last Friday, but the Army has changed the date three times following an outpouring of public concern about potential health and environmental effects.

Missouri Gov. Mike Kehoe said state agencies are working to establish additional monitoring of air and water quality, public health and agriculture resources. Middletown Mayor Rodney Johnson said the detonation must take place “so we can move on with our lives.” He also said on social media, “Improper storage and disposal is where things go wrong.”

Acting Army Secretary Adam Telle posted on social media Monday night, “This is a consensus decision,” and added, “Every expert agency agrees, and both Iowa’s current and incoming Governors say the call belongs to the experts.”
