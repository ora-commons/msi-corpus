## Bade expects gradual price effects, not an immediate consumer shock

The Yale Economic Lab estimated that the proposed tariffs would add another $400 for each American in general. PBS News reported the estimate while examining how the proposal could affect household prices and the economy. Gavin Bade of The Wall Street Journal, who first reported the proposal, said that if the tariffs went into force in full, they would be significant for consumers' pocketbooks.

Bade said consumers would not experience a large price shock at the outset. A certain tariff level was already in place entering the year, he said, before the Supreme Court knocked down many tariffs and lowered the rate. Companies had not changed their prices much because "they always knew that the Trump administration would try to jack these tariffs back up again." Over the previous year to a year and a half, Bade said, the tariffs filtered through the U.S. economy gradually as companies depleted their stockpiles and raised prices. "The U.S. consumer is paying most or almost all of the tariff price here, despite what the Trump administration says," he said.

In background, the administration proposed a new round of tariffs on China, the European Union and 12 other major trading partners. China would face the highest rate, with a new 20% tariff on most products above existing levels and the possibility of 40% if negotiations fall apart. Other countries would face additional tariffs of 10% to 20%. The tariffs could take effect as soon as January.

U.S. Trade Representative Jamieson Greer said the proposal was intended to protect manufacturing in the United States. "We have a very consumption-driven economy, and we should be producing more of that here in the United States, but it makes it really hard when these other countries are producing or creating capacity that are not really justified by the economics of the situation," Greer said.

The proposal combines targeted tariffs with negotiations. Greer said: "And so we're proposing, on the one part, targeted tariffs that apply to a subset of goods from a given country at a tariff rate that maintains the deals we made with them. And on the other hand, we're also negotiating with these countries. We expect to have a broad, even a plurilateral negotiation to talk about excess capacity and how to resolve it."

Bade said the administration was trying to rebuild its tariff wall after the Supreme Court ruled many of its levies illegal in February. This time, it reached for the more tried-and-tested Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974. Bade said the proposal would put the tariff level back roughly where it stood at the beginning of this year, before the Supreme Court decision, and Yale's review reached essentially the same assessment.

Bade said Section 301 tariffs had a much better chance of legal standing than the levies struck down by the Supreme Court. He described the earlier legal history this way: "That first round of tariffs under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act, or IEEPA, that was the first time that law had ever been used for tariffs, and the Supreme Court really looked askance at the administration trying to rewrite the entire global trading system using this emergency law."

Section 301 is intended to address unfair trading practices by other nations, according to Bade. He said the administration's rationale was that other countries were unnecessarily subsidizing their industries, allowing countries such as China to flood world markets with very low-priced products and disadvantaging U.S. producers. The administration says tariffs under Section 301 are justified on that basis.

The timing overlaps with a tariff truce with China that is supposed to end in January. Bade said he did not think the proposal would by itself blow up the trade truce or U.S.-China trade relations. The United States had telegraphed the action through a monthslong investigation, he said, and the 20% rate on some Chinese products was roughly the level China had expected for a long time. It would return tariffs to approximately the level in place when the truce was signed in October 2025.

Bade said the greater disruption would come if talks did not continue apace and China took steps the administration disliked. Under the proposal, the rate on some Chinese products could rise to 40%.

Bade also described changes in corporate behavior. He said the U.S. trade deficit with China had gone down, companies had tried moving production out of China, perhaps to other Southeast Asian nations, and a substantial amount of technology manufacturing had moved to India.

Transshipment remained a problem, Bade said. Chinese goods could be shipped through another country, receive a small amount of processing in places such as Vietnam or Thailand, then arrive in the United States labeled as products made in the transit country. The administration was trying to address transshipment, but existing laws against it were difficult to track, he said. Bade described corporate responses to the tariffs as both legal and illegal.

Lisa Desjardins said the tariff plan was one factor, along with inflation fears, in the stock-market turbulence that day. Bade expected continued anxiety, saying tariffs were probably already reflected in prices but that the AI trade was under threat amid controversy involving Nvidia, Jensen Huang and Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick. Congressional hearings were due to begin the following week, Bade said, and he expected further news about investigations into other companies that may have been smuggling chips into China. Because so much of the market is concentrated in AI and technology firms, he said, weakness in that group could spread across the broader market.
