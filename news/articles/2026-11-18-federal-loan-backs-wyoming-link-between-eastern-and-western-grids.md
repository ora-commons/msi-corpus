## Federal loan backs Wyoming link between eastern and western grids

**Subtype:** fact

- The U.S. Department of Energy announced a conditional $2.8 billion federal loan toward Grid United’s $4 billion Wyoming Intertie.
- Grid United plans to build 106 miles of high-voltage lines from the Laramie River Station to the Aeolus substation.
- Grid United’s Wyoming Intertie would allow system operators and utilities to move electricity in either direction between the eastern and western grids.
- The U.S. Department of Energy estimated $39 billion in avoided costs over 30 years for ratepayers in Wyoming and 17 other states.
- Grid United said the proposed route remains subject to county, state and federal approvals and the securing of land rights.

## Project targets a 2030 construction start after regulatory approvals

The U.S. Department of Energy announced Tuesday that it is backing Houston-based Grid United’s $4 billion Wyoming Intertie with a conditional $2.8 billion loan through the Energy Dominance Financing program. The planned transmission project would run 106 miles from the coal-fired Laramie River Station outside Wheatland to the Aeolus substation near Medicine Bow.

Grid operators and utilities could send electricity in either direction between the eastern and western grids under the proposed connection, which the DOE said would double the flow between the systems. Office of Energy Dominance Financing Director Gregory Beard said the project would strengthen the grid and give operators new tools to respond to surges in demand or outages.

“The bridging of these neighboring systems with a transmission line,” Beard added, “provides more reliable energy flows, lowers ratepayer electricity bills and stabilizes the grid, especially during extreme weather conditions.”

Wyoming sits in the western grid, and the edge between the two grids essentially traces the state’s eastern border. Wyoming Energy Authority Director of Energy Market Development John Jenks said the Laramie River Station is one of the few power plants with access to both grids, making it a likely location for strengthening the connection.

Jenks said the intertie would provide flexibility in dispatching electricity. “It adds flexibility to energy being dispatched onto the grid,” he said. “We can send electricity east if we have excess electricity, or we can take it from the east. For Wyoming electricity producers, it’s expanding the markets to dispatch and sell their electricity.”

The project does not rely on building additional electrical generation capacity, Beard said, but he said it would enable new power projects. “There are certainly growing electricity demands nationwide and on both sides of this interconnect. So this [helps] solve a current problem.”

Construction is expected to begin in 2030 and take three years. Once completed, the intertie is estimated to save ratepayers in Wyoming and 17 other states a combined $39 billion in avoided costs over the next 30 years, according to the DOE.

Beard also credited President Donald Trump for his “energy dominance” initiatives, including the Strengthening the Reliability and Security of the U.S. Electric Grid executive order.

Jenks cautioned that the intertie would not necessarily lower rates for Wyoming customers. He said it could avoid occasional price spikes passed to customers by allowing available power to move to where it is needed during periods of imbalance.

“It can dispatch generation resources more efficiently, which should — in theory — lower electricity bills,” he said. “It might be a stretch to say it will [lower monthly bills], but that’s the theory behind it.”

The route from Platte County to Carbon County consists of about 90% state and federally owned land, according to Beard, and the project may receive fast-track permitting. “This is not going to have issues around land acquisition or condemning properties to put the transmission line up,” he said. “There will not be protests in the street over condemning land to get this line up.”

Grid United said it is still working with tribal nations and landowners to finalize the route. “The route will remain proposed until it receives approval from county, state and federal regulatory bodies,” according to the company’s Wyoming Intertie website. “Construction will commence after Wyoming Intertie secures land rights and regulatory approvals.”

Until those approvals and land rights are secured, the intertie remains a proposal. Its projected savings and effects on electricity rates remain prospective, and the announced federal loan is conditional.
