## Regional sales tax to raise roughly $1bn a year, winning 62.2% support

Voters in the San Francisco Bay Area approved two transit funding measures in Tuesday's election, backing a regional sales tax projected to generate roughly $1bn a year for the region's four largest transit operators and a San Francisco parcel tax to fund Muni. Advocates said the measures could help financially distressed public transit agencies avoid service cuts that would mean less fare revenue and fewer commuters, with potential effects on residents and the regional economy.

The Regional Transit Measure, or RTM, combines a 0.5% sales tax across four Bay Area counties and a 1% sales tax in San Francisco. It received 62.2% support across all five counties that voted on the measure, including 78.6% in San Francisco. Separately, 73.6% of San Francisco voters approved a parcel tax to fund Muni, the city's main transit system and the first publicly owned and operated transit agency in a major US city.

Dependence on fares for operating revenue, together with the region's nation-leading shift to remote and hybrid work, left Bay Area transit agencies exposed to low ridership during the pandemic. As pandemic relief funds were set to expire, transit advocates shifted into action, and Connect Bay Area backed the two initiatives.

In 2023, Connect Bay Area organizer Lian Chang got involved with the fight to save public transit when she made coffins and replicas of local trains and buses out of cardboard for a mock funeral procession in front of city hall. Chang said the cardboard replicas had done considerable mileage since, with some needing new paint jobs as they circulated from press conferences to campaign events and rallies.

Chang had no experience campaigning then, but she later helped rally volunteers to paint many banker boxes into buses and trains to carry the signatures that got the measures on the ballot. The effort added a playful and visually striking element to the campaign. The biggest challenge, Chang found, was finding ways for transit riders to self-identify and organize themselves into a constituency. Transit riders tend to be immigrants, children, seniors and lower-income people. To create a sense of pride among them, Chang worked with local designers to make buttons for distribution on specific bus lines slated for cuts. The buttons also helped recruit more volunteer signature-gatherers.

Thousands of volunteers collected 77,000 of the 305,000 signatures that qualified the measures for the ballot. The Connect Bay Area campaign called that total the highest in recent memory. “We don’t know of any larger signature-gathering mobilization in the Bay Area,” Chang said.

Had the measures not passed, Bay Area Rapid Transit would have been forced to close as many as 15 stations as soon as early next year, reducing service frequency and increasing the cost of rides and parking. Caltrain, the commuter rail system connecting San Francisco with the South Bay region, would have closed 10 stations and ended weekend service. Muni would have reduced a third of its service, eliminated 20 bus lines and suspended its streetcars and cable cars. All agencies would have had to end evening services.

Less frequent service and fewer stations would have meant less fare revenue and fewer commuters, creating a feedback loop that would have left Bay Area residents without reliable, affordable ways to get around the sprawling region. The effects would have been especially consequential in a region formed around a large body of water, where freeways and bridges are heavily congested.

A recent report authored by Laura Tolkoff, transportation policy director for the San Francisco Bay Area Planning and Urban Research Association, estimated that traffic delays on the San Francisco-Oakland Bay Bridge and at other regional chokepoints would have more than doubled during peak hours. The bridge sees roughly 3.5m crossings a month.

The measures could still face legal challenges from anti-tax groups, as past transit initiatives have faced. Such challenges have tied up funds in escrow accounts for years. Rebecca Long of the Metropolitan Transportation Commission, the agency in charge of administering the RTM funds, said, “The Public Transit Revenue Measure District is prepared to vigorously defend the will of the voters and the validity of the measure.”

Jeff Cretan, a spokesperson for Connect Bay Area, the coalition that backed the two initiatives, said his aim was to keep momentum high for transit funding when the measures expire in about 15 years. “This is an opportunity for us to go to the state and say, we need to change how we do this, not just here, but in other parts of the state,” Cretan said.

The Guardian compared California with other regions in the United States and beyond, saying historical investment in public transit in the state's urban centers lagged behind those regions even as California emphasized climate policies. A 2022 report from the California Air Resources Board said existing public transit networks would need to double their capacity and service frequency by 2030 for California to meet its own climate goals. BART, AC Transit, Caltrain and Muni make up 30% of the state's transit ridership, while only 5% of BART's operating revenue comes from the state.

Tuesday's election also brought a wave of new leadership to the state. Transit advocates already see state senator Scott Wiener, who co-authored the transit measures and won his bid to succeed Nancy Pelosi in Congress, as an ally. Advocates are also hopeful that governor-elect Xavier Becerra will prioritize public transit after seeing strong support from Bay Area voters, although transit was not a central issue of his gubernatorial campaign. Becerra's campaign did not return the Guardian's request for comment.

“I hope that Governor Becerra and elected representatives take note of the messages voters just sent: transit is not controversial – it’s essential and it’s incredibly popular,” Tolkoff said.
