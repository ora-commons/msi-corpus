## Injunction would bar construction indefinitely while the case proceeds

U.S. District Judge Leonie Brinkema said at a Friday hearing that she did not see "any way" the government could win the case on the merits, and suggested that Arlington County was likely to prevail in its lawsuit challenging President Trump's proposed 250-foot triumphal arch. Brinkema said she would grant the request of the county board of Arlington, Va., which sued over the arch in October, for a preliminary injunction blocking construction indefinitely.

On Wednesday, D.C. District Judge Tanya Chutkan extended a restraining order that prevents the Trump administration from breaking ground through Dec. 3 while she considers the underlying legal arguments. That case was brought earlier this year by a group that includes Vietnam War veterans and was joined by the governments of D.C. and Virginia last month, and nine Kennedy family members moved to get involved last week. Brinkema's ruling goes further, preventing construction while Arlington County's legal challenge plays out.

Brinkema's assessment went beyond the requested pause. When lawyers focused on the government's reliance on a 1925 law, Brinkema told them, according to Reuters, "It hurts your credibility with the court by focusing on that." She also called the project a "rushed job" and seemed to find the government's analysis of the structure's environmental and economic impact inadequate, particularly concerning the impact of construction cranes — which the government has said would be taller than the arch itself — on airspace.

She reiterated that the project lacks congressional approval, which the Trump administration has not sought. The administration argues that the arch is covered by a 1925 law authorizing a pair of much shorter columns at the same location, which were never built. Brinkema and Chutkan have both frowned on that argument.

The proposed 250-foot arch would be built at Memorial Circle, which is part of D.C. but sits on the Virginia side of the Potomac River, a short distance from both Arlington National Cemetery and Ronald Reagan Washington National Airport, both in Arlington.

Arlington County argues the arch would "cause significant harm" to its residents in multiple ways, including increasing noise and air pollution and "clogging thoroughfares needed for emergency transport services." The county also says an arch that doubles as a military complex, as Trump has suggested, would pose public safety risks and require additional county resources to manage.

Arlington also argues the arch would disrupt historic sightlines between Arlington National Cemetery and D.C.'s National Mall, and pose logistical challenges for the roughly 24 to 27 funerals the cemetery holds every weekday. The county has concerns about air and road traffic, saying construction-related delays could cause more accidents and disrupt emergency vehicles far beyond the traffic circle.

In a filing, Arlington police, fire and emergency units wrote: "In the last 60 days, Arlington police, fire, and emergency units have responded to approximately 200 incidents in the area surrounding the Circle, including traffic stops and assisting with unconscious persons within Arlington National Cemetery." The filing added: "And the visitors the Arch is intended to attract will increase demand for those same emergency services."

The plaintiffs, like many critics of the arch, accused the Trump administration of rushing through federally mandated environmental reviews and failing to consider alternatives. The National Park Service has defended the federal review process, which ended in approval earlier this month, and said building the arch somewhere else would defeat its purpose. In a filing last month, the plaintiffs wrote that "The County brings this lawsuit to halt the agency's feverish pace toward construction of the Arch and to ensure that the 'hard look' mandated by [the National Environmental Preservation Act] is completed before the agency commences disruptive and harmful ground-disturbing activities."

The Federal Aviation Administration has said the arch would not pose a hazard to aircraft as long as it is lit a certain way, including with an eternal flame on top. Aviation safety experts said they were concerned that the structure would add an additional layer of risk to an already complicated airspace. They said that concern is greater if the arch has military capabilities, which were not included in the FAA's analysis.

Democracy Forward, the nonprofit representing the plaintiffs, confirmed Brinkema's decision in a statement shared with NPR. "We are pleased that the court has recognized the irreparable harm that will come to people who live and work in Arlington and stopped the massive vanity project while our case is heard and considered by the court," senior counsel Kate Talmor said.

NPR reached out to the White House for comment. Spokesperson Davis Ingle told NPR earlier this week that the administration will "continue making the case for this beautiful and fitting monument and are confident we will be fully vindicated as this litigation proceeds." Brinkema, an appointee of President Clinton, is the second judge to rule against the arch this week.

The preliminary injunction Brinkema announced would leave construction barred indefinitely while Arlington County's legal challenge plays out. The dispute centers on the project's effects on emergency services, traffic and historic views, the federal environmental review process and airspace risks, while the administration continues to defend the federal approval and its legal basis for construction at Memorial Circle.
