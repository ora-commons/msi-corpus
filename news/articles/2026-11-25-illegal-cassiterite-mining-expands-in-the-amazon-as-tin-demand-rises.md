## Federal police seized about 188 tonnes in 2025 amid rising tin prices

Brazilian criminal organizations have expanded illegal cassiterite mining in the Amazon as demand for tin used in datacentres, AI and electric vehicles has pushed prices higher. Cassiterite, the tin-bearing ore, is abundant in Brazil. Federal police and prosecutors say the organizations have seized public lands, displaced Indigenous communities and taken over equipment financing, production and logistics. Their operations have reached protected Indigenous territory, including Yanomami land, where illegal gold mining has historically been a problem.

Federal police are investigating Alexandre Pires, a popular pagode singer-songwriter, as part of Operation Disco de Ouro over his alleged involvement in a scheme to ship cassiterite worth millions of dollars abroad. Federal police discovered the link last September while searching for documents tied to an illegal cassiterite export network. The discovery connected an illegal mining and money-laundering scheme with an address tied to one of Brazil’s biggest showbusiness stars from the 90s. Police uncovered a message in which Pires allegedly admitted participating and said he had failed to deliver the illegal ore he had promised his associates. “This time, I sent crushed rock,” Pires said, “because I had no cassiterite.”

The market shift follows a 3% increase in global refined tin consumption last year as countries seek supplies for datacentres, the AI industry, electric vehicles and electrification projects associated with the energy transition. Global supply is concentrated in a few countries, and the Democratic Republic of the Congo, Indonesia, Bolivia and Myanmar all faced disruptions last year. Those production declines led the International Tin Association to predict a 13,000-tonne deficit by 2030, further increasing price pressure. On the London Metal Exchange, the price per tonne surged from a 2023 low of $22,000 (£16,800) to a record $58,000 this year.

Natalie Scott-Gray, a senior metals demand analyst at StoneX, said, “From artificial intelligence to datacentres, everything relies on tin-based solder joints,” adding, “Tin is price-inelastic; its low melting point and excellent conductivity make it hard to replace.” Silver could serve as an alternative, but Scott-Gray said it is significantly more expensive. More than 50% of tin is used in soldering applications, and fast-growing sectors such as digitalisation, electric vehicles and renewable energy are driving demand. Scott-Gray characterized the demand outlook as structural rather than cyclical and said a supply shortfall may not halt industries but could cause delays and drive up costs.

When global prices were low, illegal cassiterite mining was seldom an issue in Brazil. As prices rose, criminal organizations became increasingly involved, threatening local communities and the environment. The Guardian identified the Primeiro Comando da Capital and Comando Vermelho among Brazilian gangs that had transformed illegal mining into a lucrative venture. The report said such operations are often seen as less complicated than drug trafficking and can garner political support. It also described Jair Bolsonaro, now in prison for attempting a coup, as a far-right advocate for mining, including on Indigenous lands.

Rodrigo Pereira Chagas, an expert in socio-environmental conflicts at the Federal University of Roraima, said, “Interest in mining on Yanomami lands dates back to the 1970s, fuelled by limited regulation and support from local elites. The rise in metal prices has further incentivised this activity.” Extracting cassiterite profitably requires mining large quantities, with significant environmental impact. Criminal groups have exploited protected Indigenous lands, including the Yanomami territory, while operating with little regard for the environment.

Traditional artisanal mining methods were illegal but less environmentally damaging and are no longer feasible. According to police and prosecutors, criminal groups now seize public lands, displace Indigenous communities, finance equipment purchases and manage production and logistics. Cassiterite was previously transported in bulk by road, but it is now often transported by air. Pedro Duarte, head of the environmental division of the federal police in Roraima, said, “Air transport is being used more frequently, even for large volumes. In some cases, such as in Yanomami territory, the same aeroplane that transports gold may also carry cassiterite.”

Federal police seized about 400 tonnes of cassiterite between 2020 and 2023, while estimated seizures for 2025 totaled about 188 tonnes. Multinational companies are licensed to mine cassiterite in the Amazon, but Brazil’s federal public prosecutor’s office and the federal police report that some are working with illegal operators in supply chains exporting the ore.

Earlier this year, the prosecutor’s office launched an investigation into the Swiss company Gerald Metals and the US firm MLS Berkowitz Investments over the extraction of 733 tonnes of cassiterite from Yanomami Indigenous territory between 2021 and 2022. Investigators are also examining whether Betser, a mining company led by Brazilian businessman Christian Costa dos Santos, was allegedly involved in the extraction. Santos was arrested in connection with Operation Disco de Ouro. Prosecutors allege that brothers Diego and Matheus Possebon, agents for Alexandre Pires, sent cassiterite to Gerald Metals for more than two years.

Gerald Metals, MLS Berkowitz Investments, Betser and the legal representatives for Pires, the Possebon brothers and Santos were approached for comment. Matheus Possebon’s lawyer said his client “is a businessman in the music and entertainment sectors,” “has never been involved in the trade or export of cassiterite” and “conducts legitimate business activities consistent with his income.” In previous comments to the Brazilian news website G1, Diego Possebon’s legal representatives said he “acted lawfully” and “remains available to provide any further clarification.” Pires’s defense said their client had no involvement in illegal mining, “least of all in an Indigenous area,” and had never committed any wrongdoing.

When the Brazilian government increases environmental enforcement on Yanomami lands, cassiterite mining decreases, but miners relocate to other parts of the Amazon, particularly Venezuela and Guyana. Chagas said, “High profitability fuels greed, so people move to places where they find new opportunities.” The Orinoco region in Venezuela has become a hotspot for illegal mining because of weak controls. In Guyana, policies favoring natural-resource extraction are expanding from oil to minerals. Chagas said the Amazonian Guiana Shield—encompassing parts of Brazil and Colombia, along with much of Venezuela, Guyana, French Guiana and Suriname—has attracted many migrants seeking opportunities in illegal mining.

Bram Ebus, founder and co-director of Amazon Underworld, said Venezuela has become a global hub for smuggling cassiterite because export duties are low. Ebus noted, “In addition to tin, tungsten and rare-earth minerals may sometimes be present in cassiterite.” He added, “Many of these minerals are exported to China.”

In February, Brazilian authorities recommended creating a cassiterite monitoring system similar to the one used for gold to combat illegal mining and reduce trafficking. Federal prosecutor André Luiz Porreca Ferreira Cunha in Amazonas said, “It is very difficult to determine the origin of a mineral and whether it is legal or not. However, we can establish a sample bank, akin to the one that exists for gold.” Authorities said the system would also help protect the environment.

A 2026 study mapped rare-earth elements, uranium and thorium from illegal cassiterite-monazite mining in the Amazon. The study found that tailings were enriched with heavy rare earths and yttrium, a rare-earth element, degrading soil and potentially posing severe health risks, especially to children. Duarte said, “This mining causes more deforestation than gold mining.” He added, “What we are witnessing is even greater devastation – and we do not know if the situation will ever return to normal.”
