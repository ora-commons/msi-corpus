## Supreme Court agrees to hear challenge to Trump's asylum ban

William Brangham of the PBS NewsHour reported that more than 491,000 people were arrested by U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement officials in the last fiscal year, and more than 483,000 of those people were deported. PBS NewsHour anchor Amna Nawaz said the Department of Homeland Security reported that immigrant deportations and arrests hit an all-time high. That record high — breaking last year's record — is being touted by the Trump administration, which has said it aims to deport one million immigrants annually, and which Brangham noted argues it is only halfway there.

John Sandweg, who served as acting ICE director under President Obama, said the raw totals obscure where removals have historically come from. A significant portion of ICE's deportations, he said, came from the border: individuals not arrested by ICE agents but apprehended by Border Patrol agents and handed over to ICE, which then executes the removal. Those individuals can be deported much more quickly, Sandweg said. He noted that the prior record — 410,000 removals during the Obama administration in 2012 — included over about 250,000 of those individuals who were apprehended at the border.

The current figures, Sandweg said, highlight "the remarkable way in which the Trump administration has really wrangled the immigration enforcement system to move at a pace like we have never seen before," expediting the processing of those individuals on the back end as well as increasing the number of arrests.

The administration's goal of one million deportations a year runs into a structural limit, Sandweg said. The administration has put a lot of resources into increasing the size of ICE, which increases the number of individuals who could be apprehended annually, but the biggest challenge is on the back end: the immigration courts. At the border, officials can quickly serve a deportation order without seeing an immigration judge through expedited removal. But for the population in the interior, where most of this work is now focused, most individuals are required to see an immigration judge.

Sandweg said the Trump administration has "put a lot of pressure on those courts to expedite cases, but we haven't seen them put significant resources in there." Unless and until the immigration courts see a real expansion so that the administration's new handpicked judges can issue those deportation orders more quickly, he said, it is "really hard to see them getting up to a million deportations in a year," even with all the new ICE resources coming online.

Enforcement on the interior has drawn a great deal of blowback, Brangham said. He said children have been arrested, U.S. citizens have been arrested, and several Americans were killed, and said there was a great deal of pushback against the administration, some of which was reflected in the midterm elections.

DHS Secretary Markwayne Mullin was asked by Fox News anchor Laura Ingraham whether he had been given instructions to lighten up on enforcement. "Not at all," Mullin replied. He said ICE had shifted priorities: "We have changed our priorities from doing -- from doing, let's say, job site raids to doing warrant raids, meaning that we go into cities and we go after the worst of the worst."

Brangham asked whether those types of raids are the best way to try to capture and remove the hardened criminals in the country. Sandweg answered, "Yes, unfortunately not." What the data shows, he said, is "it's really not about the worst of the worst." Sandweg said the challenge the secretary faces, and what he called the contradiction in what Mullin says, is that in immigration enforcement, driving up the total number of deportations toward a high number like the one this administration is trying to hit "pushes ICE to focus on more on those noncriminal populations."

Sandweg gave two reasons. First, that population is generally easier to find: hardened criminals are more adept at hiding, make it harder on agents to track them down, and require a lot more work to make a single apprehension. Second, because of due process requirements and immigration court backlogs, agents focus on populations based on how quickly ICE can deport them after an arrest rather than on criminal history. The quickest populations to remove, Sandweg said, are people who were previously ordered deported five or six years ago but were given a stay for humanitarian circumstances, and people who did not leave the country but were not detained when they went through the court process. Those populations, he said, tend to be the more sympathetic ones — people who lived in the United States longer and have no criminal history — which he called the juxtaposition with the administration's "worst of the worst" framing.

The enforcement totals were reported alongside a separate legal development. Amna Nawaz reported that the Supreme Court said it would hear the Trump administration's ban on asylum claims at the southern border. President Trump enacted the ban the first day of his second term, and a lower court judge has since ruled the policy is illegal.
