## Small island coalition presses COP31 for action and climate finance

**Subtype:** fact

- The 39-state Alliance of Small Island States is pressing COP31 in Antalya for more climate action.
- The Alliance of Small Island States wants $500 million for climate resiliency programs.
- The Alliance of Small Island States wants $200 million for natural disaster early-warning systems.

## Alliance seeks $500 million for resiliency, $200 million for early warnings

COP31 is in its second and final week in the Turkish resort town of Antalya, where the Alliance of Small Island States, a small but vocal bloc of 39 small island developing states, or SIDS, is pressing for more action on climate change and financial support for adaptation and disaster recovery. The talks follow months of climate-induced disasters, geopolitical shocks and an intensifying El Niño that are making life in the world’s smallest countries more precarious.

As one of the world’s “big ocean states,” Tonga has maritime territory that dwarfs its land mass. Like many islands, Tonga’s existence is threatened by climate change. Record-breaking ocean temperatures jeopardize the marine life Tongans depend on, while melting glaciers cause sea level rise that can increase erosion or flooding. Tonga Prime Minister Fatafehi Fakafānua said, “We don’t need more studies. We don’t need more negotiations. We just need more action.”

Tonga and other small island developing states are asking larger, more polluting nations to curb emissions before it is too late. The islands are demanding that richer, more polluting countries pay for them to adapt to changing climates, mitigate their negative impacts and recover from climate-induced disasters.

The alliance wants to leave COP31 with $500 million in funding for climate resiliency programs and $200 million for natural disaster early-warning systems. Fakafānua said, “It’s only fair that those who are suffering from the effects of climate change are assisted by those who were at fault.”

Ilana Seid, chair of the Alliance of Small Island States and a U.N. ambassador from Palau, said, “On our own, individual SIDS are small economies. We have little leverage, but when you put us together and we speak with one voice, we can speak pretty loudly and pretty strongly.”

Global climate goals are off track, with the United Nations announcing that the world is unlikely to reach its ambition of capping global warming at 1.5 degrees Celsius (2.7 degrees Fahrenheit) above preindustrial levels. Island nations are demanding that the goal be kept alive.

Adelle Thomas, a senior director of the Natural Resources Defense Council who is from the Bahamas and vice chair of an Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change working group, said, “It’s really not a temperature threshold for us, it’s a survival threshold,” adding, “The real question is going to be whether political ambition is catching up with the lived reality that people are facing.”

Thomas said the climate talks have kept global warming from hitting even higher temperatures, which proves COP works but not at the same pace as climate change. She also said, “It is a long-term game, which unfortunately doesn’t align with the level of impacts that we’re seeing, but I don’t think that we should give up on COP.”

Many agreements made at the talks are voluntary and nonbinding. The world has yet to agree on how to transition away from fossil fuels that cause climate change, a gap driving growing criticism of the COP process. Leaders, negotiators and activists said COP31 needs to restore hope that these conferences make a difference on climate action.

Atiterentaai Rinimarawa, a 24-year-old youth representative from the island nation of Kiribati, said, “I feel that my future and the future of generations after me are on the line.” Rinimarawa said, “We are not asking for sympathy, but we are calling for climate justice, stronger commitment and real action.”

For the second year in a row, the United States is absent from the talks. The country is the world’s largest economy and a major emitter. Fakafānua called the process frustrating but said, “we’ve definitely made progress and we’re better with COP than without.”

Turkey and Australia are co-hosting COP31. Leaders from the Pacific islands said their role has been more prominent through Australia this year, especially with Fiji hosting a pre-COP meeting in October. Fakafānua said, “I absolutely think that our voice has been elevated,” adding, “It just speaks to how far we have come, and it’s taken this long, up to COP31, for us to be really center stage.”

Seid described the 39-state alliance as a “powerful bloc.” Seid said, “Our vulnerabilities are so consistent, regardless of if you’re from the Pacific, or the Caribbean, or the African region,” and added, “The spotlight on the small island developing states is a little brighter this year.”

Island nations have faced an especially difficult year. The world’s oceans hit their hottest temperatures on record in August, and the record-breaking El Niño is still intensifying. Geopolitical shocks such as the Iran war have sent energy prices soaring. COP31 is competing for international attention as other global summits in China and the Philippines begin.

Thomas said, “Average citizens around the world are experiencing climate impacts in their homes and in their communities and that directly links to what’s going on in these international negotiations,” adding, “It’s absolutely up to individual citizens and communities to make sure that their leaders are tackling the climate change crisis.”

Qiyun Woo, a youth environmentalist from the city-state of Singapore in Southeast Asia, said, “These climate talks need to renew faith,” adding, “Faith young people should have in these processes and in world leaders helping to secure a more livable future for generations to come.”
