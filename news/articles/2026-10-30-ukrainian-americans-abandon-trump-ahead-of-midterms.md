## Iran war, tariffs compound Republican election risk

Outside the Ukrainian educational and cultural center in Jenkintown, a quiet Pennsylvania suburb on the outskirts of Philadelphia, a banner expressed unqualified gratitude: “Thank you USA for supporting Ukraine.” Inside, however, the sentiment was altogether different, as members of the Ukrainian American Senior Citizens Association gathered to hear a presentation by the Daughters of Knyahynya, a women’s group delivering military equipment to Ukrainian special forces on the frontline.

Irene Skulsky, a retired teacher in her late 70s, voiced indignation at Trump’s sudden turn against her ancestral homeland, which she believed had come close to killing her son, Darko. “When we vote, we have to vote with Ukraine in our hearts,” she told her fellow retirees. “I know many people here are Republicans, but we can’t go according to party lines now. Ukraine needs us. They need our support. We need to shift the balance of power in Congress and in the Senate.” Her frustration also reached Trump’s fellow Republicans, whom she said were “selling our souls” rather than standing up to the president. “Republicans are afraid of Trump,” she said. “They’re afraid to lose their position. I think their heart is with Ukraine, but they’s sitting on their hands.”

Days earlier, Skulsky told fellow members at a meeting of the association that Darko had been sitting in traffic en route to work in Kyiv when Russian drone missiles slammed into five cars immediately ahead of him. She said several people in the vehicles in front were killed in the strikes; Darko escaped injury. There had been no prior alert, she said — allegedly as a result of a decision by Washington to suspend its intelligence-sharing arrangement with Ukraine, which has acted as an early warning system against missile attacks since Russia invaded in February 2022.

The Trump administration denied that it had stopped sharing intelligence this month, as relations with Ukraine nose-dived. Ukraine’s president, Volodymyr Zelenskyy, has said his country received no advance warning from US intelligence networks of two Russian missile attack waves. In recent weeks, Trump — who pledged during his victorious 2024 presidential election campaign to end the Russia-Ukraine conflict within 24 hours of returning to office — appears to have abandoned Ukraine, The Guardian reported.

He struck a deal with the Russian president, Vladimir Putin, to buy nearly 5m tonnes of diesel, overriding US sanctions on Moscow. He called on Ukrainians to replace the country’s president. His administration was accused of throttling the Ukrainian military’s access to the Starlink satellite internet service. Economists argue that the volume of diesel covered by the deal is too insignificant to reduce diesel prices, which have been inflated mainly by the effects of the Iran war rather than by the actions of Ukraine.

Trump’s envoy, Steve Witkoff, caused further consternation by praising Putin as a “humanitarian” after the Russian president agreed to release a 74-year-old US citizen, Stephen Hubbard, whom Russia had imprisoned for several years after falsely accusing him of fighting for Ukraine.

The Russia-friendly moves have stunned Ukrainian Americans, many of whom hold pro-Republican sympathies dating back to the cold war, when staunchly anti-communist presidents like Ronald Reagan were seen as champions of self-determination for the “captive nations” that comprised the Soviet Union, which included Ukraine. “We are befuddled and upset by what’s going on,” said Andrij Chornodolsky, chair of the senior citizens association, who urged his fellow members to call the White House and leave comments criticizing the recent policy moves. “How can you be doing this to us?”

The US is “helping a country that wants to subjugate Ukraine back into a new empire, the Russian empire,” Chornodolsky added. He was equally angry with Republicans who have failed to criticize Trump over his abandonment of Ukraine. “What are the comments coming out of Congress?” he said. “Silence speaks very loudly. If you do nothing, you agree with the person making the pronouncements.”

About 60,000 people of Ukrainian descent live in the Philadelphia metropolitan area — the second-largest such ethnic population in the US after New York — and an estimated 105,000 to 122,000 people claim Ukrainian ancestry throughout Pennsylvania. Peter Hewuka, 88, a retired nuclear physicist and lifelong Republican who previously voted for Trump, said he and his wife, Orysia, were likely to vote for a local Democrat because of Ukraine, even though it represented a psychological hurdle. “I am very hesitant to vote Democrat,” he said. “But I can’t vote for people who support what Trump is doing. The Republican party has left us, we haven’t left the Republican party. It’s very difficult for me and for a lot of Ukrainian Americans to vote for the Republican party based on what they’ve been doing in the past months.”

Eugene Luciw, a retired lawyer and local representative of the Ukrainian Congress Committee of America, said even Trump’s diehard supporters in the Ukrainian community had been alienated by the diesel deal with Putin. “From my interactions on social media and elsewhere, I would say more than 75% of those who supported him have now abandoned him because it’s now clear where this is headed,” said Luciw, 68, who described himself as a Reagan Republican but now intends to vote Democrat. Until the last few weeks, he said, there was hope that Trump’s swinging back-and-forth on Ukraine was a kind of negotiation. “But the diesel deal reversed all that,” he said.

Many American Ukrainians will express their outrage at the polls on 3 November, according to Luciw. “For [American] Ukrainians, I believe foreign policy is unquestionably the No 1 issue,” he said. “But I think even the average American without the added affinity of a homeland in Europe is going to see foreign policy as a very important issue. It’s beginning to reach people’s minds and hearts that something very important is brewing in Europe. If Ukraine loses this war, we have the beginnings of world war three.”

Mykola Hryckowian, director of the Center for US-Ukrainian Relations, said he voted for Trump three times and was active in an ad hoc group called Ukrainian Americans for Trump, but has since severed his last vestiges of support. “I went to the polling booth in the last election not knowing who to vote for, thinking I might write in Zelenskyy, but in the end voted for Trump, because I couldn’t vote for Kamala Harris,” he recalled. “Today, I could not vote for Trump.” He said many of his colleagues had held onto Trump until the very end, but in these last two weeks they had turned around and said: “This is an abomination.”

Not all Ukrainian Americans were surprised by Trump’s actions. Mark Andryczyk, a Ukrainian studies scholar at Columbia University and board member of the Ukrainian League of Philadelphia, said the president’s actions were predictable. “I never thought Trump would be a good idea for Ukraine,” he said. “You just have to look at how happy Russia was each time he was elected.” “All his information on the war is basically Russian talking points,” he added. “I can’t imagine why anybody would think he was pulling for Ukraine … I would think that their eyes are open now.”

Trump has blown hot and cold toward Zelenskyy. At an explosive White House meeting in February 2025, he told the Ukrainian leader “you don’t have the cards,” but with a peace deal on terms acceptable to Russia seeming ever more distant, relations appeared to thaw, with Trump even publicly mooting giving Ukraine a license to build its own Patriot missiles. In recent weeks, however, Trump appears to have definitively ditched the US’s putative ally and sided with Putin.

Trump’s latest Ukraine turn carries special resonance, not least because it risks a dramatic rupture with Washington’s European Nato allies, who are heavily invested in Kyiv’s protection as a first line of defence, The Guardian reported, and has also highlighted Trump’s complex relationship with Putin, for whom he has repeatedly voiced admiration.

The Guardian reported that the most recent Ukraine development is the latest in a trio of Trump foreign-policy decisions with the potential to hurt Republicans when Americans head to the polls next week. More relevant to voters’ living costs — a key issue in this election, according to polls — has been Trump’s protracted war with Iran, and his determination to impose tariffs on imports from much of the world.

The Iran war, launched with Israel in February, shows no signs of ending despite Trump’s early claims that it would be a short-term “excursion” certain to end in the “unconditional surrender” of the Iranian government. Trump has reportedly drawn up plans to launch renewed strikes against Iran after the midterms as he seeks to break Iran’s grip on the strait of Hormuz, an energy choke point that was the conduit for one-fifth of the world’s energy supplies before the Iranian government closed it in response to being attacked. The strait’s closure has resulted in soaring global oil prices, which have been passed on to US consumers in the form of higher prices for gasoline and diesel, vital for supply chains and US farmers.

Polls have consistently shown voters’ disapproval of Trump’s handling of the war and of his management of the economy and inflation; roughly 70% are now critical of both, according to a recent AP-Norc survey. The war’s economic impact has compounded the inflationary effects of Trump’s tariffs, which have affected the prices of consumer goods and increased costs for manufacturers by increasing the costs of imported raw materials such as steel, copper and aluminum.

The tariff regime has also strained Washington’s relations with once-close allies, most visibly Canada, the US’s second-biggest trading partner. Ties plunged to a new low in August following the collapse of bilateral trade talks, after which the administration imposed tariffs on $20bn of Canadian imports — including cars, forestry and dairy produce — triggering a retaliatory tit-for-tat response from the government of Prime Minister Mark Carney. In Michigan, which shares a border with Canada, even Republican Senate candidate Mike Rogers released an ad calling on Trump to end the trade war and declared: “Canada is not our enemy.”

Trump has articulated his vision with characteristic bullishness, The Guardian reported. After January’s US attack on Venezuela, during which it ousted the country’s president, Nicolás Maduro, he dubbed his strategy the “Donroe doctrine” — a play on the Monroe doctrine, named after the 19th US president, which holds that the western hemisphere should be Washington’s exclusive domain. This week, in Corpus Christi, Texas, he bragged about extracting Venezuela’s oil resources and fantasized about making the country “the 51st state” in a speech in which he showed off a tanker he claimed was carrying 1m barrels of oil and repeated a phrase — “to the victors belong the spoils” — that startled foreign leaders when he first uttered it at last month’s gathering of the United Nations general assembly.

The Guardian reported there are fears that, after the midterms, he could apply the same instincts to Cuba, where he has repeatedly pledged to topple Cuba’s communist government, in power for the past 67 years. Trump will go to China again next month to meet the Chinese president, Xi Jinping, having already met Xi at a summit in Beijing this year before hosting him at the White House.

He has dispensed with seasoned diplomats, relying instead on Witkoff, a real estate investor and attorney with no background in foreign policy, and Jared Kushner, his businessman son-in-law, as negotiators over matters as intricate as Iran, Gaza and Ukraine.

A clear-eyed view of Trump’s foreign policy could reap an electoral whirlwind for Trump’s Republican allies on 3 November, with the pocket-book effects of his tariffs and the war on Iran looming large as voters head to the polls, The Guardian reported. Yet such an outcome could be a double-edged sword, The Guardian reported: should Trump become hemmed in at home by a Democrat-controlled Congress in the final two years of his presidency, “where better to assert his power than the rest of the world?”
