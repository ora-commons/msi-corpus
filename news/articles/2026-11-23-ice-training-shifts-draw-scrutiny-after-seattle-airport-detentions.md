## Airport enforcement expanded from deportation orders to visa overstays

An upcoming investigation by the Homeland Security Department’s inspector general is drawing new questions about ICE training and enforcement after officers arrested a father and daughter at Seattle-Tacoma International Airport. Hamed Aleaziz, who covers immigration and the Department of Homeland Security for The New York Times, said the report details sweeping changes made under former Homeland Security Secretary Kristi Noem, including allowing thousands of new recruits to complete basic training online and sharply reducing the number of instruction hours they receive.

Officers forcibly removed Sofia Hernandez Mejia and her father, Ricardo Larrondo Mendoza, from a jet bridge at Seattle-Tacoma International Airport as the family boarded a flight. The pair said they were traveling to California for a family baptism. Videos recorded by several passengers captured the confrontation, and the arrests renewed protests over ICE enforcement tactics.

The family’s attorney said Hernandez Mejia and Larrondo Mendoza had been seeking asylum since 2015. ICE said both had overstayed their visas. ICE also alleged that Hernandez Mejia threw hot coffee at officers; that allegation was not captured on video, and her lawyers said the coffee spilled. A woman speaking in a foreign language said, “We have a work permit.”

Aleaziz said the Trump administration’s hiring surge of thousands of new officers sharply reduced training for recruits without previous law-enforcement experience, cutting it by nearly 50 percent. For recruits with prior experience, an in-person training program that lasted one month shifted to virtual instruction. Some officers zoomed through the virtual training within a day.

PBS News host Geoff Bennett said ICE agents have enormous authority, including authority to detain people and use force. Aleaziz said many instructors who taught the recruits were concerned about the shortened training. One instructor called the new regimen a catastrophic failure, and another said it could become a potential liability for the agency.

Aleaziz also said a former ICE employee had come forward earlier in the year as a whistleblower and said recruits were receiving substantially less training than necessary. He described the former employee as probably the most significant ICE official to speak publicly about the issue. Investigators found no clear internal explanation for the changes, according to Aleaziz. The head of training insinuated during an interview that ICE’s deputy director told him to make the changes and that they were supported by Kristi Noem adviser Corey Lewandowski.

Current Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin acknowledged that some of the decisions need to be reversed, Aleaziz said. The inspector general wants ICE to retrain officers through extensive training so they can perform their jobs competently. Aleaziz said ICE is under pressure for large arrest and deportation totals and that moving officers into training lasting weeks or months could reduce those numbers.

Aleaziz described ICE’s airport enforcement campaign as unprecedented. He said it began with a plan developed alongside TSA to share information about people scheduled to fly at American airports. The effort initially focused on people with deportation orders before expanding to people with visa overstays. Aleaziz said the expansion has produced airport-arrest videos for the past year.

Aleaziz said Mullin has emphasized wanting to lower the temperature and doing enforcement work under the radar, even as incidents like the Seattle arrests continue.

The potential consequences of the inspector general’s findings remained unresolved. Aleaziz said an incoming Congress, with Democrats taking control of the Senate and House, had identified ICE and DHS as a main target of investigation. He anticipated congressional staff would examine the agency’s hiring and training changes during Noem’s tenure.
