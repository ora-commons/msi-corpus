## Arc projects 50% to 60% lower operating costs for electric tugs

Tugboats that maneuver containerships through America’s busiest ports are set to become the next pieces of the freight industry to go electric. Curtin Maritime and Arc are putting an 80-foot battery-powered tug through sea trials with the U.S. Coast Guard ahead of planned delivery later this month. The vessel is set to become the first fully electric tug operating at the Port of Los Angeles. The eight-tug project is part of a push to replace conventional diesel engines with electric propulsion. The eight tugs under development would represent around a third of the fleet operating at the ports of Los Angeles and Long Beach.

Arc co-founder and chief technology officer Ryan Cook said the first tug carries six megawatt-hours of batteries, roughly the capacity of 60 Rivian trucks or 75 Tesla Model Ys. Curtin Maritime initiated the project and brought in Arc to supply and integrate the electric power system. Arc started out building electric recreational boats. Martin Curtin said Arc was selected over more established marine-engineering companies because its technology was “extremely simple and elegant.” The vessels are designed and built by Snow & Co., a Seattle shipyard part-owned by Curtin Maritime.

Martin Curtin said Curtin Maritime began developing electric tugs after he grew frustrated with the cost and complexity of diesel engines designed to meet California’s stringent emissions requirements. “Tier 4 engines are extremely problematic and fickle,” Curtin said, referring to the latest generation of emissions-compliant diesel engines. “We started looking at alternative methods.”

Cook said electric tugs have the potential to operate with costs 50% to 60% below conventional vessels, largely because they consume less fuel and require less maintenance. The first electric tugs are slightly more expensive to build than diesel tugs, but Curtin expects the companies to reach cost parity or better by the sixth or seventh boat.

The cost advantage could matter in a competitive business where operators bid for a limited number of ship movements. “There’s only so many ships that are coming into port, and each operator is basically fighting for contracts,” Cook said. “Usually the lowest price wins.”

Arc is financing construction of the first tugs and said the vessels are commercially viable without subsidies. The company has been awarded $33 million in grants for future vessels and charging infrastructure at the Port of Los Angeles through the South Coast Air Quality Management District authority, backed by the California State Transportation Agency.

Arc is also converting the 50-foot Harbor Sentinel tug, owned and operated by Pacific Maritime Group in the Port of Long Beach. Curtin said electric motors can outperform conventional engines by providing maximum torque almost instantly rather than requiring diesel engines to build power. The electric vessels use familiar maneuverable thrusters and helm controls.

Reliability remains critical because a tug breakdown can impose two costs on its owner: the operator loses revenue and may have to pay another company to perform the work. The Coast Guard trials will test the first tug before its planned delivery, while the broader fleet remains dependent on performance in regular port operations.

Seroka said around 25 tugboats operate at the ports of Los Angeles and Long Beach. At least two, but typically four, tugs are assigned to arriving vessels — about 3,000 ships a year, Seroka said. The port experiences roughly one ship-engine failure a month, he said, and safety is paramount. Tugboats can stop a vessel in less than 100 meters, according to Seroka.

Asked whether electric tugs would be up to the job, Seroka said, “We don’t know yet.” The boats “have to be tested in real-world heavy duty circumstances.”

The electric tugs fit into a broader effort to electrify port operations. Seroka said Los Angeles is working with its municipal utility on a $1.2 billion project to expand available electrical capacity from about 90 megawatts to more than 300 megawatts. The expanded capacity would supply marine terminals, trucking companies and other port users.

Arc is not the first company to put an electric tug into U.S. waters. Crowley operates its battery-electric eWolf tug in San Diego. Curtin Maritime and Arc are betting that their electric propulsion vessels can become a mainstream alternative to diesel, and other operators are watching the project. Cook said representatives from a half dozen U.S. companies have visited the shipyard. “If we’re successful here,” he said, “they’re probably going to quickly have to follow.”
