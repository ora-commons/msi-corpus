## Proposal expands dental, vision and hearing coverage before universal eligibility

Bernie Sanders has provided The Associated Press with a four-year legislative plan for Medicare for All as the Vermont independent prepares to become chairman of the Senate Health, Education, Labor and Pensions Committee in January. Sanders, who caucuses with Democrats, is the committee’s ranking member and is expected to take the chair when the new Congress begins. The agenda details how he and progressive candidates who championed universal health care on the campaign trail would translate their proposals into legislation after the election gave Democrats control of Congress.

The first-year phase would lower Medicare eligibility from 65 to 55, expand benefits to include dental, vision and hearing care, and guarantee health care for children through age 18. Eligibility would fall to 45 in the second year and 35 in the third year, before the proposal would extend coverage to everyone in the fourth year.

Under Sanders’ longstanding single-payer proposal, universal coverage would replace most private insurance with a federally administered national system. Sanders defended the gradual transition, saying, “Transforming a system which is 18% of the GDP of America, you don’t do that overnight.”

Funding would shift away from private insurance premiums and toward payroll contributions, including a combination of employer and household premiums, higher taxes on the rich and new taxes on Wall Street and large firms. Sanders said in an interview with The Associated Press, “This is no longer a utopian vision. This is where the American people are.”

The costs of lowering Medicare eligibility were expected to produce substantial debate between the two major political parties as the national deficit stands at $2 trillion, or 6.2% of gross domestic product. The Congressional Budget Office estimated in 2022 that reducing the eligibility age to 60 would increase federal budget deficits by $155 billion over the 2026–2031 period.

Sanders said Democrats could pass the first stage through budget reconciliation, a process that allows certain tax and spending legislation to advance through the 100-member Senate with a simple majority rather than the 60 votes typically required to overcome a filibuster. Democrats are expected to hold a 56-44 Senate majority when the new Congress begins in January, their largest advantage in the chamber in 16 years.

Along with greater access to primary care and lower prescription drug costs, the proposal targets insurance coverage and eligibility. House Democratic leader Hakeem Jeffries of New York has pledged to get to work “right out of the gate” to restore Affordable Care Act subsidies and reverse Medicaid cuts enacted under Republicans. Jeffries said Tuesday that Democrats know that simply returning to the status quo “is not acceptable” and described preparations to lower the Medicare eligibility age, add vision, hearing and dental coverage and ensure all children are covered.

Sanders characterized Jeffries’ position as support for the opening phase of his own plan. “Jeffries basically supported the first year of Medicare for All,” Sanders said.

Legislation Congress passes during the next two years would still require President Donald Trump’s signature. Sanders rejected the idea that Trump, a Republican, would refuse to sign a Medicare for All measure, saying, “Donald Trump may be crazy, but he is not stupid. He’s a smart guy, and he’s a very good politician.”

Sanders also plans to use the committee to advance an economic agenda beyond health care. That includes raising the federal minimum wage from $7.25 an hour to $20 over a four-year transition, a proposal that would nearly triple the federal minimum wage, which has remained unchanged since 2009. The increase could face resistance from lawmakers concerned about effects on small businesses and employment. Sanders also plans to promote the PRO Act, which would strengthen workers’ rights to unionize.

His agenda calls for sweeping restrictions on artificial intelligence, including a moratorium on new AI data centers, a pause in advanced AI development and a ban on artificial superintelligence.

Sanders previously chaired the HELP Committee from 2023 to 2025 and worked with Republicans on legislation to lower prescription drug costs and expand access to primary care. He also chaired the Senate Budget Committee from 2021 to 2023, helping advance major Democratic spending priorities.

Just weeks after the midterms, Sanders argued that the agenda reflected a shift not in his own positions but in voters’ demands for sweeping changes to the health care system. Sanders saw signs of that shift among Democratic leaders.

The proposal offers an early view of the priorities Sanders wants Democrats to pursue after the midterm election exposed competing visions for the party’s future. With the 2028 presidential election approaching, Sanders is seeking to move Democrats toward a populist economic agenda that he argues has gained broader support. He said, “I think the election, in my opinion, was a crying out for fundamental change in the way the government does business and the absolute need to protect a struggling working class,” and said, “I think this is a time to bring about very significant changes in policies so that we can actually improve the lives of working families.”
