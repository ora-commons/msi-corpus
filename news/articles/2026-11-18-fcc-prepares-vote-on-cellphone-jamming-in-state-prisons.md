## FCC prepares vote on cellphone jamming in state prisons

## South Carolina plans a phased rollout at Broad River Road complex

The Federal Communications Commission is expected to vote Thursday in Washington, D.C., on a regulatory change that would allow state prisons and local jails to join federal prisons in blocking cellphone signals. FCC Chair Brendan Carr said Wednesday in Columbia that there was “no doubt” the three-member commission would approve the measure. If approved, the change would move to the Federal Register in the coming weeks before states could apply to use the technology.

## Federal decision opens the application process

Carr said the commission unanimously advanced the regulation in a preemptive vote last year. Thursday’s vote would finalize it and allow it to move to the Federal Register in the coming weeks, he said. Once the rule is officially added to the nation’s list of regulations — a process Carr said takes between several days and several weeks — states can begin applying to use the technology. Carr said the measure was meant to prevent inmates from continuing to commit crimes while in prison.

## South Carolina has a five-prison starting point

Confiscated cellphones and drones used to deliver contraband sat on a table in the attorney general’s office in Columbia on Wednesday, Nov. 18, 2026. After more than a decade of advocacy from South Carolina leaders, Carr said, “Your officials here in South Carolina pushed this forward when no one else would.” He added, “Not at the state level, not the federal level.”

Current prison director Joel Anderson, former directors Jon Ozmint and Bryan Stirling, two longtime advocates for cellphone jamming, and retired Capt. Robert Johnson joined Carr at Wednesday’s news conference. Stirling is the state’s U.S. attorney. Carr credited South Carolina’s prison directors with building the momentum necessary to get the regulation passed.

Once authorization is available, South Carolina will submit its application right away, Anderson said. The Department of Corrections will install the technology one prison at a time, allowing time to identify and address any problems, he said. The agency will start with the Broad River Road complex in Columbia, which includes five prisons. South Carolina is ready to start jamming cellphones as soon as officials receive approval.

Over the past two years, South Carolina legislators spent $18.5 million on technology that tracks cellphones in prisons, allowing officials to report illegal phones and get them turned off. Stirling said previously that the same technology can also block cellphone signals. Once a jammer is switched on, contraband cellphones become useless. South Carolina officials, who were hopeful the FCC would allow cellphone blocking in prisons, summed up their position: “Jamming is the answer.”

## Jammers can be precise, but emergency access is contested

Opponents of allowing cellphone jamming, primarily cellphone companies, have long argued that the technology could cause more problems than solutions. Lobbyists have raised concerns that jammers might affect communication outside prisons, prevent corrections officers from calling emergency services or further isolate inmates from their friends and family. Attorney General Alan Wilson, who in January will be sworn in as governor, said those concerns were not true.

Tests in South Carolina prisons and other states have shown that the technology can be extremely precise, Anderson said. Jammers can block signals in a single room or building without interfering with technology outside the radius. Prison officials said jammers always allow calls to 911, so anyone can contact the outside in an emergency. Wilson said inmates can call their families on the monitored phones offered inside prisons.

Having a cellphone in a South Carolina prison is illegal, but officers need to find the devices so inmates can face charges. Wilson said contraband phones are expensive and can lead to serious trouble when inmates are caught with them. He asked, “Why do people go to this much trouble and spend this much money?” and said, “It’s not because they want to talk to their mommas.”

## Johnson’s shooting and 15 years of advocacy

Retired Capt. Robert Johnson was shot six times at close range in the chest and abdomen in his home in Sumter in March 2010, and the gunman left him for dead. Investigators later discovered that inmates used illicit cellphones to order the hit because they were angry that Johnson had stopped contraband, including more phones, from entering Lee Correctional Institution in rural Bishopville, where he worked. One man was promised $6,000 to kill Johnson. Johnson joked Wednesday, “Myself, I thought I was worth 10.”

In the 16 years since the shooting, Johnson underwent 32 surgeries, he said. He experiences constant pain because of his injuries. His wife, who watched him get shot, spent years afraid people were coming to attack him again. Johnson said, “No one should have to go through what I went through.”

Technology has improved and become more accessible since Johnson was attacked, allowing prisoners to hatch more sophisticated plans using cellphones, he said. Johnson said, “I call these cellphones weapons of mass destruction, because an inmate can do things with one cellphone that he can’t do from the prison with the government phones.” He added, “It’s unreal what these guys were able to do when I was there, and what they’re doing now — it just blows my mind.”

Johnson said, “Pretty soon, the only thing those cellphones can be used for is for (inmates) to hit each other,” adding, “They won’t be able to use them at all.” For the past 15 years, he has traveled the country advocating for cellphone jamming in state prisons and has testified before Congress asking for a way to keep cellphones out of inmates’ hands.

After the shooting, Johnson said he felt his calling was to make sure the same sort of situation never happened to anyone else. He said he felt the Lord knew he and his wife would be able to handle the struggles and in turn do something good for the world. Johnson said of the inmates involved, “They didn’t know that they started something.” He added, “I told my wife, ‘I’m going to finish it. I’m not going to let this rest.’”

Carr said Johnson’s story was instrumental in getting the regulation passed. “I think, more than anybody else, he has put this issue on his back,” Carr said, adding, “He is the reason why we’re here today getting this across the finish line.”

## Contraband phones are tied to crimes and prison safety

Wilson said prisoners use cellphones to commit any number of crimes. Contraband phones have been tied to cases involving drug trafficking, dogfighting, fraud, extortion and killings outside prison walls. He also said the largest organized crime ring in state history ran using contraband cellphones, with inmates ordering murders, kidnappings and sales of guns and drugs in the country and internationally.

Tired of hearing cases stemming from contraband cellphones inside prisons, South Carolina’s two grand juries called last year for a change in federal regulation in a rare pair of reports. Stirling said, “Along with threatening communities, cellphones make prisons less safe.” Decades ago, prison officials could move an inmate to another prison if violence broke out. Now, inmates can use a network of cellphones to order hits on one another from across the state, Stirling said.

Stirling said, “They go to prison, and they’re supposed to be behind bars, they’re supposed to rehabilitate, they’re supposed to be safe for society,” adding, “That’s no longer the case with these cellphones.”

## Carr says opposition has waned

As jamming technology improved and the problems involving inmate phones worsened, opposition waned, Carr said. Cellphone companies asked states to try other methods, including South Carolina’s practice of shutting down phones one by one. State officials compared that process to a game of Whac-A-Mole because new signals appeared even as existing phones were disabled. No other method proved as effective as jamming, Carr said.

Carr said, “I think a lot of people realize that, yeah, this isn’t a horrible solution now, given the steps that have been taken.”
