## Bondi beauty brand referred to NSW health regulators over cigarette platters

**Subtype:** fact

- New South Wales health officials are examining Copini Beauty after cigarettes appeared on silver platters at the brand's Bondi lip gloss launch.
- The cigarettes appeared to carry Manchester "sapphire blue" branding and no health warnings, markers Australian authorities treat as illicit tobacco.
- The federal health department says it is "deeply concerned about advertising or promotion that glamorises smoking," and cites maximum penalties of $728,000 for an individual and $7.2m for a company.
- Copini, co-founded by influencer Casey James and product developer Carla McGraw, markets "clean ingredients" and did not respond to requests for comment.

## Federal health department says it is 'deeply concerned' about glamorising smoking

The launch took place earlier in November at a venue in Bondi, Sydney. Photos circulated by the brand showed what appeared to be loose cigarettes, without health warnings, arranged on a tiered silver platter alongside a bowl of lip glosses.

The images were posted by the Copini Beauty Instagram account in a carousel that showed the cigarettes beside Copini-branded match boxes, lip glosses and glasses of wine. "Celebrating the launch of our soft gloss with our community," reads the caption on one post from the event. The same images are featured in a highlight reel from the launch on the brand's official Instagram page.

Copini is co-founded by influencer Casey James and product developer Carla McGraw.

A spokesperson for the New South Wales health minister, Ryan Park, confirmed the matter had been escalated. "This has been referred to NSW health's centre for regulation and enforcement," the spokesperson said. The centre works with state and federal authorities to regulate smoking products and holds powers to inspect premises, seize illicit stock, issue fines and prosecute offenders.

Under the Public Health (Tobacco and Other Products) Act 2023, advertising or promoting smoking or the use of tobacco products is prohibited. State and federal laws separately prohibit businesses from offering, giving or distributing a free tobacco product, and any tobacco supplied must meet strict product standards, including mandatory graphic health warnings.

The cigarettes at the Copini launch do not appear to carry any health warnings on the filter as required, indicating they are illicit products. Buying or supplying illicit tobacco is illegal in Australia regardless of the quantity.

Cigarettes in Australia also cannot legally carry brand logos. The cigarettes at the launch appeared to carry the Manchester "sapphire blue" branding, one of the most common illicit tobacco products in the country, and are considered unlabelled under Australian law because they do not carry official government health warnings and compliant packaging.

The federal health department said it was treating the episode as a regulatory concern. A spokesperson said the government is "deeply concerned about advertising or promotion that glamorises smoking or the use of tobacco products, or helps normalise tobacco use, particularly where it might reach young people".

"There is no appropriate place for tobacco products to be used as a promotional prop or to make smoking appear fashionable or appealing," the spokesperson said. "The government strengthened Australia's tobacco laws to reduce the visibility and normalisation of tobacco products and protect Australians from the harms of smoking."

The spokesperson said allegations of non-compliance are taken seriously, noting that the maximum penalty for criminal advertising or sponsorship of tobacco under federal laws is $728,000 for an individual, or $7.2m for a company.

The display sits awkwardly against Copini's stated product philosophy. "We're 100% vegan, cruelty-free, and free from skin irritants and endocrine disruptors," the company's website says, stating that "clean ingredients" are used in all its products. The company did not respond to requests for comment.

Smoking accelerates ageing and causes more than 16,000 cases of cancer in Australia each year.

Becky Freeman, a professor of tobacco control at the University of Sydney, said the imagery reflected a wider drift in how smoking is presented. She said it was "shocking to see the resurgence" of smoking in pop culture and on red carpets.

"But to see this really local example, in Bondi, shows this is not an international phenomena – it's happening in a country like Australia where we have low smoking rates through strong regulations and health policies," Freeman said.

The episode comes amid growing concerns about the use of cigarettes by brands and in images of celebrities, especially online, with health experts warning the practice risks normalising and glamorising smoking.
