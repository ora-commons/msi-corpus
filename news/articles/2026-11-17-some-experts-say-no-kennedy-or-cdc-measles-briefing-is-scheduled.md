## Pennsylvania outbreak drives more than 5,400 cases; two-year toll reaches 13 deaths

On November 17, 2026, some experts noted that neither Robert F. Kennedy Jr. nor the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention had scheduled a news conference to address U.S. measles response plans as the United States recorded more than 5,400 confirmed cases. The total was already double the 2025 tally and was driven largely by an outbreak in Pennsylvania that dwarfed earlier outbreaks. When AP sought Kennedy's reaction to the United States losing its measles-elimination status, an HHS spokesperson pointed to CDC Director Dr. Erica Schwartz's statement. During the Pan American Health Organization's news conference, HHS issued a news release promoting the latest episode of Kennedy's online cooking show.

The last two years have been the worst in a generation in the United States, with more than 7,000 cases and 13 reported deaths. Nearly all of those people were either unvaccinated or did not report whether they were vaccinated, and more than 10% of those infected had to be hospitalized. Ten measles-associated deaths have been reported this year, making it the deadliest year in more than three decades. Health officials have not released detailed information on each death, but at least five of the dead were children.

The federal government has taken some steps. In August and September, the CDC awarded $100 million to support state and local measles-response work. Kennedy and CDC Director Dr. Erica Schwartz have said vaccination is the best way to prevent measles from spreading.

“The measles elimination designation is not what protects Americans. Vaccination does,” Schwartz said in a statement Tuesday. “We know where transmission is occurring and we’ve invested $100 million in state and local health departments to reach those communities.”

State and local health officials said the cost was far higher and that the moment demanded a louder federal voice and a bigger push for vaccination than Kennedy and Schwartz had provided.

“When the people at the front of the room are not speaking loudly and frequently in support of vaccination, the program starts to falter. And that’s what we’re seeing in the U.S.,” said Noel Brewer, a University of North Carolina public health professor who chairs a committee that presented an analysis of U.S. measles data to PAHO last week. “How many kids have to die before they start getting concerned?”

Vaccination rates have been falling against a number of diseases in the United States and globally. As one of the most infectious diseases known to medicine, measles was seen as likely to be one of the first vaccine-preventable diseases to reemerge when vaccinations falter, and some experts said other diseases were likely to follow.

“It can be the canary in the coal mine to other things,” said Dr. Susan Kansagra, chief medical officer of the Association of State and Territorial Health Officials.

Measles vaccines have safely stopped the virus’ spread in the United States for 30 years. It takes a community vaccination rate of 95% to prevent outbreaks, experts said. The Associated Press reported that as fewer parents vaccinated their children and federal leaders sowed doubt and confusion about the importance of getting vaccinated, the virus found footholds in communities across the country. All but three states have reported a confirmed measles case this year.

Since the United States eliminated measles in 2000, public health officials usually logged fewer than 200 cases a year, most of them tied to international travel. Since 2025, measles has sickened thousands, starting with a major outbreak in the Southwest. In Texas, more than 760 people got sick and two children died. In New Mexico, about 100 people got sick and one adult died. Measles then spread through rural border towns in Arizona and Utah, followed by a major outbreak in South Carolina in the winter and spring that sickened nearly 1,000 people.

This year has been much worse — the worst for measles cases since 1991. The more-than-5,400 confirmed cases are already double the 2025 tally, driven largely by an outbreak in Pennsylvania that dwarfed the earlier ones. Public health experts said the outbreak tallies are almost certainly an undercount.

Measles typically begins with a high fever followed by a rash that starts on the face and neck. Most people recover, but the World Health Organization lists measles as one of the leading causes of death among young children. Serious complications, including blindness and swelling of the brain, are more common in young children and adults over age 30.

On November 17, 2026, the Pan American Health Organization said the United States, Mexico and Bolivia had lost their measles-elimination status as the virus continued spreading. The change reverses decades of progress against a virus that international health officials said had once been largely relegated to medical textbooks. In the United States, measles has spread at the highest rate in more than 30 years, largely among unvaccinated people. Mexico's resurgence included a massive outbreak in Chihuahua state, while Bolivia also lost the designation.

The declaration has few practical implications and is largely symbolic, but it marks a sharp turn in a disease largely controlled through decades of scientific advances. The change also may be seen as the beginning of an era in which diseases of the past become deadly problems for the modern world. The Associated Press reported that Tuesday's announcement signals an erosion of public health preparedness.

International health officials consider measles eliminated when the virus is not locally spreading among residents. The designation hinges on whether a single measles chain has spread uninterrupted for at least 12 months.

“Measles elimination is not a trophy that is won once and placed on a shelf,” PAHO director Dr. Jarbas Barbosa said at a news conference. “It is an achievement that must be safeguarded every day by closing immunity gaps and ensuring we reach every community.”

Dr. Daniel Salas, PAHO's executive manager for immunizations, told reporters the organization expects the United States, Bolivia and Mexico to submit plans “in the coming weeks” for how each country will try to stop transmission for at least a year and regain measles-elimination status.

Measles has been spreading throughout the Americas since 2024. The outbreaks in North America started in close-knit Mennonite communities and have since spread to areas with low vaccination rates. Last year, Canada lost its measles-free designation for the first time since 1998.

Robert F. Kennedy Jr. was a leading voice in the anti-vaccine movement before President Donald Trump appointed him to oversee federal health agencies. Kennedy and other federal leaders have argued that vaccination rates were waning for years before Trump took office a second time, that measles is not an “American phenomenon,” and that the U.S. government is handling measles better than other countries. In January, a CDC leader told reporters that the loss of the nation’s elimination status would be the “cost of doing business” globally. Other countries are also struggling with measles, and some also have lost their measles-elimination status. But public health experts and others said the outcome in the United States was not inevitable.

“This humiliating determination is the tragic yet predictable result of an ongoing campaign by grifters, quacks and charlatans to put their own greed and self-worth ahead of the health and safety of children,” said Sen. Ron Wyden, a Democrat from Oregon.

After hugely successful vaccination campaigns, the Americas became the first region in the world to be free of measles in 2016. Health officials estimate that the measles vaccine prevented 6.2 million deaths in the Americas between 2000 and 2023. Now, the entirety of North America faces the task of earning back the designation.
