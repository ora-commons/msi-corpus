## Seal Beach eggs hatch after 58-day incubation; 204 eggs raised at Long Beach aquarium

Two months after the discovery of the first sea turtle nests ever recorded on the US west coast, the eggs have hatched and California's first group of 75 native-born olive ridley sea turtles has been released into the Pacific.

The Aquarium of the Pacific announced the first emergence in a social media post on Saturday. "The hatchlings are here! We are excited to share that some of the olive ridley sea turtle eggs from Seal Beach successfully hatched after a 58-day incubation period. Working with our partners, we carefully released them from the beach in the dark early hours of this morning," the aquarium said.

A day later, the Aquarium added: "Even more egg-citing news! A second group of olive ridley sea turtle hatchlings, this time from the Huntington Beach nest, was released into the ocean early Sunday morning." In total, 54 turtles were released from the Seal Beach nest and 21 from the Huntington Beach nest.

On September 16, an olive ridley sea turtle was observed digging a nest and laying eggs on Seal Beach in the first-recorded instance of any sea turtle nesting on the west coast. Three days later, a second turtle was spotted nesting on Huntington Beach. The eggs incubated at the Aquarium of the Pacific in Long Beach.

The olive ridley is typically a tropical species that resides in the Pacific, Indian and Atlantic oceans, but has been known to nest on the southern tip of Baja California.

"It is not known why these turtles chose southern California beaches as nesting sites, but it indicates an important historical change that could have long-term implications for the species and for local sea turtle management and protection," the Aquarium of the Pacific said in a social media post.

The Aquarium said: "The behavior of these turtles was probably influenced, at least in part, by the incoming El Niño, along with, the recent tropical storm, range expansion, or perhaps navigational mistakes. The species is also known for its behavioral flexibility."

As Hurricane Polo began to form in late September, researchers with the aquarium and its federal and local partners relocated the 204 eggs from the two nests to incubate in the safety of the aquarium's animal care facility.

Jeff Flocken, president and CEO of the Aquarium of the Pacific, said: "As animals adapt in unexpected ways to changes in their natural world, humans will need to adapt and make space to ensure endangered and threatened species are given a fighting chance."

"If they successfully hatch, these baby turtles will likely one day return to nest to carry on future generations on these very same beaches, giving us a remarkable opportunity to help a species expand its range and thrive," Flocken said.

Even as the Huntington Beach and Seal Beach turtles have begun hatching and returned to the ocean, researchers are continuing to advise the public to be on the lookout for more nests. On 1 November, a third nest was recorded in Ventura County, although those eggs were swept away by unusually high tides — likely also caused by El Niño.
