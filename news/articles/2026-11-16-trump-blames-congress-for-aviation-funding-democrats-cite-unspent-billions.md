## House Democrats say more than $9 billion in approved FAA money went unspent

President Donald Trump on Monday accused Congress of underfunding the nation's aviation infrastructure, which he blamed for the weekend ground stop that caused thousands of canceled and delayed flights. But Democrats say the Trump administration has been slow to spend already appropriated funds and that decisions made by the administration have harmed the Department of Transportation.

Congress controls federal funding levels, and Trump predicted during informal remarks to reporters before departing on an overseas trip that lawmakers would soon pay to overhaul the air traffic control system after a communications failure froze air travel for hours Friday night. Air traffic control should have been updated 40 years ago, he added.

"I think the one good thing that comes out of it, I'm sure Congress now will give us the money to pay" for it, he said.

Top U.S. House Democratic appropriators said Sunday that the amount of funding has not been an obstacle to Federal Aviation Administration improvements. Their pushback followed a Friday night press conference from Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy and Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin that the Democrats said provided few answers about the cause of the disruption.

During that press conference, Duffy asked for $50 billion in new spending to upgrade air traffic control infrastructure. But the Transportation Department and FAA have failed to spend more than $9 billion of the $12.5 billion the Republican-controlled Congress provided last year, House Appropriations Committee ranking member Rosa DeLauro of Connecticut wrote in a press release Sunday.

"Instead of laying out the facts, Secretary Duffy commanded the public's attention to demand a $50 billion ransom without a real plan to deliver," DeLauro wrote.

Rep. Jim Clyburn, a South Carolina Democrat who is the ranking member of the Appropriations subcommittee that writes the Transportation Department's annual funding bill, said Duffy would have to make a more persuasive case to win any new funding.

"Recent events are not the result of a lack of funding or support from this Congress," Clyburn said in the same release. "They result from leadership decisions that continue to put politics and private interests above the public's interests."

Spokespeople for the Transportation Department and FAA did not respond to requests for comment Monday.

The dispute runs back to the disruption itself. The ground stop, which lasted for about five hours Friday afternoon and evening, meant all planes departing or arriving in the United States could not take off. Nearly 10,000 major commercial airline flights were canceled. The FAA said it issued the groundstop after a failure in its communications system.

New equipment operating on old infrastructure at an El Dorado, Arkansas, radio communications facility malfunctioned and overwhelmed the system. A radar site in Amarillo, Texas, also sent faulty data, the FAA said in a Q&A posted Sunday.

"L3 Harris, the FAA's current network service provider, had been doing work at the El Dorado site, including deployment of a new router," the FAA Q&A said. "When these inconsistencies in the data happened, L3 Harris blocked those two locations and the network stabilized. The broadcasting function was also disabled on all the new routers as a precaution."

Trump spread the blame further in his Monday remarks. Nearly two years after Trump's return to the White House, he also blamed his predecessor, President Joe Biden, Monday for the shutdown that was caused by a communications failure. But Trump also said the issue was decades in the making, with many air traffic control assets 50 years old.

The overhaul Trump and Duffy are seeking follows a troubled predecessor program. The rollout of a massive, multi-decade, roughly $13-billion initiative to overhaul the air traffic control system routinely received criticism from across the political spectrum for being behind schedule and over-budget. The program, known as NextGen, was shuttered in 2025 and the FAA folded its general "modernization" mission into other offices.
