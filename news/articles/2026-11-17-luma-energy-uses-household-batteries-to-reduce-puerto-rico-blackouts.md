## Luma reports more than 60 prevented or mitigated outages since June 1

Luma Energy, Puerto Rico’s transmission and distribution utility, is drawing on batteries from nearly 115,000 households to shore up an electric grid whose aging power plants often cannot meet demand. Luma said the system showed its potential this summer, preventing or mitigating outages more than 60 times since June 1. Luma said each battery-sharing event, typically about four hours, supports the equivalent of about 80,000 residential customers. Luma said enrolling more households would enable supplemental power for more hours.

On nearly 20 days in October, one of those households supplied power from the Tesla Powerwall attached to 75-year-old Hector Ríos’ house in Cabo Rojo, on Puerto Rico’s southwest corner. Ríos said he welcomed the arrangement because frequent outages disrupt daily life and restrain the economy. He said he likes the idea of helping prevent blackouts for other people and is paid for the energy he shares. “It just seems like a good deal, to contribute to the community and make money at the same time,” Ríos said. When Luma forecasts an energy deficit, it notifies customers digitally that a battery-sharing event is coming. Customers can opt out through an app or adjust how much of their battery they make available; enrollees typically offer 30%.

The program grew from a small pilot Luma launched in late 2023 to offset shortages, with Ríos among the first participants; more than half of solar customers now take part. The Associated Press described the virtual power plant as one of Puerto Rico’s most effective tools for energy resilience amid nearly a decade of electricity woes. It is the first virtual power plant of its kind in Latin America and the Caribbean and the second-largest in the United States by capacity. Dozens of states operate similar networks of battery storage systems, electric vehicles and smart thermostats that help manage the grid, and experts expect their role to grow amid energy constraints, rising utility prices and technology companies’ push to build power-hungry data centers. Rúa Jovet, chief policy officer at the Solar and Energy Storage Association of Puerto Rico, said, “There’s nothing virtual about it.” He added, “It’s real, and it’s better than a power plant.”

Those gains come while Puerto Rico’s underlying power system remains fragile. Nine years after its public power utility filed for bankruptcy and Hurricane Maria ravaged an already dilapidated grid, residents still face unpredictable blackouts and electricity rates higher than in almost any state. Few new utility-scale power sources have been built since Maria, and most federal dollars promised for grid recovery have not yet reached the territory.

The resilience push grew out of the damage Maria inflicted in 2017, when some communities lost electricity for nearly a year. Experts linked the infrastructure failure to thousands of post-hurricane deaths. “We had to procure resiliency in some other way, not a centralized system that will completely fail for a whole year,” Rúa Jovet said. More than 207,000 rooftop solar systems now stand on an archipelago of fewer than 3.2 million people, a 20-fold increase from before Maria. The expansion was propelled by households that could afford installations, philanthropy and some federal dollars. Because of Puerto Rico’s unique need for resiliency, nearly every installation includes at least one battery to keep the system running during a blackout.

Ríos bought a diesel generator after Maria, then installed rooftop panels and a Tesla Powerwall in 2023. “The mindset was, get yourself ready for everything,” he said. The system cut his utility bill in half, from about $400 to $200 in the summer, he said.

Solar panels create power during the day, charge batteries and send excess electricity to the grid. When the sun sets and demand rises, Puerto Rico barely has enough power to meet its needs, and any power-plant malfunction can prompt an outage. Luma calls on the household network when it predicts an energy deficit, but it does not replace large-scale generation or improvements to transmission and distribution infrastructure.

Jigar Shah, director of the Department of Energy’s Loan Programs Office under former President Joe Biden and comanaging director of advisory firm Multiplier, said, “The VPP in Puerto Rico is like an emergency escape valve, but the batteries should be a grid asset.” He said the batteries could dispatch power every day, offset the need to burn fossil fuels and absorb excess energy when solar production peaks. California and Texas use solar and batteries this way.

Customer participation has not been without friction. In a 2026 survey of solar customers by nonprofit Solar United Neighbors, some participants criticized a compensation structure that varies by solar provider. Others expressed hesitation to enroll because they did not understand the program or feared losing backup power. Ríos said he encountered that problem once, when a storm knocked out power right after he shared his battery; he now skips sharing events when bad weather is forecast. He described the payment system as “a little vague.” Ríos and his wife received $162 last year, which they redeemed as a Marshalls gift card.

Luma said its frequent reliance on battery sharing has contributed in part to recent rate increases. The utility pays solar providers three times the residential electricity price for the battery energy it uses and recovers those costs through rates. Rúa Jovet said that expense does not compare with the societywide economic losses of outages, which spoil food, endanger health and shutter schools and businesses. Puerto Rico is also set to spend more than $1.7 billion this year importing fuel for most of its power generation.

As President Donald Trump’s administration promotes fossil fuel expansion and redirects dollars away from solar adoption in Puerto Rico, proponents say the territory’s system highlights the value of distributed solar and storage for bolstering resilience in other energy-vulnerable communities. Nathalie Jordi, community solar lead at nonprofit Together New Orleans, said, “There’s a way for this technology to benefit not just the individual buildings that they’re on,” but instead be “in service to the community all the time.” Together New Orleans championed an upcoming $23 million city program to provide and network more than 1,000 batteries on homes and businesses across New Orleans.

Luma Chief Regulatory Officer Alejandro Figueroa said virtual power plants “could potentially play a larger role in Puerto Rico’s energy future.” He said a program used daily would require a different design and compensation structure to be sustainable. Rúa Jovet called daily dispatch a natural next step and said it could help Puerto Rico meet its legal mandate to reach 100% renewable energy by 2050.

Rúa Jovet said the virtual power plant alone cannot solve Puerto Rico’s energy crisis. That will require deploying billions of unspent federal recovery dollars, continuing improvements to transmission and distribution infrastructure and building new large-scale generation and storage. “You don’t want the situation to be a persistent emergency forever,” he said.
