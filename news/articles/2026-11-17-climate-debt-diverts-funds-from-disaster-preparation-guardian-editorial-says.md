## Nepal’s $20 million allocation falls below $482 million in creditor payments

The Guardian's Nov. 17 editorial calls on COP31 to change international finance, arguing that climate hazards, disaster costs and debt service leave governments with less money for resilience. Its focus extends beyond the size of climate funds to the borrowing, trade and repayment rules that determine whether vulnerable countries can use money to prepare for another shock.

The editorial cited the catastrophe that struck Nepal's Himalayas in August, which began with a collapsing glacier. An avalanche of ice and rock unleashed deadly floods that swept away villages, bridges and roads and left thousands dead or missing. The Guardian said the disaster illustrated the dangers posed by global heating to the Himalayas' increasingly unstable glaciers.

Nepal lost a tenth of its electricity generation, and reconstruction will cost about $5bn, according to the editorial. The Loss and Damage Fund, hailed as a historic breakthrough at the 2022 UN climate summit, has earmarked just $20 million for Nepal. That sum is less than 5% of the $482 million Kathmandu must pay external public creditors this year.

Nepal's delegation to this year's UN climate conference said, “The scale of resources needs to increase.” The delegation was among the voices pressing for changes to the way climate finance reaches countries facing growing disaster risk.

ActionAid's September report, *Debt Fuels the Climate Crisis*, said global-south external borrowing was 100 times more than the climate finance those countries received. The charity said poor nations become trapped in debt dependency because they export cheap raw materials but import expensive food, fuel and manufactured goods, which it said are invariably priced in dollars.

ActionAid also warned that climate-vulnerable countries are now expected to fund decarbonization while servicing dollar loans that often pressure them to export fossil fuels instead of investing in green industry. According to the editorial, the combination leaves governments with less capacity to finance resilience.

As U.S. interest rates rise, the editorial said, more cash goes to creditors in developing nations. The editorial reported that debt in those countries increased by 2% between 2021 and 2024, while debt repayments rose by 28%. Separately, the latest UN figures showed that external interest costs in the global south, excluding China, reached $125bn in 2024.

UN climate chief Simon Stiell described a “downward loop” in which climate risks raise borrowing costs, disasters increase debt levels and mounting repayments leave governments with little to invest in resilience, making them more vulnerable to future shocks. Stiell said climate finance “must help countries break free.”

Fadhel Kaboub, an economist at Denison University in Ohio and an adviser to the UN, asked whether climate action is failing because governments lack political will or because it is being pursued within an international economic system that entrenches inequalities inherited from colonialism. Kaboub said those structures never truly disappeared but evolved into the rules governing international finance, trade, investment and taxation. The result, he said, is that countries least responsible for global warming have the fewest resources to confront its consequences.

At COP31, Kaboub joined a group of experts arguing that the least developed countries should emulate Mark Carney's middle-power strategy and use collective bargaining amid great-power rivalry. The experts said poorer nations should pursue strategic autonomy and economic sovereignty by pooling resources, building regional common markets and developing green industries together. They also proposed negotiating as a bloc with western powers and China for technology transfer and investment without taking on further debt.

The Guardian connected those proposals to the half-century-old push by developing nations for a new international economic order. Its conclusion was that the climate crisis may give that unfinished struggle a new purpose, while the international financial system continues to deepen the vulnerability of countries least able to absorb disaster costs.
