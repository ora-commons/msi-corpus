## Federal government cites more than $2bn in added biosecurity funding since 2022

A large colony of red imported fire ants (Solenopsis invicta) found at Auburn in Sydney this week is the city's second outbreak of the invasive species this year, following a discovery at the Enfield intermodal logistics centre on 23 September. Both sites are industrial areas dealing with imported goods. Environmentalists and biosecurity experts said the large colony was a warning sign that the country's quarantine system was too slow-moving, underfunded and overly concerned with agriculture. Experts said the outbreaks follow years of advice to successive federal governments about how quickly the country's biosecurity system should respond to new risks.

The Enfield site had been subject to pest inspections. Invasive Species Council CEO Jack Gough said there had been “clear failings” because the site's fire ant mitigation process did not match the ant's biology. Lures were placed at some businesses only once a year, Gough said, even though the insects can move after six months, and the lures were set farther apart than the roughly 40 metres the ants roam. He attributed part of that gap to underfunding and an overreliance on mitigation in the private sector.

NSW agriculture minister Tara Moriarty said on Wednesday that the Auburn outbreak was “different to what has occurred in the past and is seemingly the result of infested imported goods.” “Our government is boosting our on-ground resources and establishing a taskforce to focus on tracing the supply chains that have led to this occurring,” she said.

Carol Booth, the policy director of the Invasive Species Council, pointed to a 2021 review by the inspector general of biosecurity of the controls for the Khapra beetle, which made recommendations to improve the entire quarantine system. The South Asian beetle is the country's second-highest identified national priority plant pest because of its risks to the grain industry. At the time, the inspector general made 13 recommendations, including that the department “improve its level of regulatory agility” and work with “an expectation of response time frames of days or weeks, not months or years” as the risk of an outbreak evolved quickly. Experts said those recommendations were among years of advice to successive federal governments that was ignored or not acted on.

The review also criticised the federal government for not acting when the risk of the beetle arriving was first identified in July 2020. “This delay is an illustration of the department’s continuing regulatory immaturity,” the inspector general found, suggesting either a poorly defined risk assessment process or an overly cavalier attitude to risk. Four years later, nothing had been done about the recommendation to improve response times, and an October 2025 review found all but one of the recommendations still open.

The response-time recommendation has since been implemented, according to The Guardian. The gap identified in 2021 remained central to the criticism this week, with Invasive Species Council policy director Carol Booth saying the recommendation was particularly significant because biosecurity risks to Australia were continually evolving and increasing, partly because of climate change.

“There are just so many things they should be doing that they’re not, or they weren’t doing well, or they weren’t auditing to ensure that their measures were being actually implemented,” Booth said. “There’s definitely some things they do well, but others, they just don’t have the resources.”

National Farmers' Federation president Hamish McIntyre said farmers “believe we are watching a national biosecurity and human health disaster unfold in front of our eyes around fire ants” because of failures in the quarantine system. “We can’t even keep [illicit] cigarettes out of the country through our borders, let alone some of these difficult-to-detect pests,” he said. McIntyre also said being an island nation was Australia's greatest biosecurity advantage.

Federal agriculture minister Julie Collins said the government had delivered more than $2bn in additional biosecurity resourcing since 2022, including more than $296m in the current red imported fire ant eradication program. In the last financial year alone, she said, federal biosecurity officers inspected almost 350,000 commercial sea and air cargoes, preventing tens of thousands of harmful pests, diseases and weeds from entering Australia.

Tom Kompas, a professor of environmental economics and biosecurity at the University of Melbourne, said it was likely that Sydney's infestation was broader than currently thought. “If it does get into metro Sydney, it would be a disaster. It would be an absolute disaster,” he said. The Guardian said Australia has a reputation for extraordinarily tough and strict biosecurity controls, but no system can block, inspect or decontaminate every container of every pest.

Kompas said the biosecurity system was hampered by its approach to risk, with funding for individual pests allocated according to concern levels. He said a better approach would allocate funding on cost-effectiveness and suggested that testing of Australian cows for foot-and-mouth disease was probably overfunded. He also said the system placed an overemphasis on risks to agriculture rather than the environment.

Gough said fire ant incursions are difficult to prevent entirely. “We’re going to have incursions of fire ants, among other things,” he said. “The key is basically how early in the biosecurity system we’re catching these things, such that then it’s a lot easier to deal with.”

The cost of eradication falls to taxpayers, Gough said, and Queensland's fire ant eradication program costs more than $100m a year. The Invasive Species Council has long called for a new tax on importers to fund the country's biosecurity system, but Gough said Australia's obligations under World Trade Organization agreements had prevented its implementation as a flat fee.

Kompas said the Sydney infestation was likely broader than currently thought. The taskforce Moriarty announced is intended to trace the supply chains behind the Auburn outbreak, which Moriarty said appeared to result from infested imported goods, while Gough said the value of the biosecurity system rests on how early it catches incursions.
