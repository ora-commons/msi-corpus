## Six Democratic-controlled states allow abortion through fetal viability

Democrats who ran on abortion rights platforms won big in the 2026 elections for Congress and state government offices. The Associated Press said it was not likely that Democrats could impose quick and vast abortion policy changes. Democrats’ 2026 midterm wins strengthened their position in Congress and state government, but the gains did not give the party the full control of legislatures and governorships needed to dismantle many of the strictest abortion bans. Some congressional Democrats plan to push forward a measure to bar states from restricting abortion before viability. President Donald Trump is expected to veto the proposal, and overriding that veto would require Republican help.

Abortion has been primarily a state government issue since the U.S. Supreme Court’s 2022 ruling that struck down Roe v. Wade and allowed states to enforce bans. Thirteen states prohibit abortion at all stages of pregnancy, with some exceptions, and four more prohibit it after about the first six weeks. Democrats did not take full control of the legislatures and governorships in the states with the strictest rules.

Some congressional Democrats plan to advance a measure that would bar states from banning abortion before viability and rescind certain restrictions, including requirements for multiple doctor visits or ultrasounds. With a Trump veto expected if it reaches him, the effort would be aimed largely at forcing him to reject a policy that has public opinion on its side. Although Democrats will control both the House and Senate, the AP reported, overriding a veto would require Republican help.

About 6 in 10 U.S. voters said abortion should be legal in all or most cases, according to the AP Voter Poll, a survey of more than 100,000 midterm voters in all 50 states.

In 2027, Democrats will control both legislative chambers and the governor’s office in Arizona, Michigan, Minnesota, Nevada, Pennsylvania and Wisconsin, states they currently share with Republicans. Those six states already allow abortion through at least fetal viability, generally considered sometime after 21 weeks of pregnancy. In Wisconsin, abortion is allowed through 20 weeks from fertilization, or about 22 weeks’ gestation; Pennsylvania’s limit is 23 weeks’ gestation.

Democrats retained the governorships in Wisconsin and Pennsylvania and gained full control of both legislatures. Wisconsin Gov.-elect David Crowley and Pennsylvania Gov. Josh Shapiro are calling for use of their new power to amend their state constitutions and create a right to abortion. Voters in a dozen states have already approved similar amendments, including Nevada and Virginia this year. Missouri voters retained protections, while Idaho voters rejected an initiative that would have added them to state law.

In Georgia, Democrats gained the House and governor’s office, but the Republican-held Senate remains an obstacle to repealing a ban that, in most cases, applies once fetal cardiac activity can be detected, around six weeks into pregnancy and before many women realize they are pregnant. Incoming Democratic lieutenant governor Josh McLaurin said he is optimistic that one Republican state senator would be willing to join Democrats to repeal the law. Republicans hold a 29-27 majority, which McLaurin said could still allow the measure at least one Senate vote if the chamber did not take a “hyper-partisan approach” to blocking it.

McLaurin said voters see the ban as a healthcare issue, particularly after a 2024 state review board found that two women died in Georgia after doctors were hesitant to perform abortions that could have saved their lives. Georgia Gov.-elect Keisha Lance Bottoms acknowledged that the Republican Senate could block action but said the party would act if it found a path forward. “If we see a pathway to do something about it this session, then we’re going to take that opportunity,” Bottoms said at a news conference. “And it may not be this session.”

Texas has another change in legal strategy. Democrat Nathan Johnson, who won the attorney general election, said he would not sue out-of-state providers who prescribe abortion pills via telehealth and ship them to patients in Texas. Republican Ken Paxton had brought such cases and dropped them in November. Johnson also could withdraw Texas from a lawsuit challenging federal policy on the abortion pill mifepristone and decide not to defend Texas abortion restrictions against lawsuits.

Those abortion pills often come from states that seek to protect medical professionals who prescribe them through telehealth and ship them to patients in places with bans. The AP said the practice was why the number of abortions had been higher since the fall of Roe and described it as the heart of current abortion legal battles. Kelsey Pritchard, a spokesperson for SBA Pro-Life America, criticized the change: “It is deeply gutting that now we don’t have Texas to be fighting with the pro-life side on this,” she said.

Abortion opponents are concentrating on medication abortion. Pritchard and other activists are urging the Trump administration to persuade the U.S. Food and Drug Administration, which is studying mifepristone, to bar prescriptions from being filled by mail or at pharmacies; the FDA says it will have more information by March. Abortion opponents are also counting on remaining Republican state attorneys general to continue the fight.

Ohio lawmakers this month passed legislation barring telehealth prescriptions of mifepristone sent into the state and reinstating a 24-hour waiting period before obtaining an abortion. It is unclear whether outgoing Republican Gov. Mike DeWine will sign the measures or how courts will rule on challenges. Ohio voters approved a constitutional amendment protecting abortion access in 2023.

Ingrid Duran, state legislative director at the National Right to Life Committee, said the group will continue pushing measures that sometimes attract Democratic support, including ways for women to anonymously and safely give up newborns for adoption. She also said the organization will promote anti-abortion bills with little prospect of passage now to build support for the future. “We’re not going to give up just because it’s going to be more challenging,” she said.

The post-election map combines targeted openings with continuing institutional limits: constitutional campaigns in Pennsylvania and Wisconsin, a possible Senate vote in Georgia, and a change in Texas litigation. Republicans retain the White House and the Georgia Senate, while abortion opponents are concentrating on mifepristone and other restrictions that remain in place.
