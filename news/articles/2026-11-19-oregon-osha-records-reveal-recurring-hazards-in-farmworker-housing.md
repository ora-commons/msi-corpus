## Oregon OSHA records reveal recurring hazards in farmworker housing

**Subtype:** fact

- InvestigateWest's review of Oregon OSHA inspections from 2020 through May 2026 found more than 50 farms exposed workers to housing hazards.
- InvestigateWest identified electrical problems as the most common violation in the inspection data.
- Oregon OSHA recorded 205 farm housing violations in 2025, compared with 192 in 2024.
- Oregon OSHA recorded 104 farm housing violations since 2020 that stemmed from worker complaints.
- Oregon farmers employed an estimated 6,000 H-2A workers in 2025, up from around 4,400 in 2021.

## Electrical problems were the most common violation in state inspection data

An investigation of Oregon OSHA inspection records found more than 50 farms where workers were exposed to health and safety hazards in employer-provided housing. Electrical problems were the most common violation in the review of all state inspections from 2020 through May 2026, which included housing on or near farms. An estimated 12,000 workers live on Oregon farms, and growers' reliance on temporary seasonal labor is growing.

On a January afternoon, two state inspectors walked through a horse stable converted to house workers at an Oregon marijuana farm. Inspectors found rats and cockroaches in the kitchen, holes in the walls, leftover food and unclean dishes and three bathroom stalls with dirty, blackened toilets that lacked doors for privacy. The stable had no smoke detectors or accessible first-aid supplies. Outside, water accumulated behind a shower building, inviting mosquitos, and barely any water came out when a shower head was tested.

Only three workers were living in the stable when inspectors arrived because the harvest season had ended and most workers had gone home. Inspectors later reported that 24 workers had lived there since July 2025. A labor contractor that supplied workers to the cannabis farm, owned by MNG Holdings LLC, was fined more than $17,000. Records showed that the contractor and farm took steps to correct the violations; neither responded to requests for comment.

InvestigateWest said the unsanitary conditions exposed workers to illnesses such as salmonella, cholera and hepatitis A, while rats and cockroaches can also spread hantavirus and E. coli. The Southern Oregon operation was one of dozens of growers and labor contractors fined during the previous five years for failing to provide sanitary and safe housing as workers planted, pruned and harvested crops across Oregon. The broader review found that workers, often immigrants or foreign nationals with temporary work visas, lived in crowded and dirty homes that lacked basic amenities such as toilet paper. Electrical problems created risks of shock and fire, and many units lacked smoke detectors or fire extinguishers. Inspectors often found the violations weeks after workers had left for the season.

In July 2025, an Oregon OSHA inspector visited Ficher Dairy in Myrtle Point, Oregon, after a worker complained about poor housing conditions. Two double-wide trailers had exposed wiring from light fixtures, and extension cords were being used to power electronics instead of fixed wiring. The violations exposed workers and their families to fire, burns and electric shock. Neither trailer had smoke detectors, fire extinguishers or an evacuation and medical plan. Ficher Dairy faced more than $30,000 in fines across that inspection and two subsequent inspections that found additional problems. The dairy did not respond to an interview request.

Other inspections found employers leaving live wires in common areas and bathrooms, using extension cords instead of fixed outlets and placing electrical outlets in wet or damp areas without precautions. Some employers used ungrounded power outlets that could cause shocks or fires. Dozens of housing units lacked smoke detectors or extinguishers that workers could access during an emergency, and many units were not clearly marked, making them difficult for first responders to find.

The Oregon data did not document any injuries or deaths specifically related to housing conditions. The investigation cited a 2023 trailer fire in North Carolina as an example of the potential consequences: the fire killed two Mexican farmworkers harvesting sweet potatoes. InvestigateMidwest described the employer-provided trailer as “barely livable,” and fire marshals said the fire may have been caused by a faulty stove. It was unclear whether the trailer had a working smoke detector that could have alerted the workers, and the fire started in the early morning.

InvestigateWest classified the electrical hazards and lack of emergency equipment at Ficher Dairy as “serious” violations, meaning they could lead to a worker's death or bodily harm. Nearly 27% of the violations it analyzed were considered serious. Another 69% were classified as “other than serious,” which refers to hazards that can affect worker health and safety but are not likely to cause serious injury or death.

Aaron Corvin, a spokesperson for Oregon OSHA, said the agency focuses its limited resources on industries and situations where workers face the greatest risk of workplace injury, illness or death. He said the citations reflect that focus, that most identified hazards can be fixed in a relatively short time and that inspectors often work with employers on electrical problems. Corvin also said the agency inspects housing units when they are first registered with the state and regularly conducts unannounced inspections.

“It is that kind of work over time —– catching and addressing those types of hazards —– that helps move employers into compliance before hazards increase,” Corvin said in an emailed response.

Farm-housing violations increased to 205 in 2025. Oregon OSHA had recorded 49 in 2021, 59 in 2022, 85 in 2023 and 192 in 2024. Corvin could not explain the increase but said the agency conducted more than 3,500 inspections in 2025 and more than 3,200 in 2024. He said violations fluctuate from year to year.

Missing screens on windows and doors were among the common findings, exposing workers to heat and pests. Inspectors found rodents and cockroaches in workers' homes and, in one case, bees. Most missing-screen violations were not considered serious. Other findings involved mold from leaking showers, rotting walls, broken toilet seats and housing units without toilet paper for dozens of workers.

Blue Heron Nursery, a tree and shrub farm in Salem, Oregon, had eight violations in August 2024, including failing to provide toilet paper at one labor camp with 92 workers and at another location with 39 employees. Inspectors often issued no penalty for dirty bathrooms, while some employers were fined up to $200. Six citations for dirty bathrooms in 2026 resulted in fines ranging from $1,450 to $2,100. One cited Rios Labor Contractor LLC for failing to regularly clean shared toilets for nearly 30 workers. Blue Heron Nursery and Rios Labor did not respond to requests for comment.

Employers received 14 citations for placing more workers in a unit than it could accommodate. In 2024, Oregon cited farmer Dick Kobayashi in Hood River for housing eight workers in three units where they cooked, lived and slept in the same room. Three workers shared a room of less than 60 square feet, roughly the size of a small bathroom. Inspectors did not classify the violation as serious and did not impose a fine. Kobayashi did not respond to a request for comment.

Oregon OSHA recently added requirements for agricultural labor housing, while lawmakers increased workplace-safety penalties that had been among the country's lowest. A May 2023 bill raised the minimum penalty for a serious violation from $300 to $1,116 and increased the maximum penalty for an “other than serious” violation from $390 to $15,625. There were 11 repeat-violator citations since 2020, and the minimum penalty for those citations increased from $200 to $11,162.

Many workers on Oregon farms are hired through the federal H-2A visa program, which is administered by the U.S. Department of Labor, making them particularly dependent on their employers and reluctant to report abuse. Oregon farmers still employ mostly domestic workers but have become more reliant in recent years on seasonal foreign workers. Oregon farmers employed an estimated 6,000 H-2A workers in 2025. That estimate has increased steadily since at least 2021, when the program brought around 4,400 workers to farms. Nearly 30,000 temporary farmworkers had come to Oregon through the program since 2020.

Farmers have also had difficulty filling jobs with domestic workers. The Oregon Center for Public Policy estimates that around 57,000 workers in Oregon may be undocumented. InvestigateWest linked the industry's growing reliance on H-2A labor to President Donald Trump's mass-deportation efforts, which it said forced many undocumented workers out of the country. Farmers have turned to the H-2A program to fill the resulting labor gap.

Federal officials recently sought to make it easier for farmers to apply for the H-2A program by attempting to lower the minimum wage requirement for workers and deduct housing costs from their wages. A federal judge later ruled that the changes violated federal law and ordered the U.S. Department of Labor to revisit the rule change.

Carmen Martinez, deputy policy director of Centro de los Derechos del Migrante, a nonprofit that supports Mexico-based migrant workers in the United States, has monitored the program's expansion under the Trump administration. Martinez said employers were responding to increased immigration enforcement and concerns about having enough labor, while members of Congress sought to expand guest-worker programs. “Unfortunately, most of the proposals to expand the program don’t contemplate the need for additional protections, and some of them even want to cut back on protections for guest workers,” Martinez said.

According to Martinez, the U.S. Department of Labor had not made significant investments in enforcement or oversight of the program, leaving state agencies such as Oregon OSHA to enforce state and federal housing requirements. The federal program requires employers to provide housing, transportation from workers' home countries to the work location, weekly transportation to a grocery store and application fees.

Centro de los Derechos del Migrante surveyed 100 people who had worked in the United States under the visa program from September 2019 to January 2020. All reported some type of rights violation involving wage theft or working conditions. “We’re truly very worried that the guest visa program expansion will exacerbate current issues for these workers in housing and in other areas,” Martinez said.

In 2026, over 100 growers registered new agriculture labor housing units on or near their farms with Oregon OSHA, as required by law.

Farmworker advocates have long criticized the H-2A program for tying workers to a single employer that decides who can enter the United States, who can retain a position through the season and who can return the following year. A worker who loses a job can also lose employer-provided housing. Temporary farmworkers can bring their families to live with them.

The COVID-19 pandemic exposed additional health risks for farmworkers living in on-farm housing. Martinez said workers often lived in crowded conditions, traveled to work on packed, cramped buses and had few places to wash their hands and bathe. A 2021 study by University of Oregon faculty and students surveyed 300 farmworkers about the pandemic's effects. The study found that 15% of the workers tested positive for COVID-19 and 49% had known exposure.

Dr. Eva Galvez, a family physician in Hillsboro and chief medical officer for Migrant Clinician Network, a network of doctors who care for immigrant patients including farmworkers, grew up in farmworker housing on the Oregon orchard where her parents worked. She remembers waking most mornings to the sound of pesticide sprayers outside her window. Her family received a separate house on the orchard, while some friends' families lived in labor camps where six people often shared one room. Galvez said she may not have become a doctor if she had grown up in some of the conditions she now sees workers face.

“Farmworkers often live in really closed, cramped quarters, and these are places where you’re going to see the spread of infectious disease,” Galvez said. “You can’t go to school, you can’t focus on your education when you don’t have your basic needs met,” she said.

Galvez said she recently treated patients living with about 10 people in a small space. Four beds might share a room and be divided by curtains, with families spilling into common living areas. Shared bathrooms presented particular difficulties for families with children and young people who were menstruating. Galvez also said she had met women who said they were sexually assaulted in shared bathrooms.

Preparing food in a small shared area exposed workers to foodborne illness, while limited kitchen time made cooking difficult. Galvez said many farmworkers therefore relied on prepared meals that lacked vegetables and contained high amounts of sodium and fat. She also sees workers struggle with mental health because they have no space to relax after work. “People’s individual health and their ability to manage chronic illnesses is harder when they don’t have a space to cook and store food, but there’s also an infectious disease risk,” Galvez said.

Oregon OSHA relies partly on worker complaints for enforcement. The data show that regulators often rely on workers and advocacy groups to discover poor conditions. Of the farm-housing violations recorded since 2020, 104 stemmed from complaints, about one-quarter of the total. Corvin said the agency receives around 2,000 worker complaints annually.

Illegal labor camps are one of regulators' biggest challenges because they are not registered with the state and therefore are not on the state's radar from the outset. The camps are often run by operators of illegal marijuana farms who subject workers to poor, unsafe conditions. Oftentimes, regulators may never discover the illegal growers, and the growers can dismantle their operations at the end of the grow season. Corvin said Oregon OSHA relies on complaints and advocates to hear about bad conditions in those situations.

Ira Cuello-Martinez, policy director for Oregon's largest farmworker union, Pineros y Campesinos Unidos del Noroeste (PCUN), said violations go unnoticed when workers do not speak up. He said complaints are rare because of the imbalance in power between workers and employers. Last year, Oregon lawmakers passed a law making labor-camp operators and landowners liable for farmworker housing on their land that violates licensing and compliance laws.

In Silverton, Oregon, a city northeast of Salem, seven farmworkers began working for hemp grower Jose Duran Villalobos in August 2024. The workers, many of whom had traveled from California, lived in tents they had purchased themselves under trees beside the hemp field. They shared makeshift showers set up by their supervisor, which appeared in photos to be made from hanging black tarps. A small cabin housed three workers, but photos showed a dirt floor and no insulation.

State inspectors said the workers should have been provided with housing that offered protection from the weather. Villalobos had not registered the housing, so Oregon OSHA learned about it through an anonymous employee complaint saying the workers were living in tents and using a makeshift kitchen and shower. An inspector wrote that a serious injury was “expected” because workers faced possible elevated heat, contact with rodents, flies and mosquitoes and unsanitary conditions.

Oregon OSHA issued one citation for the unregistered housing and a $3,000 fine. The workers did not report injuries or illness from the living conditions, and they stayed in the tents for less than a month. The inspection report said the camp was disbanded and the workers found their own housing to rent. Villalobos paid the fine but did not respond to an interview request.

Corvin said the inspector determined the workers' exposure would have been addressed before they moved in if the housing had been properly registered. He said OSHA considers whether a violation is likely to cause an illness or injury when deciding how to cite an employer.

Farmers have pushed back against new housing regulations, saying they cannot afford the improvements needed to comply. Jenny Dresler, a spokesperson and lobbyist for the Oregon Farm Bureau, said the violations show that the system works because OSHA investigates complaints. Dresler said most Oregon farmers try to comply with state rules and provide safe homes. She blamed “bad actors” who operate substandard, unregistered housing.

Advocates including Galvez said that without better enforcement and more regulations, vulnerable farmworkers will continue to face poor and unsafe living conditions. “When you toil in the fields, using your back every day in the heat, and you start doing that when you’re 12 or even 20, it catches up to you,” Galvez said. “So my patients who have done this all their life, they get to be around 60 and their bodies are exhausted.”

She recalled one farmworker she saw recently at a mobile health clinic who told her: “We leave our health on the fields.” “It’s true, farmworkers leave their health on the fields, and in return, we could at least get fair housing, safe housing,” Galvez said. “They leave their health on the fields to feed us and our families.”
