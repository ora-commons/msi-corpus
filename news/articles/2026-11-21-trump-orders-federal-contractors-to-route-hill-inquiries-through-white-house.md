## Contracts could be terminated if contractors do not comply

President Donald Trump is directing federal contractors and subcontractors to consult with his administration about inquiries they receive from Congress and to withhold any communications the White House considers privileged, according to a new executive order issued late Friday.

The order requires companies to coordinate with the White House and federal agencies on "appropriate responses" to such requests. It also warns that contractors who fail to comply could see their contracts terminated and their future work with the federal government suspended.

In a fact sheet, the White House framed the new requirements as "protecting executive privileges from congressional overreach," arguing that some congressional leaders have tried to intrude on executive branch privileges by "harassing private companies that contract with the federal government."

Rep. Robert Garcia of California, the top Democrat on the House Oversight Committee, said the order was part of the administration's "blatant attack on Congress' constitutional right to conduct oversight." He said that despite efforts to impede investigations, "we're just getting started."

The directive is the Republican administration's latest effort to resist what are expected to be wide-ranging and aggressive oversight efforts from Democrats when they take over Congress in January. Democrats have made government contracts one of their top investigative targets, noting that federal money has been flowing to companies linked with Trump's family, particularly at the Pentagon.

White House officials have already begun taking steps to exert more control over how agencies and officials respond to congressional requests, anticipating there will be a flood of investigations initiated by Democrats.

Trump's counsel, Will Scharf, told federal agencies on Monday that they must seek approval from his office and the White House's Office of Legislative Affairs before agreeing to appear before Congress. Scharf also suggested in a recent letter to Garcia that some congressional investigations may amount to "blatant criminality."

Garcia sent letters this week directing members of Trump's family and top administration officials to preserve documents. Scharf sought to dismiss the requests, noting in a letter to Democratic lawmakers that they are not yet in the majority and cannot compel compliance.

Presidents of both parties have long used executive privilege to resist congressional inquiries, but the claims can set up messy, protracted legal fights as parties dispute whether it applies. Executive privilege allows the president to keep information from the courts, Congress and the public to protect the confidentiality of White House deliberations, but the protection is far from absolute.

The latest executive order applies to all contractors and subcontractors, a universe of tens of thousands of businesses.
