## Justice Department warns broader FCC limits would seriously harm government and public

The Federal Communications Commission said Wednesday that the part of a federal judge's decision granting ABC's motion was “wrong on the facts and wrong on the law,” adding that the agency “looks forward to prevailing in full on this matter.” ABC accused the FCC of violating its free-speech rights in retaliation for programming decisions the Trump administration disliked.

U.S. District Judge Loren AliKhan in Washington, D.C., agreed with ABC that the Trump administration was chilling the network's free-speech rights. She said ABC was likely to succeed on its First Amendment claim and that ABC had detailed “a campaign of retaliation against them in response to their exercise of their First Amendment rights,” which on its own was an “irreparable injury.” AliKhan wrote, “This is not a close case.”

Government attorneys urged AliKhan to dismiss ABC's lawsuit, accusing the network of trying to ward off scrutiny by federal regulators prematurely. In a written statement, Justice Department lawyers wrote, “To foreclose the Commission from using all the tools at its disposal to investigate whether Plaintiffs are meeting their statutory and regulatory duties would seriously harm both the government and the public.”

AliKhan, whom former President Joe Biden nominated, granted ABC's request for a temporary restraining order and preliminary injunction on First Amendment grounds. ABC and its parent company, Disney, sued the FCC and Commissioner Brendan Carr in August, several months after the commission ordered the early review. The preliminary order blocks that review for now but does not grant ABC the broader protection against future FCC pressure that the network sought.

ABC asked AliKhan to temporarily prohibit the FCC from taking any further action related to the license-renewal proceedings. AliKhan granted that request but rejected ABC's additional request to block the FCC from “any actions, formal or informal,” to coerce or threaten sanctions in an effort to alter the network's “exercise of editorial discretion.” AliKhan said the network had not specified what future threats might be beyond the early license-renewal proceedings. An ABC News spokesperson said in a statement, “Today’s decision is an important victory for the First Amendment.”

Commissioner Brendan Carr, a Trump ally, ordered the early review in April, shortly after a joke by ABC's late-night host Jimmy Kimmel angered President Donald Trump, who called for Kimmel's firing. The FCC's action directed eight ABC-owned stations to submit renewal applications for their broadcast licenses years ahead of schedule.

ABC also has been fighting Carr's efforts to make the morning talk show “The View” subject to equal-time rules. Carr defended the agency's actions against ABC by saying broadcasters have a duty to “operate in the public interest.”

The case is one of several legal skirmishes between President Donald Trump's administration and news outlets, including The Associated Press, over their coverage and access to the executive branch since the Republican returned to the White House nearly two years ago.

The plaintiffs are American Broadcasting Companies, Inc.; its indirect parent, the Walt Disney Company; and eight television stations for which ABC is the indirect parent. AliKhan's order gives ABC temporary relief from the early review but not the broader protection against future FCC pressure that the network sought.
