## EPA, health officials and universities will monitor air, water and soil afterward

Eliza Billingham of Iowa Public Radio said the U.S. Army recommends residents in the Middletown area leave for most of Wednesday and that people should be able to return Thursday. Sean Kaufman of the Administration for Strategic Preparedness and Response said women who are pregnant, children under the age of 6, children in general and individuals who may have chronic disease should evacuate the area. Billingham said the EPA, local health officials, the University of Iowa and the University of Illinois will monitor air, water and soil after the planned detonation. The recommendations came as the Army prepared to detonate some 10,000 pounds of lead azide at the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant on Wednesday.

Health experts said no level of lead exposure is safe, and much of the evacuation decision has been left to individual residents. Sean Kaufman of the Administration for Strategic Preparedness and Response said: “If they are women who are pregnant, children under the age of 6, or children in general, and individuals who may have chronic disease, you definitely want to be out of that area. I would evacuate that area.”

Kaufman continued: “Well, I think you need to do what you need to do to take care of your family. That’s very, very important. Certainly, be inside, and wait until you get the information that you need.”

The highly volatile compound is used in military munitions and has been deteriorating underground. Army officials said a controlled explosion was the safest option and that the deteriorating material could detonate spontaneously. Acting U.S. Army Secretary Adam Telle said: “There is consensus across the experts across the country that a controlled, mitigated detonation of the lead azide here at Iowa Army Ammunition Plant is far preferable to an uncontrolled spontaneous detonation of that substance.”

The detonation could send a plume of toxic lead dust across Southeastern Iowa and into neighboring states. The operation has been delayed several times amid growing concerns from residents and environmental advocates about potential risks to public health.

Eliza Billingham of Iowa Public Radio, who has covered the story for weeks, said residents’ views differed, but an explosion appeared likely to most people in Middletown and the surrounding area. Billingham said a controlled detonation appeared to be the better option for those residents, leaving the main decision as when and how the material would explode rather than whether.

Some of the communication breakdown came from the Army not recognizing how many people would be interested, Billingham said. The Army spoke with Middletown residents for much of the year and held an informational meeting in October, while The Hawk Eye, a Burlington newspaper, had covered the issue since March.

The Army’s release of worst-case plume maps last week drew attention across the region, where more people thought they might be affected than just Middletown residents. Billingham said residents felt caught off guard by the potential exposure and that the Army had been playing catch up, trying to earn back their trust.

Billingham said the Army had released maps and continued updating them as weather forecasts became clearer, and that the Army’s confidence in the projections was increasing. The mitigation plan calls for wetting the land with misters and flooding the area over earthen bunkers containing lead azide with particle-trapping foam. The Army said it hoped the measures would prevent dust from leaving the ammunition plant.

Billingham said the Army recommends residents leave for most of Wednesday but should be able to return Thursday. The blast is set for the day before Thanksgiving. It had been planned for the previous Friday, moved to Monday or Tuesday after lawsuits and public concern, and then shifted to Wednesday to take advantage of ideal weather forecasts and potentially the best wind.

After the planned detonation, the EPA will monitor air, water and soil, Billingham said. Local health officials and the University of Iowa and the University of Illinois will also conduct monitoring. The timeline for results depends on initial readings, and the EPA and local public health officials said they would make the information as readily available as possible.

The Army is now moving ahead with the Wednesday operation, though the current report does not address the status of those approval conditions.
