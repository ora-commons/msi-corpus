## Experts say state gains will reshape abortion, vaccine and gender-affirming care policy

Public health leaders and politicians who made healthcare a central part of their platforms swept into office in the US midterm elections last week, setting the stage for national, state and local changes. Voters faced an unprecedented rise in vaccine-preventable diseases such as measles, alongside devastating outbreaks of foodborne illnesses, and they chose candidates who prioritized public health, healthcare affordability, reproductive health and the impeachment of Robert F Kennedy Jr.

“The American people are speaking out against President Trump and the policies that he’s been rolling out since the day he took office,” said Julie Scofield, a member of the coordinating committee at the grassroots organization Defend Public Health. “I think it’s a complete repudiation.”

Scofield said there were more candidates running in this midterm election from a public health perspective and background, making public health part of their platforms and part of the issues they were talking about. “I think that’s extraordinary,” she said.

## Election results

Democrats flipped state houses and legislatures in several states, and experts said those leadership changes would dramatically reshape actions on abortion, gender-affirming care, vaccines and more. Several of the candidates successfully argued that cuts to Medicaid, Affordable Care Act subsidies and the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (Snap) have wounded America, and said rising prices — especially in healthcare — were a big concern.

In Georgia, the home of the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, Keisha Lance Bottoms is now the first Black woman ever elected governor of a US state. As mayor of Atlanta, Bottoms clashed with Brian Kemp, Georgia’s Republican governor, on public health measures for the Covid pandemic. Jasmine Clark, a microbiologist also from Georgia, will serve in the US House. Jon Ossoff, who won re-election to the Senate, concluded his victory speech by guaranteeing “healthcare for every single American”.

Ohio governor-elect Amy Acton and Michigan senator-elect Abdul El-Sayed were public health leaders in the Covid response, which their opponents unsuccessfully tried to use against them. A photograph by Gina M Randazzo/ZUMA Press Wire/Shutterstock showed Hands Off NYC joined by a large coalition calling for the new Democratic-controlled Congress to be accountable to voters.

Voters in several states also rejected proposed restrictions on abortion, and Virginia passed a constitutional amendment to enshrine marriage equality and reproductive rights.

## State and federal authority

“It’s super important for people to remember that a lot of public health authority rests with the states,” Scofield said. Congress plays an important role in setting policies and providing funding to state and local public health agencies, but many public health decisions happen at the state and local level, she said.

On the federal level, it is likely Congress will take action around restoring cuts to Medicaid and Snap, addressing the premium tax credits, and making sure funds are deployed appropriately by agencies such as the CDC and the NIH, Scofield said. Many of the administration’s actions on gutting research and dismantling agencies were illegal, acting outside of Congress, she said.

In Texas, Attorney General Ken Paxton investigated pediatricians for giving vaccines, sued providers — even in other states — for mailing abortion pills, and declared that gender-affirming care for minors qualifies as child abuse. His successor has already announced he will be dropping several lawsuits like these.

Trump administration officials including Jay Bhattacharya, head of the NIH, and Kennedy have frequently positioned themselves in opposition to what they describe as government overreach on Covid measures and vaccines.

## Voter evidence

Mallory Harris, a scientist in Austin, almost delayed having children because she was worried about accessing life-saving healthcare, such as vaccines and abortion, if she needed it. She had doctors check her immunity against viruses including measles, and sought assurances from her physicians that she would have access to appropriate care. Harris found out she was pregnant just as 2026 officially became the worst year for measles in the US since before she was born. Texas has no paid time off for maternity or paternity leave, and Harris and her husband will try to wait until the baby is six months old and can get the measles, mumps and rubella (MMR) shot early before starting daycare.

Dealing with public health emergencies like these had been “a top issue” for Harris as a voter. After the election, “it suddenly became a lot less scary”, Harris said. “We’ve seen scientists and physicians getting elected in record numbers. We saw big shifts in places that have had measles outbreaks.”

Several Democratic candidates faced anti-trans advertising from their opponents. “The results show that those ads backfired,” Scofield said. “Anti-trans attacks didn’t work.” Anti-trans initiatives in states including Washington and Colorado appear to have passed, though Democratic leaders in those states have already begun questioning whether they are constitutional.

Tuesday’s results have reassured people like Harris, who have had to confront more preventable disease outbreaks and more restrictions on abortion and other forms of healthcare than the US has had in decades. “Voters were willing to turn out for candidates who supported public health,” Harris said. That goes “directly” against what these health officials appear to have interpreted as their mandate, she said. After the election, she added, “a lot of things are going to change quickly, and also there’s real damage that’s been done and real risk that remains.” “It has meant a lot to see people getting elected who have spoken about these fears and address them head on,” Harris said. “It’s really important to have leadership who cares about you and your family.”

## Candidate examples

Annie Andrews will be the first pediatrician in the US Senate. She campaigned on impeaching Kennedy, the health and human services secretary, and pushed for improving vaccination rates after measles tore through her home state of South Carolina. In a photograph by Mic Smith/AP, senator-elect Andrews spoke during an election night event on 3 November in Charleston, South Carolina.

Richard Pan, a pediatrician who served as a state senator in California and will now serve in the US House, led the creation of a state law eliminating religious and personal exemptions to vaccination, and he sparred with Kennedy over the change. Kennedy later apologized for calling vaccines a “holocaust”.

Amish Shah, an emergency medicine doctor, flipped a US House seat in Arizona after campaigning on improving public health, among other issues. Adam Hamawy, a surgeon from New Jersey, won a US House seat by focusing in part on affordable healthcare.

Janice Johnson showed the medications she takes for multiple myeloma at her home in Greenville, Mississippi, on 5 November, in a photograph by Ellen Schmidt/AP. Johnson cited healthcare as one of the key issues that made her vote for Democratic Senate candidate Scott Colom.

Scofield said it was disappointing to see candidates including Andrews and James Talarico in Texas change their public support for gender-affirming care. “No doubt we have educating to do,” she said. Organizations such as Defend Public Health will continue to “stand solidly and squarely behind our transgender community and full access to gender-affirming care,” Scofield added. “That is just a non-starter for us.”
