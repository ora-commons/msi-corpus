## The proposed deal would avoid direct cash payments to Russia

An investor consortium led by Eldridge has agreed to acquire Lukoil International, the international energy business of Russian oil company Lukoil. The agreement covers oil and gas fields, refineries, pipelines and other infrastructure across 11 countries. The consortium said Lukoil International’s operations currently produce about 350,000 barrels of oil equivalent per day.

The consortium also includes the U.S. International Development Finance Corp., a government lender; Catena Investment Holding, a company based in the United Arab Emirates; and New York-based investment bank Xtellus Capital Partners.

Lukoil had said at the beginning of the year that it planned to sell its international assets to an American private equity firm as it looked to withdraw from its global portfolio in the face of looming U.S. sanctions. Early this year, Lukoil pursued a sale to the Carlyle Group. The Treasury Department had given the company until Feb. 28 to sell its foreign assets.

Under the proposed terms, the consortium would exchange restricted Russian shares of Lukoil and other Russian companies for all of Lukoil International’s liquidity. That structure would avoid direct cash payments by the U.S.-led consortium to Russia in exchange for the assets.

The International Development Finance Corp. would receive an ownership interest on behalf of the United States. A DFC official, who was not authorized to comment publicly and spoke on condition of anonymity, positioned the agency’s involvement as a way to protect U.S. interests and reduce the risk of rivals getting involved. The official called Lukoil’s assets important to the energy security of the United States and its allies.

Rep. Gregory Meeks, a Democrat and ranking member of the House Foreign Affairs Committee, criticized the proposed deal. Meeks said the Trump administration was circumventing a congressional ban on DFC investing in Russia, a ban aimed at pressuring Moscow to end its war in Ukraine. He said, “DFC exists to advance development in emerging markets, not serve as a finance vehicle for President Trump’s wealthy and well-connected friends.”

Trump has taken a series of steps in recent months involving Russia, including agreeing to purchase Russian diesel in an effort to bring down global energy prices, meeting Russian President Vladimir Putin and calling him a friend, and abruptly waiving new sanctions on Russia days before they were scheduled to take effect.

The diesel agreement drew bipartisan opposition in Congress, where lawmakers argued that Russian shipments would violate a 2022 ban on energy imports from Russia. Although the Treasury Department temporarily authorized Russian diesel transactions that would ordinarily be subject to U.S. sanctions, two tankers originally bound for the United States changed course after state and federal officials warned port authorities, fuel distributors and banks that facilitating shipments could bring legal consequences.

Trump announced sanctions on Lukoil and Rosneft, Russia’s two biggest oil companies, in October 2025 to push for a ceasefire in Russia’s war in Ukraine. In September, Trump personally signed legislation aimed at renewing pressure on the Russian economy and disrupting the financial resources fueling the war. He then signed an executive order last month pausing a sweeping new sanctions package two days before its implementation deadline.
