## 62 snakes released at selected San Mateo County sites this autumn

Zoo-reared San Francisco garter snakes have been released into the wild for the first time. The San Francisco Zoo and Gardens said 62 of the non-venomous snakes were released this autumn at “carefully selected sites” in San Mateo County, part of a zoo-based conservation program intended to increase their chances of reaching breeding age.

The zoo announced the release this month and described it as a “major milestone” in the decades-long fight to bring the species “back from the brink of extinction.” The release was the first time snakes from the zoo-based head-starting program were returned to the wild.

The species is found only on the San Francisco peninsula. Its snakes are non-venomous and known for red heads, turquoise bellies and vibrant red and black stripes along their sides.

*Photo caption: A zoo-born San Francisco garter snake in its natural habitat. Photograph courtesy of Vishal Subramanyan and the Midpeninsula Regional Open Space District.*

Karine Tokatlian, a biologist with the Midpeninsula Regional Open Space District, said: “This is an extraordinary accomplishment for everyone who has worked for generations to protect this remarkable species.”

*Photo caption: A young San Francisco garter snake at the San Francisco Zoo and Gardens. Photograph courtesy of the San Francisco Zoo and Gardens.*

Tokatlian said seeing the young snakes return to the wild “represents years of research, collaboration, habitat restoration and careful conservation work.” She called the first release “an important step toward building healthy, resilient populations that can sustain themselves over the long term.”

The program, known as “head-starting,” is a first-of-its-kind effort involving state and federal agencies and nonprofit organizations. It raises young snakes in captivity during the period of their lives when they are most at risk, to “increase their chances of reaching breeding age.”

Biologists with the US Geological Survey gathered pregnant San Francisco garter snakes in 2025 and brought them to the San Francisco Zoo and Gardens. The mothers were returned to the wild, and the zoo conservation team reared their offspring until the young snakes were large enough to be released.

Scientists conducted genetic testing on each snake to determine the most ideal release locations.

*Photo caption: Dr Ian Konsker examines a San Francisco garter snake at the San Francisco Zoo and Gardens. Photograph courtesy of the San Francisco Zoo and Gardens.*

Dr Rochelle Stiles, the zoo’s field conservation director, said: “By providing these young snakes with a safe environment during their most vulnerable stage of life, we’re giving them a stronger start in the wild.”

The snakes face a range of pressures: illegal poaching, habitat loss and fragmentation from increased development and construction, habitat changes, and reductions in prey that are themselves threatened. Drought, disease, invasive species and low genetic diversity have added to the challenges.

More than 130 additional San Francisco garter snakes were born at the zoo this year and are expected to be released in 2027.
