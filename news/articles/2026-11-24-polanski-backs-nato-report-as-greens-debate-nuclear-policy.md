## Green Party sources say defence policies remain unchanged since 2024

Green Party leader Zack Polanski has endorsed a Verdant report calling for the UK to remain in NATO and maintain its nuclear deterrent. Party sources said the endorsement does not change the party's defence policies from 2024 and forms part of an internal discussion about how to reassure a broader group of voters on national security.

Written by Sacha Deshmukh, the report says the UK should remain in the military alliance while pressing for reform of its structures with allies. It also calls for the government to rethink its national security strategy with a greater focus on value for money, such as buying more weapons off the shelf rather than designing high-end bespoke systems for the British military.

On the nuclear deterrent, Deshmukh argues that the UK could not cancel the contract for the next generation of nuclear submarines, known as Dreadnought, and potentially should not cancel it either. The report says the country should seek closer cooperation with France on the missiles that will equip Dreadnought, even though the submarines are being developed to carry the US Trident missiles that the UK currently uses.

In both NATO and the nuclear deterrent, Deshmukh calls for government policy to remain largely the same despite previous calls from the Green Party and Polanski for more radical change. The support for maintaining nuclear weapons differs from the party's pre-election position: before the 2024 election, the Greens called for the UK to sign the UN treaty on the prohibition of nuclear weapons and immediately begin dismantling its Trident submarines.

Polanski framed his backing around the party's wider social goals. He said: “At its core, national security is about food on the table, warm homes and safe communities. That makes it a progressive issue – and I welcome Verdant’s call for a comprehensive security framework that brings people from across the UK into keeping our country safe.”

The endorsement came amid concern about the party's national-security standing. A YouGov poll last year found that only 17% of voters trusted the Greens on defence, the lowest result among 18 issues. Concerns about the party's defence positions have prompted some members to call for abandoning its opposition to drones and anti-ballistic missiles, a dispute that caused an internal rift ahead of this year's conference.

Deshmukh argued that both sides of British politics needed to confront weaknesses in their security positions. He said: “Historically some progressives have shied away from recognising security threats. But the right has not been willing to accept the flaws in their policy solutions, so they are not always as strong as they should be – both sides need to change that.”

The Verdant report also calls for more government investment in climate adaptation. Some within the green movement view that recommendation sceptically because it redirects attention from efforts to prevent climate deterioration in the first place.

On NATO, the report's recommendation that Britain remain a member while pushing for reform mirrors the Green Party's official policy but contrasts with what Polanski previously described as his personal view. Earlier this year, Polanski said: “Donald Trump has so much domination within Nato that I don’t believe it’s possible to reform Nato from within.”

Verdant is independent but retains close links to the Greens, and The Guardian said the report was likely to trigger another round of debate within the party. For now, party sources say the official platform remains unchanged; the immediate argument is over how to present national security to a broader group of voters.
