## Ambetter denied 25% of ACA marketplace prior-authorization requests in 2025

**Subtype:** fact

- ProPublica found Ambetter's 2025 prior-authorization denial rate averaged 25%, compared with 16% for marketplace plans.
- Ambetter customers challenged only 7% of the company's 2025 denials, but more than half of the challenged decisions were overturned.
- Ambetter reversed its denial of proton therapy for Richie Jones after he contacted U.S. Sen. Jon Ossoff's office and Georgia's insurance commissioner.
- Ambetter denied three CT-scan requests for liver-cancer patient Travis Christopher, then reversed the denial after he complained to a state senator; Christopher died months later.
- Ambetter continued to deny Zepbound coverage for Felicia after an independent-review company declined to consider her appeal as outside its scope.

## Most denials went unchallenged; more than half of contested cases were overturned

ProPublica based its analysis on 2025 disclosures from nearly 200 federal marketplace plans, Ambetter's own data and interviews with more than two dozen recent customers. An interactive chart in the report drew on disclosures from AuthDenied and insurance companies, including 21 Ambetter plans and 93 other plans. Ambetter, the most popular plan on the Affordable Care Act marketplace, averaged a 25% standard denial rate, compared with 16% across all marketplace plans. ProPublica found that Ambetter had the highest denial rates in most states where it supplied data and denied such requests at a higher rate than any other large insurer in 2025. The standard measure excludes urgent prior-authorization denials, which insurers report separately.

Federal regulators required marketplace plans to report and publish prior-authorization data for the first time in 2026, covering the 2025 reporting year. The requirement was intended to offer greater transparency into how often insurers restrict care by denying prior authorization. Since the Affordable Care Act marketplace launched in 2013, there had been little transparency about how often insurers requested and denied coverage. Insurers use prior authorization to limit spending on treatment, equipment and prescriptions. They say it also is intended to prompt doctors to try less expensive and less invasive options before costlier ones, but insurance experts told ProPublica that how and when insurers use the process remains largely a black box.

Only 7% of Ambetter's prior-authorization denials were challenged, ProPublica found. More than half of the customers who contested their denials obtained reversals. Marketplace plan holders told ProPublica that they did not appeal because they did not know their rights or how to navigate the process. Insurance experts and advocates said fighting a denial requires patience and persistence.

ProPublica reported that a small fraction of customers who faced delayed tests or disrupted treatments obtained coverage after complaining to powerful politicians. One Ambetter customer denied routine bloodwork complained to a state agency, and another whose cancer treatment was denied asked a U.S. senator for help.

The plan-level results illustrated how the policy affected different treatments. In Texas, where Ambetter's denial rate exceeded 32%, the company initially refused to pay for a medical scan for a patient with Stage 4 colon cancer because the doctor had not first ordered a cheaper, less precise scan. In Iowa, where the rate was 31%, Ambetter approved a colonoscopy but denied the bowel preparation required before the procedure. In Kansas, where the rate was 26%, the company stopped covering an anti-inflammatory treatment for Crohn's disease so the patient would try a different drug.

This spring, after his tumor-filled prostate was removed, Atlanta-area drummer Richie Jones learned that cancer remained in his body. Jones, 49, was willing to do anything to get more time with his wife and daughter. His oncologist said the best option was a pencil-sized beam of protons, a treatment that can cost far more than standard radiation but inflicts less damage to the rest of the body. Ambetter denied advance coverage based on a recommendation from an oncologist working with the insurer. Jones's oncologist appealed, but Ambetter denied that appeal too, saying in a letter that Jones did “not have a type of cancer this treatment is used for.” Jones said the different doctor advising Ambetter on the appeal did not specialize in cancer or proton therapy and was an OB-GYN. Insurers have varying policies on whether to cover proton therapy for patients with prostate cancer. In recent years, insurance researchers have found that patients with commercial plans who face a proton-therapy denial for various cancers have a good chance of overturning it on appeal.

Fearful that his cancer would spread more, Jones wrote Ambetter's support department that, “You have the power to allow my child to have her daddy around for years to come.” He contacted local journalists, wrote to the office of U.S. Sen. Jon Ossoff and filed a complaint with Georgia's insurance commissioner. In late June, after Jones reached staffers in both officials' offices, Emory Healthcare called to say his oncologists were ready to begin. Ambetter had reversed the denial.

Records obtained by ProPublica this month included a letter Ambetter sent to Georgia's insurance commissioner's office explaining the rationale for each of its denials. Ambetter had decided two days after Jones filed his complaint to consult clinical guidelines used by America's leading oncologists in determining how to proceed with covering his treatment. The insurer had previously relied on a more restrictive guideline from a contractor that handles its prior-authorization requests for cancer treatment. Over nearly 40 rounds of proton therapy, Jones later stood at Emory's cancer center with family and friends to ring a bell marking the end of treatment. He was in remission and said he could see only then how much his complaint had mattered. Jones asked, “Why wasn’t that their guideline in the first place?” and “Why does it take being a squeaky wheel to have them change their mind?”

Last year, liver-cancer patient Travis Christopher, a 48-year-old artist from metro Atlanta, left his job after becoming too sick to work and lost the insurance that came with it. Christopher bought an Ambetter plan, which covered a specialized treatment in which his oncologist placed a wire of irradiated beads in his liver to target the tumor. His oncologist needed CT scans to determine whether the radiation had worked, but Ambetter denied three prior-authorization requests. After a month of delays, Christopher complained to a state senator, saying he was “stuck waiting until Ambetter decides whether or not I live or die.” The senator referred the complaint to Georgia's insurance commissioner, and Ambetter soon overturned the denial and agreed to cover the care.

ProPublica noted that every day of delay can increase the odds that cancer spreads, but no one could say how much the wait affected Christopher's prognosis. His health worsened during the months that followed. Christopher died this past winter, shortly after his daughter was born.

Felicia, who asked to be identified by her middle name because she feared reprisals and wanted to share sensitive medical information, was a 38-year-old Texan who had spent two years taking three anti-obesity drugs covered by Ambetter, including Ozempic. Those drugs caused insomnia and migraines. In 2023, her doctor prescribed samples of tirzepatide, another GLP-1, and Felicia lost a significant amount of weight over two months without side effects. When her doctor later prescribed Zepbound, the tirzepatide medication that had worked for her, Ambetter declined coverage. Zepbound was the only treatment that made a difference in her years-long effort to manage obesity.

ProPublica reported that health insurers tend to deny requests for GLP-1 drugs that are not listed on their formularies to keep costs down, and that customers can appeal and seek a formulary exception.

Felicia had sent a detailed 2024 appeal to the head of the Fortune 500 company overseeing her health insurance plan and copied the secretary of the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services and the chair of the Federal Trade Commission. Felicia also sought help from consumer advocates. Ambetter CEO Kevin Counihan called her, and the call, which Felicia recorded and shared with ProPublica, included his acknowledgment that her reported experience had been “a tremendous inconvenience and a real pain in the neck.” Felicia told Counihan she hoped that “something gets done right by me and by my health” and said, “I have tried everything in the formulary. I exhausted all options.” Counihan, a veteran health insurance executive who had once overseen the federal ACA marketplace, assured her that “we really want to get you what you” and her doctor “think is the most appropriate medication for you.” He said, “I’m working on it as fast as possible.” On a follow-up call, Counihan said he did not have the authority to help with the fourth medication and told Felicia, “Do what you need to do. Escalate it in any way that you can.”

In September 2024, Felicia's endocrinologist backed her appeal, writing that denying that medication could hinder her progress, worsen her condition and potentially lead to more intensive and costly interventions. The endocrinologist called it the most appropriate and potentially life-saving medication given her other overlapping conditions. In November, the company overseeing the independent review declined to consider the appeal, writing that a medication not on a health plan's approved drug list was outside its review scope.

Felicia has continued struggling with obesity since the Zepbound denial and has experienced worsening symptoms for several other chronic conditions, including persistent migraines. Ambetter also denied prior authorization for numerous migraine medications Felicia's doctors prescribed. Felicia said she averaged nine migraines a month, and medical experts said obesity was a strong risk factor for migraines. By the fall, she had contacted an insurance broker during open enrollment, but the quotes she received for various plans were either much higher than Ambetter's or did not provide access to her doctors; the cost would have been far more than she could possibly afford. She said Ambetter was the only insurance she had ever bought and might be her only viable option.

Two other women obtained coverage after posting about their denials on social media. A Florida mother bought an Ambetter plan after her previous insurer stopped covering autism therapy for her 6-year-old son. The Ambetter plan she chose had covered other children treated by the providers who had treated her son for the previous two years. Ambetter denied the current year's request and paused the boy's care, but company representatives contacted her after her social-media post, said they wanted to address her problem, and the ordered treatment was provided. A Kansas woman said Ambetter denied a drug infusion for her Crohn's disease in 2025 even though the company had covered the infusion for the previous two years and it worked better for her than anything else she had tried. Her social-media post also prompted Ambetter to provide the ordered care.

Four former heads of state insurance departments who reviewed ProPublica's findings said Ambetter's high denial rate was such an outlier that current regulators should examine it further. Mila Kofman, a former Maine insurance superintendent who now oversees the District of Columbia's Affordable Care Act marketplace, called the rate “stunning — and not in a good way.” She said, “This is something insurance regulators need to understand. There’s a huge opportunity for regulators to address the improper use of prior authorization.”

U.S. Rep. Craig Goldman, a Republican from Texas, sponsored a bipartisan bill that would require marketplace plans to disclose more information about their use of prior authorization. Goldman said, “These companies represent these individuals — and yet denying them coverage and not being transparent with what they will be able to cover. They're just not there for the best interest of the people.” After Jones allowed publication of his experience in a report on denials, Democratic Sen. Jon Ossoff said in a statement to ProPublica that he had “heard from too many Georgians who get a cancer diagnosis, and then instead of being free to fight their cancer, they have to fight their insurance company.” Ossoff said, “We need to ban health insurance companies from denying or delaying needed health care.”

In June 2025, following a meeting with U.S. Health and Human Services Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr., Centene and other large insurers promised to reduce the volume of medical services subject to prior authorization. Industry representatives said the collective effort would decrease insurers' use of the process across all their plans by 11% in 2026. Health plans are not required to publish 2026 data until next year. Sarah London, CEO of Centene, said in a statement on the company's website that it was “creating a more predictable and transparent experience” for customers, and Centene has since touted “measurable progress” toward its promise.

ProPublica sent Centene a detailed list of questions about its use of prior authorization and the impact it has on its customers. ProPublica also provided Ambetter with signed releases from customers, including Felicia, waiving their right to privacy so Centene representatives could speak with ProPublica about their specific denials. Centene did not respond to ProPublica's questions, including whether Ambetter's prior-authorization use had gone down this year. In a statement, a spokesperson for Centene, Ambetter's parent company, said Ambetter was “committed to help ensure patients receive safe, appropriate, evidence-based care, and we continue to work to simplify the prior authorization process and reduce administrative burden for members and providers.” Counihan, reached by phone, retired this year and declined to comment because of “contractual commitments” with the company.

Insurance experts, consumer advocates and lawmakers questioned how far the industry's promises went. Former Cigna executive and consumer advocate Wendell Potter wrote that the insurers' commitments relied on “an impressive-sounding number to mislead.” He cited UnitedHealthcare's promise of a 30% reduction in prior-authorization requirements, saying only 2% of covered medical services qualified for the more relaxed requirements. UnitedHealthcare spokesperson Eric Hausman said the insurer had been “transparent” about its efforts and had enacted some of them. Brit Vanneman, a staff attorney for the National Health Law Program, wrote that the commitments “may simply be a strategic attempt to deflect growing public criticism” regarding prior authorization. Vanneman told ProPublica, “The commitments are vague. There’s a lot of fluff.”
