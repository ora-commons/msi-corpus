## Voters approved local care funding in Baltimore and San Antonio

Affordability soared to the top of voters' minds going into the 2026 midterms, and it helped elevate childcare, an issue advocates say has often been on the fringes of mainstream political debate. Candidates who ran on expanding childcare won races up and down the ballot, and voters backed local childcare funding from Baltimore to San Antonio.

The ballot box produced results on both sides of the ledger. Electorates from Baltimore to San Antonio approved measures that would put local tax dollars into childcare initiatives, and similar measures passed in Boulder County, Colo., and Marin County, Calif. But voters in St. Louis, Mo., and Tempe, Ariz., rejected sales tax increases intended to raise additional childcare funding, and childcare advocates say a successful statewide property tax cut in Florida, which is expected to drain local government revenues, will harm programs that support children and families.

New Mexico's Democratic Governor-elect Deb Haaland promised to expand her state's first-of-its-kind universal childcare program, and Georgia's Democratic Governor-elect Keisha Lance Bottoms campaigned on meeting the state's universal pre-K goal in part by allowing casino gambling. Some incoming members of Congress also pressed the issue on the stump, including South Carolina Democrat Annie Andrews, who is set to become the first pediatrician to serve in the Senate.

Childcare surfaced in competitive congressional races as well. In Pennsylvania's 1st Congressional District, incumbent Republican Brian Fitzpatrick and Democratic challenger Bob Harvie both talked about making childcare more affordable. Fitzpatrick co-sponsored a number of childcare bills across five terms in Congress, including the recent Child Care Modernization Act, but ultimately lost the race.

The cost side of the debate is documented. Childcare costs have surged in recent years, with the average annual cost topping $13,000 last year, according to the group Child Care Aware of America, a major financial burden for American families. A poll released by the First Five Years Fund in July found that 38% of parents who paid for childcare listed it as one of their top two financial concerns, behind only housing and groceries.

Politicians took note during the latest election cycle. Data compiled by the Bank Street College of Education show that three quarters of gubernatorial candidates in the midterms offered policy proposals or took positions on childcare, including both Democrats and Republicans. The Campaign for a Family Friendly Economy PAC, which advocates for more affordable caregiving policies, said it spent more than $40 million in competitive races across the country.

"This election in particular really felt different," said Julie Kashen, founder and principal of the initiative Women's Economic Futures. "This felt like a real momentum moment for childcare as an election issue."

Melissa Boteach, chief policy officer of the nonprofit Zero to Three, which advocates for young children and their families, said more people have come to see childcare as not just a personal family struggle but also a systemic social concern. "Twenty years ago, this was more of an outside, on-the-margins issue," Boteach said. "People have realized that this is a collective problem and that only collective action will solve it."

Kashen pointed to earlier federal engagement with childcare, including major national investments during World War II and again during the COVID pandemic, when lockdowns sent the childcare sector into disarray. That recent disruption plus continued political organizing had helped boost the issue in the public consciousness, she said. "What we're seeing now is the momentum over those years," Kashen said.

In Washington, D.C., where childcare had long been a part of the political conversation, "the election took it to the next level," said Ruqiyyah Anbar-Shaheen, director of early childhood systems at DC Action, an advocacy organization focused on kids and families. Democratic Mayor-elect Janeese Lewis George ran on a promise to cap childcare costs at 7% of household income and support the Early Childhood Educator Pay Equity Fund, a municipal program intended to raise the pay of childcare workers that has been the target of proposed funding cuts. "It's the first time we've seen childcare elevated to that level in a very long time in D.C.," Anbar-Shaheen said.

The outlook now runs through the new Democratic majority in Congress. Zero to Three's Boteach said she expects Democrats to push back on what she called "an attack and a retrenchment on child care" from the Trump administration over the past two years, including an overhaul of Head Start that advocates say will weaken the program and attempts to freeze childcare funding to certain states. House Democratic Whip Katherine Clark, who is expected to be the next majority leader, said earlier this year that Democrats would "turn this ship around" if they won back the majority. "This incoming class is definitely going to put the brakes on that," Boteach said, "and to really be doing a lot more oversight and a lot more pushing of a narrative of why we need greater investments in child care and early education."

Democrats say they will also pursue new childcare policies with a particular emphasis on affordability. Senate Minority Leader Chuck Schumer has said childcare will be one of the party's top five priorities come January, and several incoming Democrats have said they want to expand the Child Tax Credit, which helps families with children lower their tax bills. It remains unclear whether those proposals would have the support of President Trump, who has said he wants to work with Democrats but also that he is willing to veto legislation they send to his desk over the next two years.

Kashen said the Trump administration has not advanced the issue. "They have not been childcare champions to date," Kashen said. "That being said, in the past, President Trump has talked about childcare, especially when [his daughter] Ivanka was in the White House back in the day, so I think there's lots of possibilities."
