## Some pay-in-four plans have no interest but can still carry late fees

Black Friday is no longer limited to a single day but remains one of the busiest days of the year for brick-and-mortar stores, where shoppers can still stand in long lines, Nick Schifrin said in a PBS News report. The outlet reported that 90 million Americans have turned to buy now, pay later financing for purchases including sneakers, concert tickets, groceries, utilities and monthly rent. Ariel Nelson of the National Consumer Law Center and Kenneth Mohammed of American Consumer Credit Counseling said the products can leave borrowers facing late fees, interest and subscription charges, while Penny Lee of the industry's trade group said they can provide flexibility.

Stephanie Dahlgren, 55, is a schoolteacher in Madison, Wisconsin, who has used buy now, pay later to pay veterinary bills, groceries, utilities and her phone. “It's the necessities of life that I have been using it for,” she said. Dahlgren said she had never contemplated having to use such financing for those items.

PBS News reported that 54 percent of buy now, pay later customers said they would not be able to make ends meet without the financing. Mya Ferreira relies on the products, especially since her husband was laid off. “It has definitely been essential,” Ferreira said. “Being able to do things that I need to do during these really rough financial times, it's been a lifesaver,” she said.

Buy now, pay later does not describe one uniform product. Penny Lee, who represents the industry's trade group, described a common pay-in-four option that spreads the cost of a product over four equal payments, usually over six weeks. Lee said the pay-in-four product is zero-interest and can be less costly than a credit card with a much higher interest rate. She also said it provides full transparency about fees and allows payment over the course of six weeks without additive fees, while late fees often apply if the balance is not paid in full.

Kenneth Mohammed, who runs client services at the nonprofit American Consumer Credit Counseling, a group whose mission is getting Americans out of debt, said he is seeing more clients arrive with buy now, pay later accounts. He said some of the products make debt harder to manage. The interest rate is built into the monthly payment, unlike a credit card, where paying the balance off early avoids further interest. With buy now, pay later, Mohammed said, early repayment still leaves the interest already built into the financed amount.

PBS News reported that only half of loans from major firms such as Klarna, Affirm and AfterPay are pay-in-four plans, while the non-pay-in-four products are extending their reach. The other half are longer-term loans with interest rates and late fees. Some companies that have offered buy now, pay later now might offer it for rent or through rent-splitting products, according to Ariel Nelson, who studies buy now, pay later at the National Consumer Law Center. A rent-splitting product can divide rent into two installments instead of one big payment, Nelson said. Providers say there is no interest but charge a monthly subscription fee, maybe processing fees and potentially other fees, Nelson said.

Mohammed said missed-payment fees and charges that apply when an automated payment cannot be collected are expensive. Such terms usually appear in the terms and conditions, he said, but consumers rarely read them. The PBS report also noted that buy now, pay later offers at Amazon, Target and Walmart do not prominently trumpet the fees.

Harvard economist David Laibson, who helped develop the idea of present bias, said people place full psychological weight on events today and only half-weight on the future. “Sophisticated firms, sophisticated entities that we interact with are going to find a way to exploit the weaknesses in whatever armor we have,” he said. Laibson used postponing healthier eating, ordering a second beer or pepperoni pizza and delaying exercise to illustrate the tendency to pull enjoyable tasks forward and unpleasant tasks back. Buy now, pay later creates the same choice, he said: “I can have my fund now, and I can put the sacrifice into the future.” He identified payment as the sacrifice.

Nelson said some people using the products might be desperate, citing an emergency in a particular month, gig workers with varying income, or people whose benefit payments do not arrive before the first of the month in time to pay rent. Those circumstances can make the flexibility of buy now, pay later attractive, Nelson said.

Mohammed described an escalation among customers trying to bridge expenses: as the cost of goods rises, some use credit cards, then buy now, pay later, and then payday loans or even title loans. He said customers are doing whatever they need to do to reach the next week or next paycheck. “People are using whatever options they have to buy food, to pay rent, to keep food on the table,” he said.

Lee offered a different account for borrowers who fall behind, describing buy now, pay later as a lifeline. She said borrowers are usually paused from taking out another loan, which she said prevents them from going further into debt. The flexibility lets customers pay according to their paycheck or within their means, Lee said.

Ferreira works at Mohammed's credit-counseling firm but said she is not immune to financial pressure. She described small payments of $20, $30 and $35 as manageable until her balances accumulated to more than $20,000. “What I say to myself is, I need to stop. I have been trying really hard. It's very hard, and it's a very hard cycle to break,” she said.

“I am in debt because of it, and it's a debt that sneaks up on you. Because of the way that our economy is going, my regular income and education wasn't enough,” Dahlgren said. Economics correspondent Paul Solman said the financing's broader appeal reflected economic desperation even more than the human tendency to postpone paying.
