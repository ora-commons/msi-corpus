## Al Gore urges lawmakers to rethink Baaja permitting bill

**Subtype:** fact

- Al Gore said passing the Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act (Baaja) would be a "serious mistake" and urged lawmakers to reconsider.
- The Baaja bill would ease construction of oil and gas pipelines, datacenters, solar and wind projects and transmission lines by scaling back environmental protections and legal challenges.
- Senator Sheldon Whitehouse, a Democrat who helped craft Baaja, said Gore did not speak to him or Senator Martin Heinrich before opposing the bill.
- A final Senate vote on Baaja is expected this week, and the bill would move to the House if it passes.

## Senator who helped craft Baaja says Gore did not consult him before opposing

Gore opposed the legislation at the Cop31 climate summit in Antalya, Turkey, and tied his objection to the political landscape created by last week's midterm elections, in which Democrats recorded sweeping wins.

"I think it would be a serious mistake, particularly in the wake of the astonishing election results last week," Gore said, referencing the Democratic victories.

Gore pointed to two signals about who wants the bill: "The fact that President Trump endorsed it enthusiastically is a clue, the fact that the American Petroleum Institute is giving out free tacos in Washington to support it is also a clue. I hope they reconsider because I think it would be a very, very serious mistake."

The Baaja bill, devised by a group of Republicans and Democrats, passed a procedural vote in the US Senate last week. It would make it easier to build large infrastructure projects in the US, from oil and gas pipelines and datacenters to clean energy components such as solar and wind power and transmission lines, scaling back environmental protections and legal challenges in order to do so. The bill has split Democrats and green groups over whether its trade-offs are worth supporting.

Gore argued that the legislation's effect would depend on how Trump applies it. The president, who has repeatedly attempted to halt clean energy projects, said last week that wind turbines are a "major loser" and that he will prevent any more being built, even as Baaja is under consideration.

"Trump will implement any legislation given to him in a way that continues to redeem his unethical pledge to the fossil fuel industry during his campaign," Gore said, referencing Trump's promise to help oil and gas executives in return for donations before the 2024 election.

"The president of the United States is in charge of implementing whatever legislation is passed and he doesn't play by the rules, he doesn't respect the law and it's not a mystery how he would try to implement this legislation," Gore said. "It would lock in a lot of new pipelines and fossil fuel facilities. And we will have a chance with the new Congress and the new balance of power in the US government to do a much better job."

Whitehouse, a Democratic senator and longtime climate advocate who helped craft the bill, is also at Cop31 and rejected Gore's criticism. "Mr Gore neither spoke to Senator (Martin) Heinrich or me or our teams before he reached that conclusion," Whitehouse said.

Whitehouse said he was working on "a variety of amendments to see what can be done to shore up potential holes in the bill," including provisions that would prevent Trump from arbitrarily blocking clean energy developments. He said criticism of the bill from some Democrats and green groups, who argue that environmental protections are being gutted for a modest reduction in greenhouse gas pollution, was overblown. "There was a huge surge of misinformation that came kind of immediately after the text was released … we have had lots of exaggerated fears," Whitehouse said.

One analysis projects the bill could produce as much as 1.5bn tons of emissions reductions by 2040, and Whitehouse said he hoped amendments would increase that total. He argued that the pace of climate change outweighs concerns about the bill's rollbacks. "Nothing is going to be more devastating to the environment than continuing on our pace of climate change, by like a factor of 100," Whitehouse said.

Earthjustice, which opposes Baaja, has warned that the bill would weaken the legal tools communities use to challenge projects. "The prospect of losing tools to slow down and stop the cruelest projects should be haunting everyone," said Abigail Dillen, president of Earthjustice.

Whitehouse said the energy-development landscape had shifted. "Clean energy development is wildly outpacing fossil fuel development now. In the old days the developers were almost always the bad guys and a mindset developed around that pattern," the senator said. "But that has now shifted and developers are 90% plus likely to be the good guys in the energy space. I think if you have a gigaton scale decrease of emissions available, it's important to grab it while you can."

Proponents also argue that timing matters: if Democrats craft a more renewables-friendly permitting bill after taking control of Congress in January, support from Trump and Republicans will likely wither, leaving no legislation at all to stop wind and solar projects from being bogged down in lengthy reviews. Transmission-line shortages are already slowing new generation from reaching Americans, they note.

A final Senate vote is expected this week, and if it passes, the bill would move to the House. Whitehouse said that when Democrats take control of Congress there will be "far bigger environmental and climate fish to fry than just this bill."

"I think this is step one. We have to keep going because we are failing right now. We are failing to meet the obligations we have," Whitehouse said.
