## Bartomeus: housing lets Sánchez frame race as left-vs-right

Alberto Núñez Feijóo cast Spain's Nov. 29 snap election Monday as a contest to "beat Sánchez," framing the early vote as distinct from previous ballots. "At last, the Spanish people will decide. These aren't elections like the previous ones. This is about Spain beating Sánchez," Feijóo, whose Popular Party holds the most seats in Parliament, said after the announcement. Feijóo aims to take control of the government in the November vote.

Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez called the election after right-wing opposition parties scuttled two emergency decrees in Parliament that included measures to reduce evictions and real estate speculation. The setback sparked wide speculation that Sánchez could consider moving up a national election that would have otherwise taken place next year. In a televised address, Sánchez argued that a broader progressive coalition in Parliament would allow Spaniards to tackle the biggest issues facing the country today. "The time has come, therefore, for the Spanish people to decide what future you want for yourselves and for your country," Sánchez said. The formal dissolution of parliament will need to be approved by the Cabinet, which Sánchez has convened for later Monday.

In recent weeks, Spaniards have demanded solutions from the government over housing affordability, holding protests in cities across the country after public outrage grew over the televised eviction of an 87-year-old woman in Madrid last month.

Oriol Bartomeus, a professor of political science at the Autonomous University of Barcelona, said the housing issue gives Sánchez an opening to recast the contest in left-versus-right terms. "Sánchez is choosing this moment because it is arguably the best time to do so, given that the issue of housing frames the electoral conflict or debate in terms that are highly favorable to (his party's) position," Bartomeus said. "Sánchez can frame the election — or has realized he can frame it — in very classic left-versus-right terms," he said.

Speculation around whether Sánchez would call snap elections centered on the momentum that his party may now have around issues of affordability, while the opposition Popular Party is likely to define the contest around corruption.

Sánchez, 54, has been Spain's prime minister since 2018, making him one of Europe's longest-standing leaders. He has become one of the leading international progressive heads of government thanks to his defense of migration, his diplomatic efforts to defend Palestinians and his criticism of the American and Israeli war against Iran. He has also enjoyed one of the fastest growing economies in the developed world in recent years.

Back home, Sánchez has been hemmed in by a series of legal cases involving his Socialist Party as well as his wife and brother, who both deny wrongdoing. He has also struggled to account for this summer's border crossing by tens of thousands of mostly Moroccans into Spain's North African territory of Ceuta, where a humanitarian crisis remains two months after the initial crisis.

He has led a coalition minority government along with the left-wing junior partner Sumar since 2023. His reliance on several smaller parties to get anything done has hamstrung his legislative ability.

An Associated Press report described Sánchez as "a political escape artist capable of winning elections he looked set to lose."
