## Carney government projects 16-fold computing growth as Alberta proposes siting limits

## Canada positions itself for hyperscale growth

In recent months, Canada has been increasingly pitched as a hub for hyperscale AI data centers even though the number of proposed U.S. AI data centers dwarfs the Canadian total. Canadian industry leaders are sensing an opportunity as Americans have grown increasingly sour on AI data-center development. U.S. voters passed 40 of 41 local ballot initiatives in this month's midterm elections aimed at limiting or preventing AI data-center development. Lucy Hargreaves, CEO of the non-profit policy group Build Canada, wrote on X the day after the vote, "Data centres in Canada looking pretty appealing right now." In an interview with the BBC, Hargreaves said, "Some of the capital that would have been deployed to build data centres in the US is now more available for Canada." Hargreaves said, "The question really is whether or not we want to go for that."

The BBC reported that it went to Arizona before the midterms to understand the U.S. data-center boom.

Proponents argue that Canada's abundant land, natural gas and cold climate make the country uniquely positioned to address problems facing U.S. AI infrastructure, including the proximity of data centers to populated areas, noise pollution and strain on electricity grids. Unlike in the United States, pushback in Canada has been muted at least so far; many projects are going ahead without significant disruption, and lawmakers have stood firm, openly embracing them without major political consequences.

## Government policy frames data centers as strategic infrastructure

Alberta accounts for 90% of all currently proposed Canadian AI data-center sites. The western Canadian province, rich in natural gas deposits, has pitched itself as "North America's destination of choice" for AI data centers and offers investors a "concierge" service to help build projects at "unparalleled speed." Alberta has defended its plans by saying they will create jobs and generate revenue and that sites will be developed "responsibly."

The Carney government, which has made reducing Canada's reliance on the United States a priority, has framed AI data-center development as a matter of national security and sovereignty. It projects that Canada needs to expand its computing capacity 16-fold over the next four years to meet domestic demand. Ontario Premier Doug Ford has said his province needs to move "full steam ahead" with building data centers.

Canada has also released some high-level guidance for data-center development, co-signed by Anthropic, Meta and other large AI companies. The guidance calls on data centers to pay for electricity costs, minimize water use and create long-term benefits for the regions where they operate.

## Two Wonder Valley projects draw different responses

Kevin O'Leary, the Canadian businessman, television personality and ally of Donald Trump, announced plans this year to build two large AI data centers, one in Alberta and one in the U.S. state of Utah. Both are named Wonder Valley. Utah residents organized an opposition campaign that led outgoing state senator Stuart Adams to demand in June that O'Leary scale down the Utah project by 75%, from 40,000 to 13,000 acres. Financing for the Utah project is still underway, and it is unclear when, or whether, shovels will hit the ground.

The proposed Alberta project covers 7,000 acres and 7.5 gigawatts and has received strong backing from the provincial government and Greenview Municipality in north-western Alberta. It is billed as "off-grid," relying on its own power system fueled by natural gas sourced from the region. In April, the Alberta government exempted the project from an environmental impact assessment, arguing it was unnecessary, though it must still obtain permits before construction.

The Alberta project met with only limited opposition at a local town hall earlier this year. Dan Harms told Bloomberg in June that he saw the economic benefits of the Wonder Valley data center and that it was being built "out in the middle of nowhere." In an interview with BBC News, Reeve Ryan Ratzlaf of Greenview Municipality said the project was the "perfect fit" for a rural community that had been looking for an industrial tenant to fill a resource-rich parcel of land for 13 years.

Some community members have expressed concern about the amount of water needed to cool the proposed site annually. The projected requirement is six million cubic metres, about the size of 2,400 Olympic-size swimming pools, and the center would draw water from the nearby Smoky River. Ratzlaf argued the amount was a drop compared with the river's annual flow and said he believed the region's economic benefits outweighed potential risks. "It's a giant warehouse with computer equipment inside," he said, arguing that the project was less risky than petrochemical plants that could have been built in its place.

Paul Palandjian, the CEO overseeing both O'Leary projects, said the two developments were proceeding at an "equal" pace. He acknowledged that data centers had become more of a "hot topic" in the United States than in Alberta.

## Signs of public anger in Alberta

There are signs that public anger toward AI data centers in Alberta has grown in recent months. Residents have voiced objections at recent public information sessions on provincial AI data-center plans. At an August town hall reported by the Canadian Press, held shortly after Alberta announced that Meta would build a C$13bn ($9bn; £6.8bn) AI data center in the province, one resident asked, "If no one wants this, why are you pushing forward with this?"

Sturgeon Lake Cree Nation has recently sued the Alberta government over its decision to override the environmental assessment for O'Leary's Wonder Valley project, calling the decision "irrational" in court filings. This week, Alberta proposed legislation that would restrict where data centers could be built in a bid to ease some concerns. Elsewhere in Canada, there are also signs that public cooperation may be fading. On Saturday, hundreds of protesters gathered in Nova Scotia to oppose a proposed AI data center that will be used by technology giant Anthropic.

## Experts warn that backlash could reach Canada

Experts say the U.S. backlash offers Canada a cautionary tale as the country tries to capitalize on the AI boom. Sean Mullin, a senior fellow at the University of Toronto and former adviser to Canada's prime minister on the national sovereign AI compute strategy, said, "AI is generally not very popular. This is both true in Canada and the United States." He said, "I think we could experience some of the same backlashes if we're not thoughtful about it." Mullin said one reason the backlash had been less severe in Canada was simply due to scope and timing. "There has been less of these projects happening, and as a result, they've attracted less attention," he said.

If politicians do not tread carefully, Bek MacIntosh, an Alberta resident who fought a proposed hyperscale data center in her community, said she believed public discontent could translate into angry votes, like those seen in the United States. MacIntosh successfully halted a data center in Olds, Alberta, after an eight-month battle over its scale, scope and proximity to her home. She said she was not opposed to all AI infrastructure and believed Canada had the resources and landscape to build it responsibly. She feared Alberta had opened the door to "the most reckless of reckless developers" and warned that voters might have a "knee-jerk reaction" if provinces did not proceed carefully. "And I don't think any political party in Canada is prepared for it," she said.

The BBC report also includes a standalone question: "We all need data, but do we need the largest data centre in Europe here?"
