## Farm-attack claims sit beside broader evidence of violent crime and inequality

White South Africans who left as apartheid ended or in later decades are returning to South Africa, creating a reverse migration that PBS NewsHour documented in Gauteng Province. Returnees described more affordable housing, childcare, household help and health care, along with the comfort of a familiar culture, as reasons to rebuild their lives. The reporting did not portray the move as evidence that the country's crime, inequality and unemployment problems had disappeared.

The Nov. 16, 2026, report opened with at least 17 people killed in two separate shootings over the weekend, a reminder of what PBS described as a nation with one of the world's highest murder rates. It placed the migration trend against a policy fight over white South African refugees. President Donald Trump has long alleged that violence has targeted South Africa's white minority, and his administration carved out an exception for white South Africans to a near-blanket ban on refugee admissions. Of the just over 16,000 refugees admitted through the end of September, all but five were from South Africa.

Agricultural producer Nico Struwig described the June 1 break-in and said, "The next moment, the three guys stands there with pistols against my head." When the intruders found little to steal, they forced him to take them to his brother Steve's house next door. Struwig said they opened the door and shot Steve as he lay on the floor. One man continued firing, visible bullet holes marked where the bullets went through, and Struwig said, "I get like a blank." Still in shock over the loss, Struwig receives regular check-ins from neighbors who patrol a sprawling swathe of farmland outside Pretoria, trying to fill in for a scant police presence.

Struwig and his family speak Afrikaans. PBS described Afrikaners as descendants of Dutch settlers who came in the 17th century and as part of a white minority that ruled South Africa under strict racial segregation laws until democratic elections in 1994 brought majority rule. PBS identified Tucker Carlson as a conservative commentator. He said, "This is genocide. I mean, that's what that is. It's targeting a group of people." PBS reported that right-wing media outlets, including an episode from "The Tucker Carlson Show" the previous year, had for years called attention to attacks on white South African farms. PBS said Trump had embraced the issue, most prominently in a tense exchange with South Africa's president at the White House earlier in 2026. Trump said of the victims, "These are all people that recently got killed."

South Africa's government rejects the allegation. Struwig also rejected the idea that violence is uniquely directed at white farmers, saying Black farmers have the same problem. He said some attacks involved perpetrators seeking wealth or money, while others involved weapons.

PBS described AfriForum as a right-leaning Afrikaner civic group and said the group was primarily responsible for bringing Washington's attention to the farm attacks. Ernst Van Zyl, who spoke for AfriForum, said no when asked whether the violence constituted a white genocide. Van Zyl said the organization holds press conferences. He nevertheless said, "At the same time, we also say that it is a serious, unique crime in South Africa. It's undeniable that many foreign murders are motivated by a hatred, not just robbery." Van Zyl attributed some violence to Julius Malema. PBS described Malema as a far-left politician who had led crowds chanting "Kill the boar," a term often used to describe Afrikaners. Van Zyl also raised discrimination in hiring and the prospect of land being seized without compensation.

Critics of AfriForum said those claims were exaggerated and pointed to broader inequalities. White South Africans make up about 7% of the population, but the government's last land audit found they owned nearly three-quarters of the farmland owned by individuals. White unemployment stands at about 9%, compared with 37% for Black South Africans. The critics said poverty and inequality help fuel South Africa's high rate of violent crime and affect everyone.

Nicky Falkof, a professor of media studies at the University of the Witwatersrand in Johannesburg, put the criticism more directly: "People who suffer the most from this sort of thing tend to be poor and Black." She said, "So, framing violence perpetrated against white people as some sort of exceptional thing that happens to white people is not only untrue. It's also incredibly self-serving." AfriForum counted 29 murders in 2025, down from 55 in 2021. By comparison, South Africa recorded 23,642 murders from January through December 2025, averaging 65 each day. Since July, Falkof said, the bodies of a dozen Black women had been found in the Johannesburg area. She asked whether all Black women had no option but to leave the country because of the violence they faced.

Van Zyl said AfriForum does not advocate emigration. He called President Trump's refugee program good but said the organization would remain focused on building a future in South Africa for those who choose to stay. That position sits alongside the reverse migration PBS reported: some white South Africans are entering the country from abroad while others who left around the end of apartheid remain overseas.

Returnee Natasha Robson-Lovato was 12 when her family left South Africa in 1994, the year apartheid ended. Her father, engineer Howard Robson, found work in America. He said it was a good career move, but "uncertainty here was a factor." Robson described the couple of years before 1994 as "fairly tumultuous," citing protests, school tours and worry that buses traveling along the road would be stopped. Cecile Robson said the family wanted to give the children opportunities they did not think might be available in South Africa.

Three decades later, after Robson-Lovato built a life and business in Seattle, she helped pull the family home, including her American husband, Jason Lovato. Robson-Lovato said: "My history was here. And I needed to spend time on that land. And so that became a big draw for me. There's a sense of home here that I didn't have in the United States that I didn't know I was missing when I was there." They now farm pecans in a large family compound that encloses their home and those of her brother and her parents.

Arts writer Petra Mason, a fifth-generation white South African who returned after two decades in New York and Miami, said culture was another major draw: "South Africa is the type of country that is so complex and so interesting. The draw to be part of the story is enormous." At a Jazz Sunday at a nearby sculpture park, Mason noted that the surrounding contemporary artworks were mostly by South Africans.

Returnees more broadly cited a combination of cost of living and quality of life. Housing, childcare, household help and health care were more affordable in South Africa, while cultural familiarity added to its appeal. Jason Lovato said he had worried more about Natasha walking to Seattle by herself than about her going to Johannesburg. Petra Mason said she was hearing more people consider South Africa safe: "What I'm hearing a lot more about people are considering South Africa a safe place to be with all that's happening in the rest of the world."

The housing market provides another sign of the return. Rory O'Hagan, a Johannesburg luxury real estate agent with Chas Everitt International Property Group, said business had risen sharply from returning South Africans since 2023. His colleague Meg Wilson, who recently returned from Panama and was apartment hunting, said a flat she had viewed in London cost just over 400,000 pounds and was a tiny studio. O'Hagan pointed out space for a double-door refrigerator. Wilson said an airy three-bedroom model costing about $400,000 was a fraction of what most returnees from Western countries were used to.

O'Hagan said: "We're seeing a lot of younger families. There's a cross-section of people that are coming into South Africa. We talk about the brain regain." There are no reliable government figures showing how many white South Africans are returning, PBS said. One proxy is a Facebook group intended for returnees: when Wilson first joined, it had just under 2,000 members three years earlier. The group now has 27,000 members.

PBS reported that, for those who came back, South Africa's problems were real but they did not see a country they needed to flee. For the people PBS interviewed, the decision reflected a mix of affordability, cultural ties and perceived safety rather than a simple flight from violence. Fred de Sam Lazaro reported from Gauteng Province, South Africa, in partnership with the Under-Told Stories Project at Santa Clara University.
