## Alabama pipeline explosion renews scrutiny of Southern Natural Gas expansion

**Subtype:** fact

- The Pipeline and Hazardous Materials Safety Administration deployed staff to investigate a Southern Natural Gas Company pipeline explosion in rural Sumter County, Alabama.
- Kinder Morgan said the failure was a force majeure incident involving a natural gas release and fire, while the cause remained undetermined Monday afternoon.
- Southern Natural Gas Company plans to expand the affected pipeline through the South System Expansion 4 project despite opposition from community organizations in Alabama.
- Federal regulators ordered Southern Natural Gas Company to take corrective actions after other pipeline explosions in 2022 and 2026, records show.
- Kinder Morgan and local officials reported no injuries from the Sumter County explosion.
- Sumter County officials said some nearby homes were temporarily evacuated.

## PHMSA investigates cause after temporary home evacuations

A Southern Natural Gas Company pipeline segment exploded at approximately 11:27 p.m. Saturday in an empty field near the unincorporated community of Ward, about eight miles east of the Mississippi state line in rural Sumter County, Alabama’s Black Belt. The blast produced a fireball that rose hundreds of feet and was visible from more than 50 miles away. Local meteorologists also said the explosion was significant enough to appear on weather radar. Kinder Morgan and local officials reported no injuries, and local officials said some homes near the site were temporarily evacuated.

Mayor Renee Ward Pringle of nearby York, population 2,400, said she had heard from many residents asking about the seemingly apocalyptic blaze visible on the horizon. In a social media post, Pringle wrote, “We understand the concerns of our citizens, especially with the light from the flames being visible from York.” Pringle added, “While the preliminary information is encouraging, we do not want to make any premature assurances regarding public safety.”

Southern Natural Gas Company is jointly owned by Kinder Morgan and Southern Company and operated by Kinder Morgan. The company operates about 7,000 miles of pipelines distributing fossil gas throughout the Southeast, including gas for major gas-fired power plants operated by Southern Company and its Alabama Power, Georgia Power and Mississippi Power subsidiaries.

Kinder Morgan described the explosion as a force majeure incident, meaning the company claimed it was caused by circumstances beyond human control. The company characterized the event as “a natural gas release and fire on a pipeline segment.” The Pipeline and Hazardous Materials Safety Administration confirmed that staff had been deployed to the pipeline failure site and that its investigation was ongoing. No further information on the cause had been released as of Monday afternoon.

Federal regulators issued orders forcing Southern Natural Gas Company to take corrective actions following explosions along other sections of the pipeline in 2022 and 2026, records show. Still, Southern Natural Gas Company plans to expand the pipeline at issue as part of the multi-billion South System Expansion 4 project. The expansion would increase pipeline capacity by about 1.3 billion cubic feet per day—enough methane to fill nearly 15,000 Olympic-sized swimming pools.

Portia Shepherd, executive director of the environmental justice nonprofit Blackbelt Women Rising, called the explosion a warning shot. “This could have happened in any community along this pipeline,” Shepherd said. “These explosions are happening again and again, and they want to expand.” Shepherd and other community leaders across Alabama’s Black Belt oppose the expansion, which they have said will lead to increased risks for communities already suffering.

In October, attorneys for the Southern Environmental Law Center filed a lawsuit on behalf of impacted landowners and nine nonprofits across the South, including Blackbelt Women Rising. The lawsuit asks a federal court to review and set aside a Federal Energy Regulatory Commission ruling that authorized the major methane pipeline expansion across Mississippi, Alabama and Georgia.

Megan Gibson, an attorney for the Southern Environmental Law Center, said Saturday’s explosion was a “stark reminder that the risks of building another pipeline alongside aging, existing pipelines are very real for the families and communities living along these routes.” Gibson called on the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission to act immediately on safety concerns and to order a stop to any and all construction activities. She also asked the commission to fully examine and address the risks in partnership with the Pipeline and Hazardous Materials Safety Administration.

The explosion has also raised concerns about federal permitting legislation that would cut red tape for major energy infrastructure projects like South System Expansion 4. In a statement after the explosion, the Alabama Rivers Alliance said, “The Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act, or BAAJA, will fast track pipelines like this one, hindering our ability to have a voice in the process and putting more Alabama communities in immediate danger.”

Shepherd said she was grateful that no one was injured, but residents in Sumter County and across the Black Belt with whom she had spoken were concerned that no comprehensive safety plans existed for incidents like the explosion. Even where limited planning was in place, Shepherd said companies and public officials did not do a good job informing the public about what people should do in an emergency.

“Where is the plan that these communities can actually see?” Shepherd asked. “Where are the safety measures?”

Shepherd said she did not believe companies like Kinder Morgan have communities’ best interests at heart. “These companies’ executives need to look at these communities as places where they would actually live, because I think they’d be doing a better job if these risks were in their community,” Shepherd said. She added: “But the reality is these discussions don’t come up in their community, because they don’t let companies do stuff like this in their communities. It’s always us. We’re the ones who get dumped on.”
