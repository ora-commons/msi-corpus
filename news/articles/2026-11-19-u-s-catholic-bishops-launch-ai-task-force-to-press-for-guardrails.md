## Conference will not invest in AI companies that fail dignity safeguards

At the U.S. Conference of Catholic Bishops’ annual fall gathering in Baltimore on Wednesday, Bishop Oscar Cantú said the conference’s new AI task force would help lobby lawmakers for “clear guardrails on the development and use of AI.” The task force will continue monitoring labor impacts and advocating for workers, and it hopes to become a clearinghouse on best practices and AI literacy, with particular concern about young people’s use of the technology.

Bishop Oscar Cantú of San Jose, California, chairs the new AI task force from his Silicon Valley diocese. The group focuses on preserving human dignity, and Cantú described the U.S. church as well-positioned to contribute to the debate. “The Church possesses something that Silicon Valley cannot engineer: over 2,000 years of wisdom on the human person, on truth, and on the common good,” he said.

Cantú also said: “The Church’s contribution is to insist that every human being possesses inherent dignity that no algorithm can measure and no market can price.”

The task force’s stated work includes monitoring how AI affects workers and advocating for them. Cantú said the group will continue monitoring labor impacts and advocating for workers. It also hopes to become a clearinghouse on best practices and AI literacy, and the bishops are particularly concerned about young people’s use of AI.

The bishops have been meeting with technology company executives and political leaders about AI, much of which is developed in the United States. Company executives are increasingly seeking input from religious leaders as they warn about AI’s destructive potential and debate the need to develop morally good models.

The initiative follows Pope Leo, who has described AI as humanity’s biggest challenge today. His first encyclical, “Magnifica Humanitas,” or “Magnificent Humanity,” called for robust AI regulation.

The bishops also approved updates to their investing guidelines, which are used by the conference and other Catholic institutions and investors. The revised guidelines say the conference “will not invest in companies that design, develop or manufacture AI technologies that fail to safeguard the dignity of the human person.”

As part of the AI investment updates, the USCCB said it will encourage companies to protect children from online exploitation and offer “support for workers displaced by automation.”

Archbishop Paul Coakley of Oklahoma City, the president of the USCCB, spoke about AI at a news conference Tuesday. Artificial intelligence is “a faint reflection of human intelligence, which is created by God. We’re created in God’s image and likeness; it’s ours to guide and correct,” he said.

Charles Camosy, a bioethics professor at the Catholic University of America, serves as one of three academic consultants to the task force. Camosy said in an email, “I mostly bring a set of concerns related to healthcare and especially keeping human beings in the center of healthcare relationships, even as we use so-called artificial intelligence to improve outcomes.”

According to the Catholic Health Association, more than one in seven patients in the United States are treated each day at Catholic hospitals.

Cantú also acknowledged the bishops’ limited technical background. “If they’re asking what do bishops know about technology, they’re right in saying we don’t know much,” he said in a recent video produced by the conference. “But we’re learning quickly.”

The conference’s stated agenda now spans safeguards on AI development and use, labor concerns, child protection and investment standards, along with the task force’s hope to become a clearinghouse on best practices and AI literacy. Those measures respond to the human-dignity concerns Cantú described and follow Pope Leo’s call for robust AI regulation.
