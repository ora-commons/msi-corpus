## Local investigators called death suicide; FBI later ruled homicide

A federal jury in Boston convicted former police detective Matthew Farwell on Tuesday of murdering Sandra Birchmore, a 23-year-old pregnant Massachusetts woman, and the conviction carries a mandatory life sentence. Farwell, 40, was found guilty of strangling Birchmore on Feb. 1, 2021, 12 days after a tipster called the suburban Boston police department where he worked and reported that the two had an improper relationship. Birchmore had first met Farwell as a child while taking part in a Stoughton Police Department youth program, and the two later had a tumultuous relationship.

Prosecutors said Farwell killed Birchmore to keep her from revealing that he had sexually abused her when she was 15. The FBI's investigation uncovered about 40,000 texts between Farwell and Birchmore, including some in which they reminisced about having sex when he was 27 and she was 15 — too young to consent under Massachusetts law.

The texts showed how the relationship grew contentious as Birchmore pressed Farwell to get her pregnant and threatened to tell his wife about the affair. Texts from late 2020, after Birchmore was pregnant, showed the two arguing over what role, if any, Farwell would play in the child's life. A paternity test after Birchmore's death revealed a different man was the father.

Prosecutors said Farwell staged the crime scene to make her death look like a suicide, and that the ruse nearly worked. Birchmore was found slumped on her apartment floor with a strap around her neck. Massachusetts State Police officers and investigators, some with personal connections to Farwell, quickly concluded Birchmore's death was a suicide. The state medical examiner, relying partly on information from police, initially ruled the death a suicide; the office has since changed the official manner of death to "undetermined."

Federal investigators concluded the local probe into Birchmore's death was rushed and compromised. Assistant U.S. Attorney Brian Fogarty told jurors that one investigator saw nothing suspicious in Birchmore's apartment and concluded the case was that of a "troubled girl who killed herself."

The FBI began looking into Birchmore's death in 2023, partly at the urging of a former deputy chief at the Stoughton Police Department who had led an internal affairs investigation into Farwell based on the tip the department received before Birchmore's death.

Federal investigators said Farwell was interviewed for just 15 minutes in a parking lot. Investigators did not take notes, examine him for injuries or sample his DNA, and police looked at Farwell's phone only after he had deleted texts with Birchmore.

Federal prosecutors harshly criticized the initial police investigation at Farwell's trial, saying officers did not follow leads and closed the case too quickly. "Justice requires persistence, not deference, not convenience, and certainly not the protection of those who wear the badge and misuse their authority," said Ted Docks, the special agent in charge of the FBI's Boston office, at a news conference after the verdict. "No badge, no title, no position places anyone above the law."

At trial, a digital forensics expert testified that Birchmore's phone stopped moving three minutes before the lobby security camera recorded Farwell leaving her building on the night of her death. Dr. William Smock, an expert hired by prosecutors, testified that Birchmore's injuries indicated she was strangled with a duffel bag strap and that she struggled before dying.

Dr. Thomas Andrew, the former chief medical examiner in New Hampshire and a defense expert, dismissed Smock's determination that it was a homicide. Farwell's lawyers acknowledged that the married detective had an affair with Birchmore but said it began when she was 16, making her old enough to consent under Massachusetts law. They maintained that he did not kill her and argued that Birchmore had threatened suicide before and that she killed herself after he went to her apartment to break up with her.

The nearly monthlong trial, with about 50 witnesses and 500 exhibits, unfolded in federal court in Boston, partly because local prosecutors never brought criminal charges.

Birchmore's cousin, Ray Cosgrove, said the family was grateful to those who pursued answers but emphasized that no verdict could bring back Birchmore or her unborn son. "Our family knew the person. The public knew the case," Cosgrove said. "Sandra mattered before anyone outside her family knew her name."
