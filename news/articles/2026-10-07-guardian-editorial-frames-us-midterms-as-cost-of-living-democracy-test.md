## Editorial cites nine-point Democratic generic ballot lead

The Guardian's editorial board published a piece on October 7 framing the 2026 US midterm elections as both a referendum on President Donald Trump's handling of the cost of living and a test of American democratic institutions. The editorial cited surveys showing Democrats leading the generic ballot — which measures party preference rather than candidate preference — by about nine points, with the party forecast to gain a clear House majority.

The Guardian editorial board's framing comes less than a month before the November midterm elections, with both parties making their closing arguments to voters on the cost of living, immigration, and the Trump administration's exercise of executive power. The board said the outcome would be "critical to the defence of American democracy — or its continued erosion."

The board opened by writing that "for once, Democrats and Donald Trump might be in agreement," with the historically unpopular president instructing supporters to "pretend I'm on the ticket." Midterms are in large part a referendum on the incumbent, the board said, and Trump's "war on Iran" and its impact on the cost of living have boosted support for Democrats. With less than a month to go, the board wrote, Democrats have reason to be upbeat — but the party has a "mountain to climb" after the devastating defeats of 2024 and the gerrymandering of Republicans.

The board said Democrats were forecast a clear majority in the House, and that the more difficult question was whether they could also retake the Senate. That would require flipping four seats, with 35 Senate races considered in play. The board said Democrats were prospering in red states from Texas to Alaska, citing Kansas — which Trump carried by 16 points and which has not elected a Democratic senator since 1932 — as a "neck and neck" race.

The editorial attributed Democratic progress in specific races to standout candidates running against unpopular opponents. It pointed to the Texas Senate race, where Democratic candidate James Talarico, a Christian pastor and economic populist, has led Republican Ken Paxton. The board said Paxton is viewed unfavorably by a third of his own party's supporters.

The board offered a more cautious reading alongside the Democratic leads. It said voters were being "turned off the Republicans rather than drawn towards" their rivals, and that pundits described the political environment as a "red undertow" rather than a "blue wave." The board said the picture was "extremely patchy" — Democrats were struggling in some states where they had initially been competitive, such as Maine. The board also noted that Republicans had booked about $700 million in advertising since the start of September, a spending advantage the board described as now "materialising."

The board discussed Trump's role in the campaign, saying he has fared better than his party in elections, was hitting the campaign trail, and may make sure his base turns out. It said candidates — including those who benefited from his endorsement — were increasingly distancing themselves from the president because he alienates other voters. The board wrote that Trump "is visibly more energised by his construction projects than how his voters can pay their bills," and it described his war on Iran as "not only reckless and ineffectual" and as "pushing up prices."

On voter attitudes, the board said voters are now more likely to identify Republicans than Democrats as "elitist." It said Latino voters who swung to Trump are changing their minds, with Immigration and Customs Enforcement excesses contributing.

On democratic institutions, the editorial said the Supreme Court had rejected Trump's effort to restrict mail-in voting — which the board said is used by almost a third of US voters — and that Trump continues to "smear" the practice. The board said Trump had "outrageously" used taxpayer-funded adverts to laud his presidency, and that he "cannot be expected to respect the midterm results." It said Trump rejects "the norms and restraints that his predecessors recognised," relying increasingly on executive orders — "seeking to avoid checks on issues ranging from war to vaccines."

The board concluded that while most votes would be driven by economic stress rather than concern about democratic institutions, the outcome would be "critical to the defence of American democracy — or its continued erosion." It said a Democratic takeover of both chambers would return legislative control and investigative powers to the opposition.
