## Trump administration asks Congress for $50.1 billion in aviation upgrades

The Federal Aviation Administration said Tuesday that it had paused new installations of Juniper routers and disabled the functionality of all existing Juniper routers on Friday. FAA spokesperson Ian Gregor said the agency took the step after technicians linked the network problems to a new router in El Dorado, Arkansas. L3Harris installed the router.

Last week's nationwide ground stop grounded all planes and disrupted operations at the nation's airports. The FAA has also said another router, installed three weeks earlier in Amarillo, Texas, malfunctioned. The system became overloaded while the federal contractor was replacing outdated copper wires with fiber optic lines across the country.

Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy said the ground stop was needed after the new L3Harris-installed El Dorado router resulted in a cascade of intermittent communications and radar signals at 17 of the 22 regional air traffic control centers. Those centers direct aircraft cruising at high altitudes.

The FAA said "excessive data" was flooding the system "at a rate of 1,000 connection attempts every 4 to 5 seconds." According to the agency, the deluge created instability with "aircraft communications and radar feeds."

While it investigates the cause, the FAA is relying on older Cisco routers that have been in place for 25 years to keep the network operating. The routers that malfunctioned last week are being tested in a lab at L3Harris' headquarters.

Hewlett Packard Enterprise, which owns Juniper, told ABC News that the company believed the routers were working properly when the ground stop occurred. The company did not immediately respond to inquiries from The Associated Press. It said: "Based on what we know today, the device functioned as it was configured to by the installer. It did not fail or act improperly."

Democrats on the Senate Commerce Committee are demanding clear answers about what went wrong. In a letter to Duffy, the group wrote: "Identifying the device that failed does not establish why it failed. FAA must investigate further to determine the root cause and report to Congress on how it intends to prevent future issues."

Democrats on the Senate Commerce Committee also said they believed only 400 of more than 3,600 new routers had been installed. The estimate places the scope of the paused installation program alongside the congressional demands for an explanation.

Upgrading the nation's air transportation system has received significant congressional funding in recent years, but the problems with aging equipment at the FAA date back decades and past modernization efforts have come up short. Duffy has pushed Congress for billions more dollars to upgrade a system he said had been neglected for decades, saying again Monday that much more funding was needed.

The Trump administration sent key lawmakers a letter Tuesday outlining a $50.1 billion request to modernize the air traffic control system and make other improvements at airports. The request calls for $10.1 billion for updating the nation's air traffic control system. Similar amounts would go toward airport infrastructure, replacing or refurbishing terminals at high-impact hubs, constructing five new traffic control centers and improving or replacing up to 140 FAA-owned air traffic control towers.

The letter, obtained by The Associated Press, says: "Congressional approval of these funds will equip the FAA to deliver to the American people a brand-new air traffic control system that is safer, smarter, faster, and more efficient."

Congress approved $12.5 billion last year to modernize much of the key infrastructure that more than 45,000 flights a day rely on to reach their destinations safely. The funds were earmarked for replacing outdated technologies such as copper wires, floppy discs and radars built in the 1980s. Much of the money will also go toward runway upgrades and other construction projects.

President Donald Trump has blamed old technology and Democrats for the nationwide grounding of all planes and the disruption at the nation's airports. The FAA has been working to modernize the air traffic control system for decades, including before the first Trump administration, and past efforts have come up short.

As of Tuesday, the FAA had not established why the routers malfunctioned. Its immediate response was to stop installing Juniper equipment, disable the existing Juniper functions and continue operating the network with older Cisco routers while the malfunctioned routers undergo laboratory testing.
