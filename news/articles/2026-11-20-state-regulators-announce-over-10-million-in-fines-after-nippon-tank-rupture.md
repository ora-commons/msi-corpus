## State regulators announce over $10 million in fines after Nippon tank rupture

**Subtype:** fact

- Washington Labor and Industries investigators said Nippon Dynawave Packaging Co. kept tank G-10 operating while the company knew it was not safe, contributing to the deaths of 11 workers.
- State investigators said Nippon Dynawave failed to inspect or address deficiencies in 13 other tanks at the Longview mill.
- Washington state regulators said Nippon Dynawave Packaging Co. faces more than $10 million in fines, including the largest employer safety fine in state history.
- Washington Labor and Industries will refer the case to prosecutors to consider criminal charges, Labor and Industries Director Joel Sacks said.
- The state Ecology department issued 34 violations affecting air and water and is seeking $961,000 in penalties and emergency-response costs.

## Referral to prosecutors to consider criminal charges

Washington state regulators announced more than $10 million in state fines against Nippon Dynawave Packaging Co. on Friday after state investigators said the company kept the ruptured tank operating when it was known to be unsafe. Labor and Industries Director Joel Sacks said the agency will refer the case to prosecutors to consider criminal charges. The May 26 rupture at the company's Longview pulp mill killed 11 workers and injured 12 others.

State investigators said Nippon Dynawave failed to take appropriate action to address the ruptured tank's condition in violation of state law, and similarly failed to inspect or address deficiencies in 13 other tanks at the site. The more than $10 million in state fines include the largest employer safety fine in state history.

“Without repair or replacement, the tank walls were a ticking clock,” state Labor and Industries compliance manager Dan Pitts said during a press conference in Longview announcing the findings. “It was a question of time, and time ran out.”

“Nippon's decision to keep G-10 operating when they knew it was not safe contributed to the death of 11 workers,” Sacks said. “Because of that, we will be referring this case to prosecutors to consider criminal charges.”

The state Ecology department announced 34 violations that affected air and water and said it is seeking $961,000 in penalties and costs for responding to the emergency. Separately, after the May tank collapse, the state's Department of Ecology filed a violation notice against Nippon Dynawave listing 35 citations.

The state investigation mirrored an August report by the U.S. Chemical Safety Board, which revealed that inspectors hired by Nippon Dynawave in 2025 found the tank that ruptured was “not fit for continued service” because of deteriorating thickness in the tank walls.

Nippon Dynawave did not immediately respond to an email seeking comment about the state's findings. In an email Wednesday, the company said it had remained engaged with the government agencies investigating the collapse and that the mill had hosted hundreds of visits by regulators and investigators.

“The full deconstruction, forensic examination and technical analysis needed to determine the root cause have not yet been completed,” the company said. The tank is currently being deconstructed, with great care being taken to log and retain portions of the tank to determine the root cause, the company said.

Besides the 11 workers killed, 12 were injured on May 26 when the tank holding more than 900,000 gallons (3.4 million liters) of “white liquor,” a highly corrosive chemical used in paper manufacturing, ruptured. The flood of caustic material was powerful enough to blow out building walls and overturn pickup trucks at the mill.

An Associated Press review of inspections and safety complaints at the plant found that Labor and Industries had investigated more than a dozen safety complaints since 2018 and issued almost $7,000 in fines for violations. Ecology had issued 54 environmental violation notices against Nippon Dynawave since 2016 and fined the company $41,500 for eight serious offenses.

More than $2.4 million in workers' compensation claims have been paid for 169 employees injured on the job since 2015, the agency reported, including claims for injuries related to exposure to caustic substances.

Among the workers killed were brothers Tyler and Brad Covington. Their mother, Sheri Lynn King, has filed a wrongful-death lawsuit against Nippon Dynawave, saying her sons were burned and crushed by the white liquor and debris. The lawsuit claims the company failed to maintain and inspect equipment, failed to properly train and supervise employees and failed to implement safety policies and emergency response procedures.

The state findings announced Friday supply what that earlier coverage lacked: an enforcement account of the ruptured tank and 13 others, more than $10 million in fines and a referral to prosecutors to consider criminal charges.
