## State parks increase outreach as El Niño intensifies longstanding coastal hazards

Four young people have died at Panther State Beach in the past six months, and 24-year-old Se Nah Park remains missing after waves carried her into the ocean. The beach lies among coastal coves between Santa Cruz and Half Moon Bay, opening onto the Pacific. Shale cliffs rise 50 feet above small pocket beaches pummeled by large waves, drawing visitors who come to take photographs, build fires and spend time along the shore.

One of the four deaths followed a fall from a bluff. Another victim was knocked off the rocks and later died in a hospital. Two others were swept off the beach while napping. Park went to a cove with friends to build a bonfire and sing songs. After dark, she tried to cross the beach through a large “keyhole” archway the beach is known for, but powerful waves knocked her down and carried her out to sea. Her purse washed ashore this week, but her body remains missing.

Her father, Kwanghyon Park, asked people in the area to continue searching. “Anyone who’s hiking or in the area, please keep your eyes open for our beloved Senah,” Park told a local reporter. “Just pray for us.”

CalFire is the agency tasked with responding to emergencies at Panther Beach, while California State Parks manages the area. CalFire public information officer Michael Horn said, “People see the beauty and want to enjoy the beauty, but don’t understand the dangers that the beauty hides.” California State Parks operations superintendent Scott Sipes, who leads operations for the Santa Cruz division, said signs at access points and the highway parking area warn about rip currents, cold water, high tides and the risk of becoming stranded.

Gary Griggs, a geology professor at the University of California, Santa Cruz, has taken students to study the cliffs for more than five decades. He described the formation as one of the largest exposed sedimentary intrusions on the planet. Cracks and fractures have created caves, tunnels and arches throughout the cliffs.

Griggs also attributed the beach’s growing popularity partly to a paved parking area, new bathrooms and a coastal bike path. The coast is 45 minutes from Silicon Valley cities including San Jose and Palo Alto, attracting a younger, outdoorsy crowd. “It’s one of these few beaches you can actually park your car and walk down to in two minutes, whereas in some of the similar places, you have to walk 20 minutes to get to the beach,” Griggs said. “It looks like a welcoming place.”

High tides create separate hazards by bringing waves directly to the cliffs and erasing the sand, particularly during winter. Over the past year, stronger storms and higher tides associated with El Niño have created new dangers. This year, a super El Niño pushed tides to record-breaking levels and churned powerful surf. “El Niño kind of raises everything,” Griggs said. “Independent of people, we’re still gonna get high tides; we’re still gonna get big waves. I think adding El Niño just amplifies the impacts.”

Griggs described El Niño as an amplifier rather than the source of the beach’s underlying dangers. Experts said El Niño is not solely to blame; rather, the conditions have amplified existing dangers that have long existed at the beach. CalFire’s Horn said those events would happen even without the impacts of higher seas and higher tides. “I would honestly say my opinion is that this is 100% not El Niño-related,” he said, “because we get these kinds of incidents there all the time.” A local reporter’s analysis found at least 14 deaths on beaches in the region since 2021.

Some visitors do not always heed posted warnings, and others have called for additional measures. The father of one victim suggested displaying a death count at the trailhead. Horn said, “The reality is, they can’t be putting up barbed wire around every bit of California coastline,” adding, “When it starts getting really popular due to social media or people sharing the information about how beautiful an area is, even if they barbed-wired up the entire thing, people would find a way to get there.”

Lifeguards and park rangers are speaking with people at Panther Beach and other wild beaches north of Santa Cruz. California State Parks increased staffing during this week’s king-tide event to conduct preventive contacts, and Sipes urged visitors to remain vigilant on wild, unlifeguarded beaches. “Understanding how to read the water and knowing the conditions before you go – those two actions are powerful things to keep people safe.”

The report also examined a different response on the remote southern Oregon coast, where high surf and sneaker waves have injured visitors and emergency assistance can take as long as an hour to arrive. The water remains about 50°F year-round. Luke Martinez, Curry County’s aquatic safety manager and one of two lifeguards patrolling about 100 miles of coastline, said, “You only have about 15 minutes before the body starts to have a hard time swimming on its own if you’re not wearing any type of flotation device.”

Curry County introduced its beach-safety plan in 2020 as tourism increased and more beach incidents occurred. When the National Weather Service issues a sneaker-wave, high-surf or related maritime warning, the lifeguard team fans out to beaches and warns people in person. Martinez said the team averages more than 300 preventive contacts annually. He also said there have been no fatalities on Oregon’s south coast since the program began, while the team still performs about a dozen rescues each year.

On November 8, Martinez had just clocked off when he received a call about a woman clinging to rocks in the dark on Secret Beach. He and his lifeguarding partner, Shannon Ingram, rushed to the shore and suited up. They found the woman swept 200 feet down the coastline, stranded on rocks roughly 50 to 70 feet offshore. The National Weather Service had issued a high-surf warning for Oregon’s southern coast that day, forecasting breaking waves of 25 to 33 feet.

Ingram reached the woman first, secured her with a rescue tube and brought her ashore. “The woman was super lucky,” Martinez said. “It could have been a lot worse – it wasn’t a good spot to be in. But things aligned so that we were able to get her out of there.” Martinez said the lifeguard team also works with tourism organizations to include hazard information in visitor promotions. “The ocean, even though it looks docile and weak at times, is always super powerful,” he said.

CalFire’s Horn urged visitors to stay humble and maintain situational awareness because conditions can change quickly. “The reality is, if the sand is wet, the ocean was there, and it’s going to come there again probably sooner than later,” Horn said.
