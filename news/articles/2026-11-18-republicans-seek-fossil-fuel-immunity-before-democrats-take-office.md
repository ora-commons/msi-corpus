## Bill targets climate lawsuits, superfund laws and source attribution science

Republican lawmakers are trying to advance the Stop Climate Shakedowns bill before newly elected Democrats are sworn in, reviving a proposal that would shield fossil fuel companies from climate lawsuits and state liability laws. Republican lawmakers introduced companion Senate and House versions in April, with Ted Cruz and Harriet Hageman introducing the measures. Climate groups fear Congress could pass the bill by the end of the year.

Iyla Shornstein, political director for the Center for Climate Integrity, said the bill has four key parts. It would dismiss existing climate liability lawsuits against fossil fuel companies, prohibit new lawsuits, void climate superfund laws and assert that the federal government is the sole authority over greenhouse gas emissions and climate-crisis regulation, preempting states from acting.

The legal actions affected by the proposal include suits filed by 11 states, two tribal governments and dozens of municipalities against fossil fuel companies. The Guardian reported that the lawsuits rely on science connecting climate disasters with fossil-fuel emissions and on increasing evidence that the companies deceived the public about their own internal science warning them about the dangers of fossil fuels.

New York and Vermont have also passed climate superfund laws designed to recover costs by shifting the financial burden from climate disasters onto the fossil fuel companies that caused them. Both laws faced opposition, including challenges by the Department of Justice. A judge struck down New York’s law in September, and Gov. Kathy Hochul is appealing the decision.

Hundreds of elected leaders and climate groups sent a letter to Democratic leadership urging lawmakers to reject any proposal shielding fossil fuel companies from liability for the climate crisis. Shornstein said the election results showed voters did not want such legislation. “Voters at the polls sent a clear message about how costs are impacting their everyday lives. Gas prices are skyrocketing, but why would we spend our time giving special treatment to big oil, who are also simultaneously enjoying record profits at our expense? … The elections sent a pretty clear message that our elected leaders need to be spending their time helping working families, not major corporations like big oil,” she said.

The proposal still faces procedural hurdles. Stopping climate lawsuits and superfund laws was a top priority for the American Petroleum Institute this year, but the bill stalled in September when the House Judiciary Committee failed to advance it. Shornstein said Republican lawmakers could still rush the bill through or attach its immunity provisions to other legislation before the current Congress ends. “We’re at a critical moment, because Trump and the GOP and big oil’s allies want to bail out the fossil fuel industry while they still have power,” she said. “Immunity for big oil has been high on the list of priorities, so this is their last ditch effort.”

“We need to be extremely on our toes and vigilant and ready to mobilize to make sure that Congress rejects this sweeping giveaway to big oil,” said Kathy Mulvey, program director for fossil fuel accountability at the Union of Concerned Scientists. “I expect that they would see the lame duck session as a real opportunity to push through the Cruz-Hageman bill.”

The bill would also block source attribution science, a growing field that links climate disasters to emissions from specific oil and gas companies. The proposal says such scientific efforts “lack scientific credibility and are, therefore, arbitrary.” Mulvey said the focus on the field and the scientists playing a leading role showed how threatened fossil fuel companies felt about being held accountable for the harms they caused.

Cruz defended the proposal in a statement announcing the Senate bill. “Radical environmental groups have waged a coordinated campaign to weaponize our judicial system against American energy producers, including many in Texas,” Cruz said. “They’re using meritless lawsuits to bankrupt our energy industry, kill good paying jobs and drive up the cost of electricity and gasoline for hardworking families.”

The immunity effort follows a pattern of powerful industries asking Congress to shield them from liability, although not all have succeeded. In the 1990s, tobacco companies faced dozens of lawsuits seeking to recover costs from smoking-related illnesses and unsuccessfully lobbied Congress for legislation shielding them. In 2005, in response to a wave of lawsuits attempting to hold gunmakers liable for violence, the National Rifle Association achieved a lobbying victory when Congress passed the Protection of Lawful Commerce in Arms Act, a liability shield for gunmakers.

That gun law may have inspired the fossil fuel immunity push. In a letter last year to Pam Bondi, the former US attorney general, Republican state attorneys general requested a liability shield for fossil fuel companies and directly compared it with the gun-industry measure. “The fossil fuel industry explicitly invokes the liability waiver given to the gun industry in 2005 as its model for what it’s trying to achieve,” Mulvey said.

Similar efforts were underway in Republican-controlled statehouses. The Guardian cited a ProPublica investigation that reported immunity bills introduced in a number of Republican-controlled state houses were part of a coordinated rightwing network involving activist Leonard Leo and the American Legislative Exchange Council. Leo did not dispute the findings. “For decades, the left had leveraged immense resources to capture the institutions that shape our society,” Leo wrote. “That takeover resulted in a radically woke culture that does not reflect the will of the American people, or the pillars of limited constitutional government that made our country great. That is why our enterprise supports organizations that are committed to crushing liberal dominance and restoring balance in the institutions that shape society.”

Shornstein said five states enacted legislation this cycle providing expansive legal protections for the fossil fuel industry and described the approach as “a very holistic, if you will, approach to escaping accountability.” The federal bill’s immediate path therefore depends on whether Republican lawmakers can move it or attach its immunity provisions to other legislation before the current Congress ends.
