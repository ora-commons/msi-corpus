## Consumers largely left out of roughly $170 billion refund pool

ProPublica's analysis of thousands of securities filings and earnings calls found that at least nine publicly traded companies have disclosed using refunds from President Donald Trump's struck-down tariffs for bonuses rather than to repay consumers who generally covered much of the tariff costs through higher prices. Dozens of other firms have said little about how they will spend their refunds beyond characterizing the windfall as the kind of earnings they use to determine executive bonuses. Some companies have described vague plans to pay the money forward by, for example, “returning tariff refunds to our members through pricing,” though they generally do not say whether that involves price cuts or limiting future increases. Others are paying back businesses they sold products to because those businesses had itemized receipts detailing the tariff markups. Among companies that said they planned to refund customers, almost all deal with other businesses rather than consumers, the newsroom reported.

Stanley Black & Decker, one of the world's largest tool companies and based in Connecticut, told customers and shareholders it was raising prices because of the tariffs and is now getting at least $298 million refunded. The month before the November 2024 election, as Trump was campaigning on new tariffs, then-CEO Donald Allan Jr. told shareholders the company was preparing for the possibility. “Obviously, coming out of the gate, there would be price increases associated with tariffs that we put into the market,” he said on an earnings call. A few months after Trump unveiled the tariffs, Allan said his team was “remaining focused on executing our robust plan designed to mitigate tariffs,” which he said included “targeted pricing actions to improve our gross margin.” Later that year, successor CEO Chris Nelson described another increase: “We are implementing a second price increase during the fourth quarter to maintain our innovation and brand investments, given the pressures resulting from tariff-related cost increases.”

In more recent disclosures, Stanley Black & Decker has not specified how much of the refund goes to executives, saying only that the gain “was partially offset by directly attributable costs for variable incentive compensation.” The company uses “incentive compensation” to describe bonuses. It has not responded to the allegations in a lawsuit brought by a Wisconsin resident and did not answer ProPublica's questions about who receives the bonuses, their size, or whether customers should be reimbursed.

Securities experts who reviewed the disclosures for ProPublica said the windfall appeared to boost profits, which feed into formulas that help determine bonus pay for executives and other employees. The company's leaders, securities experts said, could have given money back to consumers or lowered prices. Gregory Germain, a corporate law professor at Syracuse University, said executive bonuses are among the poorest uses of the money. “Executive bonuses are probably the least just way to use this money because it wasn't anything the executives did that they should be rewarded for. It was a government mistake that resulted in a refund,” Germain said. “It really explains why it's so important for the government, when they're doing something like this, to think it through to make sure consumers don't pay extra money and not get it back.” He added: “It wasn't the executive that triggered that extra profit, so why should their compensation go up? This is one of many examples where these formulas don't do what they should do.”

The refund pipeline traces to two national emergencies declared in the first months of Trump's second term. In February 2025, Trump declared a national emergency over illegal drugs coming in across the border and unilaterally announced new tariffs on goods from Canada, Mexico and China, which he deemed responsible. In April, he declared a second national emergency over the U.S. trade deficit — the fact that the United States was importing more than it exported — and, under the banner “Liberation Day,” unveiled a poster in the Rose Garden detailing his sweeping tariffs on goods from more than 100 countries. Those tariffs stayed in place, with some tweaks, until the Supreme Court struck them down earlier this year, ruling that Trump needed congressional approval.

The ruling left the government scrambling to figure out how to refund billions in duties collected over the prior year on more than 53 million imported shipments. U.S. Customs and Border Protection created an online application system, but only importers of record — the direct payers — are eligible to apply. Businesses and consumers who took a hit downstream are not eligible. The Trump administration has not publicly detailed which companies are receiving the roughly $170 billion in refunds or how much they have requested, and it has denied ProPublica's requests for that information.

Consumers bore a substantial share of the tariffs. The New York Federal Reserve Bank, which analyzed prices across a broad swath of goods, found that 26% of tariff costs were eaten by American customers; a Harvard Business School study put the estimate at 43%. One study estimated that Trump's tariffs cost typical American households about $1,000 last year.

Burlington Stores, formerly known as Burlington Coat Factory, appears to have chosen a different response. CEO Michael O'Sullivan said the retailer received $55 million in refunds and that “rather than taking a one-time boost to earnings” the company plans “to deliver even sharper values to our shoppers.” O'Sullivan said that “over the last few years, the rising cost of living has made life difficult for many customers,” and added that the coming discounts meant investors should expect the “direct impact of tariff refunds to be neutral.” A Burlington spokesperson confirmed to ProPublica that none of the refund will go to bonuses.

Acushnet, the Massachusetts golf equipment company that includes the brand Titleist, disclosed an “incentive compensation expense of approximately $12.0 million” out of its refund. Acushnet declined to respond to questions from ProPublica.

TJX, the Massachusetts parent company of TJ Maxx and Marshalls, said it got back $331 million and, as a result, “accrued incremental expenses of $112 million for year-end incentive compensation and discretionary bonuses for eligible Associates globally.” TJX declined to answer ProPublica's questions about how far down the chain of command the bonuses would go and how much would be reserved for top managers. “Given that this is an accrual, and we have not reached the end of our current fiscal year, we have not disclosed any breakout beyond this,” a spokesperson said. The company also declined to say whether it would lower prices or repay customers with the rest of the refund, saying only that “the remaining amount will be used to support our future growth.”

Varex Imaging Corp., a Utah company that sells X-ray imaging components for medical businesses and cargo screeners, has said it will split at least some of its refund checks with customers. Varex and firms like it have not explained why they are passing along the refunds, though trade experts suspect such business-to-business vendors have detailed contracts that itemize tariff costs.

Shipping companies UPS and FedEx have set up online portals for customers seeking refunds. Though the firms are technically importers of record, they imported goods on behalf of businesses and individuals that paid them to ship products. The companies are itemizing how much customers paid in tariffs and reimbursing them.

Neither the administration nor Congress has restricted how refunds are used. After the Supreme Court ruling, a group of Democratic senators wrote to the president asking whether he would require companies to pass along part of their payments to consumers and assign penalties to those that did not. Sens. Elizabeth Warren, Cory Booker and Peter Welch said failing to do so would render the refunds “a giveaway to giant corporations that amounts to theft from the middle class.”

Though Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent has denied that the tariffs resulted in significantly higher prices, he has acknowledged that only importers of record are eligible for the refunds. “I got a feeling the American people won't see it,” he said in February at an Economic Club of Dallas event. Bessent has argued that the Democrats who challenged the tariffs in court, not the administration that imposed them, are responsible for the transfer of wealth from American consumers to companies. “The Democratic attorney generals who filed a ridiculous lawsuit against this administration wanted the money to go back to the payer of record,” he said at a press conference. “If I hear another Democratic legislator make the inane remark, ‘The American people should get the money’ — the American people had the money in the U.S. Treasury and we were forced to give it back.”

A series of lawsuits is attempting to force companies that sell directly to consumers to share some of their refunds. Dozens of firms have been sued, including big-box stores Walmart and Target and apparel companies such as Nike and Lululemon. Apparel and leather costs were among the hardest hit by Trump's tariffs. Legal analysts have cast doubt on whether the lawsuits will succeed, though some trade experts have speculated that businesses that used tariffs as cover to raise prices more than they needed to might settle to avoid embarrassing disclosures.

Stanley Black & Decker is among the defendants and is one of the companies ProPublica found using some of its refund on bonuses. The named plaintiff, a Wisconsin resident who bought a handheld blower, an impact wrench, a hedge trimmer and other tools last year, seeks to represent thousands of customers. The complaint argues that “unless restrained by this Court,” the company “stands to recover the same tariff payments twice — once from consumers through tariff-inflated prices and again from the federal government through tariff refunds.”

Nick Guest, an accounting professor at Cornell University who studies executive compensation, said the case for bonuses based on refunds can be read two ways. “If they were penalized for something out of their control,” he said, referring to the earnings hits caused by the tariffs, rewarding leaders now “seems reasonable.” “But you could say the same thing about customers. If they were penalized, then is it reasonable that they're not getting rewarded now?” Guest asked. The companies, he said, are “not obligated to give refunds or reduce prices, but doing so could certainly build goodwill with customers.”

ProPublica limited its examination to publicly traded companies, which must disclose detailed financial information. None of the companies directing refunds to bonuses had ever said they planned to refund customers, and how private companies use their refunds remains unclear because they are not normally required to disclose it.
