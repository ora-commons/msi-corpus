## Army's planned Monday operation awaits further court direction

The 8th U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals granted a request by environmentalists late Thursday to put on hold an Environmental Protection Agency permit that cleared the way for the U.S. Army's Monday operation to detonate 5 tons of bomb-making chemicals in Iowa. The ruling put the Army's plan to address dozens of barrels of degraded lead azide in jeopardy pending further court direction.

The court had yet to schedule a ruling Friday on the groups' emergency motion or say whether it would hold a hearing. Thien Chau, an attorney with Earthjustice, said attorneys expected the court to try to decide before Monday's planned operation. Earthjustice filed the petition on behalf of Iowa groups seeking review of the EPA permit.

"The Army can't detonate under the permit while the permit is stayed," Chau said. The Army did not immediately return messages seeking comment on how the court action would affect its plans. The Army has said the chemicals are unstable and that the risk of an uncontrolled explosion is growing, particularly as temperatures drop.

The Army initially planned to detonate the material Friday but announced Thursday that it would push the operation to Monday following an outpouring of public concern about potential health and environmental consequences. In a post on X, Acting Army Secretary Adam Telle announced, "This will delay the detonation until the latest possible moment prior to a forecasted temperature drop, which would risk further destabilization of the material."

An inspection earlier this year at the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant determined that the lead azide, which is used in munitions production, had started to degrade. The crystallization of the material created an unstable and dangerous situation, prompting the Army to assemble experts in munitions chemistry, engineering and environmental safety to come up with a plan.

According to EPA documents, alternatives such as neutralizing and decomposing the drums' contents with other chemicals were considered. Officials said handling the drums would increase the probability of an accidental, fatal detonation. Earthjustice and the Iowa groups disagreed, arguing in their petition that other alternatives were not fully considered. They also criticized officials for not sharing more information with the public.

On Friday, the Army, the EPA and the federal Department of Health and Human Services followed up with a joint statement saying they were working together to ensure the detonation was conducted in the safest way possible. The statement read: "Officials have been meticulously planning for this event since March, and all agencies involved are prepared to take the most extreme mitigation measures available to ensure there is no risk to the public and any disruptions are minimized."

The Army detailed its plans during a public meeting in Middletown in October, but a public advisory Sunday was the first time other communities, some as far away as 100 miles (160 kilometers), became aware of the potential impact. Nearly 400 people live in Middletown, about 160 miles (257 kilometers) southeast of Des Moines, while thousands more in surrounding areas could be affected by detonated material that could disperse in the air and land on the ground.

Some schools and businesses had planned to close as the Army recommended that people stay indoors, bring their pets and livestock inside and keep windows and doors closed. Officials also urged residents in the path of the predicted plume to clean cars, windowsills and outdoor surfaces afterward.

An advisory said: "The detonation will result in an air plume with some lead-containing particles which will disperse in the air and deposit on the ground." It added: "Lead is toxic to humans and animals."

Lucy Giehl said during a Thursday interview that she, her husband and two dogs live about 4 miles (6.4 kilometers) north of the ammunition plant. Giehl said she did not know whether she needed to wear gloves to wipe down surfaces or keep her dogs from drinking water in her pond. "There's just so many details that I feel like haven't been addressed with the public," she said.
