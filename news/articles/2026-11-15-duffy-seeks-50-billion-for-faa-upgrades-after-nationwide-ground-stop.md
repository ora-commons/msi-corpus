## Duffy seeks $50 billion for FAA upgrades after nationwide ground stop

**Subtype:** fact

- Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy demanded $50 billion for FAA upgrades after Friday's nationwide ground stop.
- Sean Duffy attributed the disruption to communication systems work by a contractor, rejecting new AI tools and a cyberattack as causes.
- Ranking Democrats on the House Transportation and Infrastructure Committee said the administration had spent $1 billion of the $12 1/2 billion Congress approved for safety upgrades.
- Democrats called for a full investigation into the ground stop after 11 House Democrats missed a procedural vote to impeach Defense Secretary Pete Hesgeth last week.

## Democrats seek inquiry after procedural impeachment vote shortfall

NPR host Ayesha Rascoe said federal officials expected air travel to return to normal by the end of the day after Friday's nationwide ground stop. Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy, speaking to reporters, attributed the disruption to communication systems work by a contractor rather than new AI tools or a cyberattack. Duffy said in an archived recording, "We don't believe this was cyber at all. This was an FAA system that didn't meet the moment."

Duffy also blamed Congress for not giving the FAA more money for upgrades. NPR senior national political correspondent Mara Liasson said he had been trying to get $30 billion for months and demanded $50 billion after the ground stop. Liasson said it was hard to tell whether the crisis would improve his chances of securing the money. Republicans, who control Congress until January, had approved $12 1/2 billion in the One Big Beautiful Bill for safety upgrades, according to Liasson. Ranking Democrats on the House Transportation and Infrastructure Committee said the administration had spent only $1 billion of those funds. President Trump had not spoken publicly or posted about the ground stop or the funding request.

Democrats were also calling for a full investigation into the ground stop, adding to a growing list of inquiries they were preparing as they prepared to take control of Congress. Last week, 11 House Democrats were missing from a procedural vote to impeach Defense Secretary Pete Hesgeth. Liasson said the absences were for various reasons, that several of the missing members had apologized and that "it wasn't a good look."

Concerns have surfaced in swing voter focus groups that voters worry Democrats will devote too much effort to investigations and not enough to affordability and bringing prices down. Democratic leaders said they understood voters want affordability addressed first. Rep. Jamie Raskin, who was set to lead many of the investigations, said he wanted to connect corruption to high prices and propose legislative fixes for problems the investigations uncover.

Liasson said Democrats do not have a filibuster-proof majority in the Senate, and President Trump can still veto any bill that both chambers pass.

Before taking charge, Democrats must vote on their leadership. Liasson said some Democrats felt neither Hakeem Jeffries nor Chuck Schumer had fought as hard as they should have against Trump for the last two years. She said it was hard to imagine Jeffries would not be elected House speaker because he had presided over the most dramatic victory Democratic House members had in a generation. Even if some progressives in the House caucus wanted someone else, Jeffries had a very big majority built by "blue dogs" and centrist Democrats who flipped red-state seats. Liasson said those members made the House majority, "not the liberals."

Schumer's path was less settled. Maryland Sen. Chris Van Hollen was challenging him for Senate majority leader, and some Democratic freshmen said they did not want to support Schumer. Liasson said Schumer represents the Democratic establishment but credited him with recruiting the candidates who won in red states and made the new Senate Democratic majority. Arizona Sen. Ruben Gallego said, "when the manager takes you to the World Series, you give him another contract."

Election results were still arriving. Scott Colom will be Mississippi's first Black senator since Reconstruction, after a race won by several thousand votes that surprised many people who thought the state was solidly Republican. Alaska's Senate race remained outstanding; Democrat Mary Peltola had the lead, but not enough yet to determine whether rank choice voting would be triggered.

On foreign policy, President Trump is meeting Russian President Putin and Chinese President Xi this week on the sidelines of the APEC summit in China. The meeting comes on the heels of what Rascoe called the Nvidia scandal. The White House says Trump will discuss the war in Iran and nuclear weapons and will not raise former Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang's affair with a woman suspected to be a Chinese spy.

The New York Times reported that Trump had been told about the affair on more than one occasion and did nothing. Liasson said members of Congress on both sides of the aisle believe there should be tougher restrictions on advanced chips sold to China. Jensen Huang opposed such restrictions, she said, and Donald Trump had been more willing than the Biden administration to send advanced chips to China. Liasson said bipartisan pushback on the issue would be worth watching.
