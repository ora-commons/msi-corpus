## Carney embraces proposal as counterweight to Trump-era pressure

European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen told an audience last week that the EU and Canada "see the world with the same eyes," sharing stances on artificial intelligence, the climate crisis, the Ukraine war, and what the Guardian characterized as "the unholy chaos unleashed by the current occupant of the White House." She went on to propose that Canada become "the first associate member of the EU." Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney, seated in the audience, "beamed with delight," according to the Guardian. The next day, Carney enthusiastically embraced the idea of an "alliance for the future" built on "collective resilience" through "deep cooperation."

But the proposal has no legal foundation. "Associate membership" is not a status in EU treaties, the Guardian reported, and creating such a status would mean treaty reform that member states will not contemplate. The Guardian noted that there are existing "association agreements" with Ukraine, Moldova, and Georgia, but those are essentially stepping stones to full membership — which Canada cannot aspire to, "for the very good reason that it isn't in Europe." A Guardian editorial described the term as "a political hypothesis," characterizing it as "a relationship of intimate strategic alignment with the EU, stopping short of actual membership, in ways that are significant but undefined." Neither the commission nor the Canadian government has published a blueprint describing what such a relationship would involve.

The catalyst, according to the Guardian columnist, is the Trump administration's posture toward Canada. Incessant tariff threats, an escalating trade war, references to Canada as the "51st state," and the renaming of Lake Ontario have pushed Ottawa to seek to diversify its international relationships, the Guardian reported. Aligning with European industrial strategy, cooperating on defense and artificial intelligence, and joining EU scientific research and student-exchange programs would offer Canada a counterweight to its southern neighbour. Trump dismissed the EU-Canada tie-up as "laughable" and threatened more tariffs, the Guardian reported. For the EU, Canada represents a stable, like-minded "middle power" rich in critical minerals and rare earths — qualities Carney highlighted in remarks that also noted Canadian "good manners." Canada's enthusiasm for deeper EU integration is a boost to the bloc after Iceland's recent rejection of further accession negotiations.

The concept of an EU "second circle" is not new. The EU has discussed an outer ring of partner countries bound by shared values, free trade, and security cooperation with an inner core of members — but without full voting rights or complete regulatory integration — since the presidency of Jacques Delors. French President Emmanuel Macron's European Political Community is a variation on the theme, and German Chancellor Friedrich Merz recently floated "associate member" status for Ukraine. Non-EU nations in the European Economic Area and European Free Trade Association — Norway, Iceland, and Switzerland — already function as quasi-associate members, enjoying single-market access while largely adopting EU rules without a vote in Brussels.

Fabian Zuleeg, writing in the Guardian, suggested that Canada and Europe need an "integrated partnership" that leaves single-market access to one side and focuses instead on concrete collaboration in defense, supply chains, and EU programs such as the Horizon research framework and Erasmus+. Alberto Alemanno, a professor of EU law, said a realistic EU-Canada landing zone would have to go beyond CETA — the existing EU-Canada free trade agreement — and might resemble the tailored framework of the EU-Swiss relationship. The real significance of any formal new status, Alemanno said, would be to make the EU "an institutional hub around which other democracies organise strategic autonomy — but without joining the union."

What concrete cooperation might look like is unclear: visa-free work access, shared military procurement, and Canadian seats on EU regulatory committees are among the possibilities the Guardian floated. For the moment, the proposal amounts to "the expressed intent of a democratic bloc and a middle power to deepen ties in the face of mounting global uncertainty," the Guardian wrote — more of a political mood than a legal status.

The proposal carries implications for Brexit-regretting Britain. Jill Rutter of the Institute for Government highlighted that the EU's sudden willingness to display "institutional imagination" shows the bloc is willing to adapt when geopolitical pressures demand. Joël Reland of UK in a Changing Europe pointed out that the vagueness of the EU offer to Canada might look promising for the UK. Canada wants "deep cooperation" without touching single-market rules, Reland said, and the UK's main problem is that it is now shut out of that same market. "Whatever Canada eventually gets, it's unlikely to work for the UK," Reland said.

Separately, German Chancellor Friedrich Merz is confronting mounting political pressure. A fortnight after the far-right Alternative für Deutschland swept to a crushing victory in one state election in Saxony-Anhalt, Merz's centre-right CDU failed to clear the 5% threshold needed to enter the state parliament in another, Mecklenburg-Western Pomerania. The CDU fared slightly better in Berlin, where it was the incumbent, but was still beaten — by a clear five-point margin — by the far-left Die Linke. The AfD also surged to a double-digit result in the capital.

Merz has vowed to fight on but faces an uncomfortable choice: revise the unpopular economic reforms he insists are essential, or risk political oblivion defending them. With no one actively seeking to replace him and Merz refusing to step aside, a volatile and damaging deadlock is likely to continue in Europe's largest economy. After 16 months in office, it is becoming increasingly clear — as a Guardian editorial noted — that Merz is "ill-suited for the job" of defending Germany's democratic future against the far right.
