## Non-development dedication renews automatically in five-year increments unless 75% of owners vote otherwise

Starwood Capital Group’s proposal to build luxury homes and condominiums on the closed nine-hole Woods Golf Course in Princeville, an 8,000-acre peninsula resort community on Kaua‘i’s North Shore, has become a widening legal and political dispute. The company, described as a $115 billion global investment company, is appealing a 2025 ruling that favored two homeowners and now faces a proposed class-action suit. A community association director elected with critical backing from Starwood faces removal, while the company separately sought the ouster of the board president and vice president.

The conflict extends beyond neighborhood disputes over property views. Princeville’s resort landscape includes pricey hotels, vacation condominiums and multimillion-dollar homes, and the community is a mix of affluent residents, investment-property owners and multinational corporations. The conflict has divided that community. On one side, homeowners whose properties buffer the abandoned golf course advocate for open-space protections, with some filing lawsuits to thwart new development. Bernard Markowicz, a civil engineer who shares his views on neighborhood matters on FriendsofPrinceville.com, said Princeville brings tourists to hotels and vacation rentals and dollars to Kaua‘i’s economy. He said Princeville’s economic engine is not being properly managed. The dispute has also divided the Princeville at Hanalei Community Association, with conflicts of interest and competing removal petitions impeding its work.

Shen and Emma Beard Schulz filed the proposed class-action suit on Sept. 24 on behalf of themselves and a proposed class of hundreds of neighbors. They allege Starwood used deceptive efforts to gain control of the association board to advance plans to convert the course into multimillion-dollar homes. The defendants include Starwood, the association and several current or former directors. The couple own a $1.9 million house on the Woods Course and two Princeville condominiums.

The broader conflict includes competing accounts. There are allegations that Starwood has used its outsized voting power in neighborhood elections to install community association board members sympathetic to its interests and oust those with differing views. Others cast the standoff as a private fight in which a small subset of homeowners who want to preserve their backyard views has tried to hijack the community association’s financial resources to fund mounting legal bills. The association’s neighborhood board meetings have been volatile to the point that General Manager Bryan Allen, a key staff member who helps keep the association running, resigned.

The Schulzes also filed a legal complaint in August against Chicago Title Insurance Company, which was involved in the purchase of their Princeville home, alleging that the company omitted key facts about the property. Emma Beard Schulz said the developer of the Princeville community disclosed the burial site in a 1975 public offering statement filed with the Hawai‘i Real Estate Commission, which required the statement to be given to new homebuyers. She and her husband, Shen, a Malibu real estate agent, received none of that information when they bought the home four years ago, she said. “It’s kind of an Erin Brockovich story,” Beard Schulz said. “I am very upset with people who don’t take it serious.” Beneath the course lie the decomposed remains of hundreds of cattle killed in anthrax outbreaks more than a century ago. The Mull and White litigation brought this nearly forgotten piece of Princeville history to light and alleged that disturbing the anthrax-tainted soil could release spores capable of sickening humans and killing animals; the focus of their suit changed to center on those alleged risks.

Starwood disputes the existence of anthrax at the site and characterized Mull and White’s claims of a possible public health threat as “self-serving fear mongering” in a statement to reporters last year. The company did not respond to requests for comment for this story. Chicago Title spokesman Sean Neary declined to comment on the pending litigation.

Starwood is appealing a July 2025 ruling by Fifth Circuit Judge Kathleen Watanabe that favored plaintiffs Lorraine Mull and Fran White. The homeowners, whose homes overlook the golf course, argued that Starwood’s development plan would conflict with Princeville’s original incorporation papers, which envisioned a neighborhood centered around open space. At issue is a 55-year non-development dedication for the Princeville Makai Golf Club’s defunct Woods Golf Course. Although the dedication expired in February, Watanabe ruled that it must be read with the community’s covenants, conditions and restrictions. Under those restrictions, an automatic five-year extension takes effect when the non-development period ends and renews in five-year increments in perpetuity unless 75% of owners vote otherwise.

The dispute began in 2018 when Princeville’s flagship hotel — a vacation destination for celebrities Kourtney Kardashian and Addison Rae — was sold to the Miami-based developer Starwood Capital Group for $225 million. After an extensive multiyear renovation, the hotel reopened in 2023 as 1 Hotel Hanalei Bay, where the cheapest rooms can cost upward of $1,200 a night. Starwood originally sought to develop a portion of the Princeville Makai Golf Club, which it acquired as part of the hotel purchase, into a luxury “glamping” resort, prompting Mull and White to sue. After the Kaua‘i County Council passed an open-space ordinance that would have prohibited the project, Starwood proposed luxury homes and condominiums on the nine-hole Woods Course, which it stopped operating in 2021. A second 27-hole course remains active in the community.

Rose Krebs — a retired health care consultant and self-described grandmother with a Bernedoodle designer dog — won a community association board seat in 2024 with critical backing from Starwood, which has two votes for each of its 252 hotel rooms. In campaign materials, Krebs described her love for Princeville’s natural beauty and declared that “most people here are nice.” After an encounter with angry neighbors outside the Aloha Room of the Princeville Neighborhood Center, where board meetings take place, Krebs said she stopped attending meetings in person and joined them by Zoom.

Rose Krebs now faces a petition for removal bearing more than 500 signatories, submitted by Beard Schulz. Krebs asserted that the petition was a maneuver by people in the anti-Starwood camp who faulted her for remaining neutral in an increasingly nasty fight in which nearly everyone in the affluent neighborhood had seemingly felt pressure to choose a side. Shortly after the petition to remove Krebs surfaced, Starwood separately sponsored a petition to remove board president Patti Thiele and vice president Dale Sands. The association allows removal of board members without cause by a simple majority.

Three of the five directors are facing removal. No date has been set for recall votes; annual board elections are set for Saturday, with two of five seats open. Thiele and Sands have recused themselves from Starwood matters after the company’s bid to oust them. Treasurer Isaiah Francis, a Starwood employee who does not live on Kauaʻi, is barred from participating in Starwood-related business. Allen’s resignation has left the association without a daily supervisor. Conflicts of interest and competing removal petitions have impeded the board’s work.

The conflict has also moved into campaign mailers, partisan websites and position papers. AI-generated caricatures dominate the debate on Nextdoor, which some residents say amount to harassment. Krebs said she called police on June 18 after an online comment read, “Ace is a place where Starwood sycophants go to die.” Ace Hodgin, a doctor and former community association board president, is Krebs’ husband. Krebs said, “There’s really multiple attacks on me by many people, and I don’t even know how to describe the feelings.” She added, “It’s hurt. It’s anger. It’s definitely fear.”

Starwood is also pursuing a separate expansion at 1 Hotel Hanalei Bay with a proposed two-story residential building, parking garage and event pavilion on a coastal bluff. In May, the Kaua‘i Planning Commission paused decision-making on the proposal and gave four community groups represented by Earthjustice an opportunity to submit evidence to help inform whether the project should move forward. Mālama Kuaʻāina, Hanalei-Wainiha-Hā‘ena Community Association, Hui Ho‘omalu i Ka ‘Āina and Conservation Council for Hawai‘i raised concerns about the project’s proximity to an eroding cliff edge.

Residents offered different assessments of the dispute’s reach. Markowicz warned that the controversy had stolen the association’s focus from neighborhood business and said, “I’m well-aware that we are somewhat of an entitled community and many of us drive fancy foreign cars and things like that.” He said the dispute has a repercussion on the economy of the whole island because Princeville brings tourists to the hotels or vacation rentals and brings dollars to the economy of Kaua‘i. “To me that’s what’s really at stake. You have an economic engine here … that’s not being properly managed.” Lorraine Mull asked, “Has it hijacked the community? Well, it certainly has inflamed a lot of people,” about the litigation’s impact on the community. She continued, “But is it just a silly neighborhood dispute? No. It’s got bigger ramifications than that. It’s kind of a Gordian knot.” Dale Sands, a former mayor of a Chicago suburb, said, “It’s kind of like the people versus a corporation.” He added, “It’s really kind of tearing the community apart.” Princeville resident Bill Schilling, 81, said, “What’s happened is I think there have been a few people just pushing their own way and causing lots of disruption for the rest of us, and the result is the board is getting a lot of split votes on a continuous basis and not getting very much done.”

The practical effects of the court fight remain visible. Neighbors say Starwood stopped mowing the golf course after losing the Mull and White lawsuit. In May, a judge denied Starwood’s request to resume ground-disturbing work on the property but said routine landscaping, including lawn mowing, would be allowed. The course remains overgrown, and some residents argue it has become a fire hazard. Princeville resident Bill Schilling, 81, also said the board could not come out with a united position on whether Starwood should have to cut the grass on the Woods Course.

Several parts of the dispute remain unresolved: Starwood’s appeal, the Schulzes’ proposed class action and title-insurance complaint, the association’s removal petitions and annual elections, and the planning commission’s review of the coastal-bluff project. The July 2025 ruling established an automatic five-year renewal mechanism for the Woods Course’s non-development dedication, which renews in five-year increments unless 75% of owners vote otherwise, while the public-health allegations and allegations of improper board influence await resolution in the overlapping cases.
