## Aloha United Way finds 52% of Hawaiʻi households below ALICE threshold

**Subtype:** fact

- About 28% of households surveyed by Aloha United Way in 2026 reported bills exceeding their income.

## One in four Hawaiʻi households spends more than it earns

Aloha United Way's report, released Tuesday morning, found that 52% of Hawaiʻi's roughly 500,000 households were below the ALICE threshold in 2026, up from 40% in 2024. The nonprofit's new report is based on a survey of 1,810 full-time Hawaiʻi residents and follows earlier versions from 2022 and 2024. ALICE stands for asset limited, income constrained, and employed, and the measure includes people both below and above the poverty line who cannot cover basic necessities.

According to Aloha United Way president and CEO Michelle Bartell, rising living costs and wages that are too low and rising too slowly are pushing households into debt and reducing their reserves. The report says residents have relied on credit cards, sold belongings and worked more than one job. People below the ALICE threshold were also more likely to report trouble sleeping or focusing at work and greater tension at home. “It’s definitely straining the social and cultural fabric of our state,” Bartell said.

For retirees on fixed incomes, the added strain is visible in Kathy Fujimoto and her husband's household. They worked steady jobs before retiring and now receive Social Security, and his pension comes from his work as an accountant for the City and County of Honolulu. Social Security places a limitation on how much both Fujimoto and her husband can earn from part-time jobs. Their rent doubled last year, leaving them just scraping by. Coupons at Safeway and Longs help, but searching store apps for discounts takes time. “You almost have to almost have a second job doing that,” she said.

Cole Mijo, 25, works three jobs as a flight instructor, cashier and bookkeeper; the flight-instruction work pays minimally while he works his way up in the industry. Even so, Aloha United Way estimates he needs roughly $46,000 to cover basic costs while living alone. He lives with his parents and two siblings on Oʻahu and pays $900 a month on a flight-school loan after refinancing it from $1,500. “So basically a rent payment,” he said. “I can’t afford two rent payments, so I’ll just pay one.”

The federal poverty level for a family of four in Hawaiʻi is just under $38,000 a year, while Aloha United Way's household survival budget is $45,804 for one adult and more than $112,000 for a family of four. The survival budgets cover housing, child care, food, transportation, health care, taxes and phone plans. Native Hawaiians fared the worst among the ethnic groups surveyed, with 74% saying their household was not making enough money to get by. Bartell noted that the threshold uses 2024 numbers, so the share of residents falling behind may be higher. The survey measures hardship rather than its causes, but Bartell said the findings reflect two difficult years.

Economists at the University of Hawaiʻi Economic Research Organization said multiple factors were likely contributing: increased costs driven in part by the war in Iran, inflation recently higher than the rest of the nation, lasting effects of the pandemic and longer-term stagnation. “We have essentially been battered by shock,” UHERO executive director Carl Bonham said. “The new normal is chaos in the economy.” Bonham said the combination makes it difficult to identify precisely why households are struggling, but added, “Regardless, it’s going in the wrong direction.”

Bartell said the increase was so unexpected that Anthology/Finn Partners, which conducted the survey, was asked to “rerun the numbers because nobody wanted to believe it.” Aloha United Way also checked its findings with the national United Way ALICE research project. “We really wanted to make sure that before we put out data that was this shocking,” she said, “that we were all confident.”

The survey found that more than one in three households struggled to afford enough food. About one in four spent more than their income each month to cover essential bills, spending a median $700 beyond their income and often taking on debt just to get by, while emergency savings waned. More than a third of households were above the federal poverty level but below the ALICE threshold, showing that the burden includes employed households. Mijo said he limits spending, Fujimoto's husband drives farther for cheaper gas, and Fujimoto finds inexpensive but healthy recipes on social media.

About 68% of families with children under 18 reported household income below the level Aloha United Way estimates is needed for necessities such as housing, food and child care. Nearly two-thirds of young adults surveyed lived in households below ALICE, an increase of almost 10 percentage points from two years earlier. More than half of respondents ages 18 to 34 said they frequently lacked enough food but did not have the cash to buy more. Renters, people without a college degree and residents of neighbor islands were also more likely to struggle with basic costs.

At the Tuesday morning press conference, Aloha United Way vice president Kayla Keehu-Alexander said local families were resilient but should not have to rely on that resilience. “We know that our families, our households here, are incredibly resilient,” she said. “But we shouldn’t have to rely on the resilience of our families to make this economy better. We should be relying on our willingness to come together and do all of that for them, so no one has to continue to struggle.” Hawaiʻi Community Foundation president and CEO Terry George said the striking results should be a call to action. “There’s three ways that we can react to this disturbing set of data. One is to blame somebody for it. Let’s blame government. Let’s blame bureaucracy. Let’s blame corporations. Let’s blame the system,” George said. “A second way is to just hope somebody else will solve it. And the … third way is the only way that we all must respond to this. Roll up our sleeves, work together, and do something about it for the sake of our friends and neighbors, and the communities that we love.”

Over the 12 months ending in July 2026, midway through the Aloha United Way survey, Honolulu inflation surged to 5.6% while inflation-adjusted wages rose 1%, according to UHERO. Bartell cited disruptions to federal funding, including the temporary suspension of SNAP benefits in 2025 and the loss of several thousand federal jobs. She also pointed to higher energy prices tied to the war with Iran and the closure of the Strait of Hormuz, the Kona lows in March, and the effects of Hurricanes Lala and Lowell. Severe weather reduced Mijo's flight-instruction hours and income. “Those months are squeaking a little bit,” he said.

Bonham said Hawaiʻi recovered more slowly from the Covid-19 era, and pandemic inflation reached the state later than in the rest of the country. As prices rose, wages continued to lag, though UHERO found they were growing only slightly faster than inflation. “We’ve had, definitely, a long string of disruptions that happened before the survey was in the field,” Bartell said, “and at the same time paychecks just haven’t kept pace.” Over the past decade, the poverty rate for the Native Hawaiian and other Pacific Islander population improved more than other groups, decreasing from 20.5% in 2015 to 15.9% in 2025, according to some estimates, but remained the highest and contributed to the group’s overrepresentation among Hawaiʻi’s homeless population.

The overall gap between income and essential costs narrowed slightly over two years, but Aloha United Way said more households could no longer make ends meet. The median household fell about $700 short of its monthly costs in 2026, compared with $1,000 in 2024. About 28% of surveyed households had bills exceeding income, up from 24% two years earlier, and about half of young adults struggled to pay every bill on time. More than three-quarters of households tapped savings, about the same share used credit cards, and 47% said they had overdrawn a checking account. Among households below ALICE, 37% had difficulty paying off debts, compared with 21% above the threshold.

The financial cushion was also thinner: more than half of Hawaiʻi households lacked enough savings for three months of expenses, up 9 percentage points from two years earlier. One in 10 households could not afford a surprise $400 bill, and almost one-fifth of households below ALICE said they could not pay that bill at all. Among households below the threshold, almost half bought less fresh food, one in three relied on credit cards, loans or pawnshops, and one quarter took additional jobs or shifts. Almost a fifth of those households said they had delayed medical treatment. Keehu-Alexander said increases in the minimum wage, income tax cuts for households below ALICE thresholds and better access to preschools and child-care subsidies were helping, but the initiatives need to be permanent because more than half of the state's households cannot plan financially for the future, which she called “one of the clearest signs of economic stability.” “Hawaiʻi’s households are making difficult trade-offs to get by. At times, those sacrifices can come at the expense of health and well-being, or even long-term financial security,” Keehu-Alexander said. “The effects extend far beyond just household budgets.”

More households also relied on SNAP and Medicaid to help cover the costs of food and medical care than in 2024. The prior year brought confusion over proposed requirements for aid recipients. A federal funding gap last fall forced Hawaiʻi to step up. The year also saw cutbacks in which food markets in the state could accept SNAP benefits. Although experts feared people would fall out of the program, Aloha United Way found a slight increase in the percentage of households receiving assistance. Financial strain is also contributing to an uptick in long-time residents leaving the state. UHERO said more Native Hawaiians now live in the rest of the United States than in Hawaiʻi. The trend manifests in elderly people with no one to care for them, parents missing their children and siblings living far apart. With ʻohana — extended family — a foundational element of life in Hawaiʻi, the phenomenon contributes to what Bartell characterized as a strain on the state's social and cultural fabric. About 44% of respondents below ALICE said someone in their home had considered leaving the state in the prior year. Most cited housing and living costs despite family and cultural ties. Households below ALICE were three times more likely to say the cost of a move was one factor keeping them in Hawaiʻi, even as high local costs made staying difficult. “For many, the cost of living is making that decision for them,” Keehu-Alexander said.
