## Foreign guests attend Trump's meme coin dinner amid conflict concerns

**Subtype:** fact

- President Trump hosted top holders of his meme coin at the Trump National Golf Club in Potomac Falls, Virginia, on Nov. 22.
- Public Citizen said about half of the people who qualified for the dinner appeared to be foreign based.
- Morten Christensen said he was told to leave because he had been sharing pictures online and was in touch with the media.
- The White House defended the dinners as part of Trump's private business rather than his public functions.
- Trump's disclosures showed he earned over $600 million from cryptocurrency last year.
- Sens. Adam Schiff and Richard Blumenthal demanded more information about the latest dinner.

## One top holder was removed before Trump appeared

Morten Christensen and his younger sister Gabriëlle Felen took an Uber to the Trump National Golf Club in Potomac Falls, Virginia, on Sunday, Nov. 22, arriving early and clearing security before the large room had filled. They had butterflies in their stomachs. Some had called the event "the most exclusive dinner in the world." "I'm excited!" Christensen said. "It's always nice to dress up for an event. My sister looks amazing!" The pair shared voice memos with NPR during the night as they tried to gain access to President Trump.

The dinner was meant to reward the top holders of Trump's meme coin, a type of cryptocurrency he had launched just days before his inauguration. Christensen, the founder of Airdrop Alert, had traded crypto since 2013 and attended each of Trump's previous two top-holder events. He said he was not political and advised others to "never buy a political meme coin ever."

A sharp decline in the coin's value made the invitation easier for Christensen to accept. The price fell from a peak of more than $70 in January 2025 to less than a dollar, sharply reducing the cost of qualifying as a top holder. He said he also wanted to bring Felen along.

The arrangement drew criticism from anti-corruption activists, who said the dinners posed enormous conflict-of-interest concerns by effectively rewarding major buyers with presidential access. Top buyers received the closest access.

Felen, a recent college graduate, noticed gift boxes containing perfumes, a watch and a small golden statue of Trump. "Pretty nice, pretty nice," she said.

Christensen bought enough meme coins to qualify as a VIP guest, but he was denied access to the area reserved for the top guests. He was told that his name was not on the list. Christensen said he appeared to have been removed from the VIP list and had received an email confirmation. "But I'm still at the event, so it's all good. It's just a shame," he said.

Dinner started promptly, with a choice of grilled filet mignon or pan-seared halibut as the main course. Christensen and Felen said they were having fun. Special guest and mentalist Lior Suchard approached them and performed a trick in which Felen wrote the name of her first crush on a piece of paper, placed it in Christensen's pocket and had Suchard guess the name. Suchard said he would guess the name and succeeded, to Felen's delight. Other top guests, including Kevin O'Leary of CNBC's "Shark Tank," former boxer Floyd Mayweather and former NBA star Tristan Thompson, also gave speeches.

Just as the night was getting underway and the siblings were anticipating Trump's appearance, Christensen was kicked out. He was approached by a couple of people and told he had to leave. Christensen said he was told to leave because he had been sharing pictures online and was in touch with the media. The organizers did not reply to an email from NPR seeking comment.

Rain continued as Christensen stood outside the golf course, still trying to understand what happened. "There's a first for everything, and I guess it was at the Trump gala," he said, sounding a little dejected.

The guest list itself drew scrutiny. Many of the top buyers of Trump's meme coin were believed to be foreign citizens, raising concerns that they could be trying to court influence with the president. Christensen said he met many people from abroad, including two who said they worked for Binance, described as a top crypto exchange. "One was from Saudi, one was from London," he said.

Christensen also said he met a couple of people from Ukraine, two from India, four from China and what he thought was one person from South Korea. He and Felen were themselves foreign nationals from the Netherlands, though both lived in Mexico, where they managed Christensen's website. Public Citizen said in a report that about half of the people who qualified for the event appeared to be foreign based.

Felen decided to stay behind because she and her brother had come to hear Trump speak. They figured at least one of them should get to hear him. Trump eventually appeared to big cheers from the crowd. Felen said his remarks were not a focused speech. "He jumped from topic to topic. It was interesting but just him rambling," she said. "Speaking, speaking, speaking — but not making a lot of important points I guess."

Seeing the president was still a significant moment for Felen, but she said she later watched other VIP guests receive one-on-one selfies and, she believed, handshakes. She said she was disappointed that Christensen had missed the opportunity.

Felen and the soaked Christensen traveled back to their hotel. Christensen said: "It happens, you know. It was a different experience this time around. Could have been better, but I will just laugh about it." He added, "All good. It's part of the game."

As they headed to the airport to catch flights out of the U.S., Felen still could not believe she had gotten to go. Felen said friends and people who knew her from her village were asking how she ended up at the dinner. Both siblings sounded subdued, and Felen said she wished her brother had had a better night. Christensen said all he wanted was a chance to meet Trump, repeated that he was not political and said he remained unsure how he felt about the event. He did know it would be his last. "That's a wrap on me on the Trump events," he said.

The White House defended the dinners as part of Trump's private business rather than his public functions. Trump effectively has a royalty deal with the company that operates the meme coin and, according to his disclosures, earned more than $600 million from cryptocurrency last year.

The latest dinner was held just weeks after corruption emerged as a defining issue for voters in the midterm elections, which handed Democrats control of Congress next year. The event also came amid intense media scrutiny. Prominent Democrats including Sen. Adam Schiff of California and Sen. Richard Blumenthal of Connecticut demanded more information about the dinner.
