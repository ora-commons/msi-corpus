## Congress seeks testimony while California investigates Nvidia

Nvidia's 10-member board unanimously rehired Jensen Huang as president, CEO and a fellow board member on Monday after discussions with Huang and what the company called “input from senior levels of the United States government.” Six days earlier, the board had unanimously accepted Huang's resignation after he admitted an affair with a woman suspected of being a Chinese spy. The reversal capped an 11-day period that included Huang's resignation and reinstatement, placing the board's independence and the president's role in corporate decision-making under scrutiny.

President Donald Trump said he believes his intervention was crucial to the reinstatement. Nvidia Chairman Harvey Jones said at the start of a quarterly earnings call that the board did not intend to reinstate Huang. Nvidia's announcement, together with Trump's statement, renewed questions about what government input the board received and why it reversed course.

Some experts have called for the board to resign. Kathleen Day, a lecturer at Johns Hopkins Carey Business School with expertise in financial crises and corporate governance, said, “To bow to pressure to bring someone back who has hurt the reputation of the company is crazy.” She said the board's duty is to represent shareholders, not the CEO, and added, “The shareholders should demand a new board.”

Corporate governance experts said long tenures for directors can compromise independence. Rules for companies listed on the New York Stock Exchange and Nasdaq require a majority of directors to be independent. Nada Kakabadse, professor of policy, governance and ethics at the University of Reading's Henley Business School, said that limiting how long people can sit on a board is traditionally good practice. She said, “They should be eight years maximum. Four years, plus another four years if they're performing well. But beyond that, they really become not independent anymore, right?”

Kakabadse said it is not unusual for U.S. tech bosses to fill boards with allies or high-profile figures who might not be aware of what is happening at the company. She described a culture of “leader heroes,” meaning entrepreneurs who founded companies and are seen as the only ones who can steer them through tough times. At some point, she said, companies can become so large that “you need proper corporate structure, you need proper governance.”

Peter Cohan, an associate professor of management practice at Babson College, argued that the board should have held to its position that it was time to find a new CEO rather than give in to Trump. He said, “In my opinion, by giving in to Trump, the board is endangering the company. That’s because Huang has seen that he can do anything he wants, and he will get away with it.”

The dispute has reached Washington, particularly in conversations about export controls. Huang's affair raised questions about Nvidia's compliance with those restrictions, and his reinstatement renewed calls for transparency from Huang and the board. Elected officials from both parties quickly responded with criticism and skepticism.

Democratic Sen. Elizabeth Warren, the incoming chair of the Senate Banking Committee, said the decision to bring Huang back was “reckless.” In a Monday statement, she said the reinstatement did not change the fact that Huang, Nvidia executives and the company's board “still owe Congress answers.”

Sen. Kirsten Gillibrand, a New York Democrat, called for both Huang and the board to testify publicly before Congress. “A boardroom vote does not resolve a national security risk,” she said in a statement Monday.

California Attorney General Rob Bonta, who is investigating Nvidia in connection with Huang's affair, said Tuesday in a CNBC interview that Trump's intervention “does not bode well for enforcement of the law by the federal government without fear, without favor.” The California Department of Justice said Thursday that it is asking Nvidia whether its advanced chips or confidential information may have gone to China and whether Nvidia misled investors and the public.

Nvidia's 10-person board combines longtime company figures with more recent additions drawn from companies including Taco Bell, PepsiCo and Eli Lilly. Mark Stevens and Tench Coxe joined in 1993 when Nvidia was founded; both were previously venture capitalists who made early investments in Nvidia. Harvey Jones and Brooke Seawell are the former chief executive and chief financial officer, respectively, of Synopsys, which makes chip-design software and counted Nvidia as its first customer. Jones joined Nvidia's board in 1993, while Seawell joined four years later.

Stephen Neal, designated as the board's “lead independent director,” joined in 2019. He is chairman emeritus of Cooley LLP, Nvidia's longtime law firm, and has advised the company in “various governance matters,” according to his bio. Melissa Lora, former president of Taco Bell International, spent 31 years at the fast-food company and joined the board in 2023. Aarti Shah, formerly a senior vice president and chief digital officer at pharmaceutical company Eli Lilly, joined in 2021.

John Dabiri, an engineering professor at the California Institute of Technology whose lab researches fluid mechanics, was added to the board in 2020. Dawn Hudson, who was the NFL's chief marketing officer and a former chief executive of Pepsi-Cola's North American division, has served on the board since 2013. Suzanne Nora Johnson was a vice chairman at Goldman Sachs and joined the board in July.

None of the board members responded to requests from The Associated Press for comment.
