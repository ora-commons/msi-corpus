## Netanyahu claims election was stolen despite no evidence of fraud

Final results released early Friday local time left Benjamin Netanyahu's right-wing bloc short of a governing majority in the Knesset, with the prime minister's coalition winning 53 seats against 48 for the opposition grouping led by retired general Gadi Eisenkot. Palestinian-led parties won 19 seats, a record high. The vote was described by many Israelis as the most consequential election in the country's history.

Neither bloc reached the 61 seats needed for a majority, so Israel's president, Isaac Herzog, is expected to invite the party most likely to be able to form a government to start coalition negotiations. Herzog has urged politicians to have an "open mind" before talks begin, and the country potentially faces weeks of rancour and uncertainty. No single party has ever claimed a majority of 61 seats, so Israel has always been governed by coalitions.

The arithmetic leaves the Jewish Israeli opposition bloc with a clear majority only if it is prepared to make a governing pact with Palestinian Israeli parties, which secured what the Guardian described as a historic result. Eisenkot has not ruled out cooperating with Palestinians because of their race, but he has effectively barred them from a future coalition by laying out conditions for any partner which are politically impossible for them to accept.

Yair Golan, leader of the centre-left Democrats, a junior partner in the Jewish Israeli opposition bloc, told Israeli media on Thursday that he was meeting Eisenkot and others for initial talks. If the opposition cannot agree on a coalition, or cannot secure Palestinian support for a minority government, Netanyahu could retain power; the Guardian reported that he is pushing for a right-wing governing coalition and would probably welcome new elections or a lengthy period of uncertainty during which he would stay on as caretaker prime minister.

Netanyahu has gone further since Tuesday's vote, claiming that Palestinian-led parties have "stolen the election" despite Israeli authorities insisting there is no evidence of election fraud. On Wednesday night he reacted to the high Palestinian turnout and the success of Arab Israeli parties by calling for the central elections committee and the Shin Bet internal security agency to investigate foreign interference in the vote. Finance Minister Bezalel Smotrich amplified the claims, calling for the arrest of Arab Israeli leaders. He was swiftly accused of using smear tactics to cling to power. Eisenkot warned that Likud and Netanyahu "have embarked on a coordinated, organised campaign seeking to delegitimise the results of the election." Herzog rebuked the prime minister, saying "we must avoid tarnishing an entire community." A spokesperson for Qatar's foreign ministry rejected "ridiculous and dangerous" accusations that the country had interfered in the election.

Israeli police and the central elections committee have said there is no evidence of election fraud, the Guardian reported. During the campaign, Netanyahu and his right-wing allies framed Palestinian voters — who make up about 20% of the population — as a direct threat to the state, with Netanyahu repeating the slogan "the right or Palestine", which rhymes in Hebrew. On election day, voters received a robocall purporting to come from the prime minister falsely claiming that Palestinian party leaders had been caught with envelopes full of cash, and Netanyahu and his allies repeatedly made claims about high turnout in Palestinian areas, in a bid to get his own voters out.

Emma Graham-Harrison, the Guardian's chief Middle East correspondent in Tel Aviv, described the tactic as "both tragic and depressing," adding that it was "attempting to paint Palestinians as untrustworthy citizens with dual loyalties, a racist trope that also appears in antisemitic attacks."

The two main Palestinian-led parties would be the likely partners. The Joint List, which represents a number of mainly leftist Israeli Arab-led parties, is led by Yousef Jabareen, who has previously said the Joint List could not join an Eisenkot-led government because of its support for Israel's military operation in Gaza and southern Lebanon, but has left open the possibility of bolstering a minority government through confidence votes.

Ra'am, led by Mansour Abbas, includes a Jewish former chief of police as number two on its slate, a signal of its commitment to working with Jewish Israelis and its desire to tackle organised crime, a major issue for the Palestinian population. Ra'am joined Naftali Bennett's government in 2021, the first time a Palestinian party entered an Israeli coalition, but the pact collapsed after 18 months, partly because of disagreements over the occupied West Bank.

Palestinian citizens turned out in historic numbers after many had boycotted previous elections. Many had done so, Graham-Harrison said, "on the grounds that participating endorses a discriminatory system or because they felt their representatives had little leverage." This year, she said, "They're watching ethnic cleansing in the West Bank, a genocidal war in Gaza, and are under huge pressure inside Israel from an openly racist government, which has allowed organised crime to soar, ramped up home demolitions, and targeted political speech. They felt that, if Netanyahu managed to get another mandate with his far right allies including [extremist security minister] Ben-Gvir, this would only accelerate."

A survey taken as polls closed on Tuesday showed that a clear majority of opposition voters supported forming a government with Ra'am in order to unseat Netanyahu. Graham-Harrison noted an inconsistency in the opposition's position: Palestinian citizens play a key role in Israeli life, with outsized representation in professions from medics to security guards, including much of the team at Eisenkot's election night headquarters. "You would think some of these parties might ask, if you trust Palestinian citizens to secure your campaign events why don't you trust them to be part of your government?"

Graham-Harrison pointed to a rarely referenced precedent from three decades ago, when Yitzhak Rabin relied on Palestinian support for a minority government as he sought a negotiated end to the Israel-Palestine conflict.

Discontent with Netanyahu was evident throughout the campaign. That did not mean a shift to the left: young Israelis are more rightwing than older generations, the Guardian reported, and the national conversation ahead of the election was dominated by security at home and abroad. Although he was in charge on 7 October 2023, when Hamas attacks resulted in the deaths of about 1,200 people, the majority of them civilians, the prime minister tried to shift blame on to the security services. His opponents pledged a state commission of inquiry into the events of 7 October and spoke to voters' concerns about the social toll of three years of multi-front wars, and anger about the draft exemption for ultra-orthodox young men when other Israelis are spending hundreds of days on reserve duty.

Graham-Harrison described the moment facing the country as existential. "This whole campaign has been based on Israeli voters urgently wanting change, because Netanyahu has led them to the brink of disaster. And now they have voted, this is an existential moment." She added: "You have a parliamentary majority to bring about that change. Is your racism ultimately more important to you? What matters more: the desire to exclude Palestinians or the desire to get rid of Netanyahu?"
