## FCC prison-jamming safeguards require outside-signal tests and movement notice

## Anna Gomez warns that improperly calibrated zones can disrupt emergency calls

State prisons that use cellphone-signal jamming must conduct multiple tests to make sure cell signals outside prisons are not affected. State prisons must also notify officials if jamming devices are moved, and the rule does not require prisons to use the technology. The jamming technology was already allowed in federal facilities, but state prisons needed FCC permission because of a nearly century-old communications law.

Anna Gomez, the lone Democrat on the panel, ultimately voted for the measure on what she called a "critical" issue. She warned of "potential unintended consequences" if jamming zones were improperly calibrated in ways that accidentally disrupted home-security systems or blocked emergency-response calls.

"I want to make sure that we don't harm public safety in the name of helping public safety," Gomez said.

State prison officials had sought the right to jam for years. State prison officials argued that blocking the signals would halt inmates' ability to use the devices to run drug rings, coordinate prison riots and order acts of violence such as attacks on guards. The Associated Press reported: "Cellphones have been smuggled inside hollowed-out footballs, whisked in by corrupt employees and sometimes even dropped by drone."

FCC Chairman Brendan Carr spoke in favor of the authorization and visited South Carolina officials during the week; the state's prison directors had led efforts to obtain permission to jam. Carr said before the vote, "This itself is not going to be a silver bullet, but I think it's time that we stop denying this tool to state and local officials."

U.S. Attorney Bryan Stirling, who headed South Carolina's Corrections Department for more than a decade, led an effort by many of his counterparts across the country to gain use of the technology. South Carolina Attorney General Alan Wilson, elected this month as the state's next governor, organized fellow prosecutors to press Congress to change the law, but they were unsuccessful.

The FCC rule allows state prisons across the United States to jam signals from cellphones smuggled to inmates. It was approved unanimously among the commissioners meeting in Washington on Thursday. The action came as the federal government continued enforcement efforts targeting contraband phones, which authorities said were the No. 1 security threat in the nation's correctional facilities.

During his South Carolina visit, Carr stood alongside Stirling, Wilson and other state officials before a display of hundreds of confiscated cellphones and massive drones used to ferry contraband over prison fencing. "People really don't understand the threat that these pose," Carr said, noting that 25,000 cellphones were confiscated in the nation's state and local prisons each year. "We're going to put a new tool in the hands of law enforcement, and it's going to make a dramatic difference."

Carr cited the case of Robert Johnson, a South Carolina corrections officer who was nearly killed at his home in 2010 in a hit that officials said was orchestrated by a prisoner using a contraband cellphone.

After nearly three dozen surgeries, Johnson said Wednesday that he was in "pain 24/7" and referred to the phones as "weapons of mass destruction" because of the potential dangers for which they can be used. He has testified in previous FCC hearings over jamming requests and has often spoken out on the issue.

"No one should have to go through what I went through," Johnson said. "But I also believe that the Lord chose my wife and I to go through this because someone had to carry the banner, and he chose us to do it."

Groups advocating for inmates' rights opposed the move, arguing that the high rates inmates must pay to use official prison phones had pushed them toward using contraband cellphones just to stay in touch with family. The telecommunications industry had long argued against jamming, saying the technology risked interfering with legitimate communications.

Carr said in South Carolina that he felt the industry was softening on jamming, partly because of recognition of the problem's size and agencies' experiments with targeted technologies.

"We aren't considering jamming outside this unique context," Carr said. "Maybe people aren't cheering it on from industry, but the opposition has receded substantially."

South Carolina has been the testing ground for state-prison cellphone jamming. In 2008, the FCC allowed the state to conduct a one-time test of a jamming system at a maximum-security prison. A briefcase-sized device emitted a frequency that immediately shut down cellphones around an auditorium, while service outside was uninterrupted.

Another test took place at a different state prison in 2019, a first-time collaboration between federal officials and state prison officials. A year earlier, seven South Carolina inmates had died in what officials called the worst U.S. prison riot in 25 years, in what officials said was a gang fight arranged in part using illegal cellphones.

The FCC has also worked on the issue, including a 2016 field hearing in South Carolina and meetings with members of Congress, prison officials and representatives of the wireless industry.
