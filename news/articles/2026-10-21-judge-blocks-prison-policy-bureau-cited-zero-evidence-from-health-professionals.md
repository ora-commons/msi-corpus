## Sauer cited institutional security; the plaintiffs alleged an Eighth Amendment violation

The case centers on the evidence behind a Bureau of Prisons policy. U.S. District Judge Royce Lamberth for the D.C. Circuit blocked the policy earlier in 2026 after finding that the agency cited zero evidence from its own medical or mental health professionals. Lamberth said the Bureau had failed to consider the merits of its past treatment of transgender inmates.

Solicitor General D. John Sauer argued that the changes were for “institutional security” in federal prisons. The transgender prisoners who sued with help from the American Civil Liberties Union said the government did not properly explain the change and that it violated the Eighth Amendment, which bars cruel and unusual punishment.

The case concerned an executive order President Donald Trump signed on his first day in office for his second term. The order directed that no federal funds be spent on medical procedures “for the purpose of conforming an inmate's appearance to that of the opposite sex.”

The Bureau of Prisons policy at issue called for tapering treatments and stopping other accommodations. If allowed to take effect, the only treatment transgender inmates would have received was mental health care and possibly antidepressants.

In its argument to the Supreme Court, the American Civil Liberties Union said enacting the policy would overturn three decades of the status quo. The group argued that the omission and the unreasonableness of the government’s decision showed that its rationales were contrived to support President Trump’s demand in the executive order.

The U.S. Court of Appeals for the D.C. Circuit upheld the lower-court decision. The appeal, filed Sept. 28, asked the Supreme Court to overrule that ruling.

On Wednesday, October 21, the Supreme Court refused to lift the lower court’s order allowing gender-affirming care for transgender prisoners. Clarence Thomas and Samuel Alito dissented, though neither side gave an explanation. The refusal left the Bureau of Prisons policy blocked.

Li Nowlin-Sohl, senior staff attorney with the American Civil Liberties Union’s LGBTQ & HIV Project, said in a statement: “This is a critical ruling for our clients and all transgender people in Bureau of Prisons custody.”

Nowlin-Sohl continued: “The denial of medically necessary health care, including gender-affirming health care, to people in prison is a violation of their fundamental constitutional rights. We will continue to defend the rights of our clients and all incarcerated people.”

In the ruling, U.S. District Judge Royce Lamberth for the D.C. Circuit wrote: “Despite providing gender-affirming care to inmates in its custody for years, the government cites in the Program Statement zero evidence from its own medical or mental health professionals to support its conclusions.” He added: “The government didn't give any evidence that the previous policy was "ineffective or harmful."”
