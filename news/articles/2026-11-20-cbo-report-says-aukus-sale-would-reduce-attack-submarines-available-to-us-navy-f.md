## Australian command would not guarantee deployment in a US-China conflict

A Congressional Budget Office report released Friday morning says the United States would have fewer attack submarines available to its Navy for many years if it sells Virginia-class submarines to Australia under AUKUS. The report also says Australia would control any transferred vessels, leaving their participation in a US conflict with China uncertain.

The Congressional Budget Office found that “the US submarine industrial base is currently struggling to meet the Navy’s demand for submarines.” The report said increasing production to replace attack submarines sold to Australia would increase the challenges facing the Navy and shipyards. It concluded: “The sale of SSNs to Australia would reduce the number of attack submarines available to the Navy for many years.”

The US Navy's latest shipbuilding plan does not account for AUKUS in its forecasts. A footnote says the transfer of any Virginia-class submarine to Australia “has not been factored into the plan,” according to the report.

Under Pillar One of AUKUS, the United States has agreed to sell between three and five in-service, nuclear-powered Virginia-class submarines to Australia beginning in 2032. The Congressional Budget Office evaluated the effect of that transfer on the attack submarines available to the Navy and on deterrence in the region.

Current US shipyards are producing Virginia-class submarines at 1.13 a year. The report said the country needs two submarines a year to service America's own needs and 2.33 a year to have additional boats to transfer to Australia.

The Congressional Budget Office said submarine-building rates had slowed over the past two decades. Virginia-class submarines were delayed between 24 and 36 months, and current schedules showed that building submarines took five to six years in the 2000s but now takes an average of 10 to 11 years.

US law sets another limit on the transfer. Legislation governing AUKUS says the president can transfer a submarine to Australia only if doing so “will not degrade the United States undersea capabilities.” The United States can refuse to transfer submarines without penalty under the agreement.

The report framed the strategic tradeoff with a question: “Would China be less deterred if the United States reduced the number of its attack submarines to help Australia develop its own submarine force?” The Congressional Budget Office argued that, because of the longstanding and robust alliance between the United States and Australia, boosting Australia's submarine fleet could help offset the Navy's “potential loss of capability.” “That loss might even be more than offset because the Australian submarines would be based in the Western Pacific region and therefore could respond more quickly to any conflict with China over Taiwan or other issues in the South China Sea.”

Control of the boats remained a limiting factor. The report said, “However, Australia would control its own submarines, and their participation in any particular conflict would not be guaranteed.” In 2023, Australian defence minister Richard Marles said Australia would make no promises to deploy its future submarines in support of any US conflict with China and that the boats would be “under the complete control of the Australian government of the day.”

The transfer retains broad bipartisan political support across the United States and Australia despite concern about whether the submarines can be delivered. Earlier this month, Marles said: “Aukus is happening, our alliance is happening, and all of that continues unimpeded.” US secretary of defense Pete Hegseth affirmed his support when he met Marles in September, saying the countries remained committed “to advance the alliance and strengthen deterrence in the Pacific.”

Former foreign affairs minister Bob Carr was among the critics. He told Guardian Australia that the two countries were “living a lie” by upholding the expectation of a submarine deal and that it was conventional wisdom in Washington and Canberra that “we’re not going to get the subs.” Critics have said there is vanishingly little chance the United States will have the capacity to make the sale.

The Congressional Research Service openly supported an “alternative division of labour” under which no Virginia-class submarines would ever transfer to Australian control. The service's latest AUKUS report, issued in January, considered a revision of the deal under which the submarines earmarked for sale to Australia would instead remain under US command and sail from Australian bases.

The Congressional Research Service report argued both for and against selling three Virginia-class submarines to Australia beginning in 2032. But it said that during a “conflict or crisis” with China over Taiwan, submarines under Australian command could not be ordered into operation, while US-commanded vessels operating from Australian bases could be deployed immediately.
