## Legal challenges and grid constraints complicate the energy overhaul

President Donald Trump has said he supports the Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act, or BAAJA, but the permitting overhaul faces an uncertain House vote. House Energy and Commerce Chair Brett Guthrie of Kentucky has raised concerns about the transmission provisions, while House Democrats, including top Democrat Hakeem Jeffries of New York, have also expressed concerns.

BAAJA would die if Trump did not sign it before the new year, and lawmakers would have to start over if they wanted to pass permitting reform in the next session. Republicans could also struggle to assemble a vote if many members stay home after losing races in the midterms. A large group of Democrats might defeat the bill if the group persuaded a handful of Republicans to vote against it.

The Senate passed BAAJA by a vote of 62 to 33, with 15 Democrats joining all but six Republicans. It moved quickly through the Senate, but its prospects are less certain in the House. The bill casts a wide net, easing construction for almost all large infrastructure projects. Supporters, including clean energy companies and prominent climate-conscious senators, say the changes are necessary to clean up the power grid, while critics say they could weaken communities, tribes and environmental groups while speeding fossil-fuel infrastructure.

At its core, BAAJA would streamline the federal process for issuing permits for large-scale energy and transportation projects. Without permits, developers cannot build infrastructure on federal land, across state lines or with certain kinds of federal funding. Companies say obtaining and keeping a permit is time-consuming, unpredictable and costly, even when a project meets all necessary environmental standards.

The National Environmental Policy Act of 1969, or NEPA, requires federal agencies to study the environmental, economic and social impacts of major government actions, including many infrastructure permits. Agencies must consider alternatives, solicit public input and publish their findings. The National Historic Preservation Act requires agencies to carry out similar reviews of projects' repercussions for historic and archaeological sites.

Although NEPA does not explicitly hold projects to a higher environmental standard, it creates a formal process that can educate the public, inform government officials and open pathways to better outcomes. That process often becomes a source of delays. Opponents of a project can sue an agency, claiming it conducted an inadequate review, potentially forcing the government to redo parts of its analysis and halting construction in the meantime, even if the agency ultimately issues the same permit after redoing the analysis. The motivations for such lawsuits can range from wealthy communities trying to protect their property values to tribes defending their sovereignty and religious freedom.

BAAJA would speed up environmental and historic-preservation reviews, narrow the potential impacts agencies must consider, restrict who can sue over them and shorten the deadline for filing suits. Supporters say the changes would prevent costly delays, which can be particularly consequential for land-intensive renewable energy projects. Critics say they would reduce public scrutiny and make it harder for communities and tribes to identify environmental harms, protect historic or sacred sites and push developers toward better alternatives.

The bill also targets the power grid. It would give the federal government greater authority to override states that block interstate power lines and require regional grid operators to approve lines that improve reliability and affordability. Transmission construction in the United States has slowed over the last decade, even as its importance is expected to grow.

Such legal challenges commonly delay renewable energy projects and the transmission lines that carry their power to population centers. The threat of lawsuits and resistance from utilities have driven down long-distance power-line construction in the United States, limiting the country's ability to use cheap renewable energy.

BAAJA would also prevent states from using the Clean Water Act to block power lines or pipelines crossing waterways unless they directly discharge into the water. The vast majority of projects built on private land within one state would see little change under the bill.

New Mexico's SunZia wind farm and power line — the largest renewable energy project ever built in the United States — took two decades to complete. Its transmission line, needed to transport wind power to cities in Arizona and California, faced opposition from birders, the military and tribes. Some disputes were resolved, but tribal concerns about impacts on historic sites remained unsettled and led to court battles that still have not been resolved even though the line was completed in June. A photograph credited to Austin Corona and Grist shows the SunZia transmission line during construction through Arizona's San Pedro Valley in September 2024. It was taken aboard a flight operated by Ecoflight.

Tribes, states and local groups would have less power to stop or delay some infrastructure projects, and projects could move forward with less time for public scrutiny. That could mean more power lines crossing landscapes and a greater risk that community concerns would not receive adequate consideration.

Experts expect the bill to lower electricity costs slightly over the next 15 years by making it easier to move cheap power between regions while helping states meet clean energy goals. Households and businesses would likely find it easier to sell electricity from rooftop solar and batteries onto the grid.

Most data centers would see little change because they are overwhelmingly constructed on private land overseen by local and state governments. Proposals on federal land exist, though they often involve significant legal issues the bill would not necessarily change. BAAJA could accelerate the power and pipeline infrastructure that benefits data centers, but it would require them to pay for needed grid upgrades and increase transparency around their electricity use. The bill could also make data centers' power cleaner by making it easier to connect to grids with clean energy rather than build polluting, off-grid gas turbines.

BAAJA would enable energy infrastructure generally, meaning fossil-fuel projects could grow alongside renewables. Projections from some organizations suggest the advantage would likely tip toward renewable energy, although predicting future energy markets is tricky. Some experts believe the bill would make it harder for future administrations to limit fossil-fuel projects, making emissions reductions more difficult.

Rhodium Group, an economic research firm, projects that natural gas pipelines would continue advancing at roughly their current pace while renewable energy deployment accelerates under faster permitting and greatly expanded transmission. Rhodium's modeling finds a modest emissions reduction even if natural gas prices drop more than renewables, with larger reductions if renewable energy becomes much cheaper than natural gas. Austin Corona's analysis in Grist argues that the bill's biggest climate effect would arguably be strengthening future clean energy initiatives: states pursuing clean electricity or electrification could more easily build the infrastructure they need, and future federal clean energy incentives could produce better results.

Corona also writes that Republicans could use faster permitting to speed fossil-fuel infrastructure while suppressing renewables. BAAJA attempts to limit presidents' ability to discriminate among energy sources, although recent amendments slightly weakened those provisions. Some opponents worry the provisions would not keep President Donald Trump from continuing to suppress wind power, while others worry they would be too successful and prevent Democratic presidents from phasing out fossil fuels.

Supporters include labor unions, clean and fossil fuel companies, a few tribes and tribal energy advocates, centrist policy organizations and some climate advocacy groups. Clean energy companies have been especially enthusiastic, arguing that permitting and transmission constraints have become some of the biggest obstacles to expanding renewable power. Opponents include environmental nonprofits, dozens of tribes, rural landowners, civil rights groups and other climate activists. Some opponents support the bill's transmission provisions but argue that its changes to environmental review would excessively weaken communities' and tribes' ability to scrutinize projects and seek alternatives.

The disagreement has split Democrats and the climate movement. Climate-focused Democratic senators Sheldon Whitehouse and Martin Heinrich co-wrote the bill, while Green New Deal sponsors Ed Markey and Mazie Hirono opposed it. Climate movement veterans Al Gore and Genevieve Guenther have opposed the legislation, while climate scientist Zeke Hausfather and utility watchdog David Pomerantz support it.

Neither camp is monolithic. Many opponents support permitting reform but believe Democrats should wait until January, when they will have majorities in both houses of Congress, to negotiate stronger protections for clean energy, communities and tribes. Some supporters counter that the country needs new clean energy infrastructure now and that there is no guarantee a better political opportunity will arrive.

Trump has said he supports the permitting reform effort, and the measure should become law if it makes it out of Congress. The unresolved questions are how many Republicans will be in Washington and whether Democrats can persuade a handful of their colleagues to vote against the bill. If BAAJA is not signed before the new year, it will die, and lawmakers would have to start over if they want to pass permitting reform in the next session.
