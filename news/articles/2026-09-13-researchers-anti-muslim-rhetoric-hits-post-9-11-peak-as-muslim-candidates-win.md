## Pew finds 76% of Republicans now link Islam to violence, up from 32%

Within hours of Abdul El-Sayed's victory in Michigan's Democratic US Senate primary, Sen. Tommy Tuberville of Alabama posted on X that the party had "just voted to send a TERRORIST" to Washington. Tuberville also called El-Sayed a "RADICAL ISLAMIST" who would "do everything he can to destroy America." Researchers who track anti-Muslim sentiment describe the posts as the opening shot in what they call the most concentrated wave of Islamophobic rhetoric in US electoral politics since the September 11, 2001 attacks — arriving at a moment when Muslim candidates are winning offices in unprecedented numbers.

The attacks extend from the party's top figures and across its elected ranks. President Donald Trump this year called El-Sayed a "jihadist" from the Oval Office and separately claimed — without naming anyone — that "jihadists" were being elected to office "all over the place." Vice President JD Vance called El-Sayed "very, very evil" at a Michigan rally and invoked his late grandfather to attack comments El-Sayed made in 2022 defending a legal challenge to an Oklahoma anti-Sharia ballot measure. Sen. Ted Cruz told Fox News that El-Sayed and New York City Mayor Zohran Mamdani were "Islamists" who "want Sharia law" and "hate Jews, they hate Christians, they hate capitalism, they hate America." Rep. Nancy Mace posted on X that "every single Muslim holding public office in America is a trojan horse, and a threat to both national security and our republic," and followed up by writing that Islam "must be removed from civil society in the US." In March, Rep. Randy Fine of Florida wrote that "We need more Islamophobia, not less. Fear of Islam is rational," while Rep. Andy Ogles of Tennessee posted that "Muslims don't belong in American society." Republican leadership has been mostly silent.

The current moment follows a documented historical pattern. Trump called in 2015, when running for president, for a "total and complete shutdown" on Muslims entering the US, and attempted to enact the policy via executive order in early 2017. After years of legal wrangling, the Supreme Court allowed Trump to implement a limited version of his travel restrictions for majority Muslim countries.

The Center for the Study of Organized Hate documented Islamophobic posts naming El-Sayed rising by nearly 1,900% in the week after his primary win, from an average of 155 to more than 3,000 per day. The wave of Muslim electoral success stands against that backdrop: four Muslim members of Congress, with a fifth likely on the way, compared with zero in 2001. Ghazala Hashmi was elected Virginia's first Muslim woman to statewide office as lieutenant governor this year. Mamdani was inaugurated as New York City's first Muslim mayor in January 2025. El-Sayed's primary win put him on track to potentially become the first Muslim ever elected to the US Senate.

Researchers describe a multiyear rise in the underlying attitudes. The Institute for Social Policy and Understanding's 2025 Islamophobia Index, a 100-point scale built using common anti-Muslim stereotypes, rose from 25 in 2022 to 33 among the general public in 2025, with the sharpest increases among white and Hispanic Americans. The Pew Research Center found this spring that 51% of US adults now say Islam is more likely than other religions to encourage violence, up from 25% six months after the 2001 attacks. The partisan gap has widened sharply: in 2002, 32% of likely Republican voters and 23% of Democrats said the same; today, 76% of Republicans and 29% of Democrats do. Anti-Muslim hate crimes reported to the FBI, which spiked to 481 in 2001 from 28 the year before, have never returned to pre-2001 levels, according to Pew. ISPU separately found that 63% of Muslims reported experiencing religious discrimination in the past year, the highest share of any faith group it measures.

The rhetoric has extended beyond individual candidates into policy fights. In Texas, Gov. Greg Abbott has spent much of the summer campaigning against what he calls the imposition of Islamic law, ordering a review of state grants to two major airports and referring both to federal regulators over privately funded wudu stations — the ritual washing facilities Muslims use before prayer — which he called illegal religious favoritism. Abbott has separately fought a proposed mosque-anchored housing development outside Dallas. Nationally, the Center for the Study of Organized Hate documented a coordinated campaign among 89 elected officials over 13 months, including 1,111 social media posts and eight bills, tied to a "Sharia Free America Caucus" that has grown to 65 members. Anti-Sharia campaigns are not new: close to two dozen states passed largely symbolic anti-Sharia laws in the early 2010s. "This isn't an issue. It's not something that's real," said Mobashra Tazamal, associate director of Georgetown University's Bridge Initiative, which studies Islamophobia globally. "It's constructed, and then these politicians claim to be tackling this constructed problem that they create."

Saher Selod, director of research at the Institute for Social Policy and Understanding, which has tracked anti-Muslim sentiment nationally for a decade, said the current moment is the worst her research has documented. "It's probably the worst of what we've seen," Selod said. "We're certainly seeing in 2025 and 2026, ahead of these midterms, really very Islamophobic ads — more so than what we saw after 9/11." The pattern, she said, is consistent with what researchers have documented since well before this election cycle. "With Abdul El-Sayed, we're seeing these kinds of intense Islamophobic attacks against him, as opposed to attacking his platform or critiquing his policies," Selod said. "It's a playbook. They're going to attack him for his religious identity to see if this will help pull voters away from him."

Tazamal described the shift as shockingly noticeable. "It is almost as if the veil has been lifted," she said. "It is so bigoted, and it's so crude, and there is no red line. It's just straight bigotry." She ties the current spike partly to political calculation. "Polling for Trump is low, and the GOP is the sitting party, and they haven't delivered on their promises," Tazamal said. "So instead of focusing on institutional failures and policies they haven't been able to put into place, they're stirring up hate and bigotry towards a certain group, which is a routine tactic that they do."

Researchers caution against treating every attack on El-Sayed or Mamdani as bigotry. El-Sayed drew criticism — including from Vance — after he responded to a March attack on a suburban Detroit synagogue by saying "hurt people hurt people," noting that relatives of the attacker in Lebanon had recently been killed in an Israeli airstrike. El-Sayed eventually apologized and said the remarks may have been misconstrued. He has also faced scrutiny over his public association with the left-wing streamer Hasan Piker, who mainstream Democrats and Republicans have attacked for past comments minimizing the September 11 attacks.

Mamdani has faced sustained criticism, including from rivals in his own mayoral race, over his refusal to unequivocally condemn the phrase "globalize the intifada," a phrase Jewish groups say amounts to a call for violence against Jews but one that he has never personally used. Mamdani subsequently said he would discourage the term after a rabbi told him it evokes memories of bus bombings and restaurant attacks for many Jewish New Yorkers. The controversy among Jewish organizations, rival campaigns and national Democrats was not, in the end, disqualifying in the eyes of voters who ultimately decided the race.

The devastation in Gaza, mass settler violence in the West Bank and the suppression of pro-Palestine speech in the US has brought an additional dimension to this discourse. US opinion on Israel has soured immensely in recent years, especially among Democrats, and criticism of Israeli policy by the likes of El-Sayed and Mamdani is cited by those describing Muslim politicians "jihadists." Mahmoud Khalil, Rümeysa Öztürk and other Muslim residents in the US were among the first swept up for pro-Palestinian campus advocacy, even though most of the students protesting were not Muslim.

That playbook resurfaced this week in New York, where Mamdani attended the ceremony marking Friday's 25th anniversary of the September 11 attacks at Ground Zero, as his predecessors have every year. A petition with more than 100,000 signatures, riddled with attacks, asked him not to. Rudy Giuliani, mayor of New York City at the time of the attacks, who is now closely aligned to Trump, claimed this month that Mamdani "doesn't agree that September 11 was an Islamic terrorist attack," and that his attendance "offends" him. Giuliani added that Muslims generally "have every desire to take us over … they want to dominate us. Mamdani is a clear supporter of that." Giuliani offered no evidence for either claim. Cheri Sparacio, one of the petition's organizers, whose husband died in the attacks, has said her objection is "not about the mayor's background or faith, but his public actions." Mamdani attended anyway, and on Friday morning, Mamdani and Giuliani shook hands at the ceremony.

Selod said the current moment cannot be separated from the post-September 11 surveillance infrastructure. "All of the surveillance programs that were put into place, your no-fly list, ICE — these are a product of 9/11," she said. "We didn't have Immigration and Customs Enforcement. We didn't have the TSA, the Transportation Security Administration. These were all put into place to protect the nation against threats of terrorism." That architecture has since been extended past its original target, Selod argued, pointing to the Boston Regional Intelligence Center, a post-September 11 "fusion center" built to detect terrorist plots. Local reporting and ACLU of Massachusetts records show the center has also been used to monitor Black Lives Matter demonstrators. "It's expanding beyond a Muslim population to target others," Selod said. "Muslims are one to two percent of the US population. My hope is when people start to see how it's expanding beyond that population, maybe they'll start really critiquing these programs."

ISPU polling suggests the sentiment tracks politics rather than faith. Americans who identify as somewhat or very conservative are 13 percentage points more likely than others to endorse anti-Muslim stereotypes, the institute found, while religiosity shows no correlation. Caleb Elfenbein, a Grinnell College professor who has mapped Islamophobic rhetoric for years, sees a small, organized network reactivating on a predictable schedule. "It really looks, and feels, like a very cynical effort to take advantage of a general sense of fear that can be activated at any given time," Elfenbein said.

Whether the rhetoric works for a Republican Party that polling indicates is likely to face heavy losses in November's midterm elections is the open question hanging over the campaign. Republicans are simultaneously contending with an unpopular war with Iran and rising costs that have squeezed their approval ratings. Whatever happens, Tazamal does not expect the party to abandon the effort. "I don't think the GOP is going to do away with it," she said.
