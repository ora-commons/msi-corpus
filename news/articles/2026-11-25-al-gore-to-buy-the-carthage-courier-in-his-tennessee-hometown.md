## Al Gore to buy the Carthage Courier in his Tennessee hometown

## Sale terms undisclosed as Gore's role in Courier operations remains unclear

The Lake family said Tuesday it will sell the Carthage Courier to former Vice President Al Gore in coming weeks, putting the 78-year-old former presidential candidate in line to own the weekly newspaper in his Tennessee hometown. The paper serves Smith County in central Tennessee, which has roughly 20,000 residents and sits about 55 miles (88 kilometers) east of Nashville. The Lake family has operated the weekly for five decades.

The sale terms were not disclosed. The announcement left Gore's operational role unclear, and his office and the Courier would not answer questions Tuesday or provide additional details. The Courier's announcement omitted Gore's political resume and presented him simply as a local man buying his small town's paper to keep it locally owned.

Gore characterized the purchase as a move that preserves an independent local voice in Carthage and Smith County as the news industry struggles with newspaper closures and growing news deserts. “Having spent my early years as a military reporter in Vietnam and later at the (Nashville) Tennessean, I am a firm believer that a free, independent local press is irreplaceable for the enduring health of our community,” Gore said in a statement.

Before entering politics, Gore spent six months as a military reporter in the U.S. Army during the Vietnam War. He returned to Tennessee and worked for a few years as an investigative reporter at The Tennessean before running for Congress in 1976.

His political career included service as a Democrat representing Tennessee in both chambers of Congress, service as President Bill Clinton's vice president from 1993 to 2001, and two presidential campaigns. After losing the 2000 presidential election to George W. Bush, Gore went on to win the Nobel Peace Prize for his climate activism and an Academy Award for the documentary *An Inconvenient Truth*.

The Courier's website notes that it covered the early political pursuits of Gore's father, Albert Gore Sr., who represented the state in the U.S. House and Senate.

Tim Franklin, chair of local news at Northwestern University's Medill School of Journalism, Media, Integrated Marketing Communications, said Carthage residents may have “some healthy skepticism” about a politician owning the paper. “But I would be surprised if the Carthage Courier becomes a mouthpiece for the Democratic Party,” he said. Franklin wondered whether Gore might write a column and whether the publication would remain narrowly focused on local news.

Politicians have a history of owning newspapers. Throughout the 1900s, several senators, governors and even a U.S. president, Warren Harding, had local papers in their business portfolios. More recently, consumer advocate Ralph Nader, one of Gore's former political rivals, helped launch a local newspaper in Winsted, Connecticut, in 2023; a national media company purchased it months later.

The sale is expected in the coming weeks, but the Lake family has not disclosed terms, and Gore's involvement in the Courier's operations remains unclear. Franklin's questions about a Gore column and the Courier's continued focus on local news remain open.
