## Nvidia shares rose more than 30% after Huang’s reinstatement

The Wall Street Journal framed President Donald Trump’s successful campaign to reinstate Nvidia co-founder Jensen Huang as chief executive around the claims that, in the AI era, some people are “too big to fail,” the “Imperial CEO” must be “protected at all costs,” and “the individual outweighs the institution.” The report described Nvidia as the AI chip maker “at the heart of the technology,” and its shares rose 31.48% Monday.

The reversal came only 11 days after Huang’s ouster. Nvidia’s board had removed him after revelations that he had two extramarital affairs: one with a subordinate at the company and another with a customer suspected of having links to Chinese intelligence. Huang had failed to disclose the indiscretions to the board, which The Wall Street Journal said created an organization full of corporate-governance red flags.

Trump made his defense directly to CNBC. “I believe that you have to protect our geniuses,” he said shortly after Nvidia announced Huang’s return. “We don’t have enough of them.” He also said, “He did nothing wrong.” The Wall Street Journal wrote, “To keep the AI play alive, Trump is effectively endorsing Founder Mode.”

Founder Mode, the report said, is “the growing dogma within Silicon Valley that founders of companies hold a unique power to create value.” Supporters argued that founders should be given special leeway as they pursue growth, innovation and disruption, and that their foibles should be overlooked because of the value they create. The Wall Street Journal said investors “largely support the philosophy when the returns go in their favor.”

Nvidia shares soared more than 30% on Monday after the company had lost about $1 trillion in market value while Huang was gone. The Wall Street Journal wrote that “the real catalyst for change seemed to be the market selloff.” Trump acknowledged the market calculus on Monday and pointed to Huang’s unique role in guiding future chip development. The Journal concluded, “Essentially, he offered Huang as a kind of market bailout.”

The Wall Street Journal interpreted Trump’s championing of Huang’s return as tying his second term and legacy to the rise of artificial intelligence and the investment boom around it, even as increasing signs emerged that the market had become overheated.

The Wall Street Journal wrote, “That Huang’s company was under federal investigation didn’t appear to matter.” Trump told CNBC, “He did nothing wrong.” The report likewise said Huang’s failure to disclose the affairs to Nvidia’s board and the resulting corporate-governance red flags did not alter Trump’s stance. His friends in the “New Boys Club”—Elon Musk and others in the pro-founder movement—argued that the trust he engendered among the industry’s “delicate supply chain” mattered more.

The Wall Street Journal said Huang’s biggest problem “would seem to be” that he “lacked a dual-class share structure to protect his power in the first place.” Unlike Elon Musk at SpaceX and Mark Zuckerberg at Meta Platforms, who have a tight grip on corporate decisions by virtue of their ownership stakes, Huang was vulnerable to pressure from a board “jittery about his scandal.”

The report added, “But with powerful friends, like Musk and Trump, even that didn’t matter.” Last week, Musk argued Huang was irreplaceable, “effectively the Michael Jordan of AI.”

With the backing of the White House, The Wall Street Journal wrote that Huang joined Musk and Zuckerberg among other “Imperial CEOs.” It added, “He’s now bigger than a board of directors, than shareholders, than the norms of corporate governance” and, “At least, for now, with Trump in power.”

The Wall Street Journal wrote, “That Huang oversaw a company that exploited a gap in U.S. export controls to the benefit of China didn’t matter.” Trump replied, “He’s in the business of selling chips.”

Some supporters argued that Huang was critical to ensuring the U.S. remains ahead of China in the race to develop AI. The Wall Street Journal described the position as a national-security argument suggesting “the country is at war for the future—a zero-sum game” that guides much of the public debate around AI policy.

The market rebound did not resolve Nvidia’s federal investigation or the export-control concerns surrounding the company, and it left broader questions over founder authority and White House influence over corporate governance.
