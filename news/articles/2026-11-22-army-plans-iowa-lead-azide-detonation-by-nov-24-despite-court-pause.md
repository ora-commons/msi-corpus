## Justice Department cites risk of unplanned explosion as temperatures fall

The Department of the Army told a federal court late Saturday that it intends to move ahead with detonating five tonnes of degraded explosives in Iowa despite a ruling to halt the operation indefinitely, and said it would invoke an emergency exclusion to skirt the court-issued pause. The plan was set out in a new court filing and would have the Army act by 24 November.

In the filing, the Justice Department notified the court that "To protect its personnel and the surrounding communities, the Army needs to take swift action." It added: "Protection of human health and the environment demand action now, not delay."

The detonation had originally been scheduled for Friday at the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant in Middletown, Iowa. A federal court suspended it that day after residents of the town expressed concern about the risk of toxic lead particles spreading across the surrounding area.

Environmental groups had filed lawsuits on Thursday to stop the blast, arguing the Army failed to consider safer alternatives.

The Army argued that a controlled detonation was necessary to protect the public from an unplanned explosion, a risk it said would become more likely as temperatures were set to fall in the coming days.

"There is an immediate risk that the lead azide at the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant will explode as temperatures are expected to drop below 30 degrees Fahrenheit (-1C) in the next several days," the filing states.

Lead azide is a highly volatile compound used as a detonator. Earlier this year, the ammunition plant found that the material had degraded over time to the point where it was now too risky to move, according to Iowa Public Radio.

Military and environmental officials determined that detonating the material in place was the safest way to eliminate the explosive safety hazard.

The Environmental Protection Agency, which granted an emergency permit to detonate 68 drums of the material, said it did so because the explosion risk "presented an imminent and substantial threat to human health and the environment."

The Iowa Environmental Council, one of the environmental groups suing to pause the detonation, argued that many Iowans living within the risk zone were unaware that the explosion was scheduled to happen. "There is no safe level of lead exposure, and Iowans were given just days to prepare for the November 20 detonation," the organization said in a press statement.

Earlier, the Army had warned residents to leave their homes for six hours on the original scheduled day of the explosion, for the duration of the blast and until the dust settled. An advisory warned "all humans" to avoid being outside in the affected areas while the demolition detonation was set to take place.

On Friday, the military said it was working with the Department of Health and Human Services and the EPA to ensure the explosion was done in the safest manner possible. The Army has not released details for the rescheduled detonation.


The weekend filing caps a week of litigation: MSI reported that the Army had [delayed the operation to Monday after two environmental groups sued](/articles/2026-11-20-u-s-army-delays-iowa-detonation-of-degraded-lead-azide-after-lawsuit/), and that the [8th U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals granted a request to put the EPA permit on hold](/articles/2026-11-20-appeals-court-stays-army-lead-azide-detonation-permit-in-iowa/).
