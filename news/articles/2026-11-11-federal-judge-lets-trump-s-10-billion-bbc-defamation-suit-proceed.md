## Spliced documentary editing may support actual-malice claim

A federal judge appointed by Donald Trump on Tuesday allowed the president's $10 billion defamation lawsuit against the BBC to proceed, partly denying the British broadcaster's effort to dismiss it. Florida district court judge Jeffrey T Kuntz said the BBC documentary "Trump: A Second Chance" gave Trump a plausible claim that the editing caused reputational harm around the election, though he made no finding that the BBC was liable.

The ruling concerned the BBC's motion to dismiss, and Kuntz expressly accepted the complaint's allegations as true for that stage. He wrote: “It would be difficult for the Court to accept a different framing without completely negating the significance and fragility of political elections.” The order allowed the central defamation claim to continue but did not decide the underlying merits.

The lawsuit stems from a Panorama documentary that spliced together disparate comments Trump made before the 6 January US Capitol insurrection. The BBC has apologized for the edit. The sequence drew from statements nearly 55 minutes apart and was presented as a 12-second segment; the BBC argued that it was ordinary editing to condense a 70-minute speech.

Kuntz said he was not persuaded by that explanation. “On its face, the editing may independently establish the plausibility of actual malice,” he wrote. He reasoned that the long gap between the statements made it plausible to infer that the BBC knew or should have known its segment would not be truthful. The judge described the change as creating something different from what Trump had said, rather than trimming around the edges.

The court's language was framed as plausibility, not a final factual finding. Kuntz said Trump had sufficiently pleaded actual malice, the legal standard for a public figure to win a defamation case, but Trump still had to prove the allegation at trial.

Kuntz also relied in part on the BBC's record of past errors in its coverage. He said those examples could be probative of the broadcaster's state of mind regarding recklessness because of the BBC's non-action toward false statements about Trump. He also said Trump's allegations that the BBC departed from journalistic standards supported a plausible claim of more than mere negligence.

The BBC responded that it was surprised by the decision but respected the court's authority and noted that the case would proceed to the next stage. The broadcaster said it remained confident in its position and would continue responding through the legal process.

Trump called the ruling a “huge victory” on Truth Social. He wrote, “We will continue fighting against, and defeating, all of those who try to deceive the American People!”

The court did grant the BBC partial victories. Kuntz dismissed Trump's claim that the documentary violated Florida's Deceptive and Unfair Trade Practices Act. He also ruled that Trump would have to show “actual damages” from the broadcast, noting that the documentary was not shown in Florida.

Another procedural deadline remains in the case. In late October, a Florida magistrate judge ruled that Trump would have to sit for a deposition conducted by lawyers for the BBC by 11 December or face possible sanctions.

Taken together, the order leaves the core defamation case active, dismisses one state-law claim and requires proof of actual damages. The BBC must still answer the actual-malice allegation at trial, while Trump must prove the elements necessary to win his defamation claim.
