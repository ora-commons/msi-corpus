## One in five Neets have a mental health condition, DfE data shows

A review led by former Labour minister Alan Milburn will describe the number of young people not in work, at school or in college as both an economic and a moral crisis when it is published later. The 241-page report will set out a raft of recommendations for the government to consider, the BBC reported. Ahead of its release, four young people not in education, employment or training — the group dubbed "Neets" — told BBC education reporter Hayley Clarke why they cannot get a job and how the government could help them get one; Branwen Jeffreys and Kate McGough contributed additional reporting.

The review comes as anxiety and mental health problems have risen sharply among young people. About one in five Neets have a mental health condition, according to 2025 Department for Education data — more than double the rate in 2012. Milburn's interim report, published in the summer, said 45% of Neets reported having a disability. He called the situation for young disabled people "devastating".

It was reported ahead of the report's publication that Milburn would recommend young people should no longer be able to claim those additional health-related benefits, as part of a major overhaul of the welfare system. Lots of Neets receive Universal Credit: for under-25s, the standard rate is £338.58 a month, which Michael receives. For some people who cannot work because of a health condition, disability or long-term illness, an additional £217.26 to £429.80 per month may be available.

Michael's story is not unique: there has been a large rise in anxiety and mental health problems in young people. Michael, 19, who lives with his dad in the Wirral, receives the under-25 Universal Credit rate. After signing up for Universal Credit, he was put in touch with the Merseyside Youth Association to help him find a job. He says mentors there have helped him improve his mental health. He dropped out of college during his A-levels after his social anxiety "got really bad" and spent a year at home, leaving him trapped inside his bedroom. It is "very easy" to fall out of the system, he says, if there is little support available.

Jobs in his area are scarce, he says, and those that do come up tend to go to older people with more experience. Michael says he is trapped in a cycle of not having the experience needed to build more. "Everything is experience, experience, experience - that's the one thing that I always used to hear when going for jobs on my own without the help," Michael said. The BBC followed Michael for a day, meeting him after his 05:00 start for a work-experience shift at a local supermarket that the Merseyside Youth Association helped him get. Michael says getting his dog Dave motivated him to get out of the house and speak to people more.

More recently, he has been doing shifts at the Merseyside Youth Centre's on-site cafe to gain more much-needed experience. His hope is to be in a steady job earning money this time next year, with space to have his own art studio one day. "From three months ago I'm a different person," he said. "I can do stuff, keep going." He acknowledges some young people may be "taking advantage" of the benefits system, but says most "just want the money to get by so they can get a job".

Maariyah, 22, a history and politics graduate, finished her university course last summer and is unemployed, looking for any paid job after working a temporary six-month contract for a housing association earlier this year. Her dream is to work in museums, but she is applying for roles in retail and restaurants, alongside unpaid work experience opportunities in the heritage sector. She spends hours each day in her local library — where she says she feels more able to focus than at home — submitting at least 20 tailored job applications a week, including tailoring her CV and cover letter, she says. Those who have not achieved GCSE-level qualifications are more likely to remain Neet for longer periods, though an estimated one in 10 Neets are university graduates.

Voluntary roles have also turned her away, citing too many applicants or too little experience. "I'm actively going out of my way to do unpaid work, only to get rejected from these positions," Maariyah said. Not hearing back from employers "happens so often now that it's like second nature", she said, adding that she finds it frustrating that many of her applications are likely to be screened by AI before a real person sees them. On the day the BBC met her, Maariyah headed to the job centre for her Universal Credit appointment after a library job application session. She says she has noticed an improvement in the support she gets there since she first received the benefit in 2025, and that she is due to receive some mandatory hospitality training she is "really glad" to have. She feels there is a growing sense of disillusionment among others her age, and she says being on benefits has become normalised because it is so hard to find a job. That makes her feel that going to a good university, getting certain grades and doing extracurricular activities "doesn't make a difference in the end". "I would like employers to give young people a chance to gain experience and take a chance on hard-working people who at the end of the day want a job so that they can contribute to our economy and contribute to their own lives," she said.

Matthieu, 19, from Edgware in north-west London, is autistic and volunteers at a gardening club and a computer repair shop. The BBC met him in Finsbury Park, where he was helping to mulch newly planted trees; working in nature is calming, he says. He is looking for paid work with the support of the charity Ambitious About Autism and his mentor, Annie, and previously completed a year-long supported internship with the Whittington hospital, where speaking to people face-to-face helped his confidence and he enjoyed being part of the team. At his family home, his mentor Annie told the BBC that not having maths and English GCSE qualifications is a barrier to finding employment. Those with learning difficulties, including autism, make up an estimated 12% of Neets, Department for Education data shows.

Matthieu says he is more of a manual worker and wants work in a factory, warehouse or doing deliveries, so that he can contribute financially for his family. To make work accessible to him, he says, would require an employer to take their time with him. "I don't learn that quickly," he said. "Honestly, you might be surprised what the people can do when you take the time with them and give them that chance." He says he remains hopeful because of the support from his internship and job coach. Many employers say they cannot afford to do so following rises to National Insurance contributions and the minimum wage, which is increasing further as part of announcements made in October's Budget. Some businesses have told the BBC they are struggling to provide new jobs for young people due to these changes, as well as other factors like rising energy bills.

Alex, 20, from St Helens, has been looking for work for a year. She struggles with anxiety, is looking for roles as a cleaner or in retail, and shares caring responsibilities for her mum with other family members. The BBC met her at Spear, an employment and mindset coaching programme, where Alex says her dream is to work with animals. Alex says she had been studying Level 3 animal management at college before dropping out last year because of her mental health. "I was so burnt out," she said. "I was struggling to get out of bed, I couldn't do anything. I was dragging myself to college. I was like, 'No, I need to have a break for a bit and try and figure out what to do after that.'"

She now works on building up her resilience and on job applications with her mentor, Grace, at Spear; the two work on her CV during one-to-one sessions. Alex says she is applying for "anything and everything", including cleaning and retail roles, and wants to get off benefits. She is also currently receiving Universal Credit, which she says is "all the money that I've got until I get a job and I'm earning more". Asked about potential benefit cuts for young people out of work, she said not being able to access benefits would "make life so much worse". Finding work in a place like St Helens is harder still, she said, because many businesses in the town centre have "gone or moved or shut down". "Lots of people think, 'well, young people just don't want to work,'" she said. "But we do. We so desperately want to work. Places don't want to hire us."

Milburn has already said the system isn't working for everyone, and that serious action is needed to avoid young Neets becoming a "lost generation".
