## Fifth Circuit sided with Hobby Distillers Association members

The U.S. Supreme Court agreed Monday to review the Fifth Circuit decision after two federal appeals courts issued conflicting rulings on Congress's 1868 ban on distilling spirits in private homes. The decisions came in April, only 11 days apart: a three-judge Sixth U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals panel upheld the federal ban in John Ream's case, while a Fifth Circuit panel ruled for Hobby Distillers Association members and concluded the prohibition fell outside the scope of Congress' taxing power under the Constitution. The Supreme Court chose the Fifth Circuit decision for review, which was the Justice Department's preference.

The case asks whether Congress had constitutional authority to impose that prohibition more than 150 years after the law was enacted. Federal law makes home distilling a crime punishable by a maximum sentence of five years in prison and a $10,000 fine.

John Ream is a brewpub owner from Newark, Ohio, who wants to distill whisky at home for his personal consumption. The other plaintiffs are members of the Texas-based Hobby Distillers Association. Plaintiffs in both cases describe the 1868 law as an antiquated relic of Reconstruction-era politics and argue Congress overstepped its taxation powers when it enacted the home-distilling ban as part of a tax reform measure designed to curb fraud by commercial distillers.

Robert Alt, an attorney for the plaintiffs, said they were “thrilled” that the Supreme Court agreed to hear the case. Alt said in a statement: “Our country’s founders would be rolling over in their graves to learn that a pastime in which even George Washington partook, which is as American as apple pie and certainly a lot older, has quite absurdly been turned into a federal crime.”

Attorneys for the plaintiffs in both cases argue Congress could ban a broad array of activities inside private homes — including cooking, gardening and remote work — under the government's interpretation of the 19th-century law.

The plaintiffs' lawyers wrote: “The importance of this case and the questions it presents could hardly be overstated.” They added: “It presents fundamental questions of the extent of federal power and the powers reserved to the states and the people.”

Justice Department attorneys counter that Congress properly restricted where spirits can be distilled to prevent producers from evading federal excise taxes. They warn that lifting the ban would violate Supreme Court precedent and erode congressional powers.

Justice Department attorneys also emphasize the history of tax evasion. They said federal regulation and taxation of distilled-spirit production is as old as Congress itself and that Congress outlawed home distilling to prevent producers from evading taxes or government inspections. In a filing, they wrote: “And because the location restrictions were enacted only after lengthy experience with tax evasion, upholding those restrictions would not open the door to hypothetical laws restricting other conduct lacking that history.”

The government says Ream asked the Supreme Court to resolve an “additional question” of whether location restrictions on home distilling exceed the scope of Congress' power to regulate interstate commerce. But the government urged the high court to avoid that question, saying it was not part of Ream's case or the government's current arguments in either case.
