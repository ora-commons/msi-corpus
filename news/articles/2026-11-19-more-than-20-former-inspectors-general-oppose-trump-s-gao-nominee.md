## Peters alleges OMB refused GAO cooperation; commission review was skipped

More than 20 former inspectors general earlier in the week signed a letter opposing Eric Ueland’s confirmation as comptroller general of the Government Accountability Office. The letter said the credibility of oversight depends upon strict adherence to statutory safeguards and leadership defined by proven, nonpartisan fidelity to objective evidence. “Mr. Ueland’s nomination fails on both counts,” the letter said. It was sent to Sen. Gary Peters and Sen. Rand Paul of Kentucky, the Republican chairman of the Senate Committee on Homeland Security and Governmental Affairs.

During his confirmation hearing in Washington, Peters, the ranking Democratic lawmaker on the committee, said the White House Office of Management and Budget has refused to cooperate with GAO audits during Ueland’s tenure. “The GAO is part of being that umpire,” Peters said. “You’re clearly not that umpire.”

Peters also said voters have made clear they want stronger checks and balances in Washington. He said moving ahead with Ueland’s nomination would do “irreversible damage to the GAO.”

By statute, a 10-member commission recommends at least three GAO candidates to the president after an extensive review and interview process. That lengthy review did not happen this time. Democrats voiced concerns about Ueland’s independence and objected that the White House was advancing him without the commission review.

Ueland, of Virginia, is deputy director of the White House Office of Management and Budget. The White House is seeking his confirmation as GAO’s next head before Republicans lose control of the Senate. If confirmed, Ueland would oversee the agency for a 15-year term. The GAO has repeatedly found the administration’s spending freezes and impoundments unlawful over the past two years.

At his hearing, Ueland sought to assure skeptical Democrats that he would not serve as a rubber stamp for an administration that has repeatedly clashed with lawmakers over the power of the purse. Ueland said he would act in a nonpartisan way and fully discharge the responsibilities of the comptroller general, citing more than two decades of work in the executive and legislative branches of the federal government. He also told lawmakers, “I’m not here as a plant, a stooge, a lackey for anybody.”

The GAO works for Congress as its primary investigative arm and auditor. With a budget of more than $900 million and a workforce of nearly 3,000 employees, the agency examines how taxpayer dollars are spent and provides nonpartisan recommendations to help the federal government work more efficiently.

It remains unclear how quickly Republicans could attempt to advance Ueland’s nomination to a final vote. Republicans have confirmed batches of nominees during this Congress with a simple majority and a shortened period of debate, and some Republicans have voiced concerns about using that process for Ueland.

Sen. Thom Tillis, R-N.C., said he would oppose including Ueland in a group package of nominees. Sen. James Lankford, R-Okla., said he believed Ueland was a great candidate to lead the GAO. Lankford also said he believed the White House followed the letter of the law in nominating Ueland, though he was not sure it had followed “the spirit of the law.”

Those questions remain unresolved in the Senate: whether a deputy Office of Management and Budget director can lead Congress’s auditor without compromising the agency’s independence and whether the White House satisfied the statutory safeguards governing the selection process. The former inspectors general’s letter emphasizes strict adherence to safeguards and nonpartisan fidelity to objective evidence, while Democrats have challenged Ueland’s independence and the selection process.
