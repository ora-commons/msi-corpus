## Lawsuit seeks restoration of hundreds of removed park items

In the soft light of dawn, an Ohio tourist studied an interpretive sign at the summit of Cadillac Mountain in Acadia National Park, reading about how magma formed the ground beneath her feet. One marker she and the park’s more than 4 million annual visitors could no longer see explained the mountain’s sacred significance to the Wabanaki people in the region. It said, “Today, visitors from everywhere find meaning on the mountain.”

Park staff took down the summit marker on orders from Interior Secretary Doug Burgum. According to the National Parks Conservation Association, it was among 30 markers removed from Acadia in a Trump administration campaign targeting materials the administration deemed disparaging to American history. The takedown also unraveled some of the trust built over decades between Acadia and the Wabanaki people.

A nonprofit coalition of conservation, history and science organizations filed a lawsuit in February against the federal government, seeking to restore the Acadia signs and hundreds of other items. Under Burgum’s 2025 campaign, signs, books, films and exhibits covering topics such as slavery, climate change and the massacre of Native Americans were flagged and removed from national parks and monuments nationwide.

Angelo Villagomez of the Center for American Progress said, “Some of it’s been put back, and then some of it has been removed again.” His team examined a database leaked earlier that year and found that a third of national parks had flagged exhibits featuring history or science. Of those, Black and Indigenous history were most targeted.

Portions of all flagged material have since been reviewed and cataloged by a committee led by Joy Beasley, a National Park Service associate director. In a June affidavit, she wrote that the spreadsheet was “unlikely to capture every removal, revision, or replacement.” The Interior Department did not respond to Associated Press questions about the current status of sign and exhibit removals or the ongoing lawsuit.

Todd Martin, a National Parks Conservation Association plaintiff, said the parks “are America’s storytellers.” “It’s their duty to preserve and interpret American history, the good, the bad, and the ugly.” Jenny McBurney, a government publications librarian at the University of Minnesota and co-founder of Save Our Signs, said documenting revised signs had become more important. “People are going to go to the parks and think that they will get to learn history there when in fact they will be learning an incomplete history,” she said.

The Wabanaki Confederacy, also known as “the people of the dawnland,” has inhabited the northern seaboard for more than 10,000 years. The Wabanaki people were the first known stewards of land that includes much of the park. The land encompassing much of the park was a gathering place to trade, hunt, fish and pick sweetgrass.

Some removed signs were developed over years of partnerships with organizations including Friends of Acadia, the Schoodic Institute and the Native Plant Trust, with consultation from citizens of the Wabanaki Nations. The Wabanaki know the mountain in Maine as Wapuwoc — “white mountain of the first light.” From October to March, Cadillac Mountain is the first place to see the sunrise in the continental United States.

Maulian Bryant, the Wabanaki Alliance’s executive director, said, “Getting to a place where our language is in these parks and it is educational … erasing that or setting that back has big implications for how far we’ve come.” She said the removed signs reflected “the good stuff.” “These signs don’t say 96% of Wabanaki people died from smallpox, or there were 26 nations and now there’s four,” she said. “If we really want to get into the real history, that’s a whole other slice of cake.”

Other signs removed from Cadillac Mountain’s summit addressed climate change. They told visitors that “Acadia’s future will be warmer and wetter” and that “In the last century, average annual precipitation has increased by six inches.”

During their tenure under the Biden administration, former National Park Service Director Chuck Sams and former Interior Secretary Deb Haaland — the first Native Americans to hold those positions — aimed to reflect the experiences of all Americans throughout the national parks. Before leaving his post in February 2025, Sams saw parts of that work unraveled by the current administration. Sams called the “attempt to rewrite history into a single narrative … also counter to our democracy. And counter to the idea that we are a plural nation.”

Despite job cuts, threats of land reduction, talks of privatization and even demolition within some parks, Indigenous groups and park partners have persevered. In Maine, members of the Wabanaki Confederacy — comprised of the Maliseet, Mi’kmaq, Passamaquoddy, Penobscot and Abenaki principal nations — carry on the cultural traditions and spiritual practices that helped them maintain sovereignty and a connection to their ancestral land after settlers arrived. That includes sharing their culture with tourists.

Penobscot Nation citizen Jennifer Neptune said she had mixed emotions about the attempted erasure of her people from Acadia and their ancestral homeland. “It’s hard to get good signage in national monuments, in national parks,” she said. “It’s a real gut punch when it (removal) happens, not just for Indigenous people, but for Black people, for the people whose stories don’t get shared.” Over the past decade, she said, the National Park Service helped bring Wabanaki voices to the table and create Indigenous-designed spaces.

In partnership with Acadia, the privately owned Abbe Museum, which is largely overseen by Wabanaki people, hosts a program that brings Native voices into the park through music, arts and culture. Nick Bear and members of a Wabanaki drum circle perform at Cadillac Mountain annually as part of the collaboration. “That’s just like bringing those ceremonies right back to the original sites,” he said.

In July, Passamaquoddy basket maker Lydia Soctomah demonstrated traditional Wabanaki basketry using pounded ash bark and sweetgrass outside the Sieur de Monts Nature Center. She said the ash tree and sweetgrass are part of the Wabanaki creation story. “Making a basket is worth 1,000 memories for me,” Soctomah said. “I was always told it holds a part of your spirit.” Her grandmother was a well-known basket maker.

Access to sweetgrass has long been difficult for Wabanaki people who were pushed from coastal lands onto reservations, and it has been nearly nonexistent in Acadia since the park’s creation in 1916. Over the past decade, park staff and Wabanaki members have collaborated to change that.

In 2017, scientist Suzanne Greenlaw, a citizen of the Houlton Band of Maliseet Indians, partnered with the U.S. Forest Service, Acadia’s Schoodic Institute and 14 traditional gatherers to conduct a Wabanaki-led sweetgrass study in hopes of allowing tribal access. “It was really impactful because we had never had those kinds of co-stewardship relationships,” Greenlaw said. She added that those relationships, built over time, have not changed.

By the end of the Biden administration, there were over 400 co-stewardship or co-management agreements with tribes, including 159 within the National Park Service alone, Sams said. Programs in Acadia were among them. He said the programs allow tribes to fulfill their obligation to protect, preserve and enhance natural resources while the federal government fulfills its obligation to allow tribal access for hunting, fishing and gathering foods and medicines.

Sams cautiously hopes there will be a rebuilding period for the National Park Service under the next administration. “There are always ebbs and flows with every administration,” he said. “Nobody has seen that more than American Indian tribes.”
