## GOP seeks first Minnesota statewide win since 2006

Minnesota voters head to the polls Tuesday to elect a new governor and a new U.S. senator, after retirement announcements by two prominent Democrats caused a reshuffling throughout the ballot. Voters will also decide races for other federal and state offices, including contests across the state that could determine control of both chambers of the narrowly divided state Legislature.

The state has been a focus of President Donald Trump's attention in his second term, as the Republican president has directed much of his criticism at Minnesota and its Democratic leaders.

In the Senate race, Democratic Lt. Gov. Peggy Flanagan and Republican Michele Tafoya, a former sports broadcaster, are among four candidates vying to replace Democratic Sen. Tina Smith, who announced in 2025 that she would not seek a second full term.

Flanagan defeated Rep. Angie Craig in the Aug. 11 primary, 59% to 39%, in one of several Democratic primary matchups this year where progressive candidates outperformed establishment favorites. Smith backed Flanagan in the primary, as did Sens. Elizabeth Warren of Massachusetts and Bernie Sanders of Vermont and Rep. Ilhan Omar of the 5th Congressional District. Outgoing Gov. Tim Walz did not make an endorsement.

Tafoya defeated eight candidates in her primary, including former Navy SEAL Adam Schwarze, who had the state party's endorsement. Trump did not back any candidate in the primary but endorsed Tafoya on Monday for the general election.

In the race for governor, Democratic Sen. Amy Klobuchar and Republican state House Speaker Lisa Demuth are among three candidates running to replace Walz. Demuth was the top vote-getter in a seven-candidate primary field that included prominent 2020 election denier and MyPillow founder Mike Lindell, who had Trump's endorsement. Klobuchar faced minimal opposition for the Democratic nomination.

All seats in both chambers of the state Legislature are up in 2026. Democrats hold a one-seat advantage in the 67-member state Senate. In the 134-member state House, Democrats and Republicans were deadlocked at 67 seats each until a Republican member vacated his seat after the session adjourned for the year.

Republicans have not won a statewide contest in Minnesota since Gov. Tim Pawlenty's 2006 reelection, and the last Republican to win a U.S. Senate race in the state was Norm Coleman in 2002. Trump lost Minnesota in all three of his presidential campaigns, with his best showing in 2016, when he came within 1.5 percentage points of victory; he lost by about 4 points in 2024, receiving 46.7% of the vote.

In statewide races, Democrats typically post big wins in the Minneapolis-St. Paul area in Hennepin and Ramsey counties, and they typically carry the Duluth area in the northeast and the Rochester area in the southeast, while Republicans have performed best in smaller, more rural counties across the state.

Polls close at 8 p.m. CT, which is 9 p.m. ET. The ballot includes U.S. Senate, U.S. House, governor, secretary of state, auditor, attorney general, state Senate, state House and a statewide ballot measure. About 2.5 million votes were cast in Minnesota's 2022 election for governor, and about 27% of that vote was cast early in person or by mail.

Counties are expected to release results from early in-person and mail voting throughout the night, along with results from in-person Election Day voting, and the first vote reports of the night are expected to include results from all vote types. Hennepin County, the state's most populous, expects to release complete results from all vote types in its first vote update, while Ramsey and Dakota counties are expected to release relatively few results in their first updates. In the 2024 general election, the Associated Press first reported results at 9:08 p.m. ET, eight minutes after polls closed; the vote count reached the halfway point at 11:40 p.m. ET, and the last update of the night came at 5:19 a.m. ET with more than 99% of total votes counted.

The Associated Press does not make projections and will declare a winner only when it determines there is no scenario that would allow a trailing candidate to close the gap. If a race has not been called, the AP will continue to cover any newsworthy developments, such as candidate concessions or declarations of victory, while making clear that it has not yet declared a winner and explaining why.

Recounts in Minnesota are not automatic, but the losing candidate may request one under certain circumstances. A publicly funded recount can be requested in federal, judicial or state constitutional offices, like governor, if the margin between the winning and losing candidate is less than 0.25% of the vote; for state legislative races, the required margin is less than 0.5% of the vote. In races in which fewer than 400 votes are cast, the margin is 10 votes or fewer. Candidates may pay for a recount in races in which the vote margin is beyond the threshold for a publicly funded recount. The AP may declare a winner in a race that is subject to a recount if it can determine the lead is too large for a recount or legal challenge to change the outcome.
