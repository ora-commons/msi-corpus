## Earthjustice pursued two lawsuits challenging the American Samoa lease sale

The Marine Minerals Administration, a body set up within the Interior Department and dedicated to ocean mining, said Westone U.S. Ventures and Adepth Minerals made the successful bids on two lots covering 31.5 million acres of seabed around American Samoa. Each lot carried a $3 million minimum price. The leases allow the companies to map, explore and potentially mine their allotted areas.

The sale was the Trump administration’s first-ever deep-sea mining lease sale. It covered waters around American Samoa, a Pacific island territory about halfway between Hawaii and New Zealand, and was intended to unlock new sources of metals and minerals for the United States.

Secretary of the Interior Doug Burgum called the deal a historic step for America’s energy and mineral security. “A dependable critical minerals supply chain is essential to American manufacturing and the technologies that power our economy and national defense,” Burgum said.

Environmental groups have argued that deep-sea mining endangers one of the last remaining untouched habitats. Local opposition in the Pacific has been strong.

Earlier in the week, a federal judge in Hawaii struck down approvals the Trump administration granted ahead of the lease sale, ruling that the government violated the Endangered Species Act. Judge Jill Otake wrote that the government had “failed to even attempt to consider the potential effects on ESA-listed species.” Earthjustice had brought a lawsuit before Otake’s ruling. After the ruling, the environmental group said Thursday’s auction was unlawful as a result.

Earthjustice also represented a council of Samoan chiefs and community groups in a separate lawsuit filed Oct. 19, before Otake’s ruling. The groups argued that the Interior Department’s Marine Minerals Administration lacked the authority to auction the seabed in U.S. territories.

“More than 76,000 public comments, repeated calls for meaningful consultation, and now a federal court ruling that the government violated the Endangered Species Act,” said Sabrina Suluai-Mahuka, founder of Finafinau, American Samoa’s youth and environment nonprofit. “Yet here we are, watching our ocean being auctioned to the highest bidder.”

The sale came roughly a year and a half after President Trump signed an executive order calling for deep-sea mining in U.S. and international waters. Since then, the United States signed agreements with the Cook Islands and Japan to partner on deep-sea mineral exploration.

The seafloor around American Samoa and the Cook Islands is scattered with small rocks known as polymetallic nodules, which are about the size of a potato. The nodules contain metals like cobalt, manganese and nickel, which are key to producing the batteries needed for electric vehicles, drones and data centers.

Advocates said deep-sea mining would provide a source of minerals away from China, which controls much of the battery metal supply chain. “China is moving forward, and the United States should as well,” said Robbie Diamond, executive chairman of Glomar Minerals, a deep-sea mining company.

On Thursday, the Senate passed the Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act of 2026. The legislation includes a provision to amend permitting laws and expedite energy projects even if they could potentially harm endangered species, including offshore projects such as oil drilling and deep-sea mining.
