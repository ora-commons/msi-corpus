## Parents of infants too young for the vaccine curtail travel and errands

Measles was effectively eliminated in the United States more than 25 years ago, and health officials revoked that status this week, NPR host Scott Simon said in the interview. More than 5,000 cases have been reported just this year.

Dr. Joanna Parga-Belinkie, a practicing neonatologist in Philadelphia and a spokesperson for the American Academy of Pediatrics, told Simon the country's position follows a steady decline in how many people are vaccinated. "We need 95% of the community to be vaccinated to keep the spread of measles at bay, and we've seen an erosion in that vaccine rate," she said.

She described the vaccine itself as highly protective. "It's 97% effective in preventing measles when you get the two doses," Parga-Belinkie said.

The practical burden falls hardest on households with babies who are not yet eligible for the standard schedule. Kendra Doty, a new mother in Washington, D.C., told Simon her 2-month-old has not been out much — which means she has not been out much either. The family asked their pediatrician about vaccinating him early.

"He can't be vaccinated for about a year, and it's changing where we take him and whether we go see family," Doty said. "So it's been a little bit hard."

Doty said she would like to visit family in Idaho but is holding off for now. "I don't really know anything about the measles - just that it, you know, used to not be a thing that we needed to worry about," she said.

Caitlin Jones lives in Chester County, Pennsylvania, which Simon described as a major center of the outbreak. Jones and her husband had planned to take their 4-month-old to a family reunion in the Caribbean this Thanksgiving, but their pediatrician discouraged putting the baby through an airport.

"The larger family is still going, but we are no longer going 'cause we can't go to the Philadelphia Airport," Jones said. Her daily routines have changed as well: "I can't take my son to any, like, reading activities at the library. I can't take him into any stores. I can't go and get food. I can't go shopping. It kind of is very reminiscent of COVID times."

Parga-Belinkie said that account landed hard. "It was hard to listen to that family that's out in Chester County because they're in the epicenter of our very large outbreak here in Pennsylvania," she said. The first thing she tells families, she said, is that she is sorry. "I am sorry that our community rates are so low that we're seeing a spread of this virus that people haven't seen," she said, adding that vigilance when taking babies out is warranted.

She also pointed to an option that parents may not know about: vaccination can begin earlier than the standard schedule. "You can get the vaccination as early as 6 months old," Parga-Belinkie said. That dose does not count toward the two doses that produce 97% immunity, which are usually recommended around age 1, she said. The second dose was once given at age 4, she said, but is now recommended earlier for children in areas with active measles spread.

For families who are traveling, Parga-Belinkie advised planning ahead. "Make sure you get that vaccine two weeks before you travel so that you can make sure to have immunity if you do travel," she said.

She said distance from an outbreak offers limited reassurance. "It's such a contagious virus. And it can travel and spread very easily," she said. "So even if it's not in your community, just the visit of one person with measles to your community can change all of that."

Kate Anderson, who dropped off her 5-month-old and 2-year-old at a daycare in Washington, D.C., told Simon the calculus is difficult even within one household. "I feel nervous, and I know that our younger hasn't had the vaccine," Anderson said. "But you can't really prevent your older child from bringing home things to your younger child."

Looking toward the end of fall and into winter, Parga-Belinkie said the season itself is a concern. "Measles is, and had been in the past, typically a winter virus. So I am worried that we may see more cases going through the winter," she said. She said she also worries about other vaccine-preventable diseases, "in particular, as a doctor that takes care of babies, I worry about things like pertussis, for example, which requires vaccination as early as 2 months of age."

For parents who are reluctant, Parga-Belinkie recommended finding a clinician who will take questions and steering toward established medical sources rather than what circulates online. "I would say to find a healthcare professional that they can really talk to about the vaccine, to look at resources from the American Academy of Pediatrics, because there's a lot of bad information or confusing information online about vaccination," she said. She also pointed to the Children's Hospital of Philadelphia Vaccine Education Center, where, she said, families who cannot find a clinician nearby can talk with doctors about vaccines.
