## Communities will shape remediation work funded by the commitment

Anglo-Australian miner Rio Tinto has agreed to voluntarily contribute US$250 million (A$370 million) toward remediation of the abandoned Panguna gold and copper mine on Bougainville, an island in Papua New Guinea. The commitment, reached after years of consultation with landowners and government, is intended to move the work from assessment toward practical remediation, but it will not fully address the site’s environmental damage and physical hazards. The contribution is being seen as a major step toward rehabilitation.

Mark Davies, Rio Tinto’s chief safety and technical officer, described the fund as an “important milestone … intended to support the transition from the assessment to practical remedy implementation.” He said community engagement would identify priorities and that Rio Tinto remained committed to working with communities on activities supported by the fund. The company was forced from Bougainville and has never returned, though it has participated in a detailed consultation process in recent years.

Theonila Roka Matbob, a former cabinet minister in Bougainville’s autonomous government and a Panguna community leader from Mako ’Osi below the mine, called the fund a vital contribution toward repairing the “devastation left by the Panguna mine.” Roka Matbob said a lot of work remained ahead, but the fund gave residents a chance to find solutions to “the huge problems we have been living with since the days of the mine’s operation.”

Roka Matbob also said no monetary amount can undo the environmental damage, but described the fund as a foundation for current environmental work and investment in children’s futures. She said the most fundamental remediation was to improve “accessibility” for people living around Panguna. She identified access to safe, secure drinking water and infrastructure, including bridges, as an immediate need. Bridges could improve residents’ access to trade, schools and health care, she said.

Ishmael Toroama, president of Bougainville and a leading figure in the island’s long-running independence movement, said the money would make a “meaningful difference” to villages and communities affected by mine pollution. He said the work could benefit both people living now and future generations, and that continued engagement with communities would be needed so it reflected local priorities and delivered lasting outcomes.

The commitment emerged from the Panguna Mine Legacy Impact Assessment consultation, which began in response to a human rights complaint by the Australian-based Human Rights Law Centre, with Roka Matbob as the lead complainant. Keren Adams, the group’s legal director, said establishing the fund had taken years of work. She called the effort “a testament to the courage and persistence of community leaders” and cited the willingness of all parties to engage in “difficult but constructive dialogue.” She said the funding created “a real opportunity” to address an environmental disaster that had remained unresolved for decades.

The mine pit was carved from a mountain in central Bougainville and remains surrounded by vast pillars of rubble at risk of collapse. The Jaba River, which flows from the mine’s tailings, runs bright blue and stains rocks along its banks with toxins. A recent environmental impact assessment found that the abandoned mine remained an acute threat to people’s lives through landslides, levee collapses and floods. If left untouched, the river delta would remain polluted for a century.

Bougainville, a group of islands off Papua New Guinea to the north of Australia, has variously been controlled by Britain, Germany, Japan, Australia and Papua New Guinea. It declared independence in 1975 as the Republic of North Solomons but was absorbed into the newly independent country weeks later. Its close ethnic ties and proximity to the Solomons archipelago have long complicated its relationship with Port Moresby.

Panguna operated from 1972 until 1989 and was once among the world’s most profitable gold and copper mines. The dispute over the lucrative mine came to a violent head in 1988. The shutdown followed violent protests over environmental damage and the inequitable division of profits, and those protests helped spark a decade-long civil war on Bougainville.

At one point, Panguna generated more than 45% of Papua New Guinea’s national export revenue. Rio Tinto and other international investors earned nearly US$2bn over 17 years of operation, while less than 1% of the mine’s profits went to Bougainvilleans, whose homes and lands the mine had destroyed. Under the leadership of former mine worker Francis Ona, disaffected Bougainvilleans ran a sabotage campaign that forced the mine’s closure in 1989.

Papua New Guinea responded to the unrest by sending police and then soldiers to restore law and order. Bougainville was blockaded and descended into a civil war that lasted a decade. Estimates placed the death toll between 15,000 and 20,000, roughly 10% of the island’s population. Papua New Guinea later hired international mercenaries in the Sandline affair before a peace agreement was signed in 2001.

In a 2019 referendum, 97.7% of Bougainvilleans voted in favor of independence. The Papua New Guinean parliament is considering whether to recognize the UN-backed vote and, after lengthy debate last month, appears almost certain to reject the result. Regardless of the parliament’s decision, Toroama has said Bougainville will enter a period of “self-government” from September next year before seeking to become a fully independent country by 2030.

Panguna remains tied to that political future, and the funding commitment comes at a sensitive point in Bougainville’s independence campaign. Many, including Toroama, see restarting the mine, which is believed to hold at least $60bn in unrealised reserves, as a fundamental financial basis for an independent Bougainville. Indian miner Lloyds has received a 25-year large-scale mining lease, though any future mining operation remains years away.
