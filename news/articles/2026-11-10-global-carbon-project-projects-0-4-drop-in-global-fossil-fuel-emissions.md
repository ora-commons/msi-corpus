## More than half the decline comes from renewable generation

A new analysis from the Global Carbon Project expects global fossil-fuel emissions to fall 0.4% in 2026 even as the global economy continues to grow. The projected reduction comes from increased renewable-energy generation and a sharp increase in oil and fuel prices associated with the Iran war, which depressed consumption of petroleum products. U.S. fossil-fuel emissions are projected to fall 1.3% in 2026, while China’s are expected to decrease 0.9%.

Global emissions have declined significantly in the past 40 years only during sharp economic slowdowns or recessions — the early 1990s as the Soviet Union collapsed, the financial crisis of 2008 and the Covid-19 pandemic. This year, a surge in energy prices sparked by the blockage of the Strait of Hormuz cut oil consumption but had remarkably little impact on growth, surprising many companies and economists.

The International Monetary Fund said last month that it expected global growth of 3.1% in 2026, unchanged from its forecast a year earlier. That earlier forecast preceded the U.S.-Israeli attack on Iran. Analysts said the global economy now relies less on fossil fuel, creating a disconnect between greenhouse-gas emissions and economic growth.

Pierre Friedlingstein, director of the Global Carbon Budget Office at the University of Exeter in the U.K., said, “It’s probably a first that we don’t have a major gross domestic product reduction when we have some reduction in emissions,” and added: “We can’t say yet that it’s going to happen every single year, but it’s going in the right direction.”

The Global Carbon Project, a consortium of dozens of scientists that provides estimates for United Nations climate-change reports, projected fossil-fuel emissions of 38.24 billion metric tons in 2026, down 0.4%. More than half of the reduction was attributed to increased renewable generation. The rest of the drop was largely attributed to a sharp increase in oil and fuel prices, which the analysis said depressed consumption of petroleum products.

The Iran war has added momentum to the embrace of renewables around the world, the analysis said. Developing countries increasingly rely on renewables, particularly solar power, to meet their electricity needs. Consumers in Europe and the developing world splurged on electric vehicles in the face of record-high gasoline and diesel prices.

The analysis also shows how economic growth has become increasingly disconnected from fossil-fuel emissions. By the end of last year, 59 countries had registered a drop in carbon-dioxide emissions and positive economic growth over the previous six years, compared with 20 countries with that combination in 1996.

In the U.S., fossil-fuel emissions are projected to decline 1.3%, mainly because coal burning surged last year due to cold weather and high natural-gas prices, the analysis said. China’s emissions, the largest source of carbon-dioxide emissions and nearly one-third of the global total, are expected to fall 0.9% because of higher oil prices. China’s massive rollout of wind, solar and electric vehicles continued to accelerate during the year.

Emissions could increase again next year if an end to the Iran war causes oil prices to fall, the analysis said. The Global Carbon Project also said governments have just four years’ worth of annual emissions left before the buildup of greenhouse gases in the atmosphere would be too high to limit warming to 1.5 degrees Celsius over preindustrial temperatures, the main temperature target of the Paris climate accord.
