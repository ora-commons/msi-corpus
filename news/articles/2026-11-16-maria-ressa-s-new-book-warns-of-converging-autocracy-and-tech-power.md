## Ressa cites report finding democracy at 1978 levels globally

Maria Ressa has written a new cri de coeur against the twin threats of authoritarianism and tech oligarchy — and how, in her account, citizens can fight back. The Nobel peace prize laureate had just been to a screening in Britain of The Social Reckoning, which offers an unflattering portrayal of the tech titan Mark Zuckerberg, a man she regards as a "dictator," before speaking to the Guardian at a hotel in Washington's Dupont Circle a few days before Americans voted in critical midterm elections.

"I feel like the train is coming at us in slow motion and we're standing on the train tracks," Ressa, a 63-year-old Filipino and American journalist, said.

The Filipino and American journalist has been sounding alarms for a decade. In 2016 she warned Zuckerberg and other Silicon Valley executives that an authoritarian disinformation model being tested in the Philippines would soon be exported to the west. Like Cassandra, she was not believed.

The Fight Back: A Citizen's Guide to Standing Up to Dictators is a survival manual for confronting the convergence of authoritarian governance and tech oligarchy. It finds democracies facing a crisis of truth (the breakdown of facts and shared reality), a crisis of order (the breakdown of law and international rules) and a crisis of accountability (institutions are captured, the law is weaponised and the most powerful economic and tech actors operate without democratic oversight).

Ressa's warnings are rooted in experience. She spent nearly two decades as an investigative reporter and bureau chief for CNN in Manila and Jakarta, covering terrorism, ethnic conflict and the precarious transitions of post-authoritarian societies across south-east Asia. In 2012, convinced that digital technology offered a tool to empower citizens and demand transparency, she co-founded Rappler, an independent, digital-first news organisation in the Philippines, which blended investigative journalism with data science and community engagement. Rappler quickly established itself as a watchdog in a fragile democracy.

When Rodrigo Duterte was elected president in 2016, Ressa found herself at the front line of a global experiment. As Duterte unleashed an extrajudicial drug war that claimed thousands of lives, Ressa writes that his regime weaponised social media to silence critics, deploying state-backed troll armies to poison public discourse and wage psychological warfare. Rappler's reporting exposed the machinery of manufactured outrage, and Ressa endured an onslaught of online harassment, including rape threats and death threats, alongside a legal offensive by the state, with 11 cases filed against her, including charges of cyber libel and tax evasion. Rather than flee or capitulate, she posted bail 10 times and turned her courtroom battles into an international campaign for press freedom. In 2021 she was awarded the Nobel peace prize, alongside Russian journalist Dmitry Muratov, for safeguarding the freedom of expression that underpins democracy.

The personal toll, Ressa said, has been immense. "I wish I could get more sleep," she said. "I worked in war zones and the only difference is you'd walk into conflict and stay two weeks to a month and then you could come out and decompress for a month. This is a decade of non-decompression."

Ressa's experience in the Philippines informs The Fight Back, but she argues that the threat is now much wider. A report published earlier this year by the V-Dem Institute found that democracy had fallen back to 1978 levels for the average global citizen, with three-quarters of the world's population living under autocratic rule. In the US, the level of democracy had fallen to its lowest point since 1965. The report contained a sentence Ressa said she had to read three times: "The speed with which American democracy is currently dismantled is unprecedented in modern history."

Early in Trump's second term, comparisons with authoritarian leaders such as Duterte in the Philippines and Viktor Orbán in Hungary were commonplace. Ressa writes: "What took Hungary's Orbán four years, Serbia's Vučić eight years, India's Modi and Turkey's Erdoğan a decade, Trump accomplished in one year. Legislative constraints lost one-third of their value in 2025, the lowest in more than one hundred years. As in the Philippines, Congress has effectively abdicated its oversight role. At least 55% of the 320 objectives set out by the far-right Heritage Foundation's Project 2025 have already been achieved."

The rise of technology has become a driving force behind democratic decay, Ressa argues. "There are two dictators in my story: Duterte and Zuckerberg. This is not hyperbole. This is a statement about power, accountability – and impunity – in the 21st century."

Ressa cites a scene from Trump's 20 January 2025 inauguration as symbolising what she calls the fusion of state power and oligarchy: Zuckerberg, Elon Musk, Jeff Bezos, Sundar Pichai, Sergey Brin, Tim Cook and Sam Altman were given prominent seats ahead of Trump's cabinet nominees as he took the oath of office. Ressa describes the relationship as symbiotic, operating through mutual self-interest: in her account, autocrats require tech platforms to distribute propaganda and paralyse public opposition, while tech oligarchs require state deregulation, tax relief, military contracts, and protection from international antitrust enforcement.

Asked which poses the bigger threat, Trump or big tech, Ressa had no hesitation: "The big tech, because they themselves, the CEOs, are now saying: stop us. That's kind of performative. You can stop yourself! You're the one building this."

Even Rappler was built as a digital-first newsroom that partnered with platforms and engineered tech. "We're frenemies. I like tech. Rappler is a partner of every tech company. We build tech," Ressa said. But when she begged tech companies to "prioritise facts," she said she realised facts have never been part of the AI equation. "It's a probabilistic machine, which actually discounts facts. When you put that as the operating system of public information you create what we're living through now. Then they made it worse because they added algorithms that will keep you scrolling longer so that means fear, anger, hate. It is the worst of who we are."

Ressa regards big tech as predatory and extractive, arguing that monopolies built valuations by ingesting journalism without compensation. "The raw material is the world's knowledge. It is what we write that is expensive. They just scrape it," she said. By engineering algorithms for sensationalism and an "outrage machine," platforms altered human psychology, Ressa argues. "The incentive structure of our world changed with big tech. I always say to my friends who have kids: imagine if you keep telling your child, 'Lie. I'm going to keep rewarding you, and then keep lying and I'm going to keep rewarding you.' What kind of adult does your child become? That's the world we're creating."

The new generation of tech overlords are, in her view, dictators. "They make decisions for us without our inputs, without any accountability, and it impacts all of us. This is a public safety issue. It's like taking the lead out of your water. Every day that governments do not regulate, they lose more and more of their power."

No figure illustrates this accountability gap more starkly than Elon Musk, who poured hundreds of millions into electing Trump and ran the "department of government efficiency" (Doge) — and who, Ressa said, weaponised X: "Look at Elon Musk. He now has the architecture of the information ecosystem. He runs public information through X and he has political power and yet he's accountable to no one. He just decides to stop Starlink for Ukraine. What happens to Ukraine?"

For Ressa, the central problem is not merely the power of these companies but the absence of accountability. She argues that, while institutions such as the international criminal court exist to bring political leaders to book for crimes against humanity, there is no equivalent system of accountability for tech executives who shape the information consumed by billions of people.

"Rodrigo Duterte was arrested in March 2025 and his trial at The Hague for crimes against humanity begins November 30. So at some point, we still have a system that demands accountability for states," Ressa writes. "For tech? Mark Zuckerberg has just gained more power, has taken the same culture of move fast, break things and brought it to the frontier models, and he has been appointed to President Trump's council of advisors on science and technology. He's gained more power and the impunity continues."

In January 2025, Meta ended third-party factchecking in the US. Ressa calls this cutting a vital "lifeline to reality" as Trump returned. "He stopped fact-checking in America. Think about that. We still have fact-checkers in the Philippines. Imagine, why did he do this in America? Is that to curry favour with Trump?" she said.

Ressa had initially been struck by what she saw as the passivity of US society in the face of what she described as Trump's assault on democratic norms. "Americans stayed like deer in headlights longer than Filipinos. I thought, maybe it's because we actually did get rid of a dictator: we had a People Power revolt in 1986," she said. "But [here there is] American exceptionalism, the American idea that, 'We're not manipulated.' I was worried until Minneapolis. When push comes to shove, that actually defines who you are."

That is a reference to the early 2026 immigration raids that led to the killings of Renée Good and Alex Pretti. Citizens responded with mutual aid networks, car horns and volunteer "constitutional observers". Ressa compares Stella Carlson, who filmed Pretti's death, to Aika Rey, a 24-year-old reporter who livestreamed Ressa's arrest in Manila in 2019. In The Fight Back's prologue, Ressa writes: "Courage isn't the absence of fear; it's the decision that something matters more."

The Fight Back also gives readers practical steps, beginning with simple habits: track phone screen time for seven days to understand exposure to algorithmic manipulation, then delete the one social media app that makes you angriest. A few days after this interview, midterm voters delivered a broad defeat for Trump's party despite another cash influx from Musk.

Ressa keeps talking like she's running out of time. "I feel like we have months," she said. "I'm going to keep sprinting, and it's been a decade of sprinting. If we don't get this right in the next few months, the French elections are in April; we already saw what's happening in Germany. If we don't get this right, the world will fundamentally change and it will be far more difficult to claw back rights. In which case, I'm going to go be a hermit somewhere."
