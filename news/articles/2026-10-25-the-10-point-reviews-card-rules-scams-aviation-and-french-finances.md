## Card agreements let issuers seize rewards and close accounts

The lead item in The Wall Street Journal's October 25, 2026 edition of The 10-Point examined escalating friction between premium card issuers and customers who intensively pursue sign-on bonuses and perks. Chase and Citi have introduced lifetime bonus limits, while American Express uses dedicated analysts to identify “rewards leakage” and abuse, The Wall Street Journal reported.

The 10-Point described a devoted class of credit-card holders and said tracking sign-on bonuses and perks had become a “full-time obsession” for them. Armed with calendar reminders and viral hacks, these cardholders are constantly looking for ways to earn points. Banks have begun pushing back, The Wall Street Journal reported.

Card companies have tightened controls on customers they describe as so-called gamers. Issuers say some customers cross the line and violate their rules, while reward-seeking cardholders say they are playing by the rules. The lifetime limits at Chase and Citi and the dedicated analysts at American Express are examples of the controls described in the report.

Card agreements give issuers broad discretion to seize rewards and shut down accounts. Ben Glickman and Oyin Adedoyin examined the escalating friction between premium card issuers and customers testing issuers' limits. Taken together, the controls frame linked questions about how cardholders track bonuses, what monitoring and bonus limits issuers apply, and what issuers can do when rewards activity is disputed. The report presents the competing claims about rule compliance.

The edition's other reports broadened the focus from card rewards to public finances and household financial risk. The 10-Point called France the biggest victim of rising global bond yields. It said France had little room to raise taxes and that voters were rejecting austerity. French net debt was projected to top 111% of GDP in 2026, while France's 10-year yields recently breached 5% for the first time since 2002. Columnist James Mackintosh examined the figures.

On household fraud, Garrick Stafford swapped dinner updates and made plans for the future with his online girlfriend, Sarah, while draining his savings, running up credit-card debt and moving tens of thousands of dollars into crypto wallets, The Wall Street Journal reported. A South Carolina investigator found that “Sarah” was a married couple in Nigeria taking turns texting him.

The 10-Point connected Stafford's heartbreak with what it called a troubling evolution in romance scams. It said thieves use victims' savings not only to steal money but also to launder money from other schemes through the U.S. financial system. Joe Barrett chronicled Stafford's unraveling and what the newsletter called the growing peril of digital love traps.

Consumer demand provided another thread. The newsletter said searches on Yelp for all-you-can-eat buffets jumped in the course of a year, signaling a surge of interest in unlimited dining as diners faced economic uncertainty and rising food prices. Traditional chains such as Golden Corral continue catering to budget-minded diners, while newer restaurants—from Korean barbecue to Uzbek delicacies—pair value with variety.

Aviation and an airport contest rounded out the edition. Riyadh Air was meant to become a key pillar in Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman's economic ambitions: a high-end airline to funnel wealthy tourists to what the newsletter described as “futuristic desert cities” and “rival neighbors” like Qatar and the U.A.E. The airline instead started commercial flights in summer 2026 in the thick of a resurgent conflict.

Months after getting off the ground, Riyadh Air has evacuated all aircraft from its home base and halted almost all flying after Houthi missile strikes battered the capital. Benjamin Katz and Stephen Kalin reported on how the fighting has upended Saudi Arabia's airline industry.

Boston's Logan Airport launched a contest last month for someone with a “voice that feels like home” to greet and guide the roughly 130,000 people who fly in and out of the airport every day. More than 5,000 hopefuls lined up to show off their best “lobstahs” and “hahbahs,” and judges narrowed the field to eight people. The newsletter said the contest examined what separated the true locals from the pack.
