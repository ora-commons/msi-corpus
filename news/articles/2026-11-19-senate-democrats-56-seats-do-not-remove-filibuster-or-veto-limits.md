## Party leaders signal health and tariff agenda as Israel aid divides caucus

The 56-seat Democratic majority that will take control of the Senate next year does not remove the chamber’s institutional limits. The Guardian reported that Democrats are not large enough to get around the filibuster without at least some Republican votes. Overriding a presidential veto would require a two-thirds majority in both chambers, also requiring Republican cooperation.

Trump has two years left in his second term. One of the first things he did after Democrats’ congressional victory became clear on election night was post on Truth Social an image of a red cap with the word “VETO.” Democrats have vowed to try to pass bills anyway. The Guardian said the new majority remains bound by the same restrictions that have snarled both parties under divided government and even when they have had full control of Washington.

Over the past two years, the dynamic was reversed, with Democrats halting Republican efforts to pass party-line bills imposing new voting restrictions, among other measures.

The result was confirmed on Tuesday, when Alaska’s lengthy ballot-counting process ended with Mary Peltola defeating incumbent Dan Sullivan. Her victory gave Democrats 56 of the Senate’s 100 seats and made Sullivan the ninth Republican to lose his seat to the opposition in a midterm election. The 56-seat majority is Democrats’ largest in nearly two decades and the largest for either party since the first two years of Barack Obama’s presidency, when his allies briefly controlled 60 seats.

The Guardian described the result as a substantial accomplishment two years after voters removed Democrats from control of the Senate and sent Trump back to the White House. Its analysis said the path to the majority required gaining at least four seats, the majority of which were from states that backed the president in 2024. The Guardian called that requirement a tall ask, but said Democrats won more than twice that number as voters soured on Trump’s handling of the economy, immigration and the war with Iran.

The incoming caucus pairs long-serving lawmakers from blue states with new arrivals from states that have not sent a Democrat to the Senate in decades, including Texas, South Carolina and Mississippi.

Schumer, the top Senate Democrat who is seeking to serve as majority leader next year, called the result: “Mary Peltola’s victory last night was an exclamation point on what has been a great election year.” He added, “The American people have given us their votes. Now we have to earn their trust.”

Schumer and Hakeem Jeffries have said they plan to pursue an aggressive agenda of investigations and lawmaking once the new Congress starts. Jeffries was nominated by his party on Wednesday to serve as speaker of the House after Democrats won a decisive majority in that chamber. Schumer said, “We have to lower costs. We have to end this corruption of the most corrupt administration in American history and we have to end the chaos that is so, so prevalent in this administration.”

The Guardian said the agenda depends on Democrats staying united. So far, party leaders have signaled that they will tackle issues on which Democrats appear to have reached a consensus, such as recreating subsidies for Affordable Care Act health plans or trying to force repeal of Trump’s tariffs.

The Guardian said Senate Democrats must first sort out their internal affairs. Maryland senator Chris Van Hollen announced a challenge to Schumer last week, and the caucus will take a behind-closed-doors vote on 1 December to settle the matter. The 120th Congress is scheduled to be sworn in on 3 January.

The Guardian said aid to Israel was fraught on the campaign trail and would come to the fore after the 120th Congress was sworn in. Over the past year, many sitting lawmakers backed efforts to halt shipments to Israel of bombs and bulldozers. Israel still has allies among Senate Democrats, the report said.

Over the summer, Pennsylvania senator John Fetterman warned that he would consider leaving the party if it became “anti-Israel.” Had he done so, it would have made it even harder for Democrats to reclaim the chamber’s majority. The Guardian said that, with the party’s sweeping midterm wins, Fetterman’s objections may carry less weight.
