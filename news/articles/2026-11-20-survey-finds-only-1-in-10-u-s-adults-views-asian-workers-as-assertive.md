## Advocates connect workplace experiences to longstanding stereotypes

The Associated Press described the reveal earlier this month that billionaire Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang had extramarital affairs as having “sent shock waves through the tech world, particularly with Asian Americans who revered him as a once-in-a-generation talent.” The report said the discussion among Asian American women centered on the allegation that Chinese businesswoman Alice Huang, at the center of the scandal, was possibly a spy. Almost immediately, videos and memes reviving tired tropes that fetishized Asian women hit social media.

The Associated Press reported that Jensen Huang has since resigned. It said the U.S. Justice Department had not found sufficient evidence that Alice Huang, who is not related to Jensen Huang, was a spy. President Donald Trump told reporters this week, “I don’t know if that’s been determined yet.”

Misa Chien, founder of The Authentic Asian, a network for Asian American women leaders, said, “So few Asian women in general make it to the news for positive things. This is just going to continue to perpetuate negative stereotypes or biases for Asian women.” She said that, whether or not the allegation was true, “just the news will definitely influence people’s opinions of women who have broken the ceiling or are trying to.”

The Associated Press reported that Asian American women working in tech, civil rights advocacy and academia have felt a sense of dread that the episode will diminish how their peers are viewed and treated in their workplaces. AP described the episode as a reminder that no accomplishments will completely dispel centuries-old stereotypes.

AP also reported that Asian workers, on top of having their integrity questioned, deal with misconceptions that they cannot lead. The annual Social Tracking of Asian Americans in the United States Index from The Asian American Foundation found that at least half of U.S. adults characterized Asian Americans in the workplace as “respectful” or “reliable.” Only 1 in 10 viewed them as “assertive.” Chien said the leading theme in onboarding interviews for new members of The Authentic Asian during the previous week was, “I’m mistaken for the secretary, I don’t look like the typical face of leadership.”

One social-media post was a collage of photos showing CEOs with Asian female partners. Author May-lee Chai, who has written about the treatment of Asian women throughout history, called it “disgusting.” Chai, a creative writing professor at San Francisco State University, said depicting the women as “trophy wives” “implicates these spouses like they’re somehow similar to this suspected spy.”

April Moh, chief marketing officer for a financial technology company and board member of Stand with Asian Americans, a legal civil rights organization specializing in combating workplace anti-Asian discrimination, said the speed with which the narrative portraying Asian women as spies spread was shocking. The online chatter also led women to share stories of uncomfortable interactions. Moh said, “I have women in technology who tell me that they get really weird looks and they get questioned about laptop security by their colleagues when they’re visiting China.” She added, “I have another person telling me that she has been jokingly asked, ‘So, are you a spy?’ by people who think that’s a joke now. It’s not funny to her or me.”

Rachel Lee, Stand with Asian Americans’ president and general counsel, said many of the organization’s clients were Asian women who had proved their competence yet were still accused of being fraudulent or deceitful. Lee said, “It is deeply disturbing to see this not just unique to the Nvidia Jensen Huang case, but as a part of a long-standing dangerous narrative that Asians are perpetual foreigners, untrustworthy, disloyal.”

The Associated Press traced the stereotypes to federal immigration policy and popular culture. Years before Chinese laborers were fully banned from entering the United States under the 1882 Chinese Exclusion Act, the federal government targeted immigrant Asian women. In 1875, Congress passed the Page Act to control immigration from “any Oriental country.” It included a specific prohibition of women coming for prostitution.

Chai said, “Chinese women were deemed — and East Asian women in general — as being untrustworthy.” She said it was presumed they would be coming for “illicit means as prostitutes to the United States and that would cause harm to white men.” By the early 20th century, the Asian woman as a seductress, spy or “dragon lady” started becoming an archetype on Hollywood screens, Chai said.

Twenty years ago, Chien played a spy in a building-security advertisement in Hong Kong. She was instructed to wear three bras to resemble Angelina Jolie’s figure. Chien wrote on Instagram to point out “how the media enjoys putting Asian women in that kind of role” and urged women not to let people’s assumptions keep them from success. “So many followers messaged me saying ‘thank you so much for speaking up,’” Chien said.

The Associated Press reported that suspicion toward Chinese people in the United States has dramatically heightened geopolitical tensions. Over two dozen states in the last few years have proposed or enacted “alien land laws” to prevent Chinese nationals from buying property. The number of international students enrolling in American universities has fallen. The first Trump administration created a plan known as the “China Initiative” to profile Chinese professors as part of espionage investigations.

Critics said the China Initiative contributed to the 2024 suicide of Jane Ying Wu. Wu was a neuroscientist at Northwestern University who had lived in the United States for 40 years. The 60-year-old came under federal investigation in 2019 and was never charged with anything. Chai said, “This type of pernicious stereotype can actually destroy lives.” She added, “It’s not in any way an accurate portrait of an entire race and ethnicity.”

Rachel Lee said jokes rooted in stereotypes under the guise of compliments also do not help Asian female workers. “Whether it’s a compliment or not, a trope is a trope,” she said. “I don’t care if it comes out of the mouth of the U.S. president.” Ideally, Lee said, more companies should allow education and training around the harm and history of stereotypes. She encouraged any woman who had uncomfortable interactions to reach out to advocacy groups such as Stand with Asian Americans if she had nobody to turn to at work. At Stand with Asian Americans, people do not need a legal case of discrimination or harassment to receive assistance, including education on their rights and mental health support.

Individuals also have the right to draw boundaries and make clear what’s not OK to say, Lee added. “It’s in employers’ best interest to make sure no federal laws against discrimination and harassment are being violated,” she said. “It’s a win-win to have that conversation,” Lee said.
