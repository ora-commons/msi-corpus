## CalOSHA often declined to investigate X2 deaths and injuries

**Subtype:** fact

- ProPublica and L.A. Material found that California regulators often declined to investigate reported X2 injuries and deaths at Six Flags Magic Mountain.

## Regulators alleged improper riding in more than 35 X2 injury cases

CalOSHA officials often determined that injuries reported on the X2 did not warrant further investigation, according to a ProPublica and L.A. Material investigation. The finding centers on 17-year-old Christopher Gibson’s death and on Pamela Guillen and Naomi Greer-Wilkinson, who suffered severe brain injuries after riding the attraction in July. The records do not resolve whether earlier regulatory action might have avoided the later injuries.

The X2 was billed as a “fourth-dimension” roller coaster, with seats that swiveled in all directions as the attraction twisted and turned at times up to 76 mph. The ride drew more than 16 million riders. The investigation, based on court filings, public records and internal regulatory documents, found that regulators often conducted minimal investigations or none at all after Magic Mountain reported head injuries to California’s amusement-ride safety unit on at least 70 occasions since 2010. CNN reported in August that the X2 had been “linked to more than a dozen reports of serious injuries” and two deaths over nearly two decades; Gibson’s death was a third.

Gibson was autistic and, according to his mother, healthy. He had recently graduated from high school and was headed to Colorado Mesa University to study theater. His mother, Leanndra Fascaldo, described him as “high-functioning and verbal” to a sheriff’s detective. A family tradition allowed each of Fascaldo’s five children to choose the destination for a celebratory senior trip. Gibson researched and chose Magic Mountain. Chadwick McGrady, an attorney representing the family, said, “He wanted to see Six Flags,” and added, “The marketing worked.”

Fascaldo and Gibson began riding coasters May 31, including the X2 and the Viper, which Gibson called a “neck breaker” in a sheriff’s report. On their fourth day, June 3, he rode the X2 and then the Great American Revolution before complaining of a headache. Soon afterward, he texted from a park restroom, “I don’t think I can do any more today. My head is hurting me.” They returned to the hotel. The pain worsened, and Fascaldo gave him Advil. He moved into the closet, a dark space he used for self-soothing at home, and went to sleep. Around 2 p.m. June 4, Fascaldo noticed his snoring had stopped. She found his lips blue and his face covered with “white and pink foam,” called 911, and paramedics pronounced him dead at 3:16 p.m. Fascaldo later said her son “had so many dreams, and had his whole life ahead of him.”

The County of Los Angeles Medical Examiner found a large subdural hemorrhage. The cause of death was “blunt force head trauma,” and the reason for the injuries was recorded as a “history of theme park rides.” The examiner and homicide detective Yoon Nam spoke with Fascaldo on June 6. A source familiar with the investigation said she gave a chronology that included Full Throttle, the Viper and at least two X2 rides. On July 2, Nam emailed Bobby Park, who runs CalOSHA’s Amusement Ride and Tramway Unit: “I cannot attribute his death to his roller coaster ride experiences with 100% certainty. However, at this time, it is possible that he suffered an injury during his visits to the theme park.” Nam offered to help and asked whether the case warranted follow-up. Records show Park did not answer until Nam contacted his office again two months later.

Three days after Nam sent the email, Pamela Guillen, 39, rode the X2 while visiting from Hawaii with her daughter. According to a lawsuit the family recently filed, Guillen’s daughter saw her “sitting with a vacant stare and her mouth open.” Guillen managed to stumble off the ride, but park EMTs found her unconscious at the exit ramp. Two hours later, Gary Newman, Magic Mountain’s vice president of maintenance, called CalOSHA to report the accident and relayed a relative’s account: “My cousin seemed out of it when she got off the ride so we took her to a bench and she went unconscious.” The next morning, CalOSHA senior safety engineer Scott Prather emailed his supervisor. According to a state official who viewed the email, Guillen had been diagnosed with a brain bleed and was going into surgery. The email said the ride had closed for inspection and g-force testing. Six Flags hired a firm specializing in amusement-park rides, and state officials reviewed the results and authorized reopening, according to a CalOSHA source familiar with the investigation. Six Flags had not filed a response to the family’s lawsuit.

On July 9, a woman from Nevada emailed CalOSHA after her head repeatedly struck the seat. “The back of my head was repeatedly and forcefully slammed against the back of the seat,” she wrote. “I tried holding my head forward and tried pushing it back against the seat to stop the banging, neither worked.” She could not stand or turn her head from side to side. She also reported nausea, sensitivity to light and a headache that reached “approximately 9 out of 10.” The woman said, “Three of the eight people riding together experienced similar difficulty with their heads repeatedly striking the backs of their seats.” She filed a formal complaint with Six Flags but said the company had not responded.

Two days later, on July 11, Naomi Greer-Wilkinson, a healthy 25-year-old from Los Angeles, rode the X2. According to a lawsuit her family filed in September, she came off the ride “confused and having difficulty walking,” stumbled and lost consciousness. She has since undergone multiple emergency brain surgeries. The lawsuit says she “remains bedridden, ventilator dependent, unable to speak.” Six Flags had not responded to the lawsuit. The X2 was temporarily shut down on July 12.

Theme park rides are regulated by states, not the federal government. In 2000, the California legislature gave CalOSHA, the state worker-safety agency, oversight of roller coasters following a series of high-profile accidents in the 1990s. The law authorizes annual inspections and requires theme parks to report serious injuries immediately. California also requires CalOSHA to audit records for every permanent ride each year, including accident reports. ProPublica and L.A. Material reviewed state reports on X2 riders who went to hospitals from 2009 through 2022. In more than two-thirds of those cases — 17 out of 23 — CalOSHA checked “no” on the form determining whether an investigation was warranted.

In 16 cases involving hospitalized X2 riders, officials used identical or similar language. A typical form stated, “At this time, this accident does not warrant an investigation” and “The condition which developed while the patron was riding does not appear to be related to the maintenance or operation of the ride.” That report followed a woman’s 2016 hospitalization for a head injury.

CalOSHA and Magic Mountain frequently blamed riders for their injuries, and in more than 35 cases alleged that people had ridden the attraction incorrectly by failing to hold their heads in the right position. In another case, officials attributed a 14-year-old’s post-ride unconsciousness to her menstrual cycle. Mariusz Ziejewski, a biomechanical engineer who has worked with the military to reduce brain injuries and served as an expert witness in a lawsuit against Magic Mountain, asked, “How many more people have to die before you think, ‘Well, maybe we should look at that?’” He added, “I think one is already way too much.”

The pattern extended to deaths. Hilda Farias, a 28-year-old mother of a 5-year-old, suffered a massive brain hemorrhage after riding the X2 in October 2010. CalOSHA officials adopted similar language and conducted no inspection, and the state report did not note Farias’ death. Her family sued the theme park, and the case settled in 2015. When Christopher Hawley, 22, died after riding the X2 in June 2022, Magic Mountain initially told CalOSHA the cause appeared to be heatstroke. The coroner later determined that Hawley had died of a massive brain hemorrhage. CalOSHA shut the ride down briefly while Six Flags tested the effects of g-forces and examined maintenance records. It ultimately determined that the ride was operating normally.

Less than three months after Hawley’s death, a 28-year-old woman passed out as she exited the X2 and hit her head, according to Magic Mountain’s accident report. Paramedics took her to Henry Mayo Newhall Hospital. Two days later, Park determined no investigation was necessary, writing: “Patron has pre-existing TBI [traumatic brain injury], diabetes, and has not eaten or consumed anything throughout the day.” The woman sued Six Flags, which denied fault, and the case settled in 2025. In 2011, state officials declined to investigate a 21-year-old woman’s head injury after noting a “history of panic attacks.” In 2016, officials said a 49-year-old woman’s condition “does not appear to be related to the maintenance or operation of the ride” and added that she “did not keep her head back” as riders are instructed.

Some of the failures appear related to staffing shortages. A 2025 staffing study by a Sacramento consulting firm commissioned by the state found that the annual audit for a ride in the X2’s class lasted an average of 3.7 hours. The review covers accident reports, training and maintenance records and inspections. The study also found that CalOSHA’s ride unit had been short-staffed for at least three years, with vacancies for its top job and two of four supervisor posts. There were “insufficient” supervisors for “thorough field supervision” and “inspection report review,” the report said.

Park had documented his frustration with staffing since at least 2019. In an email to his supervisor and the state department overseeing CalOSHA, he wrote: “We have done workload studies, classification studies, begged, pleaded, and warned of the impending doom, and we are still no further along than we were 11 years ago.” He continued: “We have failed. I have tried to do this, but I cannot continue as the dwindling supply of tools has run out.”

In late August, after the X2 had been closed for more than a month, Nam followed up with Park. “I never heard back from you regarding my previous email,” she wrote. “I saw news about multiple injuries involving the X2 ride at the Six Flags. Is there anything else I need to do?” Park answered, “Apologies, I thought we responded.” He told Nam that because Gibson died in a hotel room, officials could not confirm which coasters he had ridden and it would be “difficult to determine which ride to assign this to or if it even is associated with a ride.” A few weeks later, state officials ordered a more sophisticated g-force test, citing the summer injuries and death. CalOSHA said the standard test focuses on the torso and does not “necessarily establish the full range of movement or physical impacts experienced by an individual rider, including the potential effects on the head and neck.” The order did not name anyone but referred to the June 3 incident. The results were pending.

Six Flags and CalOSHA did not answer detailed questions from ProPublica and L.A. Material. Denisse Gómez, a spokesperson for the state department overseeing CalOSHA, said regulators evaluate whether a ride was operating correctly and according to state safety rules. The agency is “committed to public safety,” she said, adding that “because that investigation is ongoing, we are not commenting on matters involving X2 at this time.” Six Flags said the X2 had consistently passed safety tests and that its parks use preventative maintenance and a comprehensive third-party inspection program. It said its investigation remained ongoing and that it was cooperating with CalOSHA. “Safety is our ethos and is at the heart of everything we do,” a spokesperson said.

In lawsuits over wrongful death or injuries involving the X2, Six Flags maintained that injuries were rare and the ride was highly regulated by CalOSHA. In a recently settled lawsuit related to Hawley’s death, Magic Mountain’s director of public safety said in a declaration that the state has never notified the park of any “patron safety-related concerns related to the acceleration forces on the X2.” In September, Magic Mountain president Brian Oerding announced the ride’s permanent closure without acknowledging the injuries or deaths connected to it. “While X2 consistently passed a multitude of safety tests, we have decided to close the ride because we believe it’s the right thing to do,” Oerding said. He added, “Ride safety is a cornerstone of our business, and when we see guest confidence affected, we take it seriously.”

The California investigation was part of a deadly year for Six Flags. In October, an Atlanta school worker died after riding one of the company’s “hypercoasters” at Six Flags Over Georgia, prompting Six Flags to temporarily close nine attractions at six theme parks across North America. The more sophisticated g-force test results remained pending, and Six Flags had not responded to the lawsuits filed by Guillen and Greer-Wilkinson. The records leave unresolved whether earlier state follow-up after Gibson’s death could have avoided the later brain injuries.
