## Environmental groups sue EPA as Army postpones Iowa lead-azide detonation

**Subtype:** fact

- Earthjustice sued the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency on behalf of the Iowa Environmental Council and Sierra Club Iowa Chapter over an emergency permit authorizing the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant to detonate 68 drums of deteriorated lead azide.
- The U.S. Army postponed the detonation until Monday, Nov. 23, citing a forecasted temperature drop that could further destabilize the material.

## Initial Army model projected a lead-particle plume across the Mississippi River into Illinois

The U.S. Army postponed until Monday, Nov. 23, the planned detonation of 68 drums of degraded lead azide at the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant in southeast Iowa. Earthjustice filed a lawsuit on behalf of the Iowa Environmental Council and Sierra Club Iowa Chapter on Thursday afternoon, hours before the Army announced the postponement, suing EPA over its decision to grant the plant an emergency permit for the intended detonation. The filing described the deteriorated lead azide as an explosive that is unstable in its deteriorated state. The groups asked the U.S. Appellate Court for the Eighth District to either rule on the motion before Friday at 10 a.m. or issue an administrative stay until the motion could be fully heard in court.

## Schedule and legal challenge

Acting Secretary of the Army Adam Telle said Monday was the “latest possible moment prior to a forecasted temperature drop, which would risk further destabilization of the material.” He also said “public safety is the Army’s top priority.” U.S. Sen. Chuck Grassley said on social media that the delay was to “further educate the public.”

The environmental groups said they did not dispute the Army and EPA’s assertion that the lead azide posed a risk if left untreated. They argued that the Army left the explosive “to degrade for roughly two decades until it decided that the only safe option became detonating them near populated areas.” Petitioners alleged that EPA’s emergency permit failed to incorporate applicable environmental performance standards, require all conditions necessary to protect human health and the environment, consider less dangerous alternatives that would avoid widespread dispersal and deposition of toxic lead, and adequately notify the public.

Iowa Environmental Council Executive Director Sarah Green said it “could be the case that the U.S. Army has no other safe alternative to disposing of the toxic lead azide.” She said, “But our federal government needs to do better at communicating the potential health risks of lead contamination in our communities and providing more time to keep our communities and vulnerable groups safe, like children and pregnant women.” The lawsuit said, “That turned the Army’s storage management failure into a public health event for people in at least two states.” It added: “This rushed final week is a symptom of that; the Army has been aware of the risk since at least March, yet it gave public notice of its plans a mere five days before intended detonation.”

The environmental groups asked the court for a stay because, they said, “once lead dust is dispersed, there is no undoing the harm.” They told the court: “What the Court must balance is the risks associated with the possibility of unplanned detonation while crucial additional time is given to take actions necessary to protect human health and the environment, against the very significant known harm of a massive lead dust plume.” EPA and Army media contacts did not respond to a request for comment Thursday regarding the lawsuit.

## Material and dispersion models

The lawsuit described the planned detonation as involving 9,700 pounds of explosive lead material. “The Army’s planned detonation of 9,700 pounds of explosive lead material poses a significant risk of irreparable harm that cannot later be remedied,” the lawsuit said. According to the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant’s proposed action report, the lead azide was submerged in a water/ethanol storage solution. Temperature fluctuations caused the material to “dissolve and recrystallize” into a form that was “acutely sensitive to shock, friction, static electricity, and heat.” The emergency permit says the Army determined in March that the material had degraded and had a briefing with EPA in April.

According to Army news releases, the planned detonation was to occur inside concrete bunkers surrounded by sand barriers approximately two stories high. An Army slideshow shared at a public house in Middletown showed protective barriers around the detonation site. The Army said the operation would cause a plume of air “with some lead-containing particles.” Nearby areas were warned to stay inside while the lead particles were expected to be airborne and to spray down outdoor materials after lead-containing dust had settled.

The Army held an open house in mid-October to inform residents about the planned detonation, though the Friday schedule was not announced until Nov. 15. The Army provided air-dispersion models that day showing that lead particles could spread up to 100 miles from the detonation site. Its dust-deposition models showed a potential spread of around 40 miles.

The initial plume map showed air dispersion was intended to stretch across the Mississippi River and into New Boston, Illinois. More recent maps released by the Army on Thursday showed the plume was expected to move more directly north, rather than northeast of the detonation site, and not to stretch as far as the earlier model showed. The Army said it would continue releasing updated maps up until the detonation with information accurate to the latest atmospheric conditions.

## Competing safety positions

The U.S. Army and EPA said the intentional detonation would allow them to mitigate the “explosion risk” of degraded lead azide, which they described as an “imminent and substantial threat to human health and the environment.” They said the planned operation allowed for precautions and public awareness that would not be possible if the material exploded on its own.

The environmental groups alleged that EPA’s permit “largely ignored” the potential for lead to spread to groundwater, surface water, soil and the atmosphere. They said the permit contained no conditions addressing the detonation’s potential for damage to domestic animals, wildlife, crops, vegetation and physical structures.

The lawsuit alleged that Illinois officials had not been notified of the detonation or its potential risks. On Thursday, the Illinois Department of Public Health issued public-health guidance about the detonation. The Iowa Department of Health and Human Services posted safety information and said it had been “only very recently made aware” of the plan. On Wednesday, the Iowa Department of Agriculture posted guidance for livestock producers near the detonation site, urging them to move livestock elsewhere or into a barn if possible and to move or cover feed and water supplies to “prevent possible contamination.”

For people in affected areas, safety precautions included staying indoors, running air purifiers and turning off heating and cooling units while there was a potential for air dispersion, which the Army estimated would last about an hour. The Army and state health departments said people in the affected areas should consider washing outdoor surfaces, including playground equipment, doors and cars, to minimize the risk of consuming lead dust. The lawsuit pushed back on that advice, noting that there is no safe level of lead exposure for children. “The idea that widespread lead dust can simply be rinsed away with no harmful effect is absurd, counterfactual, and extremely harmful misinformation to share with the public,” the lawsuit said.

## Public response and monitoring

The EPA permit requires the Army to collect samples before, during and after the detonation at “all parks, public facilities, and private properties” where the Army has a right of entry. The lawsuit said those testing requirements were not robust. Researchers at the University of Iowa met with reporters Thursday afternoon and said they were conducting independent work that included deploying air sensors and particle filters and collecting soil samples and surface wipes across the affected area. David Cwiertny, director of the Environmental Policy Research Program at the University of Iowa, said, “We’re doing all of this to add to the public record, not because we have any real doubt in the official monitoring that’s planned by the EPA or the Army.” He said, “In an unprecedented event with so many unpredictable factors, we believe that having more independent data is always better for public transparency and for public health.”

The dispute drew significant attention on social media and in news beyond Iowa. Democrats U.S. Sen.-elect Josh Turek and Iowa Senate Democratic leader Janice Weiner criticized a lack of information and coordination among the public agencies. Turek called for the Army to “pause and delay the detonation” until the health impacts to Iowans could be fully understood. He said, “Iowans deserve to know that every safety precaution has been taken and exactly what the associated health risks are.” He added, “Until those questions are answered, the detonation should not proceed.”

Republican U.S. Rep. Mariannette Miller-Meeks, who represents the affected communities, said in a social media post that the Army had been “public” about the situation and that detonation was the “safest and best option.” Grassley said he had “emphasized the need for increased communication” and that the Army was “responding to the questions and concerns I’ve shared from Iowans.”
