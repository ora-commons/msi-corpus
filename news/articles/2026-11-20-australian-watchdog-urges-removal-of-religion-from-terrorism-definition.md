## Muslim groups welcome proposal; Australia’s peak Jewish body disputes it

In a report released Thursday, Australia’s Independent National Security Legislation Monitor recommended removing religion as a separate motivation from the federal criminal code’s definition of terrorism. The proposal would place religious motivation within a broader ideology category while leaving people who satisfy the legal test prosecutable. Under existing law, agencies must prove that an act intends to cause serious harm to people or property to advance a political, religious or ideological cause.

The monitor reviewed how terrorism has been defined in Australia over the past 25 years, a period that followed a suite of laws introduced after the September 11 attacks in the United States.

Independent National Security Legislation Monitor head Jake Blight said the requirement to prove a religious cause gives accused terrorists false credibility. “Having a law that requires proof that terrorists were advancing a ‘religious’ cause gives these terrorists false credibility and reinforces their narrative that they are doing holy work,” the report said. “The same individuals will still be able to be prosecuted, but should be described as violent ideologues.”

The monitor also said the legal reference has harmed religious minorities. “Reference to religion in the definition of terrorism has also, in practice, contributed to discrimination and violence against religious minorities, especially Muslim Australians. This has a negative impact on social cohesion,” the report said.

The report said placing religion within the broader ideology category aligns with international best practice. The monitor noted that the UN special rapporteur’s report came earlier in 2026 and recommended defining terrorism acts by political and ideological causes and omitting religious motives, as references to religion have “discriminatory and stigmatizing effects that outweigh the benefits of criminally denouncing them.”

The proposals come as the Australian Federal Police and the Australian Security and Intelligence Organisation warn that the rise of extremist, violent nationalism has changed the threat landscape and that the motivations behind some terrorist attacks cannot be categorised into single ideologies. ASIO director-general Mike Burgess has warned of a rise in violent nationalist extremism in recent years.

In an October submission to a parliamentary inquiry into radicalisation, ASIO said around 65% of its counterterrorism caseload related to religiously motivated violent extremism, while 35% related to ideologically motivated extremism.

In his submission to the Independent National Security Legislation Monitor, Aftab Malik, Australia’s special envoy to combat Islamophobia, said keeping religion in as a terrorism motivation created a “climate of suspicion” around Muslims and “provided ammunition for far-right extremist narratives.”

Eleven Muslim advocacy and community groups, including the Australian Muslim Advocacy Network, welcomed the report’s recommendations and urged the Australian government to amend the laws. Their joint statement said, “Every time someone in authority says that a motivation is ‘religious’, it uplifts ISIS, far-right and harmful media narratives all at once, by officially perpetuating the false premise that Islam encourages terrorism.” The groups added, “This makes all Muslims feel under siege.”

The Executive Council of Australian Jewry, Australia’s peak Jewish body, described the report as “high-quality” but said removing religion as a motivating factor made little sense because many notorious terrorist acts have featured motifs, words and images that unmistakably refer to religious doctrines and beliefs.

The organisation’s co-chief executive, Peter Wertheim, said, “The inclusion of ‘faith’ as a type of ideology does not solve the problem. Faith is not the same as religion and religion is more than an ideology.” Wertheim also said, “It is clearly not appropriate for Australian law to decide which beliefs are an authentic expression of a religion and which are not. Even the adherents of a religion may have divergent views about such matters.”

The report made six other recommendations, including separating credible threats of terrorism from actual acts. Under existing law, a person found guilty of making a credible terrorist threat can receive life imprisonment, with a mandatory minimum sentence of six years. The monitor called that punishment “disproportionate, unnecessarily complex and out of step with international standards” and recommended treating credible threats as a separate charge with a maximum sentence of no more than five years.

Another recommendation would raise the threshold for designating damage to property and electronic systems as acts of terrorism while excluding advocacy, protest, dissent and industrial action from that category.
