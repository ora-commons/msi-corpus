## The E5 joined EU leaders and US envoys in negotiations

At Friday’s Dublin summit, EU leaders chose a seven-person team for US-mediated talks aimed at ending Russia’s war on Ukraine, leaving the bloc’s top diplomat outside the lineup. The Guardian reported that Ukrainian president Volodymyr Zelenskyy had urged the EU to field one negotiator, but leaders instead named European Council president António Costa, Commission president Ursula von der Leyen and representatives from France, Germany, Britain, Poland and Italy. The E5 countries were to work with NATO alongside US negotiators Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner.

The choice placed a question at the center of EU diplomacy: whether the bloc can present one clear foreign-policy voice while its major member states negotiate over the war in Ukraine. Guardian Europe correspondent Jon Henley wrote that the EU is repeatedly described as wanting and needing to be a global player and geopolitical heavyweight capable of shaping its own foreign and security policies, or at least speaking with one clear, unambiguous voice on the world stage. He said the Dublin decision showed how national turf wars undermine Brussels’ global ambitions and that the bloc was unlikely to find such a voice anytime soon. The talks were aimed at ending Russia’s war on Ukraine, which the publication called the gravest security crisis the EU has faced in decades.

Kaja Kallas is a former Estonian prime minister who was appointed the EU’s High Representative for Foreign Affairs and Security Policy and a European Commission vice-president in 2024. In theory, Kallas was supposed to embody a more assertive Europe. She chairs the monthly Foreign Affairs Council, the decision-making forum where national foreign ministers agree on common foreign policy, sanctions and crisis responses. She also runs the European External Action Service, the EU’s permanent diplomatic corps and de facto foreign ministry, comprising civil servants and more than 140 embassies around the world that conduct day-to-day EU diplomacy on the ground. The role was created by the Lisbon Treaty in 2009 to answer Henry Kissinger’s famous, possibly apocryphal question: “Who do I call when I want to talk to Europe?” The Guardian cited one commentator who called the role a “poisoned chalice.”

At the summit, Kallas said leaders had agreed in 2024, when it was clear talks with Russia would be necessary, that she would represent Europe in foreign and security policy. That understanding did not put her on the team Costa announced. The US-mediated negotiations were accelerating when leaders selected the format, and Kallas was not among the announced negotiators.

Relations between Kallas and von der Leyen have been particularly difficult, The Guardian reported, with critics accusing both of sharing exactly the same kind of power-hoarding and centralising instincts as some EU national leaders. Active proposals in Brussels would overhaul the EU’s diplomatic service by folding parts of the European External Action Service and key foreign-policy powers directly into the Commission president’s remit, and The Guardian said those proposals had aggravated the friction.

The Guardian offered three explanations for the bypass. Several Western European capitals viewed the Estonian envoy as too overtly and fiercely anti-Russian. More broadly, it said the EU’s major member states resisted ceding real foreign-policy authority to Brussels, with Paris and Berlin in particular resisting a single EU envoy and preferring to protect their own seats at the table. Diplomats also pointed to a wider shift in which international questions once handled by foreign ministers were being taken over by premiers or presidents.

Von der Leyen defended the decision briskly: “We decide for ourselves who will represent us, and we made that decision today.” Germany’s chancellor, Friedrich Merz, separately argued that intergovernmental formats such as the E3 — France, Germany and the UK — are “a well-established format” and “I see absolutely no problem with keeping it that way.”

Spain and Lithuania both criticized the team approach. Lithuania’s foreign minister, Kęstutis Budrys, said: “Europe needs one voice,” adding, “We cannot speak with 27 microphones. We need one.”

Supporters of the Dublin decision said hard security and ending the war belong to the EU’s major military and financial powers rather than supranational officials, and that bringing those states to the table gives the bloc geopolitical weight. One EU diplomat said the problem was that EU institutions “do not have sufficient political legitimacy in the eyes of voters. And this matters big time. The E5 solution is an attempt to bridge that gap.”

Critics see the choice as a damaging own goal and a profound hypocrisy. Brussels commenter Dave Keating argues there is a fatal contradiction inherent in Europe’s integration. Keating said member states “know what the right thing to do is in the abstract, so they create these positions and institutions to make a stronger EU,” before adding: “But then … they undermine [them] … because they always give in to the urge not to hand over power to someone else.” Keating described the chosen lineup as a “European cacophony” that Moscow and Washington would inevitably dismiss as irrelevant and said the choice proved Kallas’s job “is not real, and the EU doesn’t actually have a foreign policy.”

Jennifer Rankin, who spoke from Brussels, said: “Personally, I think Kallas has an impossible job: having to represent the positions of 27 member states that often disagree on foreign policy, and don’t want to cede real decision making on it to the EU.” Rankin was less categorical about the format, saying the E5-plus approach could be seen as “a realistic answer to represent the EU as it is, not the idealised union of the treaties.” It “needn’t be a cacophony if everyone in the room has the same message,” she said. Rankin added: “So far, on Ukraine at least, there is a large degree of consensus. But if, for example, Marine Le Pen becomes French president, all bets are off.”

Kallas said she would concentrate on “substance” while leaders handled “format.” The choice leaves the authority of her office unresolved: whether a team built around major national powers can secure one message without displacing the EU institution created to represent the bloc abroad. The Guardian said the suspicion remained that Europe’s leaders want the bloc to be a global power but will not let anyone else speak for them. The publication warned that the EU could be stuck on the diplomatic sidelines, “forever punching below its geopolitical weight.”
