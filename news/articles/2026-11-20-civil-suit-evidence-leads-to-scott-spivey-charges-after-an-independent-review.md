## Recovered calls and camera note emerged after the decision not to prosecute

Spivey’s family filed the civil lawsuit after the South Carolina Attorney General’s Office decided not to prosecute Weldon Boyd and Kenneth Williams over Scott Spivey’s killing. The suit helped unearth calls made on Boyd’s own phone, including remarks in which he bragged about the shooting and asked a police officer friend to intervene. The family also obtained body-camera footage of another officer writing a note to the men on the day of the shooting that said “act like a victim camera.”

Spivey’s family said the material showed police officers influencing the original inquiry. The charges came after another independent look at the case pushed by Spivey’s family. Following the new evidence, the two Horry County officers were fired, and the Attorney General’s Office brought prosecutor Barry Barnette from Spartanburg in to review the case. Barnette called for another grand jury, which led to Thursday’s murder charges against Boyd and Williams and misconduct in office and obstruction of justice charges against former Horry County Deputy Chief Brandon Strickland and former Sgt. Damon Vescovi.

The case started, according to investigators, when Boyd and Williams chased Spivey for several miles after Spivey pulled a gun on them during a road-rage incident. Both pickups reached speeds over 100 mph (160 kph) before Spivey pulled off a side road, got out of his truck with a gun and told the men to stop following him, investigators said. The men then shot through the windshield of Spivey’s pickup, hitting him in the right side of his back near his armpit, authorities said.

The local prosecutor turned the case over to the South Carolina Attorney General’s Office after Boyd posted a message on Facebook a week after the shooting thanking police and prosecutors for their work on the case. Seven months later, the Attorney General’s Office decided not to prosecute Boyd and Williams, ruling that the men had been threatened and therefore justified in firing on Spivey under the state’s stand-your-ground law.

Boyd’s recorded calls detailed what the lawsuit uncovered. In one, Boyd talked with Williams about the two men getting teardrop tattoos since they had killed a man. In another, Boyd told someone that Spivey had figured out during the chase that he could drive faster than Boyd and Williams and was terrified. In a third call, Boyd said about the shooting, “I know it’s (expletive) up to say, but I had a (expletive) blast.”

Boyd called Strickland several times from the scene and afterward, and according to the phone calls, Strickland told Boyd, his friend, that he had spoken with investigators and prosecutors. Strickland’s lawyer said at Thursday’s bond hearing that his client’s comments may have been inappropriate but that no evidence showed Strickland influenced the investigation.

Vescovi passed along the note about the camera. His lawyer said Vescovi made a poor choice but believed at the time that Boyd and Williams were victims of a road-rage incident.

Lawyers for all four men said publicity had tainted the case and that their clients were innocent. Boyd’s lawyer, Shaun Kent, said the charges should be dropped in part because evidence from the civil case was used to build the criminal case, a tactic that led to the dismissal of sexual-assault charges against Bill Cosby.

Kent also cited reasons prosecutors initially cleared the men, including findings that Spivey had alcohol in his system. “He was intoxicated. He had a firearm. He was pointing it at other individuals,” Kent said in court Thursday.

Jennifer Spivey, who pored over the evidence in her brother’s killing until she found the note and heard Boyd’s phone calls, attended Thursday’s hearing and asked the judge to make sure justice was finally served. “Scott tried desperately to escape the violence that ultimately took his life,” she said.
