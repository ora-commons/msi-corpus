## Regulations would control the procedure and limit it to specialised facilities

The NSW Parliament passed legislation on Wednesday that made New South Wales the first Australian jurisdiction to legalize terramation, also known as natural organic reduction, as a third end-of-life option alongside burial and cremation. The private member's bill gives the NSW government 18 months to create regulations that would control the practice and limit it to specialised facilities. Terramation is allowed in some parts of Germany and the United States, where independent Sydney MP Alex Greenwich traveled to research how it operates. Several NSW MPs said they wanted their own bodies treated through terramation after their deaths.

A photograph caption in The Guardian's report said NSW Independent MP Alex Greenwich introduced a terramation bill to the legislative council after traveling to the United States and Germany to research the practice. In the main text, The Guardian said Animal Justice Party MP Emma Hurst introduced the bill into the legislative council. The two passages conflict over who introduced the bill in the legislative council.

The report described the procedure as natural organic reduction, terramation or human composting, and referred to the practice as “human compost” while MPs discussed the proposal as terramation. Terramation involves placing a body in a temperature-controlled vessel with organic matter such as alfalfa, straw or mulch. The vessel is turned or rocked to circulate air; the introduced matter reacts with the air and feeds the body's naturally occurring microbes to rapidly decompose the body. The process creates heat.

The heat-generating process transforms a body into soil after about 30 days. When rested for about 30 days more, the vessel contains about 110kg of “dry, rich soil that is fertile and free of pathogens,” Greenwich told the legislative assembly earlier this year.

In the legislative council on Wednesday, Libertarian MP John Ruddick said he backed the position Greens MP Dr Amanda Cohn took in supporting the bill. Ruddick thought it was likely he would choose terramation over a burial plot or cremation. “Terramation will be voluntary. It is a choice. A lot of people may not choose it … I am pretty sure that I will put in my will that I wish to be terramated,” Ruddick said.

Ruddick also said, “I like the idea of being buried somewhere and having a nice big gum tree grow there for 100 years.” He added, “The idea of being cremated or buried has always freaked me out. This is a much better option.”

Labor MP Cameron Murphy also said: “I look forward to being able to utilise this service in the long-distant future after it becomes law.”

Liberal MP Susan Carter urged the NSW government to consider how to ensure the dead were respected when implementing the bill. “Terramation should not simply become a human compost bin,” Carter said. She added: “Like all the many ways in which we approach death, it should help those who remain to mourn, and the respect shown to that person in life should continue into death. It is important for that person, for their family, and for the whole way in which we go forward as a society.”

Emma Hurst, the Animal Justice Party MP whom The Guardian's report said introduced the bill into the legislative council, told parliament on Wednesday that terramation had significant environmental benefits. Hurst said it could avoid the need for large burial plots and can be wholly carbon neutral. She compared that with cremation, which she said was energy intensive and released substantial greenhouse gas emissions.

Hurst said terramation “provides possibilities to grow living things, like trees and plants, in honour of a life.” She said: “Because terramation is a slow process, there are multiple opportunities for ceremonies, including for the laying of the body in the vessel and for opening the vessel after a body has been terramated.” Facilities overseas have allowed families to visit during the terramation process, Hurst said, describing it as “a natural and gentle process that, for some, can help with grieving.”

The bill passed on Wednesday gives the NSW government 18 months to create regulations to control the practice, which would be limited to specialised facilities.
