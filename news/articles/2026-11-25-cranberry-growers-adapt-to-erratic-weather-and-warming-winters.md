## Researchers use AI to breed tougher cranberry varieties

It was harvest time in Wisconsin, where the Badger State's most colorful crop was painting nearly everything red. Special correspondent Ben Tracy of Climate Central reported from a Wisconsin cranberry bog for the PBS News Hour series "Tipping Point," in a segment introduced by Lisa Desjardins. Nicole Hansen, who has grown Wisconsin cranberries for nearly 30 years, worked the harvest as specialized machines shook fruit loose from low-lying vines in flooded beds. The berries floated to the surface, making them easier to collect. During the harvest, Hansen gave instructions: "We need to corral all these berries," "Keep it level," and "You're just going to skim the top."

Hansen said Wisconsin is the number-one cranberry-producing region and produces more than 60% of the nation's crop. The United States produces the vast majority of the world's supply, and cranberries are a fixture on many Thanksgiving tables. Growing the crop relies on a narrow window of favorable conditions, a window that is shifting as climate change brings more unpredictable weather. Lisa Desjardins said in the segment introduction that scientists and growers are working to keep the seasonal favorite on the menu.

Cranberries are one of the few fruits native to North America. They grow on low-lying vines in dry soil, not in water, although growers flood growing beds at harvest so specialized machinery can dislodge the fruit and let it float to the surface.

Hansen said she checks the weather five to 10 times a day because everything she does is controlled by weather. Tracy said he did not think he had ever seen so many cranberries. Of this year's crop, Hansen said: "Yes, the fruit looks amazing. The size is really good here. The color is really uniform."

In cranberry-growing states such as Massachusetts and New Jersey, extreme heat can damage berries on the vine, while heavy rainfall can cause disease and rot. The weather is becoming increasingly unpredictable. In Wisconsin, however, problems can come after harvest.

To protect plants through winter, Wisconsin growers flood the fields again and insulate the vines under several inches of ice, shielding them from bitter cold and damaging winds. Amaya Atucha, a fruit scientist at the University of Wisconsin-Madison, said: "I love cranberries. I spent a lot of time thinking about cranberries." She said the plants can sustain cold damage if they are not protected.

Atucha said winters in Wisconsin are warming. Climate Central data show that Upper Midwest winters are among the fastest-warming in the country and that cranberry-growing areas now experience an average of 14 more warm winter days than they did in 1970. The additional warm periods can produce erratic thawing and freezing.

She described the danger in February temperatures that melt field ice and leave vines exposed before cold returns: "We know for certain that the climate is changing. We have seen a couple of years in which we get these warmer temperatures in February, and then the ice melts and the vines are exposed, and then it gets cold again." Tracy compared the loss of field ice to having your parka taken away in the middle of winter, and Atucha replied: "Yes. Yes, exactly."

The cycle can disrupt that standing ice-based protection because the protective layer can melt while vines remain exposed before temperatures fall again. Atucha said cold temperatures can damage plants that lack that insulation.

Researchers are trying to make cranberries more resilient by using AI to find and breed new varieties that can better handle weather extremes and disease. Growers are also using drums to apply fertilizers more precisely and monitor temperatures in their bogs.

Atucha said changing conditions require growers to revise established systems: "When you have a system that works, and then out of nowhere you change the rules of the game, then you need to adjust." Tracy said Hansen sees adapting to change as part of the job.

Hansen also said: "I love it. It's a great way to raise a family." On a harvest morning, she said: "We get to live this every day." Her comments came as growers and researchers worked to keep the seasonal favorite on the menu despite shifting weather conditions.
