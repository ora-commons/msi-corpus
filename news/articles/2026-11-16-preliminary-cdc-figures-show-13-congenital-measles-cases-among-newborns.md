## Falling vaccination rates heighten measles susceptibility among women of childbearing age

The new attention centers on congenital measles, which occurs when a pregnant woman passes the virus to a fetus in the womb. Babies are considered to have congenital measles if they were exposed in the uterus and doctors find evidence of measles very soon after birth. The condition can lead to serious complications or be deadly for newborns.

The preliminary count comes from a public health alert published Monday in NEJM Evidence in partnership with the University of Minnesota’s Center for Infectious Disease Research and Policy. CDC scientists authored the alert, which said 13 congenital measles cases had been reported to the CDC from the start of 2025 through the end of September during large outbreaks. It also recorded 112 confirmed measles cases during pregnancy.

Michael Osterholm, who directs the University of Minnesota’s Center for Infectious Disease Research and Policy, called the development a new phenomenon. “This is a brand new phenomenon,” he said.

The preliminary account attributes the emergence of congenital measles to declining vaccination rates and the outbreaks they have fueled. The CDC said the United States has had more than 7,500 measles cases in the past two years, and the Pan American Health Organization was expected to announce Tuesday that the country had lost the measles-free status it held since 2000. “We’re now seeing levels of measles that we haven’t seen in 30 years,” said Dr. Adam Ratner, a member of the American Academy of Pediatrics’ infectious diseases committee. “When you have measles under control,” congenital measles doesn’t happen.

The vulnerability reflects how control of measles has changed. People gain immunity by contracting the disease or receiving the vaccine. Before shots existed, many people contracted the extremely contagious virus in childhood; later, high vaccination rates protected everyone. Osterholm described a new and historic reality shaped by the vaccine’s success and recent skepticism about it.

Osterholm said a large group of women of childbearing age had neither been vaccinated during the previous 25 years nor encountered measles because the country had beaten it back during that period. “Now, this group is highly susceptible to measles,” he said.

The American Academy of Pediatrics said the best prevention is for women to receive the MMR vaccine before pregnancy. Antibodies pass from mother to baby through the placenta, and protection can last a few months after birth. MMR cannot be given during pregnancy. For an unvaccinated woman who has never had measles and is exposed while pregnant, an antibody solution given within six days can reduce the risk of infection and complications.

The academy estimated that 25% to 30% of babies born to someone who contracts measles shortly before delivery may become infected. Common symptoms include fever, jaundice and a measles rash. Severe complications include pneumonia, encephalitis and a rare condition known as SSPE, a brain disorder that generally develops many years after a measles infection and is almost always fatal.

Ratner said there are indications that children with congenital measles are at higher risk for SSPE and may develop it sooner than other children. “That’s something that sadly we’re learning more about,” he said.

A 95% MMR rate in a community is the level at which measles outbreaks are considered unlikely, but rates in many communities have dipped below that threshold. Nationally, the CDC said 92.4% of U.S. kindergarteners received required MMR shots in 2025-26.

The vaccination gap also leaves infants too young to be vaccinated at risk. The first MMR dose is routinely given at 12 to 15 months old, though babies as young as 6 months can receive it in outbreak areas.

The American Academy of Pediatrics has begun sharing guidance on congenital measles with doctors. Pennsylvania’s health department separately published an advisory last month for healthcare providers, schools, childcare centers and others in the state. Pennsylvania currently has the largest measles outbreak in the nation.

“We need to get back to the point where everybody who can get vaccinated is getting vaccinated, and that’s gonna take some doing,” Ratner said. “It’s going to be a lot of work on the part of pediatricians and public health institutions and lots of others. But it’s an important thing for us to do.”
