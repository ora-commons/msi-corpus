## A drone attack on a Russian cargo ship tested the leaders' reassurance

Finnish President Alexander Stubb and Norwegian Prime Minister Jonas Gahr Støre called Russian President Vladimir Putin in October with an urgent mission beyond ending the war in Ukraine, the stated focus of their official agenda. According to people familiar with the matter, the Nordic leaders wanted to prevent a clash with Russia in their own region by reassuring Putin that they did not intend to cut off a vital Russian shipping route in the Baltic Sea. The Baltic Sea had become a potential flashpoint between Moscow and the West.

The stakes are both strategic and economic. Russia ships more than 60% of its seaborne oil through the Baltic Sea, providing a lifeline for its wartime economy, and the sea is surrounded by NATO members. In recent months, Western officials said Moscow took a series of steps that signaled its deepening fears of a potential blockade. According to Western officials and Russian statements, Russian warships carrying nuclear-capable missiles were ordered to escort oil tankers through the Baltic and prevent Western navies and coast guards from boarding them. In just over a year, the German coast guard registered 260 such escorts in the Baltic and the North Sea.

In May, Russia passed new legislation allowing military force to protect commercial vessels. Western intelligence officials said Moscow also reinforced its exclave of Kaliningrad with drones, powerful radars and electronic-warfare equipment.

In August, Putin called European efforts to confront tankers “piracy and banditry” and vowed a military response. A month later, the Kremlin delivered a written warning to NATO that Russia was prepared to launch pre-emptive nuclear strikes to defend its interests in the Baltic, even without an attack on Russian territory. The warning was earlier reported by Reuters.

On the Oct. 27 call, Stubb told Putin, according to two people present for the conversation: “We have no intention of blockading the Baltic in peacetime, you can rely on that, we will act in accordance with the law.” The Kremlin did not respond to a request for comment.

Interceptions added another source of tension. Moscow uses shadow-fleet tankers to evade Western sanctions on its oil exports. A tally of publicly reported incidents showed that suspected Russian shadow-fleet tankers were seized or boarded at least 14 times since late 2024, including 10 early this year. Interdictions in the Baltic largely stopped earlier this year after Russia’s navy began confronting Western ships attempting to stop the tankers.

On June 30, the Kira K entered German waters from the east and made a rendezvous with the Russian Baltic Fleet flagship Soobrazitelny, which was stationed there at the time to secure the passage of Russian oil tankers. The Soobrazitelny raced toward a Greenpeace flotilla of five speedboats approaching the Panama-flagged shadow-fleet tanker to prevent the tanker from being boarded, apparently mistaking the activists’ boats for coast-guard craft that were planning to board it. Its captain transmitted over an open maritime channel: “Here speaks Russian warship 531, stay away from the Kira K,” according to a recording released by Greenpeace. The nearby German coast guard vessel Bayreuth placed itself between the warship and the activists.

A MarineTraffic graphic accompanying the report noted that the Soobrazitelny corvette’s path was not shown because AIS transponder data was unavailable.

The German commander was able to defuse the situation by reassuring the Russian warship that the speedboats were civilian protesters and nobody planned to board the shadow-fleet tanker. The Russian captain first asked the Kira K whether it was being boarded but received no clear answer because of communication problems. He then asked the German coast guard captain, who twice denied that anyone planned to board the tanker, explained that the speedboats carried protesters and ordered them ashore. The Kira K continued its voyage under the Soobrazitelny’s escort, while Danish ships Diana and Freja shadowed the vessels until they left the Baltic. The German naval intelligence ship Oste trained surveillance equipment on the corvette.

The German commander later described the encounter to superiors as carrying a high risk of escalation, according to two officials. Greenpeace said the Russian warship felt “threatening to all of us,” leading the group to scale back the protest to avoid escalation. Thomas Röwekamp, the conservative chairman of the German parliament’s defense committee and a participant aboard one speedboat, argued that German authorities should stop shadow-fleet tankers. “The escalation comes from Russia, not us, and we must not be intimidated,” Röwekamp said.

The confrontation was not isolated. On Sept. 14, the Soobrazitelny fired signal flares at a Danish military helicopter monitoring the corvette. On Oct. 18, two Russian warships prevented Danish and German coast guards from approaching a tanker that had suffered an engine malfunction off Bornholm.

Western officials said comments from their side also fed Moscow’s fears about Western intentions in the Baltic. On Sept. 11, Polish Foreign Minister Radosław Sikorski suggested NATO stage military exercises near Kaliningrad so “aggressive” that Putin would fear an attack and be forced to divert troops from Ukraine. Days later, Estonian Foreign Minister Margus Tsahkna said at the United Nations that “Estonia, Finland, also NATO” could close the Gulf of Finland “within hours.”

Alexander Voloshin, Putin’s onetime chief of staff who now works in the private sector and occasionally assists the Kremlin’s diplomacy with the West, said: “Any blockade of the Baltic or Kaliningrad could not be prevented by conventional means.” He said Russia’s forces were tied up in Ukraine, leaving the Kremlin with no choice but to respond with “immediate, pre-emptive nuclear strikes.”

In late October, Pentagon officials warned Baltic counterparts to tone down rhetoric about targeting Russia in the region to avoid a flare-up, according to European and U.S. officials. The Pentagon did not respond to a request for comment. The warning had been reported earlier by Reuters.

Western officials then scrambled to convince Moscow that NATO had no intention of imposing a blockade, and Finland summoned the Russian ambassador to defuse the crisis. Officials familiar with Stubb’s thinking said he feared the message was not getting through. They turned to a Russian oligarch with a direct line to the Kremlin. According to those people, the man traveled to Norway for consultations with top officials before returning to Moscow to convey a message to Putin. Days later, both leaders were speaking to Putin to dispel his fears of a naval blockade.

Afterward, Putin publicly praised the two leaders but suggested that “someone” wanted to turn the Baltic into another Strait of Hormuz, where the U.S.-Iran war had temporarily disrupted oil shipments and sent energy prices soaring. “We don’t want that, but we will respond to any challenge,” Putin said. If a confrontation went too far, he warned, “there will be no winners.”

Stubb’s reassurance that Finland would adhere to the laws of the sea in its dealings with Russia was tested in recent days when Ukrainian drones attacked a Russian cargo ship near Finland. Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky told Bloomberg, in an interview published Tuesday, that the ship was a legitimate target because it carried weapons, but he knew some of Kyiv’s European partners were worried about the Baltic. He said the drones struck the ship in Russian waters but that it drifted into Finnish waters.

Finnish authorities launched a rescue operation in coordination with Russia. The fire was extinguished, and the Russians were allowed to tow away the disabled ship, Finnish officials said. Coordination between Finnish and Russian authorities to rescue and recover the ship helped prevent the situation from escalating.

Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov said Monday that the attack escalated tensions in the Baltic and that Russia expected Baltic states to use their influence over Kyiv to stop such assaults.
