## Minority Veterans of America is challenging the VA rule in federal court

The U.S. Department of Veterans Affairs’ abortion restrictions are likely to remain in place for at least six months, if not longer, as the court timeline in a federal lawsuit stretches. Justice Department attorneys representing the VA asked the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit to extend their response deadline from Dec. 9 to Feb. 8. The court had not approved the request; approval would extend the overall case by several months, and oral arguments may be at least six more months away.

The VA policy, in effect for nearly a year, follows a military medical-benefits system that, dating to the 1990s, provided abortion care when a pregnancy resulted from rape or incest or when the care was necessary to save the pregnant patient’s life. Beginning in 2022, the VA provided abortions for health-preserving reasons and allowed members to be reimbursed for travel when they were stationed in a state with an abortion ban.

At the end of 2025, the VA announced that it would provide no abortion care even in cases of rape or incest, allow no abortion counseling and rescind the travel-reimbursement policy. The department received thousands of public comments on the proposal but said it would make no changes in response. The rule was finalized Dec. 31, 2025.

One of Secretary of Defense Pete Hegseth’s first directives after taking office in January 2025 was to rescind the 2022 policy. The VA began the formal rule-adoption process in August that year.

The VA’s legal position relies on a section of law written in the 1990s that the department says excluded infertility services, abortion and pregnancy care from veteran medical benefits. Opponents dispute the administration’s narrow application of the section and argue that it does not exclude abortion counseling. The VA says ectopic pregnancy terminations and miscarriages are not considered abortions and can be treated.

In its response to public comments, the agency said: “As explained in this rule, VA can provide care to pregnant women in life-threatening circumstances under the medical benefits package.” It added: “In all other circumstances, VA will not discuss options for abortions outside VA and will not refer veterans and CHAMPVA beneficiaries to abortions outside VA.”

Minority Veterans of America filed the lawsuit in May. The organization, which has more than 3,600 members in 49 states and the District of Columbia, argues that the VA made the change without a reasonable explanation, violating administrative law. It also says its members have already been harmed because they cannot discuss abortion options with care providers.

The case remains pending. Oral arguments may be at least six more months away, making any department-level changes unlikely in the meantime. Minority Veterans of America also argues in its complaint that veterans of reproductive age have high rates of chronic medical and mental-health conditions that can increase risks associated with pregnancy. The complaint specifically cites post-traumatic stress disorder, hypertension and kidney disease, which it says can heighten the risk of hemorrhage and placental issues.

According to a friend-of-the-court brief submitted by 212 congressional Democrats, the VA told staff in February 2025 that records showed about 462,000 women veterans of reproductive age were enrolled in VA health care. The brief also cited an estimated 112,000 women of reproductive age covered through the Civilian Health and Medical Program of the VA, or CHAMPVA, which provides coverage to veterans’ families, including children.

The VA says it is complying with the statute and adhering to a U.S. Department of Justice legal opinion issued before the rule was finalized that stated the VA could not legally provide abortion care. The department also said the government had no obligation to subsidize or “facilitate” abortion. The current policy does not allow reimbursement for travel expenses or special leave to seek abortion care, both of which were permitted under the 2022 rule.

All 47 Democratic U.S. senators and 165 Democratic U.S. representatives signed a friend-of-the-court brief in October asking the court to override the rule. In March, those senators voted in favor of a resolution that would have allowed Congress to block the rule, but it failed in a 48-50 vote. Democrats will hold nine more Senate seats in January, but Stateline reported that the new congressional majority could seemingly do little to change the policy without passing legislation, which would take time.

Jordan Goldberg, policy director of the Reproductive Freedom Alliance, a coalition of state governors who support reproductive rights, said a limited window of a few months for challenging an agency rule had passed.

So far, no Republican elected officials or anti-abortion groups have filed briefs in support of the VA’s policy in the lawsuit. When the rule was initially proposed, Susan B. Anthony Pro-Life America, one of the leading national anti-abortion organizations, called it a “major win.”

“We thank President Trump and VA Secretary (Doug) Collins for restoring the rule of law and ending the Biden administration’s illegal policy that forced taxpayers to fund abortions through VA hospitals and violated state pro-life laws,” SBA President Marjorie Dannenfelser said in a statement at the time. “Thankfully this injustice is now corrected.”

Some states with the highest numbers of service members and veterans, including Alabama, Florida and Texas, also have some of the country’s most restrictive abortion laws. According to estimates from the nonprofit National Partnership for Women and Families, more than 400,000 women veterans lived in states that already had an abortion ban or were likely to ban it in 2023. The organization said the figure represented more than half of the women veterans in the country.

“The decision to rescind that rule was both harmful and cruel,” Goldberg said. California and other states with Democratic leaders are working on state-level policies to help veterans who need abortion care and cannot receive it through the VA, but Goldberg said those efforts have limits. “A state cannot take the place of the federal government,” Goldberg said.

Sylvia Andersh joined the U.S. Air Force in 1974, when women were new to the military and enlistment limits remained. The 1973 U.S. Supreme Court case Roe v. Wade, which affirmed a constitutional right to abortion access, was barely a year old. Just before the end of her four years on active duty, Andersh needed an abortion. She said military personnel supported her decision and helped her obtain care at a private clinic. She said she does not know what the current military stance and restrictive laws in 13 states might have meant for her care.

Andersh, who left home at 18 to join the military as one of few women service members, went on to have a son in 1986. Four years later, she had a miscarriage at 13 weeks and needed treatment. “It’s been very painful to see that women who have served their country do not deserve the respect of having control over their own bodies,” Andersh said.

Andersh said she believes the new policy is designed to drive women away. “It puts them in a precarious position, because when you’re in the military, you’re supposed to be combat ready at any time, and how do you navigate that when you have responsibilities as a mother and a parent?” she said.
