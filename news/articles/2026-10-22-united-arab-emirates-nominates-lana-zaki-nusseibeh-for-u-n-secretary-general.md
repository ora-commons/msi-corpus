## United Arab Emirates nominates Lana Zaki Nusseibeh for U.N. secretary-general

**Subtype:** fact

- The United Arab Emirates nominated former deputy foreign minister Lana Zaki Nusseibeh to succeed António Guterres as U.N. secretary-general.
- The United States, China, Russia, France and the U.K. each hold a veto over the selection of a U.N. secretary-general.
- Numerous U.N. member states have demanded that a woman lead the organization for the first time.
- Ammar Mahmoud criticized Nusseibeh’s nomination by citing the UAE’s human rights record and support for Rapid Defense Forces in Darfur.
- The United Arab Emirates rejected the characterization that it supplied weapons to any party in Sudan’s civil war.
- Nusseibeh said her priority as secretary-general would be spotlighting the humanitarian situation in Sudan.

## Nominee would be first Gulf national and the UAE's first Muslim

The Wall Street Journal wrote that “A late entry by a seasoned Emirati diplomat in the race to be the next secretary-general of the United Nations has shaken up a fraught process in which none of the other candidates has emerged as a clear favorite.” The UAE formally nominated its former deputy foreign minister, Lana Zaki Nusseibeh, this week to succeed António Guterres. Nusseibeh, 47, would be the first secretary-general from a Gulf nation and the UAE’s first Muslim to hold the post. Guterres, a former prime minister of Portugal, is due to step down at year-end after a decade.

International Crisis Group head of U.N. affairs Daniel Forti said Nusseibeh’s entrance “marks a pivot point” in the race and noted that she is well known around the United Nations. He said that jumping in after no leading candidate had emerged “is deliberate, and suggests her team believes she will not face similar roadblocks.”

Nusseibeh said a number of governments had approached her about running, in part to encourage generational change. “I’ve built up over a decade a strong level of trust amongst major powers, but also middle powers who have become so influential today on the global stage,” she said. In a vision statement, she pledged “speaking frankly with every government and answering to none. There will be no exemptions for friends and no grudges against critics.”

The contest has no fixed deadline and is running later than in past cycles. Numerous U.N. member states have demanded that a woman lead the institution for the first time. The Journal reported that Latin American leaders have argued it is high time for the next secretary-general to come from their region and have promoted some of the strongest candidates in this year’s race, though none had drawn broad enthusiasm from the wider U.N. membership.

In five of six nonbinding Security Council ballots held among members since July, Rebeca Grynspan Mayufis, a former vice president of Costa Rica, prevailed, The Wall Street Journal reported. Like other candidates, she had been discouraged by some key players, possibly including the United States, during what The Journal described as a complex and secret voting process. Guyana nominee Carolyn Rodrigues Birkett, who has served as its U.N. ambassador and held senior diplomatic roles, has drawn both support and detractors and sits slightly ahead of Argentine Rafael Mariano Grossi, director general of the International Atomic Energy Agency.

A candidate ultimately needs backing from the United States, China, Russia, France and the United Kingdom because each permanent member holds a veto over the choice. None of the five has made its preferences public, but leaked polling suggests every leading candidate has encountered at least some resistance from a permanent member. The Journal said Nusseibeh could benefit from divisions within the group, including a deepening U.S.-China rivalry, because the UAE could point to a strong working relationship with each of the five.

Nusseibeh spent much of her career at the United Nations, including more than a decade as the Emirates’ ambassador, and The Wall Street Journal described her as one of the country’s most experienced diplomats. The Journal said the UAE transformed during that period from a small player on the world stage into a formidable middle power with huge resources for financial diplomacy and a small but professional military. Her nomination was presented as an effort to capitalize on the UAE’s efforts to establish itself as a trusted global mediator. The Journal described the UAE as “a federation of monarchies governed largely by absolute rulers” and said Nusseibeh was known as one of a handful of technocrats who drive the country’s policies.

Nusseibeh is much younger than any other candidate in the field, though she would be slightly older than the youngest-ever elected U.N. secretary-general, Sweden’s Dag Hammarskjöld, who took office at 47 in 1953. The Journal described her as coming from an important Palestinian family in Jerusalem responsible for opening and closing the doors of the Church of the Holy Sepulchre, one of Christianity’s holiest sites. Her family moved to the UAE in 1967 after the Israeli-Arab war, and her father, Zaki Nusseibeh, became an important adviser to Sheikh Zayed al-Nahyan, the Emirates’ founding father.

Among her biggest achievements as ambassador to the U.N. was helping steer passage of Resolution 2720 in December 2023, The Journal said, adding that the resolution helped accelerate humanitarian aid into Gaza “during Israel’s war on Hamas after the militant group’s Oct. 7, 2023, attacks.”

The Journal reported that Nusseibeh’s bid comes amid high Middle East tension following attacks by the United States and Israel against Iran starting in February, and that Iran’s response included firing missiles at neighboring nations, including the UAE across the Persian Gulf. The nomination also drew immediate criticism of the UAE’s human rights record. Critics pointed to the UAE’s conduct at home, where they said it repressed dissent, and to its sizable economic influence abroad, which they said had undercut democracy and funded violent players in Sudan, Libya and Yemen.

The UAE rejected any characterization that it supplied weapons to any party in Sudan’s civil war and denied any role in Libya’s ongoing conflict.

Ammar Mahmoud, a top U.N. diplomat for Sudan, criticized Nusseibeh’s nomination based on what he described as the UAE’s poor human rights record, including its support for the Rapid Defense Forces in Darfur. The U.N. has called Darfur the world’s worst humanitarian crisis. Mahmoud said, “She is very much part of the propaganda machine that the U.A.E. is using to try to whitewash its image.” He cast doubt that the UAE would nominate her if it believed she would act independently of state interests.

Nusseibeh pointed to her decision to take a leave of absence from the UAE government as evidence of her commitment to the United Nations. “As U.N. secretary-general, if I were successful, my priority would be to continue spotlighting the desperate humanitarian situation in Sudan,” she said.

The secretary-general serves as a global mediator and administrator of a vast bureaucracy created after World War II, but the office has limited power and an increasingly pinched budget. The Journal reported that the Trump administration “has starved the U.N. of funding by accusing it of mismanagement and misalignment with U.S. interests,” even though New York hosts its headquarters. Nusseibeh appeared to nod toward Washington’s efforts to refocus the United Nations on its core mission, The Journal said, by acknowledging in her vision statement that funding cuts were likely permanent. She said, “A U.N. that responds by asking for more of the same will not be heard.”

Forti said the uncertainty overshadowing the selection, which is taking longer than the previous two cycles, “reflects the consequences of an eroding international order and questions about the U.N.’s relevance in world affairs.” Along with political and financial pressures on the organization, he said, “This has rendered the job of secretary-general far less appealing but no less important.”
