## Peltola leaves majority leader choice open after Democrats reach 56 seats

Mary Peltola, the newly elected U.S. senator from Alaska, told The Associated Press on Wednesday that the state’s voters sent a clear message that they wanted relief from “crippling” prices for fuel, healthcare and other necessities and did not elect her to be partisan. Peltola said she would work across party lines on issues important to Alaska, including affordability and infrastructure needs.

She called both directives “clear and resounding,” saying voters had given her two messages: reduce costs and avoid partisan behavior. She had not yet decided whom she would support as Senate majority leader, noting that her race had just been called Tuesday night.

Peltola defeated two-term Republican incumbent Dan Sullivan in the last U.S. Senate race to be decided after the Nov. 3 midterm elections. Democrats secured a 56-44 majority after flipping nine GOP-held seats, while Peltola said she had not committed on the majority-leader vote.

Current Senate Minority Leader Chuck Schumer, who is facing a challenge from Maryland Sen. Chris Van Hollen, told reporters in Washington, D.C., that Peltola’s victory was “an exclamation point on what has been a great election year.” Schumer said he and Peltola spoke Tuesday night. “The American people have given us their votes. Now we have to earn their trust,” he said. He said Democrats “have to produce,” lower costs and end the “corruption” and chaos in President Donald Trump’s administration.

Republican incumbent Dan Sullivan’s campaign did not respond to messages seeking comment Tuesday or Wednesday. Peltola said she had not yet heard from Sullivan but had “every confidence that we’ll be in communication in the next few weeks.”

Spikes in fuel prices caused by the war in Iran have hit many Alaska communities hard, particularly in rural areas where fuel must be flown or barged in. Her commitment to work across party lines came as she described fuel, healthcare and other necessities as central concerns for voters.

Peltola said she was with family and close friends when the race was called. “You know, for so many days we’ve wondered if today would be the day,” she said. Alaska accepts absentee ballots postmarked by Election Day and mailed within the U.S. up to 10 days after the election and up to 15 days for overseas voters.

From the start of the campaign, Peltola said she had “a good feeling about this race.” The Democrat, who is Yup’ik, previously held Alaska’s only House seat and was the first Alaska Native to serve in Congress. She lost her bid for a second full term in 2024 but became central to Democrats’ efforts to retake the Senate this year.

Late in the campaign, Peltola faced allegations that members of her team helped recruit a retired teacher named Dan J. Sullivan to run as a way to pull votes from the incumbent. President Donald Trump called for Peltola’s prosecution after The New York Times reported the allegations the week before the election. Peltola denied any involvement.

On Wednesday, Peltola called the allegations and Trump’s comments “a very unfortunate distraction.” She said, “I personally know that I am innocent, so I don’t spend a lot of time handwringing” about it. Peltola added, “I know my involvement has been zero, and I believe in the American justice system and I believe in the processes that we have. And I have had no involvement in any of the unfortunate distractions that Alaskan voters have been subjected to.”

The U.S. Justice Department last month confirmed the existence of a criminal investigation, but Alaska’s U.S. attorney did not say what potential crimes authorities were looking into. Peltola said the Justice Department had not contacted her.

The Alaska Bureau of Investigation opened an investigation into the allegations at the request of outgoing Republican Gov. Mike Dunleavy. Austin McDaniel, deputy commissioner with the state Department of Public Safety, said by email Wednesday that the investigation was ongoing and the agency could not provide additional details.

Dan J. Sullivan, who was in fourth place with less than 2% of the vote, has denied any contact with Peltola’s campaign or running at the Democrats’ behest. He has not returned messages since The New York Times story was published.

Peltola’s remarks left three questions unresolved for the incoming senator: whom she will support as majority leader, what potential crimes the federal criminal investigation may involve, and whether the state inquiry into the recruitment allegations will produce additional findings.
