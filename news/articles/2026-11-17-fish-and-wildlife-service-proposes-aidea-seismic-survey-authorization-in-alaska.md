## Gwich'in and Earthjustice dispute federal finding of minimal polar bear disturbance

The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service proposed Monday an incidental harassment authorization that would allow the Alaska Industrial Development and Export Authority to run seismic surveys for oil in the Arctic National Wildlife Refuge coastal plain, a step that could disturb polar bears managed under the Marine Mammals Protection Act. The proposal is set to be published in the Federal Register on Tuesday, opening a mandatory 30-day public comment period. If granted, the authorization would bring AIDEA one step closer to actual oil exploration work in the refuge.

AIDEA is the state development agency that holds six active oil leases in the coastal plain, and it would conduct the survey through contractors. Seismic surveys use soundwaves sent through the ground to map geologic features and help identify potential deposits of oil and gas; the loud sounds associated with seismic operations pose potential hazards to polar bears and other noise-sensitive animals.

Incidental harassment authorizations are the mechanism the Marine Mammals Protection Act provides for activities that disturb species such as polar bears. In the proposed authorization, the Fish and Wildlife Service said it has concluded that the seismic surveys planned by AIDEA will result in only minimal and short-term disturbances to polar bears.

AIDEA's plan, as described in the proposed authorization, begins with aerial infrared surveys to locate polar bear dens that should be avoided, followed by on-the-ground surveys with seismic equipment, supported by worker camps. The Fish and Wildlife Service had noted in the authorization that AIDEA planned to start survey work as early as this month. AIDEA now expects the program to start no earlier than January, according to a status report AIDEA filed earlier this month in federal court as part of a pending legal case that seeks to block oil exploration in the refuge.

AIDEA's holdings in the refuge comprise six leases acquired in a January 2021 sale conducted at the end of the first Trump administration, plus three more leases the agency acquired in a U.S. Bureau of Land Management auction in June. In May, AIDEA approved an expenditure of up to $190 million for exploration work in the refuge, mostly for the seismic program. Gov. Mike Dunleavy described it at the time as "a major investment" in Alaska's future.

Supporters of oil drilling in the refuge coastal plain have characterized AIDEA's seismic program as a necessary step toward responsible development that will help the state. Dunleavy said in a statement released by AIDEA: "ANWR represents one of many opportunities to secure long-term economic prosperity for future generations of Alaskans while strengthening American energy security."

Opponents of oil development said Monday that the Fish and Wildlife Service's analysis vastly understates the impacts that seismic exploration would have on the Arctic environment. The groups weighing in included the Gwich'in Steering Committee, which represents Gwich'in Athabascan tribes in Alaska and Canada, and Earthjustice. Kristen Moreland, the organization's executive director, said in a statement: "The Gwich'in Steering Committee is appalled by the level of industrial and human activity that AIDEA is outlining in their request." Moreland added: "Seismic testing, and everything that comes along with it, would have far-reaching and devastating impacts on the Coastal Plain."

The Gwich'in Steering Committee and a coalition of conservation groups represented by Earthjustice are plaintiffs in the pending lawsuit seeking to prevent AIDEA from carrying out its planned exploration. AIDEA's status report, which sets the earliest start at January, was filed in that case.

Scientists with the University of Alaska Fairbanks have cautioned that seismic surveys in the refuge coastal plain will likely damage tundra and permafrost terrain and harm the area's wildlife. In a 2020 report, they concluded that winter snow cover in the coastal plain is too patchy to provide protection from heavy equipment moving across the ground, and that freshwater sources are too scarce in that part of Arctic Alaska to support temporary ice roads that are used for winter work.

The refuge's coastal plain has been the subject of a decades-long national debate between development advocates and conservationists, with different Alaska Native groups on either side. The proposed authorization adds a federal step to that dispute: the 30-day comment period beginning with Tuesday's Federal Register publication, and a decision afterward on whether AIDEA's contractors may disturb polar bears while mapping the ground for oil.

For now the authorization remains a proposal, and the lawsuit seeking to block the exploration remains pending in federal court. If both clear, the seismic survey would bring AIDEA a step closer to actual oil exploration work in the refuge.
