## Projections show current policies leave Australia short of its 2035 target

**Subtype:** fact

- Australia’s federal government projects that current policies will cut climate pollution 48% below 2005 levels by 2035, below the country’s 62% to 70% target.
- Official projections put emissions 42% below 2005 levels by 2030, within reach of Australia’s 43% target, if renewable energy supplies 82% of electricity nationally by 2030.
- A separate government report says climate pollution fell 1.3%, or 6.2 million tonnes, in the year to June.
- The Climate Change Authority says meeting the 2030 and 2035 targets requires sustained annual emissions cuts of about 25 million to 30 million tonnes.

## The 2030 outlook depends on renewables reaching 82% of electricity

Australian climate department projections published on Nov. 26, 2026 show that current policies would cut climate pollution 48% below 2005 levels by 2035, far below Australia’s target reduction of 62% to 70%. The same projections put 2030 emissions 42% below 2005 levels, within reach of the 43% target, but only if the government reaches its goal of 82% of electricity coming from renewables nationally by 2030. Climate Change and Energy Minister Chris Bowen acknowledged progress while warning of “undeniable headwinds.”

Several experts said the 82% renewable-electricity goal was unlikely to be met on schedule because of delays in rolling out large-scale wind farms and new transmission lines. The department’s assessment said that if unforeseen delays and supply-chain bottlenecks pushed the goal back to 2032, Australia would cut emissions by only 38% below 2005 levels by 2030.

In an annual climate statement to parliament on Thursday, Bowen said Australia was “making good progress, we are facing undeniable headwinds.” The projections said progress toward the 2035 target had not improved since the previous year’s release.

Speaking after failed climate talks at Cop31, Bowen criticized the Queensland Liberal National government’s decision to “abandon its renewable energy targets and extend the operation of coal-fired power plants,” saying it would “significantly impact” national emissions. He said: “Continuing to sweat intermittent and unreliable coal just isn’t the answer to the question of our future energy security. And walking back previously approved renewable projects creates uncertainty, which then goes on to jeopardise future renewable investments.” He added: “Our system needs certainty. It does not need sovereign risk.”

A separate report released Thursday found that climate pollution fell 1.3%, or 6.2 million tonnes, in the year to June. Bowen called the result “a reduction at one-and-a-half times the rate of the last 10 years.” Australia’s total annual emissions were 25.3% below 2005 levels. Emissions from generating electricity fell 3.6%, reflecting “record renewables generation and the ongoing displacement of coal and gas.” Gas generation fell 23.2%, while coal generation decreased 3.6% over the year.

The Climate Change Authority’s annual progress assessment said the pace of emissions reductions must “accelerate substantially.” It said achieving the 2030 and 2035 targets would require sustained average annual reductions of about 25 million to 30 million tonnes.

The authority said transport was approaching a “tipping point,” with electric and plug-in hybrid vehicles accounting for 29% of new light-vehicle sales in 2026, up from 13% in 2025. It said more responsibility for emissions cuts would shift toward “industry, resources, agriculture and other harder-to-abate activities.”

The authority also warned that additional action was needed on methane, described as a potent greenhouse that amplifies global heating over a short period. It recommended deploying established technologies to detect and repair leaks at mining sites and minimizing avoidable venting and flaring of fossil fuel gas.

Shadow energy and emissions reduction minister Dan Tehan focused his response on electricity prices, the Cop31 breakdown and the cost of free coffee at the Australian pavilion at the summit. Tehan said national emissions had increased since Labor was elected in 2022. The Guardian said his comparison did not mention that climate pollution was suppressed in 2022 because of economic shutdowns during the Covid-19 pandemic.

Greens deputy leader and climate spokesperson Steph Hodgins-May said the government’s climate rhetoric “is just so far apart from the action.” She said the strategy relied on further electrification while the government continued to approve new coal and gas. “They’re telling us they want to take climate action, but [are] simultaneously approving 38 new coal and gas mines,” Hodgins-May said.

Australian Conservation Foundation head of advocacy Gavan McFadzean said the government report card showed strong progress in some areas but clear room for improvement. He said the renewables build was progressing but faced headwinds from “uncooperative states” and opposition to wind and solar in rural communities “for political purposes.” McFadzean said exported emissions from burning coal and gas overseas, totaling 1.1 billion tonnes in 2025-26, “is not in this statement and remains a blind spot.” He called exported emissions “the biggest handbrake on Australian climate action” and said Australia needed to phase out coal and gas exports.

Taken together, the official material sets out a 2030 path dependent on renewable-energy and transmission delivery and a 2035 path requiring stronger policies and faster annual reductions. Bowen’s warning over state policy and the Climate Change Authority’s call to “accelerate substantially” frame the work still ahead.
