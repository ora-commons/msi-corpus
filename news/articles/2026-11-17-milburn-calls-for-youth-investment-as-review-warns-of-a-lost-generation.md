## Whately demands benefit cuts; Labour backbenchers warn young people could lose benefits

Alan Milburn presented a Neets review at Google HQ on Tuesday, calling for the UK government to invest in young people and recalibrate how the state engages with them. Guardian columnist John Crace wrote that the announcement “told us nothing we hadn’t already heard, but it still felt momentous.”

In Crace’s account, Milburn rejected the characterization of young people as lazy, arguing that they had been let down rather than written off. He called for investment in their futures on the model of other northern European countries and said the “Great British promise” to young people was being broken.

Recounting the review’s highlights, Crace wrote that Britain was “in danger of creating a lost generation,” adding that Britain might already have created one. Milburn said more than 10% of young people under 25 had been out of work, education or training for more than 20 years. He said hundreds of thousands of people could not be returned to work merely by cutting benefits, describing that approach as delusional. His review called for a culture shift and a recalibration in how the state engaged with young people.

Crace described the review as one of the country’s worst-kept secrets, saying there was no one left who had not heard of it, let alone read most of it. He said no day had passed without some part of it leaking to the media and that it had become part of society’s fabric for the past six months. Milburn had also been giving interviews to everyone, whether or not they wanted one, Crace wrote.

At the Commons, the speaker asked that concern be recorded that copies of the report had not been laid before the House. Crace said nobody needed a copy because the report was already known “off by heart.” Work and Pensions Secretary Pat McFadden, surrounded by Andy Burnham and most of the cabinet, then gave a 10-minute statement repeating much of Milburn’s account. Crace said McFadden sounded gloomier than Milburn because he was more enthusiastic. Labour benches were packed, and barely a dozen Tory backbench MPs were present for what Crace said might be a seismic shift in policy.

Crace placed the session in the context of two and a half years of criticism that the government lacked vision and lurched from one crisis to the next. He wrote that the session provided, at last, the missing vision and described the review as a major shift in how the state would engage with a generation of young people who had been ignored or left on benefits. Crace noted that Keir Starmer, albeit late in the day, had commissioned the review but that Burnham would receive the credit.

Shadow work and pensions secretary Helen Whately demanded that billions more be cut from benefits. In Crace’s account, Whately claimed Milburn was “just following Tory policy” while calling the review a “Labour benefits bonanza.” The Commons dispute centered on whether reducing benefits would help young people return to work or whether the government needed to expand investment in employment, education and training.

A few Labour backbenchers supported most of the review but warned McFadden that some young people might lose out on benefits, Crace wrote. He described the intervention as a warning shot ahead of a battle to be fought later. Suella Braverman also asked about immigrants during the session, drawing little attention, according to Crace. He described the remainder of the session as passing with little incident.

In a comic passage about the review’s lengthy pre-release period, Crace wrote that ITV had commissioned a four-part docudrama on the report’s writing, Andrew Lloyd Webber’s *Neets: The Musical* would open just before Christmas, and Mud’s “That’s Neet, That’s Neet, I Really Love Your Tiger Feet” was the hot favourite for the UK’s entry for next year’s Eurovision.

Crace said it remained unclear whether new jobs would exist or become Milburn’s Potemkin village. He wrote that in 10 years, the review might mark a shift in society’s attitudes toward young people or be forgotten and filed away as “too difficult.”
