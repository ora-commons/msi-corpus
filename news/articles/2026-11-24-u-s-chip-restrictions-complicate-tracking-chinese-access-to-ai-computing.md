## Holding-company webs obscure the ultimate user of data center services

Data centers and chip makers are working to determine whether Chinese companies are secretly accessing their computing power amid U.S. chip export restrictions, Risk Journal reported. The immediate question is which company ultimately uses the capacity, not only which legal customer appears on a provider’s list.

Legal and artificial intelligence technology experts who spoke with Risk Journal said that giving customer lists a once-over and separating out China-based firms is not enough. A customer list can show the customer named on a service contract and that customer’s location, but it may not identify the party behind the service.

Data center customers can shield ultimate users through complex webs of holding companies. Such arrangements can place the named customer at one level of ownership while the party actually using the computing power sits behind other entities.

For chip makers and data centers, identifying the ultimate user bears directly on the question of whether Chinese companies are obtaining computing capacity under U.S. chip export restrictions. The end-user check therefore remains central to the compliance problem described by Risk Journal.

The report does not establish that Chinese companies have accessed the capacity. It identifies a verification gap: the entity named in a customer list may differ from the company using the service.

For data centers, the distinction is between the entity that contracts for a service and the company that ultimately uses it. The same distinction matters to chip makers assessing whether a Chinese company is the ultimate user of computing capacity.

Names and locations alone cannot answer whether a Chinese company is the party using the computing power. Risk Journal’s legal and artificial intelligence technology experts said an end-user review requires more than separating China-based firms from customer lists.
