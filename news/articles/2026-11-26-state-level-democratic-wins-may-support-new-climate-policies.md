## Spears says cost, not climate, was clearly on the ballot

State-level Democratic victories in the 2026 midterms may give state governments new opportunities to advance climate policy, Climate Cabinet executive director Caroline Spears told PBS NewsHour. More than 100 of the roughly 120 races the group targeted were won by candidates it backed, she said. Spears said cost was clearly on the ballot and climate change was not a leading issue, while governors, state legislatures and public utility commissions could make progress on clean energy over the next two years.

Lisa Desjardins opened the PBS NewsHour segment by calling climate change a national issue that fell under the radar during the midterm elections, even as Democrats won nationwide and at the state level. Brangham said the Trump administration had rolled back many of the ways the United States addresses the warming of the planet, including closing major research centers and blocking renewable energy projects. He described Climate Cabinet as an advocacy group that promotes and supports candidates who back climate action.

Brangham asked whether climate change itself had been spoken about or placed on the ballot in many of the races. Spears said what was clearly on the ballot was cost. Knocking on doors in Bucks County a few weeks before the election, she said she heard residents' utility bills rising and gas prices going up as a primary concern. She described Bucks County as a critical battleground district that people across the country watch every year to gauge what is happening.

Spears also said clean energy is fast and inexpensive to connect to the grid and can lower people's bills and other costs, especially when gas prices are high. She acknowledged that climate change was not a top-line issue, but said there were clean-energy moments throughout the campaign that were interesting to see.

Brangham said gas prices, utility bills and other affordability issues were being argued as climate-change issues, even if they were not often called that. Spears agreed and said one reason home and property insurance prices had risen so much over the last few years was climate disasters and climate-fueled disasters across the country.

Climate Cabinet considers both the impact of a candidate's office and the candidate's track record. Spears said its first criterion was whether a person was running for an office that has a big impact on "our ability to build clean energy jobs and solve climate change." The second criterion was a record that included previous votes to expand solar access and neighborhood-level work that let residents take climate action into their own hands locally.

Arizona was the first state Spears cited. She said the state has so much solar potential but that policies she described as bad had held it back, and she was excited about a change in state leadership. Sen. Sundareshan had worked in water and energy law before running for office and was set to become Arizona Senate president next year, Spears said. She said that relevant expertise, concern for the issues and the ability to bring solutions to the table contributed to her optimism over the next two years.

Brangham described data centers as central to the election and asked whether there was a climate connection or whether the candidates Climate Cabinet backed were making one. Spears said what voters generally raised about data centers was cost and economic anxiety. She described data centers as a physical manifestation of broader economic anxieties and said people were asking whether they were raising utility bills or why utility rates had risen so much in recent years.

Spears cited Peter Hubbard, who ran last year for the Georgia Public Service Commission and had to run again this year. She said he would be in the majority at the commission and that Hubbard had already said he was really thinking about how to lower people's bills with clean energy.

Brangham then asked about skeptics who argued that, without a change in federal policy, state-level action would remain piecemeal. His question cited the federal government's demonstrated ability to block renewable energy projects in multiple states and questioned whether states and localities could deliver on their promises. Spears called that the wrong way to look at energy policy. She said each of the 50 states has an enormous role in building energy projects and the clean-energy economy. Recalling her work in the solar industry, she said projects could live or die under policies at the governor, public utility commission and state legislature levels, and she said that role would continue.

Spears said states pass policy at a rate five times that of the federal government. She said the federal government can be slow and dysfunctional and argued that states are where progress can be made over the next couple of years. No specific measure or timetable was identified in the interview; the developments cited were Arizona's incoming Senate leadership, Hubbard's expected service in the majority at the Georgia Public Service Commission and states' existing authority over energy projects.
