## AI hardware and related tech products account for more than one-tenth of world goods trade

Kristalina Georgieva used her appearance at the 2026 Apec summit in Shenzhen to connect the AI investment boom to monetary policy. The managing director of the Washington-based International Monetary Fund said demand for AI hardware and related technology products was pushing consumer prices higher around the world, adding to the inflationary effects of the energy shock tied to the war in the Middle East.

Her warning was that the world’s most powerful central banks would have to raise interest rates. “This calls for a tightening of monetary policy in many economies,” she said.

Georgieva said AI demand and the war-related energy shock would push global inflation to 4.7% this year. The rapid rollout of AI was also contributing to stronger-than-expected economic growth in several countries, she said, despite headwinds from the Iran war.

The European Central Bank raised rates in June for the first time since 2023, followed by the US Federal Reserve in September and the Bank of England earlier this month. Those increases followed the February outbreak of war, which drove the global oil price above $100 a barrel and reignited inflationary pressures around the world.

Financial markets expect further increases in the cost of borrowing next year as the rapid rollout of AI supports growth against the volatile backdrop of the conflict. Global oil prices have fallen back in recent weeks following the US midterm elections and an upswing in crude supply.

Other risks could interrupt the AI-driven growth. Fears about an AI bubble and febrile conditions in global equity markets could dent business investment. Economists also warn of a slowdown in demand as high inflation squeezes households.

Georgieva drew a contrast between the durability of the two forces affecting the global economy. While the war-related energy shock would eventually dissipate, she said, “AI is for ever,” making it a significant driver of world trade, competitiveness, productivity and economic growth.

AI hardware and related technology products now account for more than one-tenth of the world goods trade, and that share is rapidly rising, Georgieva said. She said some countries were benefiting more from AI investment than others.

Her recommendations focused on economic readiness and risk controls. “To put it simply: you snooze, you lose,” she said. “Countries need digital infrastructure, labour markets and workers that are ready for AI, and risk mitigation – especially to control financial stability and cybersecurity risks.”

Georgieva’s comments came a day after the European Central Bank expressed concerns about the debt AI companies were taking on to fuel their growth. In its financial stability review, the central bank said there were “historic parallels” with the dotcom bubble that led to a technology market crash at the turn of the millennium and the sub-prime mortgage bubble that set off the 2008 financial crisis.

The European Central Bank warned that any fallout from an AI crash “could lead to spillovers affecting euro-area financial stability.”

Against that risk backdrop, the AI sector was still booming. Anthropic, which makes the Claude chatbot, debuted on the US stock market on Thursday, pricing at $83 per share and raising the largest investment of any initial public offering. The company was expected to become one of the world’s most valuable publicly traded businesses at $1.5tn, despite reporting losses of $80bn in the first half of 2026.

Georgieva’s warning paired tighter monetary policy with preparations for AI’s broader economic effects. She said the war-related energy shock was temporary, while AI’s influence on prices, trade, competitiveness, productivity and growth would endure, leaving central banks and governments to address both borrowing costs and financial stability.
