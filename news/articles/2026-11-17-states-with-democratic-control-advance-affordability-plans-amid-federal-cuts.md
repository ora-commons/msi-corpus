## State Medicaid costs rise while most scheduled reductions remain ahead

Following state-level election wins, states with new or strengthened Democratic trifectas — control of both statehouse chambers and the governor’s office — have the most ambitious affordability plans. The proposals include subsidizing child care and housing construction, expanding tax credits for parents and other caregivers, using state funds to forgive medical debt and offering free school meals to more students. At the same time, federal reductions to Medicaid, food assistance and other programs are forcing states to tighten budgets.

Beginning in January, Democrats will hold a majority of governorships for the first time since 2010 and unified control of two dozen state legislatures. The election results will add six trifectas, bringing the total to 22. Minnesota Democrats want to offer new health-care tax credits, Wisconsin Democrats are eyeing free school meals for every child and property-tax relief, and Pennsylvania Democrats want to invest state funds in housing construction.

State finances have improved in recent years, boosted by stronger economic growth, federal pandemic stimulus funds and rising sales- and income-tax collections, according to S&P Global analyst Oscar Padilla. Federal cuts to Medicaid, food assistance and other programs are beginning to strain those budgets as they phase in.

The Medicaid reductions from the tax and spending bill President Trump signed last year have been ramping slowly, but S&P Global Ratings estimates that 90% will occur in fiscal years 2029 through 2034. Reduced funding for the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program, or SNAP, also is already forcing states to tighten their belts, the report said. “Shifting federal policy dynamics could narrow states’ funding options to balance budgets while they execute large-scale investments that aim to address cost-of-living pressures,” the report said.

In Michigan, Gov.-elect Jocelyn Benson will be sworn in on Jan. 1 with a newly reclaimed trifecta behind her plans. She aims to pass a $5,000 tax credit for caregivers, expand medical-debt relief and widen access to child care and preventive healthcare. Benson also wants to guarantee teachers a minimum starting salary of $60,000 a year. Sarah Anthony, a Democrat who chairs Michigan’s Senate Appropriations Committee, said: “In the background of many individuals expecting Democrats to expand affordability initiatives, I have to remind folks that we have to keep the lights on.”

Anthony said her priority is maintaining programs Michigan has already adopted to improve affordability, including an expanded earned-income tax credit and free school meals. She said voters have demanded “bigger and bolder” assistance. “New revenue would be the only way to truly deliver new sweeping affordability measures,” she said.

Some Democrats want to scrap the state’s flat income tax in favor of a graduated system with higher rates for top earners, although that would require voter approval through a ballot measure.

Bob Schneider, a budget and tax policy expert at the Citizens Research Council of Michigan, said Michigan faces about a $700 million budget deficit for fiscal year 2028, partly driven by federal funding cuts. The state may receive higher-than-expected revenue this year from corporate income-tax collections, which could help close the gap, Schneider said. Major new financial commitments for affordability programs would require new taxes or spending cuts elsewhere, he said. Benson has said she won’t raise taxes on the middle class and that she will bolster revenue by closing tax loopholes, driving economic growth and making government more efficient.

Colorado voters replaced the state’s flat income tax with one that imposes higher rates on people and businesses making more than $500,000 a year. The new levy is expected to generate at least $2 billion in extra revenue annually. By law, the money must be spent on health care, child care and education.

Democratic Rep. Emily Sirota, chair of the state legislature’s Joint Budget Committee, said the new funds will more than close the $1 billion budget deficit Colorado had been facing for the coming fiscal year. But she said it is not clear how much of the extra money will be available beyond Medicaid as state Medicaid costs grow and federal Medicaid funding falls. “My personal goal is to ensure we are doing something transformational with these dollars,” Sirota said. She also said there is “a tremendous opportunity to make investments in our child care program,” while noting that a state commission is working on proposals to rein in Medicaid spending growth.

Democratic Gov.-elect Phil Weiser is promising to expand down-payment assistance for first-time home buyers, lower the cost of college and expand universal preschool. Democrats already had a trifecta in Colorado but gained supermajorities in both chambers of the legislature. Sirota won an election that will soon send her to the Colorado Senate.

In Nevada, Democratic Gov.-elect Aaron Ford unseated Republican incumbent Joe Lombardo, giving Democrats a trifecta. Ford has pledged to expand programs helping renters cover security deposits, utility payments and rent, increase funding for down-payment assistance for first-time home buyers, and invest in community health centers and mobile clinics. Medicaid insures about a quarter of Nevadans, and Ford has acknowledged the coverage threat posed by federal cuts. His campaign site pledged to work with state agencies “to mitigate the impact for Nevadans who will lose coverage.”

The common constraint is fiscal rather than legislative. State finances have improved, and the incoming Democratic trifectas give lawmakers authority to act, but 90% of the scheduled Medicaid reductions are still ahead. Lawmakers must weigh new commitments for housing, child care, education and health care against existing Medicaid obligations, a tension reflected in Michigan’s revenue debate and Colorado’s tax plan.
