## Lawmakers dispute environmental safeguards and judicial review limits

The Senate is expected to vote as soon as Thursday on legislation formally known as the Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act of 2026. The vote would be Congress’s first major action since this month’s midterm elections, which The Associated Press said “soundly repudiated President Donald Trump and congressional Republicans.”

The final vote was held up this week as Oregon’s two Democratic senators, Jeff Merkley and Ron Wyden, pushed to add amendments, including one to ban data centers on public lands. The bill’s four main champions — Republican Sens. Shelley Moore Capito of West Virginia and Mike Lee of Utah, and Democratic Sens. Martin Heinrich of New Mexico and Sheldon Whitehouse of Rhode Island — released a detailed set of amendments designed to bolster support. Democratic Sen. Maria Cantwell of Washington state called for a formal process to debate and amend the bill.

The bipartisan legislation is aimed at speeding permitting reviews for power plants, transmission lines and other energy infrastructure as lawmakers seek to increase the nation’s energy supplies to lower electricity prices and meet surging demand. Its sweeping changes would shorten the timeline for starting large projects, both clean energy and fossil fuel, and require data centers that propel artificial intelligence to pay for the energy they use. Anger over those facilities and rising electricity prices helped prompt Democrats at state and federal levels to pledge tighter limits on massive, energy-hungry data centers.

President Donald Trump urged approval of the bill in a recent social media post, calling it a “BIG WIN for U.S. ENERGY INDEPENDENCE and DOMINANCE.” He wrote that the legislation would ensure the United States could build pipelines, mines and power plants more quickly while “lowering Energy Costs for American Families.” Major industry groups representing developers in clean energy and the oil and gas sector support the bill as a way to deliver more electricity to the grid and avoid delays for infrastructure projects that can stretch a decade or more.

Wyoming Sen. John Barrasso, the Senate’s No. 2 Republican, said, “In America today, it takes too long and costs too much to build.” He said energy projects that would help lower prices “remain frozen for years” as they awaited federal approval. Barrasso also said, “Construction cannot start without first spending years in the regulatory penalty box,” driving up costs and creating uncertainty.

The bill would overhaul several environmental laws to make energy infrastructure easier to build. Democrats and environmental groups criticized limits on judicial review and changes to the National Environmental Policy Act, Clean Water Act and Endangered Species Act. The Sierra Club and National Wildlife Federation oppose the measure, citing concerns about the judicial-review limits and changes to the National Environmental Policy Act and other laws. NEPA requires agencies to consider a project’s possible impacts and allows the public to be heard before approval. The current review process often takes five years or more, and shortening that time frame is a key goal.

Many environmental organizations have denounced the measure, saying it would lock in fossil-fuel development, weaken environmental safeguards and make it harder for communities to challenge energy-project approvals. Jen Duggan, executive director of the Environmental Integrity Project, said, “Make no mistake: This backroom deal will pave the way for even more fossil fuel projects like gas power plants for data centers, oil and gas pipelines and (liquefied natural gas) terminals while sacrificing fundamental and common-sense public health and environmental protections for communities across the U.S.” Duggan and other critics said the bill’s benefits for renewable energy would be outweighed by likely increases in greenhouse gas emissions and air and water pollution from the fossil-fuel industry. She called the measure “a gift to some of the biggest polluters in America” and said it was being rushed through Congress without public hearings or sufficient debate.

Merkley and Wyden were among several Democrats in both chambers who say the party should use its stronger position in the next Congress to pass legislation boosting clean energy, such as solar and wind power, while limiting expansion of oil, natural gas and other fossil fuels. Merkley said, “Getting more renewable electrons where they are needed doesn’t tackle climate change if you simultaneously promote the burning of more fossil fuels. Unfortunately, this bill does exactly that.” The four champions lead the chamber’s energy and environment panels and negotiated the deal a few weeks before the election. They said Americans are tired of paying more in utility bills while projects that could lower costs sit stuck in the planning phase for years. Sheldon Whitehouse, a co-sponsor of the permitting bill, said the measure is “the floor, not the ceiling, of our climate and environmental aspirations,” and said he hopes “to accomplish big things with our big Democratic majorities” over the next two years. His support for the measure drew criticism from longtime allies.

Cantwell said the bill had been rushed and its impacts were not fully considered. She met Tuesday with tribal, outdoor recreation and conservation leaders and said the legislation would undermine tribal nations and weaken protections for the nation’s bodies of water, land and wildlife. Cantwell said, “Now is the time to slow this process down, to actually have a formal process” to debate and amend the bill. She warned, “Don’t sacrifice the Clean Water Act or hunting or fishing grounds, historic or sacred sites, or protections of tribal sovereignty. We all need new sources of energy and power. But this bill, as it’s currently drafted, is not the answer.”

Oneida Nation Vice Chairman Jameson Wilson said the bill threatens the tribe’s ability to protect its sacred spaces, ancestors, traditional ceremonies and culture. “Any effort to accelerate development must strengthen, not diminish and erode tribal sovereignty and meaningful consultation,” he said.

Former Energy Secretary Jennifer Granholm, who led the agency in the Biden administration, said she understands those concerns but believes recent changes by the bill’s sponsors strengthen protections for tribal consultation and restore important state authority over water quality. In a social media post, Granholm wrote, “A bigger Democratic majority can write a stronger bill,” and added, “But it would still need this president’s signature.” Lawmakers “have a bipartisan deal in front of us today that can get more clean power onto the grid and protect ratepayers,” she said, urging them to take the opportunity.

*Associated Press writer Jennifer McDermott contributed from Providence, Rhode Island.*
