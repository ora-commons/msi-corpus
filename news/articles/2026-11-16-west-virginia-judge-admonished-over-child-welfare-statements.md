## West Virginia judge admonished over child welfare statements

- The Supreme Court of Appeals of West Virginia admonished Ritchie County Judge Tim Sweeney on Thursday for making statements about pending matters, undermining public confidence in the judiciary and failing to fairly uphold the law.
- Sweeney drew scrutiny after he ordered leaders at the West Virginia Department of Human Services to his courtroom last February to address a shortage of child protective services workers, then contacted the media.
- The Judicial Investigation Commission found Sweeney violated ethics rules about speaking publicly about pending cases, warned him not to make similar comments in the future, and separately found his Facebook activity violated judicial conduct rules because his posts suggested he favored police and prosecutors over defendants.
- Chief Justice Haley Bunn wrote that an admonishment "adequately deters similar misconduct in the future and is a proportionate sanction for Judge Sweeney's conduct viewed in its totality," noting the judge had never been disciplined before.

## Court orders Sweeney to pay costs, citing no prior discipline

Sweeney, whose district includes Pleasants, Doddridge, Ritchie and Wirt counties, had ordered CPS workers in October 2025 to send a severely traumatized child to an out-of-state mental health treatment facility. When the child still had not been sent the following February, workers told him the child had fallen through the cracks because the office was understaffed. One of them told him they carried about 100 cases.

Sweeney ordered DoHS officials to report to his courthouse to start working as case workers. He later acknowledged that he wanted those leaders to solve the understaffing problem rather than actually take new positions.

"I think somebody needs to stand up for the public, particularly the most vulnerable members of our community — the children," Sweeney told the Parkersburg News and Sentinel. "From a legal point of view, abuse and neglect cases are of the highest priority, and they should be, in my opinion, for the department and the government in Charleston as well."

The conduct came before the Supreme Court last month after it drew the attention of the Judicial Investigation Commission. The commission warned Sweeney not to make similar comments about pending cases in the future, and separately found that his Facebook posts suggested he favored police and prosecutors over defendants. Sweeney appealed to the Judicial Hearing Board, which also found violations.

In Thursday's opinion, the justices found that Sweeney violated the code by making statements about pending matters, by undermining public confidence in the judiciary and by failing to fairly uphold the law. Bunn wrote that judges are not supposed to affect the outcomes of cases before them, and that Sweeney had created a case by ordering health officials to his court and then affected its outcome when Mayer met with him to discuss staffing.

"While Judge Sweeney's conduct happened to concern a matter that elicits universal agreement—the well-being of children—judges who stray so far outside their role such as to violate the Code are not immunized from discipline based on the popularity or success of their misconduct," the justices wrote.

The court noted that Sweeney has never been disciplined before and that his intentions were noble. "He was cooperative and forthright in this matter, and his misconduct was neither self-promoting nor dishonest," Bunn wrote. "And while his motivations do not justify his actions, we find that an admonishment adequately deters similar misconduct in the future and is a proportionate sanction for Judge Sweeney's conduct viewed in its totality." The court ordered Sweeney to pay for the court proceedings.

Public admonishments are a milder form of discipline for judicial misconduct compared with others such as suspension or forced retirement.

Sweeney said Friday that he respectfully disagreed with the opinion but intended to comply. "Sometimes you just have to take a punch for the kids," he said. "Abused and neglected children were helped and shortcomings were addressed. That was and continues to be my goal."

Shortly after Sweeney ordered DoHS officials to his courtroom in February, former DoHS Cabinet Secretary Alex Mayer met with him to develop a plan to resolve the staffing problem, and the hearing was canceled.

The Sweeney case was not the only one of its kind to reach the justices. The same month Sweeney ordered health officials to his courtroom, Kanawha County Judge Maryclaire Akers ordered former DoHS Cabinet Secretary Alex Mayer to her courtroom after hearing children had been placed in hotels and 4-H camps. Akers discussed the foster care system and the placements on MetroNews the following month. That case also went to the Supreme Court, but was dismissed over insufficient evidence.
