## Crash-test alternative targets drivers who do not buckle up

The National Highway Traffic Safety Administration said in July that it plans to seek public input on a proposal allowing automakers to install seat-belt interlocks as an alternative to conducting complex crash tests to evaluate safety features for unbelted drivers. The Wall Street Journal reported that roughly 10,000 people die in U.S. car crashes each year while not buckled up, accounting for about half of all vehicle-occupant fatalities.

According to federal data, the 90% U.S. seat-belt use rate is below Canada's rate and the 95% to 99% rates in most major European countries. The Wall Street Journal said the roughly 10,000 annual deaths among unbuckled occupants had not budged for more than a decade. “While seat-belt use is near its all-time high, we recognize that progress has flatlined for more than a decade,” an NHTSA spokesman said. The outlet said improved safety in new cars had not meaningfully reduced roadway fatalities largely tied to speeding, drunken driving and seat-belt misuse.

The auto industry has spent billions in recent decades on occupant protection, including air bags, cameras and emergency-braking systems. Yet those technologies fall short against the roughly 10% of people who still do not wear seat belts, according to The Wall Street Journal. Thomas Broberg, Volvo Cars' top safety engineer, said chimes, warning lights and other alerts have proven effective for most drivers. But nearly 70 years after Volvo pioneered three-point seat belts, getting the remaining holdouts to buckle up remains difficult, he said. “I’m not sure if technology is the solution, but you cannot give up on the 10%,” Broberg said. “So you need to grasp for anything.”

A new regulation set to take effect in 2028 requires the current 30-second seat-belt alerts to continue nonstop until a driver buckles up. Automakers have extended audible reminders, which now drone on longer than ever. Researchers said seat belts are the best way to protect drivers in a crash and are far better than air bags alone. Mandatory seat-belt laws and buckle alerts helped raise national use from below 20% in the 1980s to about 90% during the middle of the previous decade, according to national surveys. “It’s frustrating, as I know exactly what will happen to you if you don’t wear a seat belt and get into a crash,” said Cecilia Sunnevång, Autoliv's vice president of research. Autoliv is a global car supplier that makes seat belts.

Researchers studying nonusers found that some disable or ignore warning systems, drive drunk and forget to buckle up or forgo belts voluntarily, find belts uncomfortable, or do not know how to adjust belts that do not fit. Passengers, particularly those in the back seat, are less likely to buckle up. Researchers said public messaging should focus on groups more commonly associated with belt nonuse. NHTSA said it has been trying to target young men, partly through podcasts and social media.

In an August episode, sports podcaster and media executive Bill Simmons surprised a guest when he disclosed that he rarely buckles up while driving. “I’m not proud of it, I just don’t like seat belts,” Simmons said. “Listen, I know that I’ll get skewered. I’m a bad example. Sometimes I’ll put it on—if I’m going 95 on the highway, I might throw it on.” Simmons did not respond to requests for comment.

NHTSA also said stronger enforcement of seat-belt laws is needed. Thirty-five states authorize police to pull over a driver for not wearing a belt, while 14 allow a citation to be issued as a secondary reason during a traffic stop. “We cannot save lives without enforcing laws around impaired driving, distraction, speeding and seat-belt use,” NHTSA Administrator Jonathan Morrison said at an August law-enforcement conference.

Spurred by the public furor over Ralph Nader's book “Unsafe at Any Speed,” Congress required seat belts in cars starting in 1968 and created the agency that eventually became NHTSA. One of the agency's first initiatives was to increase seat-belt use. In 1972, NHTSA required automakers to install interlocks that disabled the ignition unless all front-seat occupants were buckled up.

Ford Motor safety engineer Priya Prasad was among the interlock rule's earliest detractors. He said the system made his Mustang convertible difficult to start and that unreliable sensors could misread what was happening inside the car. Prasad, a regular seat-belt user, pulled out a component and disabled the system. “This is a pain in the ass,” Prasad recalled thinking at the time. The devices were primitive and finicky, drivers revolted, and Congress later prohibited NHTSA from permitting interlocks as a way for automakers to comply with safety regulations, effectively sidelining the technology for decades.

Instead, Congress mandated short warning buzzers in 1974. Seat-belt use remained depressed, at just above 12% of vehicle occupants by the early 1980s. Under President Ronald Reagan, NHTSA proposed requiring automatic seat belts or air bags by the end of the decade, but the proposal would have dropped the air-bag requirement if states representing two-thirds of the U.S. population adopted mandatory seat-belt laws. Auto executives were reluctant to bear the cost of adding air bags and mounted a major lobbying effort.

In May 1982, Chrysler chief executive Lee Iacocca told lawmakers that Americans rejected seat-belt laws for ideological reasons. “They believe such a law is an intrusion on their freedom,” Iacocca said. “And they are willing to let hundreds die, and thousands be injured in the name of that ideology.” Over the next two decades, every state except New Hampshire passed laws allowing police to ticket drivers for not using seat belts, and usage climbed to around 70% by the turn of the century.

Prasad later led development of Ford's Beltminder system in 2000, a first-of-its-kind feature that added a flashing dashboard warning light and intermittent alerts to the usual alarm. Ford patented Beltminder but allowed any automaker to use it. Before the system was introduced, Prasad met with his boss, who showed him notes from suppliers and dealers warning that Ford would lose market share. “We were really expecting a backlash,” Prasad said. Ford went ahead anyway. Shortly after Beltminder was introduced, Prasad said, Ford executives were surprised to find some owners posting on the internet about how to disable the technology. A company study found that Beltminder increased seat-belt use by 5 percentage points.

In 2013, Germany's BMW told NHTSA that interlock technology had evolved and was likely to be accepted by drivers. It could lock out a driver or limit a vehicle to low speeds when the driver was unbuckled. BMW asked NHTSA to allow the systems as an alternative to testing the effect of crashes on unbelted occupants for cars in development. NHTSA denied the request, saying it needed to “fully evaluate” the effectiveness and acceptability of modern seat-belt interlock systems.

General Motors tested a variant in the mid-2010s and found increased use among part-time belt users, though it did not roll the system out widely. Jeff Ronne, General Motors' director of global vehicle safety technology, strategy and regulations, said: “In theory we could put everybody in a race-car harness and a suit and a helmet and say, ‘That’s the safest way you could drive a car,’” adding, “But who’s going to want to do that, right?”

This summer, NHTSA seemed more open to the concept. The Alliance for Automotive Innovation, which represents most major automakers, pushed the proposal with help from Prasad, now 83. The group said further increases in seat-belt use would be difficult but estimated that each 1% gain would save hundreds of lives a year. Parts suppliers are working on potential systems if such a rule were to go into effect. Driver-monitoring cameras already used to detect inattention could determine whether belts are properly buckled, making an interlock harder to disable, executives said. ZF Lifetec, a division of the global German supplier, began developing infrared seat-belt webbing in 2019 to help automakers meet new European safety requirements. Scott Franz, a senior technical specialist for seat belts at ZF Lifetec, said the webbing is visible to monitoring systems, allowing automakers to reliably determine whether a seat belt is properly buckled. “We’re targeting that last 10% in the U.S. market,” Franz said, referring to unbuckled drivers and passengers.

Any interlock system would reach only new cars, while research shows older vehicles are associated with a higher frequency of unbelted occupant fatalities. The Wall Street Journal characterized law-enforcement officials such as Colorado State Patrol Chief Matthew Packard as likely to keep wondering which deaths might have been prevented if drivers and passengers had buckled up. Packard recalled one crash from about two decades ago. A young man who was not wearing a seat belt lost control while street-racing his cousin down a highway. His vehicle rolled into the next lane, he was ejected, and his cousin struck him. “I cannot say for certain he would have survived the crash had he been belted,” Packard said. If the young man had buckled up, Packard said, “his cousin wouldn’t have had any wonder about what happened.” NHTSA's stated next step is to seek public input on whether automakers should be allowed to offer interlocks instead of conducting complex crash tests to evaluate safety features for unbelted drivers.
