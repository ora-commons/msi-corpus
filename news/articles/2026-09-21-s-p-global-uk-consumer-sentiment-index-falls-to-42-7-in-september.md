## Chancellor's October budget expected to include tax increases

UK consumer confidence fell to a three-year low in September, driven by fears of an interest rate increase by the Bank of England and a deteriorating jobs market, according to a survey published on Monday.

The S&P Global UK consumer sentiment index dropped to 42.7 in September from 42.9 in August, indicating "a notable strain on financial confidence across UK households." More than half of the 1,500 respondents said they expected the cost of borrowing to rise over the next year, S&P Global said.

The findings land ahead of Chancellor John Healey's first budget next month, which analysts expect to include tax increases to offset rising government borrowing costs linked to the conflict in the Middle East. Healey could limit his tax rises to wealthier groups, including a rise in capital gains tax, but analysts say he is unlikely to raise sufficient funds from narrower tax rises alone to cover higher defence spending and the rise in debt costs while preserving a reserve buffer, often referred to as headroom, of at least £20bn.

Mortgage costs have already climbed to multi-year highs. The average two-year fixed residential mortgage rate rose to 5.88% on Monday, its highest since 16 April and up from 5.84% on Friday, according to data from Moneyfacts. The average five-year fixed rate stood at 5.92%, its highest since October 2023, up from 5.88%.

The rise in mortgage costs has added about £150 to monthly mortgage payments on a typical loan of £250,000 over 25 years since the start of March 2026. Official figures show the average monthly direct debit covering mortgage costs has risen from about £600 to £900 over the past four years.

Consumer confidence in the jobs market fell to its lowest level in three-and-a-half years. The threat of artificial intelligence and caution among employers about hiring new staff drove the drop, alongside a steady decline in the number of payrolled employees over the past six months and a fall in vacancies to a five-year low, which showed employers are worried about their profitability over the coming year.

Maryam Baluch, an economist at S&P Global Market Intelligence, said a downbeat mood was spreading across UK households "as improved sentiment surrounding the new government is eroded by renewed worries over energy prices, the cost of living and job prospects."

"Against a backdrop of rising volatility in energy markets linked to tensions in the Middle East, households increasingly reported difficulties accessing credit, suggesting that expectations of tighter monetary conditions are beginning to feed through to borrowing conditions," Baluch said. "Together, these factors point to cautiousness about both financial prospects and pose some downside risks to the broader economic outlook."

Analysts believe the jobs market has proved resilient since the US attack on Iran and a steep rise in the cost of fuel and energy, but the steady decline in payrolled employees, the drop in vacancies and the AI-driven uncertainty have shown employers are worried about their profitability over the next year.

The British Chamber of Commerce has called on the government to give businesses extra support after a series of tax rises over the past two years, including an increase in employers' national insurance payments. A BCC survey of 5,000 companies in the second quarter of 2026 found that only 17% were planning to increase investment in the coming months, a post-pandemic low.

In response to the growing clamour for tax cuts to support businesses and households hit by the cost of living crisis, Liberal Democrat leader Ed Davey said at his party conference that the UK should cut fuel duty to offset some of the rise in petrol and diesel prices at the pumps. The German government on Monday announced plans for a cut in tax on fuel of about 15p to take effect on 1 October.

Last week, Andy Burnham said he will need to take "difficult decisions" on the economy to ensure that it "remains on track."
