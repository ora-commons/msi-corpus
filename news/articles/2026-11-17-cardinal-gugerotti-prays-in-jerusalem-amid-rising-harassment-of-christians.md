## Six-day visit includes Bethlehem stops and meetings with church leaders

On Tuesday in Jerusalem, Cardinal Claudio Gugerotti, prefect of the Vatican's Dicastery for the Eastern Churches, visited the Holy Land to convey the presence of Pope Leo XIV during particularly troubled times for Christians in the region. Gugerotti prayed before the Holy Edicule inside the Church of the Holy Sepulcher, which marks the site where Christians believe Jesus was buried and rose from the dead.

Gugerotti and other Christian leaders processed through the narrow stone alleyways of Jerusalem's Old City toward the centuries-old church. Rows of Franciscan friars and a large cross preceded the group, while bagpipers led the procession. Inside, clergy in solemn robes participated in an incense-filled ceremony attended by leaders of other Christian denominations.

The basilica's unusual arrangement under which denominations have sometimes shared worship space dates to the 19th century, and the ceremony offered a show of unity. Leaders of other denominations listened intently as Gugerotti prayed.

“Lord, you were here physically,” Gugerotti prayed. “Lord, give hope to the world.”

The visit came as harassment of clergy and faithful in Jerusalem was rising. A rare violent attack on a religious sister in April sent shock waves across Israel.

“We know we are not always welcome,” said Sister Marie Reine, a Catholic nun who joined the procession in her white habit. “But our presence can bring peace, too.”

Most of Israel's Christians are Palestinians, who have been the target of heated rhetoric after Arab-led parties made historic gains in the national elections last month. Neighboring Jordan is also reeling from an attack that killed nine worshippers during a service at a Greek Orthodox church.

“We pray that these days may confirm in faith and in hope our priests and religious, who serve with great fidelity in often demanding conditions; our families, who choose, day after day, to remain; and our young people, who need to know that the Church stands beside them as they seek to build a future here,” said Cardinal Pierbattista Pizzaballa, the Latin Patriarch of Jerusalem, in a statement before the visit.

A Catholic father who participated in Tuesday's procession said his children want the family to leave Jerusalem, where many Christians say tensions have grown since the war triggered by the Hamas-led attacks on Oct. 7, 2023.

“To be here is a vocation,” Andre Haddad said. “We can’t just flee our reality.”

Church and lay leaders say Christians' emigration from across the Holy Land is an intensifying concern. Many Christians in Israel feel especially vulnerable to organized crime because they are a tiny minority and believe the state provides them little protection “because I’m another Arab,” said Wadie Abunassar, a Catholic community leader from the northern Israeli city of Haifa.

Gugerotti's six-day visit was also scheduled to take him to Bethlehem, where he was to visit the Basilica of the Nativity, where Christians believe Jesus was born, and a mosque. Bethlehem is in the occupied West Bank, where Palestinian Muslims and members of the dwindling Christian minority have denounced growing violence by Israeli settlers.

He was also planning to meet the leaders of the Greek Orthodox and Armenian churches in Jerusalem, several of whom attended Tuesday's prayers. During his first papal trip to the Middle East last fall, Leo prayed that Christians might again be united after more than 1,000 years of division between Eastern and Western churches.

Gugerotti's public schedule did not include meetings with Israeli leaders. Leo has reached out to Jewish faith leaders since the inauguration of his pontificate last year, raising hopes for a new start after a strained relationship under his predecessor. He has spoken against rising antisemitism while pleading for an end to war and displacement in Gaza.
