## Women cast 7% of votes in Wilson's church polity

Conservative Pastor Doug Wilson told the BBC from Moscow, Idaho, that he wants the United States to repeal the 19th Amendment, which granted women the right to vote. He said repeal was "low" on his priority list but that he and his congregation believed in it. Wilson also favors "household voting," a system in which the head of the household — usually a man — votes for the family.

The connection between Wilson and the Trump administration runs through Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth, who has called Wilson his mentor. Hegseth worships at the Washington, DC branch of Wilson's church network and, according to Wilson, other administration members worship there too. A Pentagon spokesperson told the BBC that Hegseth is a "proud member" of a CREC-affiliated church and that the "secretary very much appreciates many of Mr. Wilson's writings and teachings."

Wilson said he and Hegseth have a "warm relationship," but he described it as "largely a pastoral one, not a political one." On stage with Wilson, Hegseth said, "thank you for your leadership, for your mentorship, for the things you've started, the truth you've told, your willingness to be bold." Wilson has led prayer services at the Pentagon, including one in mid-November.

Wilson co-founded the Communion of Reformed Evangelical Churches, or CREC, which has around 150 churches in the United States, compared with roughly 350,000 other Christian congregations in the country. Wilson said membership continues to rise. The BBC described his position as a fringe perspective among U.S. Christians while reporting that some of his views were gaining support. It said the vast majority of Christians support women's voting rights and dismiss his message as misogynistic and extreme.

Wilson said a society led by men is "a very great blessing" and that "Patriarchy is in accordance with the way God made the world." The self-described Christian nationalist said the United States should be a theocracy in which gay sex is criminalized, abortion is outlawed and people of other faiths cannot hold public office. In a sermon titled "The Lost Virtue of Sexism," he argued that the Bible calls for women to be excluded from leadership of the church and certain roles in civic affairs.

At the center of Wilson's political case for repealing the amendment is the household system used in elections within his church. The head of the household — usually a man — votes on behalf of the family. Wilson said women in his own family do vote in civic elections to counter votes of liberals. He said women should assume the role of head of household only in what he called "odd" circumstances, such as divorce, bereavement or "where the husband was a quadriplegic."

Just 7% of votes in Wilson's church polity are cast by women. When challenged by the BBC, Wilson admitted that the system gave men "outsized influence."

Under the process described by the BBC, repeal would require a proposal from two-thirds of both the House and Senate or two-thirds of the state legislatures. Three-quarters of the states would then need to ratify the amendment. The BBC reported that repeal was extremely unlikely, and Wilson said reversing it could take a century or more.

Julie Ingersoll, a professor of religious studies at the University of North Florida, said Wilson's beliefs are misogynistic and extreme and reflect a worldview that calls for complete submission to men. She said the threat to women's rights should be taken seriously and compared the campaign with the battle against Roe versus Wade. The BBC reported that the Supreme Court decision granting abortion rights was overturned in 2022 after years of legal battles. Ingersoll said people once assumed abortion rights would not be undone but "they chipped away and they kept chipping away until they were able to overturn it," adding, "And I see this as directly parallel."

At Washington National Cathedral, Vicar Canon Dana Colley Corsello rejected Wilson's understanding of the Bible. "In my mind, there is no biblical precedent or standing for what [Wilson has] proposed," she told the BBC. She added: "I'm married, I have a husband, I have children. My husband and I are complete equals in everything we do."

Corsello also interpreted the midterm election results as a repudiation of efforts to restrict women's rights. "Cultural issues did not translate in this election," she said, adding: "When people are preaching love, empathy, inclusion... that actually resonated with people."

Other conservative pastors are pressing for women's voting rights to be removed more immediately. Last February, Pastor Dale Partridge posted on Instagram, "We will repeal the 19th Amendment within ten years." Partridge, who has around 900k followers across social media, credited Wilson as someone who "broke the ice" for "strong politics and theology." Partridge has said that "the majority of women are not capable of responsible voting" because too many of them are liberal.

Joel Webbon, who frequently collaborates with Partridge on podcasts and whose New Christian Right YouTube channel has more than 190k subscribers, has said women's "desires are wicked" and advocated taking away women's votes. Webbon was once friendly with Wilson, although they have since fallen out. Wilson said, "Those guys have overshot by a long way," calling the two younger pastors "disruptors and basically rage baiting."

Kelly Marino, an associate professor at Sacred Heart University who studies women's suffrage, argued that the last decade brought major gains for the women's-rights movement and liberal political ideology. She said, "any time where there was sort of this peak of feminism, there've been people who respond aggressively and try to push back."

Marino also said post-Covid uncertainty and conflict have contributed to a craving for tradition and security. She said that helps explain the popularity of notions such as "tradwife" or "soft girl," which reject career goals in favor of traditionally female roles of wife and mother. Marino does not think the Constitution could be changed in the immediate future, but she said the debate over repeal is shaping how people think about women's roles.

Wilson's views have also been gaining ground beyond the United States. CREC has more than 30 churches internationally, including in Poland, Japan and Brazil, where right-wing influencers have pressed for the removal of women's suffrage, sometimes using religious language. Wilson recently accepted a church in England into his network. He said CREC congregations have distinct local characteristics but share the same core beliefs. His stated goal was for outsiders to admire the system practiced by his congregations.
