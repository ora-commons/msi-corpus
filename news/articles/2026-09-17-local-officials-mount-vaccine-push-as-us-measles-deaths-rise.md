## Local officials mount vaccine push as US measles deaths rise

## Thirty states now source vaccine guidance outside the CDC

On an early September morning, dozens of families stood in line outside a clinic in Yeadon, Pennsylvania. It was "exclusion day," the deadline by which students without complete immunizations are barred from returning to class. Instead of turning families away, school district leaders partnered with the county health department to vaccinate children nearby so they could go back to their desks. That day, 42 middle and high school students received vaccines to protect against tetanus, pertussis and meningitis, among other diseases.

"We've been doing this for a couple of years now, and it's really effective," said Lora Werner, director of the Delaware County health department.

The vaccination day is part of the county's back-to-school immunization campaign, which includes a mobile vaccine clinic — a 33-foot traveling RV that brings vaccinations to pop-up community events — school district partnerships and vaccination data transparency, all aimed at keeping immunization rates from sliding. The push is a local answer to a national problem: Pennsylvania had logged 693 measles cases as of September 14, and four residents have died of the disease this year.

With federal guidance no longer reliable, the responsibility for reassuring parents and caretakers has shifted downward to governors and state legislatures, state health departments, healthcare advocacy groups and the doctors on the frontlines. Across the country, they are mounting an urgent campaign to defend childhood vaccines against what advocates describe as a wave of misinformation. Robert F. Kennedy Jr., the country's top public health official, has spent his time in office questioning the efficacy of vaccines and undermining vaccine science, advocates said. The administration's claims about vaccine safety are not just false, but are actively costing lives, advocates said.

"Our responsibility is to demonstrate what the science shows and put out a [vaccine] schedule that people can depend upon that is science-based and evidence-based," said Andrew Racine, president of the American Academy of Pediatrics. "Measles is a canary in the coal mine and we have to now worry about other vaccine-preventable diseases, which is why the local effort at the state level is so critical."

The pattern has culminated in a recent Trump executive order that seeks to break apart the combined measles, mumps and rubella (MMR) shot and shrink the list of vaccines recommended for American children. As children got up to date on their immunizations before the start of the school year, Trump signed the "gold standard childhood vaccine recommendations" executive order, which called for reducing the number of universally recommended childhood vaccines from 17 to 11 and splitting the MMR shot into three separate injections delivered across additional doctor's visits. The order arrived with no new scientific evidence behind it.

Paul Offit, director of the Vaccine Education Center at Children's Hospital of Philadelphia, trains doctors to share with their patients that the MMR vaccine has been used safely in the United States for about 60 years. Manufacturers have made clear they have no plans to split it into separate formulations, since doing so would offer no safety benefit, Offit said. Experts have also noted that more doctor's visits would lead to more opportunities for missed appointments.

For many of the doctors and officials fighting the disinformation, the executive order is the latest development in a pattern set by Kennedy since his confirmation. Offit recalled Kennedy appearing on Fox News last year and saying the measles vaccine causes blindness and death, claims Offit called false. "Could he be more dangerously irresponsible?" Offit said. "He should have been fired right there, but he wasn't. And that's when he knew: I can say whatever I want with impunity."

Vaccine misinformation has led to vaccine hesitancy, Racine said. CDC data shows that the number of kindergarteners claiming vaccine exemptions for the 2025-26 school year hit a record high, while vaccination rates for kindergarteners decreased from the year before. Hesitancy is higher in certain rural areas, Racine said, and states that once had the highest vaccination rates in the country have fallen as they expanded vaccine exemptions.

"This is the last thing parents wanted on their back-to-school list, to be confused about the childhood vaccine schedule or to be worried about their child coming home with measles," said Shaughnessy Naughton, the founder of the non-profit advocacy group 3.14 Action. "It's why so many doctors and health professionals are now stepping up and fighting back against these dangerous actors."

With the federal government having abandoned its traditional role as a trusted, science-based source of vaccine guidance, that responsibility has fallen to state lawmakers and health departments, local public health officials and doctors. Following Kennedy's appointment as health secretary, some state legislators examined their statutes on vaccines and removed references to the CDC. In January, dozens of states announced that they would reject the CDC's guidance. Now, 30 states report relying on a source other than the CDC, such as the American Academy of Pediatrics and the American Academy of Family Physicians, for information about childhood vaccines.

Fifteen states sued the Trump administration earlier this year over its changes to the childhood vaccine schedule and the dismantling of the CDC's vaccine advisory panel. They have also banded together. California, Oregon, Washington and Hawaii formed the West Coast Health Alliance, and 15 states — including Connecticut, Maine, New York and Massachusetts — formed the Northeast Public Health Collaborative, two voluntary coalitions to strengthen regional awareness and preparedness on infectious diseases.

Racine said clear and consistent communication and maintaining vaccine access are key to building trust with the public. That's why states are reassuring practitioners of the standard vaccine schedule, regulating insurance to make sure companies continue to cover vaccines and launching public service announcements to communicate the importance of getting measles vaccines. The Pennsylvania health department says it is ensuring residents understand the risks of remaining unvaccinated by sharing videos and infographics that explain how contagious the disease is. The response has been overwhelmingly positive, a spokesperson for the health department said, with more people choosing to vaccinate themselves and their families far more often than not. In August, healthcare providers in the state administered more than 46,000 MMR doses, compared with roughly 31,000 in the same month the year before. Pennsylvania also began publishing school-level vaccination data for the first time this year, letting parents and administrators see exactly where local immunization rates stand and where support is needed.

Hawaii Governor Josh Green, a physician who formed the West Coast Health Alliance, said he treats vaccine communication "like a family physician," sharing granular local data with the public rather than issuing vaccine mandates. "I believe that people can and should make some decisions based on their belief system," Green said. "Of course, however, they should also be given the best information possible."

Green's urgency is shaped by his experience as a volunteer doctor during Samoa's catastrophic 2019 measles outbreak, which killed 83 people, most of them young children, after a vaccination scare fueled partly by anti-vaccine activists' visits to the islands. Kennedy, as part of his career as an anti-vaccine activist, visited Samoa in 2019 to study the MMR vaccine months before the outbreak.

"We would go into a village, and we'd find families huddled around a child who had just died," Green recalled. "We would have a funeral service in the bedroom, and then we would have to walk outside and ask: 'Does anyone want to be vaccinated?'" Green traveled from village to village vaccinating family members. "We saw the same thing over and over again, people dying right in front of us," he said. Samoa vaccinated nearly 37,000 people in two days, and the outbreak stopped within a week, Green said. He called the parallels to Kennedy's current rhetoric "the same kind of stuff" he saw before that crisis. "It's RFK's playbook," Green said. "He went into a vulnerable community of people that didn't have a lot of education [...] and he scared people, and they didn't get vaccinated, and the country got crushed. Now they're doing the same thing with other rural communities."

Countering federal misinformation has fallen disproportionately on doctors and nurses who never expected to be doing that kind of work, health officials said. "Doctors are facing a lot — many of them have never seen measles cases before, and now they're seeing it for the first time in their lives," said Jeremy Funk, deputy director of the public health project at the non-profit Protect Our Care. He described an "enormous amount of burden" now being placed on pediatricians and state health officials to walk parents through basic vaccine safety information they never previously had to explain.

In Pennsylvania, that responsibility has landed heavily on county health departments — some of which face funding challenges or didn't exist a few years ago. Delaware County was the largest county in the country without a health department until the pandemic, when the community built its own from scratch. "We wanted to make sure we were meeting people where they were," said city council member Monica Taylor. Today, that means school partnerships and a mobile health van bring shots directly to parking lots, parks and community events. "It's really about being out in the community as much as possible and being accessible," Taylor said.

Werner said Delaware County has not seen a surge in outright vaccine refusal, but has seen rising anxiety, including parents asking whether funding for free vaccine programs will survive, or parents confused by shifting federal signals. "We saw more parents concerned that things might not be available," Werner said, describing a summer awareness campaign the county launched, aimed particularly at reaching Spanish-speaking families. She credited the state health department with holding regular briefings for county officials and continuing to follow "the science," even as federal recommendations shifted. "It's a really hard place for public health right now with so much divisiveness being promoted," Werner said. "As public health officials, we really want people, from those who are underinsured to those who are undocumented, to keep themselves safe and have good information to make decisions about it. We want to help people hear the information they need to hear."
