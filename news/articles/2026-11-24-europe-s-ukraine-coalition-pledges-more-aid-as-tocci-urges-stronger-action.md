## Tocci finds Shenzhen summit rich in symbols, short on substance

In an opinion column published by The Guardian, Nathalie Tocci, a professor of practice at the Johns Hopkins School of Advanced International Studies, Europe, and a senior fellow at Bocconi University's Institute for European Policymaking, contrasted two events held at the same time: the trilateral summit in Shenzhen and a coalition meeting in Berlin.

She called the Berlin gathering a more concrete display of strength and said it showed that Europe, rather than the three leaders at Shenzhen, held much of the leverage over its own security. Tocci particularly advised people in eastern Europe who feared another historic betrayal to remember that Europe held most of the cards. She framed the larger danger as Europe's "learned helplessness" toward the United States, a political and psychological problem she said amplified fears of another Yalta-style betrayal.

The Shenzhen summit brought together Xi Jinping, Donald Trump and Vladimir Putin for what Tocci called the first ever trilateral summit of the three leaders. Her assessment was that it was "rich in symbolism and empty of substance," not "Yalta 2.0." She noted that many Europeans, anxious about the Chinese, Russian and US presidents potentially colluding, saw chilling echoes of the 1945 Yalta conference, when Joseph Stalin, Winston Churchill and Franklin D Roosevelt redrew the map of Europe. Tocci said the Shenzhen meeting brought together three autocratic leaders who would like to see the world carved up into spheres of influence. She wrote: "Nothing of the kind was ever going to happen in Shenzhen, nor will it at any future gathering of Xi, Trump and Putin alone." She said Putin and Trump cannot win the wars they have launched against Ukraine and Iran respectively, while Xi, whom she described as by far the strongest of the trio, was doing little more than bask in what she called the colossal mistakes of his American and Russian friends.

Tocci also said the Shenzhen agreement to oppose the alleged overreach of international tribunals — established to disprove that might makes right — reflected a preference for spheres of influence rather than an ability to dictate Europe's future. She noted that Trump, Putin and Xi may like a world in which, borrowing from Thucydides, the strong do what they can and the weak suffer what they must. Tocci wrote that the three men were not as strong as they wanted to believe and that today's world was not the Hobbesian wasteland over which Roosevelt, Churchill and Stalin had the power to rule unchallenged.

Tocci described the original Yalta as taking place as the second world war drew to a close. International law and multilateral organisations were little more than a pipedream then, when the world was still living in an age of empire, with many colonies in the hands of European countries and the Soviet Union eager to consolidate its own in eastern Europe. Tocci wrote that it was hardly surprising the war's victors — the US, UK and USSR — held the keys to Europe's future. She contrasted that era with the present, saying international law and multilateralism are under attack but not dead and that middle powers in the global south are rising and making their voices heard.

The leaders nevertheless remained consequential, Tocci said. Russia and the United States hold the world's largest nuclear arsenals, while China, in its scramble for superpower status, is racing to expand its nuclear arsenal and catch up with the US and Russia. Had Trump, Putin and Xi committed to nuclear arms control, as Trump wanted, Europe and the rest of the world would have applauded, but Tocci said the Russian and Chinese leaders were never going to indulge Trump on that score. She wrote that possessing nuclear weapons is the only reason Putin can still claim a seat at the top table, and that without them Russia would be little more than a second-rate power.

She also noted that Russia's military assault on Ukraine was continuing as the country faced its hardest winter since Russia invaded. Russia's cyber-attacks, drone incursions, sabotage, attacks on critical infrastructure and disinformation campaigns across Europe had escalated, according to Tocci, to the point where it makes little sense to keep talking about "hybrid war", or even "shadow war". She said it would perhaps be more honest simply to call it what it is: Russia's war against Europe. A photograph accompanying the column showed a bombed residential building in Dymer, in the Kyiv region, following an overnight Russian air strike on 23 November 2026.

Russia, Tocci said, was hardly fighting that war alone. China is not a direct participant, she wrote, but has provided economic and technological support that was vital to Russia's war machine. She said there was no sign that Ukraine, or indeed any other European country, would fold, even though Trump's US had all but switched sides.

Washington, Tocci said, no longer provides economic or military support to Ukraine. The United States continues to cooperate on intelligence and satellite communications, but she said it had sickeningly tried to pressure Kyiv into capitulating by temporarily cutting off or slowing down those services. Tocci acknowledged that the United States still had the capacity to inflict real damage on Ukraine and Europe.

Trump had failed to subjugate Ukraine over the last two years, Tocci wrote, and, given his Republican Party's bruising defeat in the US midterm elections, he was unlikely to succeed in the next two years. Her broader point was that the three leaders remained relevant to European security even if Europe did not need their approval to act.

Another photograph accompanying the column showed French armed forces minister Catherine Vautrin, Poland's deputy prime minister Wladyslaw Kosiniak-Kamysz and UK defence secretary Wes Streeting during military exercises in Poland on 20 November 2026. The Berlin meeting supplied Tocci with a more concrete answer. Representatives from 36 countries in the French- and British-led coalition of the willing gathered to shore up support for Ukraine. The group agreed to close Ukraine's funding gap for 2027, with the European Union pledging an additional €45bn and non-EU coalition members such as the United Kingdom, Norway and Canada providing a further €25bn.

Tocci said the new European Union loan to Ukraine, guaranteed by the EU budget, would not permit the opt-outs allowed under the previous agreement, when Hungary and Slovakia could sit out, and that the European Union was in no mood to tolerate dropouts. With negotiations over the next seven-year EU budget entering a critical phase, Germany, the bloc's largest net contributor, would oppose any budget deal unless all EU members agreed to support Ukraine.

For Tocci, that condition exposed the coalition's political test: European governments had to turn financial commitments into common policy. Her diagnosis was that Europe's main problem was not material but political and, above all, psychological. Learned helplessness, toward the United States in particular, she wrote, fueled fears of the ghosts of Yalta, and a new betrayal.

Because Europe continued to take the Trump-Putin peace-talks charade too seriously, Tocci argued, it spent too much time squabbling over who should represent the continent at that table rather than delivering on the coalition's plans and pledges.

Her conclusion was that no peace plan pushed through by Putin and Trump would deliver Ukraine's surrender, let alone a new security architecture for Europe. On their own, she said, the two autocrats lack the power to impose their will. Tocci argued that Europe would become more secure and peaceful by spending more energy on delivering on the plans and pledges of the coalition of the willing and less on chasing the two failing leaders in Moscow and Washington.
