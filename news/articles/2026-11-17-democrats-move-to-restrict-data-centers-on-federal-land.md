## Interior Department strategy includes sales, exchanges and land transfers

A dozen Democratic members of Congress are calling for limits or bans on data-center construction on public lands, with legislation filed in the House and oversight planned in the Senate. The moves follow ProPublica reporting that Interior Secretary Doug Burgum, who leads the White House’s artificial intelligence push, has tens of millions of dollars invested in AI companies.

Sen. Catherine Cortez Masto of Nevada, who serves on the Energy and Natural Resources Committee, said in a social media post pointing to ProPublica’s reporting: “Our public lands aren’t a piggy bank for Trump’s powerful friends.” Her office said she is working on legislation to regulate data centers on public lands and will use the panel’s oversight authority to address transparency concerns.

At a recent event hosted by the Daily Caller, Burgum said his financial reporting and ethics were “clean as a whistle.” He said he invested in software companies before they turned to AI and added, “I don’t even know all the names of the companies that are in the portfolio.”

ProPublica found that hundreds of President Donald Trump’s political appointees in the executive branch, or their spouses, held stocks, owned equity or had other financial ties to AI companies and data-center developers. The Environmental Protection Agency and U.S. Department of Agriculture defended their political leadership, saying the appointees followed lawyers’ guidance and had not violated ethics rules.

ProPublica also reported that a U.S. Geological Survey study released earlier this year identified an area of Interior Department land nearly the size of Montana that might be suitable for data centers.

Several members of Congress pledged to introduce legislation. Rep. Maxine Dexter of Oregon said, “These lands are treasured and must be protected for future generations to benefit from and enjoy.” In mid-November, she filed the Protecting Our Public Lands from Data Centers Act, which would ban data-center construction on land managed by the Interior Department and Forest Service.

Rep. Rashida Tlaib of Michigan filed the No AI Data Centers on Federal Lands Act in July, which would also prohibit data-center construction on federally managed land. The measure was dead on arrival in the Republican-controlled House of Representatives. Tlaib said: “I’m going to continue building the coalition for our bill, and at the same time, I’m going to push the Oversight Committee to investigate the outrageous corruption of the Trump Administration.” The White House did not respond to a request for comment on the pushback from Democrats in Congress.

Rep. Jared Huffman of California and Sen. Martin Heinrich of New Mexico, the ranking members of the natural-resources committees in their respective chambers, are in line to take over the committees’ gavels in January when Democrats retake the majority. Heinrich said, “These lands belong to the American people, not private interests looking to cash in. Public lands belong in public hands.”

Heinrich is pushing the Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act, described as a generational overhaul of federal permitting for major developments. Republicans in Congress and the White House support swift passage, as do some Democrats. Members of the Democratic caucus focused on environmental issues are asking Heinrich to slow down and seek a better deal after Congress reconvenes with Democratic majorities.

Environmental policy experts said permitting reform is needed to address climate change by accelerating renewable electricity generation and transmission lines. The Sierra Club, Natural Resources Defense Council and League of Conservation Voters oppose the legislation because it would likely lead to more fossil-fuel projects while limiting input from Native American tribes.

The legislation presents a mixed bag for data centers. A spokesperson for Heinrich said it would require data-center companies to bear the costs of connecting to and upgrading the electrical grid. It would also streamline approval of natural-gas-fired power plants, which power many data centers, and make permits harder to revoke once issued.

Meanwhile, the Interior Department released a five-year strategic plan last week that includes a proposal to sell public lands or pursue “strategic rights-of-way agreements, land exchanges and direct transfer of lands.” It was unclear whether the department intended to offer land to companies or only to local, state and tribal governments.

Researchers who study natural-resources policy said the country’s foundational land-management law lays out steps that must be followed to dispose of federal land and that those steps would be difficult for the Trump administration to navigate legally. With data-center permits pending before the Interior Department, the strategic plan nonetheless sparked fear that developers might try to buy federal land. Jayson O’Neill, a spokesperson for Save Our Parks, said the plan was another attempt by Burgum to “sell off what’s left to AI data center developers and his extractive resource buddies.” The Interior Department did not respond to a request for comment on its land-sale proposal.
