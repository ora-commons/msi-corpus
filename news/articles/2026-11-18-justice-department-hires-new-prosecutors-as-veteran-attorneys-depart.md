## More than 50 hires had fewer than three years of legal experience

The U.S. Justice Department has begun recruiting recent law school graduates and other attorneys with less than three years of experience as experienced federal prosecutors departed, according to a ProPublica analysis published Nov. 18, 2026. ProPublica found that by the end of January 2026, the department had lost more than 1,300 assistant U.S. attorneys and replaced about 470; its court-data analysis found immigration charges rose almost 50% nationwide while drug prosecutions fell about 10% in the first 18 months of President Donald Trump's second term.

In an early October LinkedIn post, Preston Bigley, 29, wrote after passing the bar: “With this milestone complete, I will begin serving as an Assistant United States Attorney in the Criminal Division of the U.S. Attorney’s Office for the Western District of Texas.” Bigley had graduated from Texas A&M's law school in May and had been hired by the Justice Department in August as an “incoming” assistant U.S. attorney “pending bar admission,” according to his résumé. After becoming officially licensed to practice law, he updated his profile to show that he had become a federal criminal prosecutor.

Bigley is part of a growing group of attorneys ProPublica identified through bar-membership records and social media. The outlet found more than 50 new assistant U.S. attorneys with less than three years of experience who, according to legal experts, traditionally would not have been considered for such positions. Some were hired before passing the bar with the intention of becoming prosecutors afterward, public disclosures showed. About 20 of those identified passed the bar within the previous year, and at least seven were in the same May graduating class as Bigley, from a range of schools.

The total was likely higher because Justice Department staff identities are redacted in government personnel data. ProPublica examined federal court data and records, federal workforce and state bar information, and social-media accounts, and it interviewed more than two dozen former assistant U.S. attorneys. Some of the new hires welcomed the pathway. Jacquelyn Kilcommons, a May graduate of Pace University's law school, wrote, “I look forward to beginning my DOJ career by serving the Del Rio Division.” She added, “Upon receiving my bar exam results, I will transition into an Assistant United States Attorney (AUSA) position.” As he announced a new assistant U.S. attorney role in the District of Columbia, Ellis O'Donnell wrote, “In the past few months I have had the honor to graduate from Vanderbilt University Law School and to sit for and pass the Wisconsin Bar Exam.” None of the recent law school graduates responded to requests for comment.

“The fact that the DOJ is hiring, and indeed actively recruiting, right out of law school — it’s an astounding break with past practice,” said Juliet Sorensen, a Loyola University Chicago law professor and former assistant U.S. attorney. “It reflects a lowering of standards for a job that is important and requires lawyers who are meticulous, committed to doing the right thing, and bring with them experience beyond just law school.”

More than half of the assistant U.S. attorneys who departed during Trump's first 18 months in his second administration had at least nine years of legal experience. Those who left had worked for the government for an average of more than 13 years, ProPublica's analysis showed. ProPublica described the loss as amounting to thousands of years of experience. Legal experts and court dockets showed that experienced prosecutors were the ones most likely to handle complex matters such as money laundering, organized crime and drug trafficking. The departures produced the largest number of exits two years into a presidential term in at least two decades.

Before Trump's second term, the Justice Department typically did not hire new prosecutors unless they had at least three to five years of legal experience, former officials and law professors said. That standard has disappeared, and ProPublica noted that Bloomberg Law had earlier reported the department waived a policy requiring at least one year of experience. The Justice Department's longstanding Honors Program places graduates in rigorous training through its main office in Washington, but experts said the program was small and not intended to replace prosecutors.

Barbara McQuade, a University of Michigan law professor and former U.S. attorney for the Eastern District of Michigan whom President Barack Obama appointed, said the people she hired had come from partnerships at large law firms or state prosecutors' offices. She described them as “superstars” who had been “very successful at large law firms or in state court prosecutors' offices.” Private law firms routinely hire promising graduates before they pass the bar to begin training, but McQuade said the Justice Department's hiring of new graduates was a striking departure from its once-rigorous hiring process. She said Trump's creation of a less experienced Justice Department would have longstanding consequences. “It means that some very serious crimes will go unpunished,” she said. “It takes experienced prosecutors at the federal level to bring down corporate fraud, transnational gangs and corrupt public officials.” ProPublica shared its analysis and detailed questions with the Justice Department, which did not respond. The department previously said it “remains committed to investigating and prosecuting all types of crime.”

The department's caseload shifted as its staffing changed. An analysis of Federal Judicial Center data showed felony fraud cases in the Western District of Virginia fell 66% amid an exodus of prosecutors. Those cases dropped 14% in the Southern District of Florida, a hotbed for white-collar crime, where many prosecutors have left in protest of what the outlet described as Trump's retribution campaign against people it considered political enemies. Nationally, drug prosecutions fell about 10% during Trump's first 18 months in office, while fraud prosecutions were flat.

The number of people facing immigration-related charges rose almost 50%, clogging the courts and depleting resources at U.S. attorneys' offices nationwide, ProPublica found. Even in border districts, where immigration proceedings can dominate the caseload, prosecutions rose sharply. Immigration cases increased 300% in New Mexico, 125% in the Southern District of California and at least 36% in the Western District of Texas. In the Western District of Texas, Bigley and at least two other recent graduates had just become prosecutors. The workload included tens of thousands of unlawful-detention cases filed in civil court, and former officials said the Justice Department had drafted hundreds of attorneys from other federal agencies and the military to keep up. ProPublica also tracked nearly 100,000 habeas corpus petitions filed by immigrants who argued that their detentions were unjust, each requiring an assistant U.S. attorney to defend the government's position.

That pressure led some offices to reassign veteran lawyers from financial crimes, civil rights and human trafficking to immigration work. In Washington state, an acting U.S. attorney said his office was so overbooked with unlawful-detention petitions that it could not handle every case. ProPublica said the immigration surge coincided with violent deportation sweeps led by masked immigration agents that killed U.S. citizens and with nationwide protests. It also reported that many Justice Department cases against people protesting immigration authorities' actions fell apart and that the truthfulness of Trump's prosecutors had come under question in courts. The department had previously said its attorneys “represent the United States with integrity.”

In South Florida, where an exodus of veteran lawyers presaged a drop in fraud and financial crime cases, one former white-collar crimes prosecutor said he and his colleagues could not decline an immigration case without permission from U.S. Attorney Jason Reding Quiñones, who abruptly resigned that month. Quiñones had led the “grand conspiracy” investigation targeting former officials Trump alleged were part of a “deep state” plot against him. The former prosecutor requested anonymity because he hopes to return to the Justice Department after Trump leaves office. “Every case takes time, and you want to put in effort, and now you’re wasting your time on cases you don’t believe in or think are unjust,” he said. “It affects morale.”

Justice Department personnel data remains secret, but assistant U.S. attorneys are compensated under a unique pay scale set by the attorney general that dates to the 1950s. Tracking that scale allowed ProPublica to estimate the department's attorney ranks. About 6,200 assistant U.S. attorneys worked there as of January 2025. Elon Musk's Department of Government Efficiency offered federal employees financial packages to resign by September 2025, and about 1 in 10 assistant U.S. attorney departures were associated with that offer, the data suggested.

Former officials said the ranks shrank much faster than the Justice Department anticipated and that it was unable to hire enough replacements. By the end of January 2026, the Justice Department had lost more than 1,300 assistant U.S. attorneys and replaced only about 470 of those attorneys. Quarterly departures had held fairly steady for most of the previous two decades until Trump's reelection, when they peaked at nearly 500 in the second quarter of 2025. The department reached its lowest staffing level since 2014 in March. By August, signing bonuses and a hiring spree focused on less-experienced attorneys had raised the ranks to about 5,600, still 10% below the total at the end of the Biden administration.

Government data and recruiting materials showed that the Justice Department had ramped up recruitment efforts in recent months as it tried to replace the lost expertise. In early 2026, former White House official Chad Mizelle posted on X, the social media platform owned by Musk: “If you are a lawyer, are interested in being an AUSA, and support President Trump and anti-crime agenda, DM me.” ProPublica wrote that the post hinted at the Justice Department's growing desperation to find new hires. Mizelle had recently left his Justice Department role as chief of staff to then-Attorney General Pam Bondi and did not respond to a request for comment.

A month after the department reached its lowest staffing level since 2014, the University of Miami hosted an event titled “Lunch & Learn: Entry Level Opportunities with The U.S. Attorney’s Office.” The invitation targeted third-year law students and promised a new route into criminal prosecution in the Southern District of Florida, one of the country's busiest districts. “The U.S. Attorney’s Office for the Southern District of Florida is now recruiting 3Ls for entry-level positions beginning immediately after graduation — an exciting expansion of opportunities for Miami Law students interested in federal prosecution,” the invitation said.

U.S. Rep. Jamie Raskin, the ranking member of the House Judiciary Committee and the presumed chair once Democrats take charge, said the shift toward inexperienced attorneys was showing in courtrooms. “They’ve sacked some of the finest lawyers in America, by tradition, and replaced them with a large group of inexperienced lawyers,” said Raskin, himself an attorney. In Maryland, fraud cases fell 31% and drug cases fell nearly 16%, while immigration charges nearly doubled. “Those numbers are jaw-dropping,” he said.

Former prosecutor Jim Fang was among the veterans who left. After working in technology for about a decade, he graduated from Georgetown Law in 2009 and practiced intellectual-property law before joining the District of Nevada as a federal criminal prosecutor in April 2020. He said 11 years handling complex legal work had prepared him for difficult white-collar cases. He recalled that he had wanted to “take down bad guys,” and that joining the office in Las Vegas was his dream job. After Trump's election, the president selected former Republican political candidate Sigal Chattah to run the Nevada office.

Through court records and sources, ProPublica confirmed that more than 30 staff had left under Chattah's tenure, though she disputed that number. The district became so depleted that for months it had no prosecutors in its Reno office, about 450 miles away, and Las Vegas prosecutors were asked to fly there for court appearances. Chattah acknowledged that Las Vegas-based staff flew to Reno “to ensure that our federal and local partners had competent and aggressive prosecutors.”

ProPublica reported that the Nevada office dropped cases it had been building as Chattah emphasized immigration and politically motivated prosecutions. To reduce its workload, Chattah's civil-defense unit, which handles lawsuits demanding taxpayer money, was instructed to settle every case that could be resolved for $250,000 or less, according to multiple former employees. “We were ordered to settle those cases even if they didn’t have merit,” one former assistant U.S. attorney said. “It was just throwing money at a problem.”

Chattah called the allegation that she issued such a directive “patently false.” She wrote that some former attorneys “failed to meet the expectations of competent workmanship with the Department of Justice” and either left, retired or were terminated, and said replacements included “aggressive prosecutors who will keep our state safe.” Fang took the Department of Government Efficiency offer in September 2025 and returned to private practice as a defense attorney. He suspected the loss of financial-crime experts would mean fewer successful prosecutions. “It’s a good time to be a white-collar criminal,” he said.

Fang was now mostly busy representing clients who had filed habeas petitions. He said he was recently surprised to learn that one new hire in his former office had just passed the bar. “They’re taking a gamble on them,” he said. Fang said the new hires “may well end up being fine attorneys,” adding, “There’s no way I could have taken that job and done it well right out of law school.”

ProPublica based its findings on workforce data from the Office of Personnel Management covering January 2025 through August 2026 and the Federal Judicial Center's Integrated Database. It measured turnover by counting attorneys compensated under the “Administratively Determined” pay scale within the Executive Office for United States Attorneys and the United States Attorneys' Offices, under OPM code DJ09, and estimated legal experience from experience ranges tied to pay grades. The court analysis covered defendants charged from Jan. 20, 2025, through June 30, 2026, and compared that period with 527 days at the end of the Biden administration. Each defendant was counted once under the most severe charge, and a separate comparison of the last six quarters of Biden's term with the first six quarters of 2025 and 2026 produced similar results.

ProPublica grouped charges into broader categories using a classification system developed by the Administrative Office of the U.S. Courts. Its fraud category includes embezzlement and forgery. It counted the “Nationality Laws” category, which the Administrative Office of the U.S. Courts groups with fraud, as immigration. It also counted two other types of charges as immigration offenses: failing to report arrival into the United States (19 USC 1459) and, in the districts of New Mexico, Western Texas and Arizona, entering military-designated national defense areas along the border (50 USC 797).
