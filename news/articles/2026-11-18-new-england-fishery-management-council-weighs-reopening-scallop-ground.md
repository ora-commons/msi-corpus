## Stokesbury and Fuller differ over the science behind the 1994 closure

The New England Fishery Management Council will vote in December on whether to make reopening the Northern Edge of Georges Bank to commercial scallop fishing a priority in 2027. The area, around 171 square miles of Atlantic Ocean, has been closed to the scallop fleet since 1994. The council took up the question after NOAA instructed regional regulators to discuss reopening it, following a June 11 Oval Office meeting at which John Lees asked President Donald Trump to consider the closed waters.

Lees, the chief executive of the New Bedford-based scallop operation New England Marine, had spent the previous year building relationships with Trump administration insiders through the Sustainable Scalloping Fund, a coalition of industry members, to move along what the coalition considered the industry's biggest issues. Lees received a call on a Tuesday in early June asking whether he could come to Washington, D.C. By Thursday, White House officials were briefing him on what to expect during a gathering with President Donald Trump. That Thursday, June 11, Lees stood in the packed Oval Office among seafood industry leaders and federal officials as Trump proclaimed thousands of miles of previously restricted Pacific waters open again to commercial fishing.

Guests were told not to speak unless the president acknowledged them. Lees said he had thought through what he might say if called on. When Trump turned to him, Lees directed the president's attention to the Northern Edge, describing it as “the best scallop ground in the United States.”

“None of these people were aware of… the Northern Edge, and how it’s been closed for 30 years,” Lees said. After the meeting, Trump instructed a top-ranking National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration official to work with Lees on the issue.

Federal officials closed the Northern Edge through an emergency action on Jan. 3, 1994, and made the closure permanent a few months later, to protect the scallop population and because the area was thought to be valuable for codfish and other species, including lobster. Kevin Stokesbury, dean of the University of Massachusetts Dartmouth School of Marine Science and Technology, said a codfish population plunge caused by overfishing in the early 1990s was the initial reason for the closure.

Shortly afterward, Stokesbury said, a series of research papers published between 1997 and 2008 led to the prohibition against scalloping. The papers compared undisturbed sections of seafloor with sections that scallopers had fished, and Stokesbury said they argued that “scallop fishing is bad for the seafloor, disturbs the bottom and therefore if you want to keep it in a natural state, it should be protected.” The closure excluded the lobster fishery, which was not covered by the scallop prohibition.

On the Canadian side of the Northern Edge, Stokesbury said researchers were doing some work on the scallop population. The Canadian government voluntarily closed a portion of the area under its jurisdiction in 2014 for a year, reopened it for fishing and then allowed it to rest, a voluntary choice by fishing industry leaders described in a 2022 paper, Stokesbury said. The scallop population rebounded there despite vessels fishing it “quite hard,” he said. “It means that it’s a very productive scallop ground,” Stokesbury said. “And if you did open it for fishing… you could expect that it would reestablish itself in two to three years.”

Stokesbury said the findings support considering different factors and management strategies in the scallop fishery, which has been stagnant “for about 20 years.” The options include revisiting when scallopers could enter the area and when it should rest while more of the species spawn, or factoring predators such as sea stars into the management plan, a factor currently lacking from the regulations. He called the Northern Edge “a prime experimental area to do it.”

Stokesbury also said new data is needed. Cod, which the closing was meant to protect, remain scarce on Georges Bank decades later, some researchers say, and data collected by NOAA's North East Science Center shows very low numbers of cod being added to the population since the 1990s. “I’m not sure how good the evidence is there that cod do spawn up there,” Stokesbury said. “It’s been closed since 1994 and the cod stock has not really come back on Georges Bank, so it’s fairly arguable whether it’s having much of an effect up there.”

He wants a “thorough scientific reexamination of cod spawning and the theories behind why that area is critical and what they’re doing with it,” and has had trouble securing funding for that research. The area may still open to commercial scalloping before such a reexamination occurs.

Erica Fuller, senior counsel for the Conservation Law Foundation, said the area should remain closed and that there is “absolutely no new science that indicates that the kind of rationale the council’s provided in the past is flawed.” Fuller said the closure protects not only the scallop resource but other fish species. She called the federal push “really short-sighted” given the number of overfished species in the region, said “The importance of ecosystem resilience and climate change can’t be underestimated,” and said the effort was part of a broader plan “to prioritize additional fishing and short-term economic gain over habitat protection.”

The council has considered limited scallop access before. It voted in 2024 to discontinue work toward allowing limited access, citing concerns about protecting cod spawning and disrupting scallop growth, the council said in a news release from that year. A 2025 council news release said regulators had “significant discussion” around the topic, but the council ultimately voted again to table the work, citing similar concerns and “potential conflicts with offshore lobster fisheries.” In July, NOAA asked the council to discuss allowing the scallop fleet into the Northern Edge, one of six issues included in a letter from the agency. A vote in September delayed any action until the body's December meeting.

Fuller said the federal instruction amounted to “an unusual sort of micromanagement by a presidential administration to tell councils that are essentially following requirements of the Magnuson-Stevens Act to take these kinds of actions.” During the council's September public meeting, she said, “This is a bizarre world where settled expectations are upside down.”

Members of the state's lobster fishery also oppose the entrance of scallop dredges in the Northern Edge. In a Sept. 8 letter to the council, the Massachusetts Lobstermen's Association wrote that “At a time when fisheries managers should be taking every reasonable measure to protect lobster recruitment, reproductive capacity, and stock resilience,” allowing gear like that used on a scallop boat “would move management in the wrong direction.”

Drew Minkiewicz, an attorney with the Sustainable Scalloping Fund, said seeing the Northern Edge in the July list of priorities handed to New England regulators left him “Gratified, relieved, but not surprised.” “The presidential meeting was what really put us over the top in many ways, but it was also one of many meetings and a result of a lot of hard work,” Minkiewicz said.

If the council declines to take up the issue, its September statement said, the policy could be left to the federal government, which could pursue a secretarial amendment. The New Bedford Light reported that the Secretary of Commerce, through NOAA, had already said it would consider that route for another scallop proposal. Whether that would happen with the Northern Edge remained unsettled, and a NOAA spokesperson said the agency does not “speculate about things that may or may not happen in the future.” Lees said the conversation with the Trump administration was continuing. In mid-October, he told a New Bedford Light reporter that he had a meeting with the White House later in the week. “We’re still behind the scenes talking with the administration,” Lees said.
