## More than a quarter of buyers took on 84-month-or-longer auto loans

A Kelley Blue Book report released Nov. 10 put the average U.S. transaction price for a new vehicle at $51,109, about $500 above the previous record set in December 2025. Kelley Blue Book is part of Cox Automotive.

The all-time high came with a 2.1% increase over the previous year. Erin Keating, an executive analyst at Cox Automotive and a co-author of the report, said, “A 2.1% year-over-year increase is actually below inflation.” From 2012 through 2021, cars became 3.4% more expensive per year on average, she said.

The Kelley Blue Book transaction measure reflects what buyers paid: the sticker price, plus add-ons for fancier features, minus incentives such as dealer discounts. It excludes interest on auto loans. The average includes SUVs, pickup trucks and electric vehicles from automakers including Toyota, Subaru, Tesla and Mercedes-Benz. It does not include used-car sales, which normally make up about two-thirds of total car sales.

NPR's report said car prices had been “stubbornly high for years, especially since the pandemic, mainly driven by supply chain issues.” The report also cited tariffs and larger, fancier vehicles favored by buyers and produced by automakers as factors in more recent price increases.

The cost of borrowing has risen over the past several years as the Federal Reserve kept interest rates high in an effort to drive down inflation. Edmunds put auto loan rates at about 11% for used vehicles and over 7% for new ones. Keating said infrequent purchases and interest rates can combine to produce sticker shock. “I mean, we are seeing a true shift in the percentage of your monthly payment that's going towards interest versus towards principal,” she said.

Edmunds' 2026 data showed that, regardless of whether buyers chose a new or used vehicle, they financed with lower down payments and longer terms. Ivan Drury, Edmunds' director of industry insights, said, “We've just seen those explode.” Edmunds found that car buyers financed more than ever, taking out loans worth an average of $44,664 in the past quarter. More than a quarter financed with loans of 84 months or longer. Down payments were sometimes in the range of $500 or $1,000, and Drury said that combination can become expensive over the long term, especially with high interest rates.

Drury attributed some of those choices to competing household bills: “So people just say, ‘Oh, forget it,’ you know. ‘I'll just, I'll just choose the longer term length and put less money down. I've got other bills that are pulling at me,’” he said.

Supply-chain challenges, including a semiconductor shortage during the COVID pandemic, meant fewer cars were made. Carmakers had to choose which vehicles to prioritize and selected their most profitable models. As a result, fewer used vehicles entered car lots in the early 2020s, leaving less inventory for consumers today.

Used-vehicle prices have risen alongside new-vehicle prices. Drury said, “No matter what you're looking at, the price is up.” Cox Automotive put the average used car at close to $27,000, 6% higher than last year, while Drury said vehicles costing less than that were a “sliver” of the industry. Lower inventory can benefit people who already own a car because they can upgrade without much additional money up front, he said. But Drury said, “The consumer that I sympathize with the absolute most? The first-time car buyer.” Those buyers face a steep down payment with nothing to trade in.

Production inputs have also added to vehicle costs. More tariffs apply to steel and aluminum and to goods imported from Canada, affecting American automakers that assemble vehicles on both sides of the border. Prices for computer chips used in sensors and electronics and for copper used in automotive wiring have risen as data centers compete for demand.

Demand has moved toward larger vehicles. Americans prefer bigger cars, SUVs and trucks, which tend to carry higher price tags. Kelley Blue Book puts the average sticker price for a full-size pickup truck at nearly $72,000. American carmakers have largely shifted away from compact sedans and often cited demand for larger vehicles in earnings calls with investors. Mike Wall at Mobility Global said smaller vehicles offer automakers lower margins. “They were sort of that loss leader. It gets the eyeballs in the door,” he said. Automakers make more money with larger vehicles equipped with pricier features.

Buyers have also become accustomed to 360-degree cameras, heated seats and parking-assist systems, Drury said. “No one wants entry-level anymore,” he said. “Everybody goes mid-to-upper trim levels.” Drury continued: “People love walking up to their car that already has the air conditioner going when it's 110 degrees outside, and think, ‘You know what? I don't have to get in here and potentially burn my hands.’” Consumer preferences for more features and options added to vehicle costs, and automakers were aware of that, he said.

Over the past few years, “automakers have been under incredible stress,” Keating said. Tariffs and other rising production costs weighed on automakers, and many had invested heavily in electric-vehicle technology. Electric vehicles still accounted for only about 6% of new cars sold, according to Edmunds.

The market for new-car buyers is also smaller. About 16 million people bought new cars every year, about 1 million fewer since the pandemic. Fewer people in their late 30s and early 40s were buying new vehicles. Keating said, “They're just simply holding onto their cars longer.” Her hypothesis was that the age group was dealing with different household pressures for its money and choosing to remain in the vehicles it had. For the most part, people buying new cars tended to be older and wealthier. Keating said that might reflect older drivers remaining on the road longer, adding that more people aged 65 and older, including those 75 and older, were purchasing vehicles than ever before.

For automakers, the smaller pool of buyers meant greater competition for sales. Keating said, “At the end of the day, each of these competitors are sharing, trying to go after a pie that isn't necessarily growing.” Automakers were taking share from rivals rather than relying on more consumers entering the market, she said.
