## House Democrats set to examine conflicts of interest in temporary staffing

The White House plans to scale back its use of special government employees, according to people familiar with the matter. The designation, commonly abbreviated SGE, allows people in the private sector to work temporarily in the administration without giving up outside salaries or investments. White House chief of staff Stephen Miller has told associates he wants to wind down the designation, the people said, and two people familiar with the move said the White House will not ban all SGEs but will use them sparingly.

A White House official said Miller wants to give priority to hiring full-time employees and plans to crack down on what he sees as abuse of the SGE designation. The change comes shortly after President Trump appointed Miller, a longtime ally, as chief of staff. Miller has already taken early steps to limit White House officials' interactions with lobbyists, seeking to tamp down on allegations of self-dealing that polls show hurt Republicans in the midterm elections.

Only a sliver of SGE cases require public disclosure of clients or conflicts of interest. Federal rules limit SGEs to 130 days of work in the administration in any given year, though the administration can extend the limit. The designation has typically been used to bring subject-matter experts onto advisory boards or into narrow, specialized roles without requiring them to leave their private-sector jobs, as regular government employees are required to do.

The Trump administration expanded that use, temporarily installing political allies of the president across the government. Several high-profile SGEs are leaving or have left the administration in recent weeks. Businessman Elon Musk, the former head of the administration's Department of Government Efficiency, Corey Lewandowski, a top adviser at the Department of Homeland Security, and David Sacks, a tech entrepreneur who was AI and crypto czar, no longer work in the administration.

Current SGEs at the White House include Trump's faith adviser, pastor Paula White; White House pardon czar Alice Johnson; and Johnny DeStefano, a former Trump adviser who joined the administration to help with midterm strategy and relationships on Capitol Hill. Danielle Alvarez, Trump's former campaign spokeswoman, served in a temporary role under the designation helping with communications and left the White House earlier this month.

Katie Miller, Stephen Miller's wife, drew scrutiny when The Wall Street Journal reported last year that she was working at the White House as an SGE while advising private clients. She later said she was taking a leave of absence from her job at a top Republican communications consulting firm while she worked at the White House. Katie Miller left the White House in May 2025.

Jason Miller, a longtime Trump adviser and former campaign spokesman, worked as an unpaid adviser at the White House this fall even though he was never officially a special government employee, according to a person familiar with the matter. The Washington Post reported that he made $15 million this past year, according to a sworn financial statement he filed in court.

Stephen Miller is also putting his own stamp on the White House's operations. He brought back May Mailman, who previously served as deputy assistant to the president and senior policy strategist, in a temporary role to help with West Wing operations and policy issues.

Democrats in Congress are expected to launch a wave of investigations into the president and his advisers once they take control of Congress next year, examining whether private-sector work by special government employees created conflicts of interest. Rep. Robert Garcia (D., Calif.), who is expected to lead the House Oversight Committee, has requested information on Lewandowski's employment as an SGE and a complete accounting of the days he worked at the Department of Homeland Security to assess whether he exceeded the 130-day limit. This week, Garcia sent letters to Trump family members and some top aides, including Lewandowski, asking them to preserve documents relevant to future investigations.

Ethics experts have raised concerns about the administration's broad use of SGEs and the way the role allows powerful government workers to shield their finances from public scrutiny, potentially blurring the line between private enrichment and public service. Richard Briffault, a Columbia Law School professor who specializes in government ethics, said SGEs are often brought in for subject-matter expertise. “The value is the expertise, but the concern is that since they’re only there in the short term, they might still be paid by an outside employer or they may have a conflict of interest in the entity the government is regulating,” he said. One former White House official said it has been frustrating for staffers to see some people serve in the administration while continuing to make money working for outside private clients.

Previous administrations have also used the designation. Huma Abedin, Hillary Clinton's deputy chief of staff at the State Department, was an SGE for a time while she worked for Teneo, a consulting firm. Anita Dunn, a top aide to President Joe Biden, held the title while keeping her ties to SKDK, a Democratic consulting and lobbying firm.

The White House move does not end the arrangement outright. Two people familiar said SGEs will remain available but will be used sparingly, and Miller's stated priority is full-time hiring. The change comes as Democratic oversight looms, and Garcia's request for Lewandowski's accounting of days at the Department of Homeland Security asks specifically whether the 130-day limit was exceeded.
