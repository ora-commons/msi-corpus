## Hormone data document miscarriages; field observations document calf mortality

In Washington waters, The SeaDoc Society and the San Diego Zoo Wildlife Alliance are working to collect health information on the Southern Resident killer whales, an endangered population whose recovery is faltering despite years of protection. The whales have spent less time in the Salish Sea, the inland waters between Washington State and British Columbia, and during the last half-decade have hunted coho and chum just north of Seattle in the fall. That shift has given the researchers an opportunity to collect samples from whales whose movements have become less predictable.

Each fall in recent years, Deborah Giles begins her work by asking where the orcas are. “They were here before us, long before us,” said Giles. “They’re a group of animals that are uniquely bonded to this place and to each other.”

The collaboration uses drones mounted with petri dishes and microscope slides to collect droplets from whale spouts, while a dog locates stool samples. The breath and fecal material can support wellness checks for as many of the 76 remaining whales as the team can find.

Before the colonization of the Pacific Northwest, the orcas’ main prey, Chinook salmon, weighed more than 100 pounds (45.36 kilograms) each and were abundant along the West Coast, Giles said. She said American settlers built dams and overfished salmon; as cities and ports grew, boat noise hindered orca hunting and toxic chemicals flowed into seawater. Chinook now average about 12.5 pounds (5.66 kilograms), Giles said. “We have completely changed their ability to live out their daily lives,” she said.

The lack of fish has pushed the whales into the open ocean, where collecting research samples is more difficult. The whales used to reliably spend their summers around the San Juan Islands, where Giles is based, but no longer do. She moves her 22-foot (6.7-meter) boat farther south during the fall in hopes of finding whales and collecting samples.

The team motors quickly toward sightings reported by Giles’ contacts. With the orcas in view, James Sheppard, a San Diego Zoo Wildlife Alliance drone pilot and senior scientist, launches the drone from the boat’s bow. He first hovers high to locate the whale, then descends to just a dozen or so feet above the surface. He focuses on an adult male with a massive dorsal fin punching through the water. The spout spray reaches 6 to 10 feet (1.8 to 3 meters) into the air and is as wide as a basketball, Sheppard said. As the whale breathes, the drone sweeps horizontally across the falling droplets. “Once we’ve got the sample it’s important we bring the drone back to the boat as quickly as possible so that the samples don’t get degraded with the wind and the sunlight,” Sheppard said.

Conditions on this November day make the work difficult. Less sunlight makes the whales harder to see, and the animals forage and surface unpredictably. The drones also use cameras to record video and infrared sensors to capture a whale’s body temperature. “The few times that they’ve actually noticed the drone they’ll just roll on their side, maybe look at it for a few seconds, and then just keep on swimming. To them, it’s just a strange seagull that’s been following them for a few seconds,” said Sheppard, who recently won an award for his piloting and research.

After the drone, it’s Eba’s turn. The 11-year-old dog heads straight to the bow and sniffs the air, with her head extending beyond the boat. Jim Rappold, Giles’ husband and research partner, holds Eba’s leash. Eba picks up a scent, lets out a few whines and paces on the bow to alert the researchers to her find. On this day, however, the handlers cannot locate the feces she detected.

Over the years, the team has collected hundreds of fecal and breath samples. Those samples contain hormonal, bacterial, fungal, parasitic and DNA information that the San Diego Zoo Wildlife Alliance lab analyzes. Researchers compile the data into health profiles for individual orcas. “If we need to intervene, if we need to have a discussion about something that might need to happen to an individual animal, we have that animal’s entire record right there,” Giles said.

By comparing pregnancy hormones over time, researchers have found that many females are having miscarriages. The research also documents a high calf mortality rate, illustrated by mothers seen carrying dead calves.

The Southern Resident whales’ situation contrasts with that of Bigg’s killer whales, which Giles said are thriving. Bigg’s killer whales eat sea mammals, including seals, sea lions and porpoises, that are plentiful in the Salish Sea. Some Bigg’s females have calves every two years, Giles said, while gestation alone takes 17-18 months.

Authorities have taken steps to help the Southern Resident whales, including reducing vessel noise, Giles said. She said the most important action is restoring salmon runs. Salmon that spawn in the Salish Sea can travel as far north as Alaska, where they are caught, preventing them from returning to areas where the orcas could intercept them. “It’s our job to turn back the clock,” she said.
