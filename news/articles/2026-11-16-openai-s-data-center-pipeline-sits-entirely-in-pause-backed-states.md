## OpenAI's data-center pipeline sits entirely in pause-backed states

**Subtype:** fact

- All of OpenAI's roughly 14 gigawatts of planned data-center capacity sits in states whose governors back a pause in data-center development, according to data compiled by Cleanview.
- About 10 gigawatts of OpenAI's planned capacity comes from a single Ohio project, SB Energy's SBE Ports-Pike Technology Campus, which is equivalent to roughly 10 nuclear reactors.

## Amazon leads rivals with about 16.5 gigawatts already under construction

The November elections have complicated data-center plans across the technology industry, but the exposure is unevenly distributed. OpenAI's planned footprint is the most concentrated: every gigawatt of its roughly 14 gigawatts of proposed capacity lies in a state whose governor backs pausing data-center development, Cleanview's figures show, counting only projects that have not yet begun construction.

The largest single piece is a 10-gigawatt project in Ohio, SB Energy's SBE Ports-Pike Technology Campus, which would be among the largest announced data centers in the United States. Michael Thomas, founder of data provider Cleanview, called the concentration a high-stakes gamble. "If they can get it built, then they get 10 GW all in one go. If they can't get it built, they lose 10 GW in one project," Thomas said. The rest of OpenAI's planned capacity sits in Georgia and Texas.

Ohio's governor-elect, Amy Acton, has called for a conditional moratorium on data centers until they meet requirements such as 100% union labor. Georgia and Texas have each just elected Democratic governors.

Microsoft and Amazon carry more moderate exposure, with 45% and 42% of their planned capacity in pause-backed states, respectively, per Cleanview. Amazon nevertheless has the largest number of projects in its pipeline and, as a result, the most planned capacity in relatively data-center-friendly states at 17.6 gigawatts. Anthropic and Google have the least exposure at about 20% each. Anthropic's planned data centers are all leased and include Texas as well as Indiana and Kentucky, which are more data-center-friendly states. One of those projects is AWS Hobart, a roughly 2-gigawatt project Amazon plans to build in Indiana.

State policy outcomes remain hard to predict, and legislative makeup may matter. In Texas and Georgia, Democrats flipped the state House but not the state Senate; in Ohio, Republicans retained both chambers. Even in the best case, delays may follow as legislators work out the terms under which data centers can be built.

Geographic spread now looks like an advantage. Google's planned footprint is among the most geographically diversified, according to Thomas, with project sites in Wyoming, Missouri, Indiana, Arkansas and Oklahoma — all of which will have Republican governors next year. Google has also bought renewable-energy developer Intersect Power and invested in its ability to shift computing loads.

A deep pipeline of projects already under construction should also help. Amazon leads on that measure with about 16.5 gigawatts of projects under construction, including self-built and leased capacity, according to data provider Aterio. Google has 13.5 gigawatts under construction and Meta Platforms has 11 gigawatts.

Some of that underway work is itself at risk. Developers often break ground and put capital on the ground before securing every required permit, and those projects look more vulnerable after the elections. Infrastructure risk analytics firm Occam Edge tracks at least 13 large data-center projects that have begun construction but are still waiting on air permits for on-site power generation, plus two projects facing legal challenges over their air permits.

SpaceX's high-speed strategy of building out data centers has, so far, been effective: rather than wait for grid interconnection, the company has built its own grid to power its data centers, and its Colossus 2 campus has added computing capacity faster than any other campus Occam Edge has tracked. That is where the strategy now looks riskier. The air permit for SpaceX's Starbase AI campus in Texas, which has begun construction, is in limbo after current Gov. Greg Abbott's data-center pause, and the approved air permit for the power plant serving Colossus 2 near Memphis is under legal challenge. Occam Edge rates both projects at high risk of construction delays.

SpaceX's Elon Musk said last month that the company is "fairly confident" of bringing 10 gigawatts of additional computing capacity in 2027. That goal looks more challenging after the midterms, though SpaceX may target friendlier states; the company has recently filed for a permit to build a 6-gigawatt data center in Arizona, where the Democratic governor-elect has supported ending tax breaks for data centers but rejected calls for a moratorium.

Oracle and OpenAI's Project Jupiter in New Mexico is another project to watch. It has a planned capacity of about 2.5 gigawatts and does not yet hold an air-quality permit. The project has been under construction since September 2025 and has thousands of workers on site, according to a report in the Albuquerque Journal. Governor-elect Deb Haaland has called for a moratorium on new large-scale data-center projects, departing from the current governor's data-center-friendly stance.

For developers, the calculus has shifted: geographical diversity and slow but safe grid-connected projects look more valuable than they did when friendly states offered speedy build-outs.
