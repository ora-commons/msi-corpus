## Some student accusers were not interviewed during state investigations

California’s Commission on Teacher Credentialing allowed former elementary school counselor David Braff to continue working with children after Santa Paula Unified School District reported allegations of sexual misconduct in 2019, according to a ProPublica and KQED investigation that found the agency failed to conduct a thorough investigation. Police arrested Braff in 2024, by which time he had taken jobs at three more Southern California schools. Prosecutors allege that another student complained Braff had touched him at one of those schools. The commission suspended his credentials after the arrest. Braff is set to stand trial on charges alleging that he sexually abused 15 students, including 13 from Santa Paula. He has pleaded not guilty and has been in jail since November 2024. He did not respond to a request for comment sent through the Ventura County Sheriff’s Office’s electronic messaging system, and his attorney declined to comment.

Five years before police arrested Braff, complaints had been sent to California alleging that he had sat students on his lap, rubbed their legs and touched them near their vaginas.

Santa Paula’s 2019 report to the state followed complaints by at least seven students that Braff had touched them inappropriately. Braff had served as a guidance counselor at McKevett Elementary School for four years and resigned during the district’s investigation. State law requires districts to report educators when they resign or are fired after allegations of misconduct. The district told the state it had considered but did not initiate discipline before accepting Braff’s resignation. It said student interviews made it appear that Braff had “engaged in at least inappropriate conduct.”

The 20-page summary submitted two months after Braff resigned included principal interviews with at least seven accusers and two witnesses. It also included Braff’s defense and noted multiple inconsistencies in his account of what had occurred.

The district’s report documented allegations that Braff locked an office door, grabbed a fifth-grade student by her hips and forced her onto his lap. The girl said she did not like being touched and cried every time she had to see him for counseling. She requested a transfer because she wanted to “be away from” Braff. Two other girls alleged that Braff touched them near their “privates,” and neither knew of the other’s claim.

One of those girls, a fourth grader, said she had met regularly with Braff for counseling since second grade. During those visits, he would make her sit on his lap. She said Braff tapped her leg with his hand “in a way to try to make her feel like she was doing a good job,” though the tapping hurt. After two school years of regular counseling, she alleged that he began tapping her higher on her thigh, close to her “private.” She contrasted Braff’s conduct with another teacher’s practice of patting students on the head when they did a good job.

Braff told district investigators that he did not touch students near their private parts or have them sit on his lap. He said he gave students side hugs, high-fives, fist bumps and taps on the knee. The report said Braff described “keeping an office environment where kids can move around; and he would not be closer than arm’s length,” adding that, “It was not his intent to make them afraid but rather feel that there was someone in their corner; someone to talk to.” During the investigation, Braff acknowledged touching students on the knee. “He explained if someone is crying, he taps on the knee to say they are doing a good job to get the sad feelings out,” the report said. “When asked to demonstrate, Mr. Braff showed a slap on his own knee.”

Santa Paula Unified Superintendent Jeffrey Weinstein, who was not working at the district when students made the original accusations, said the district investigated Braff and reported him to the state and police. Weinstein said he reviewed the district’s investigative file and “vehemently disagreed” with the state’s decision not to revoke Braff’s credentials.

After Braff’s arrest, one parent asked Weinstein in an email, “Who dropped the ball and allowed this monster to still be around kids?” Weinstein replied: “We sent all the evidence we had gathered,” and, “The district did everything in its legal capacity it could.” Santa Paula administrators did not respond to requests for comment.

One accuser, identified by her first initial, J., told KQED and ProPublica that Braff had told her that whatever happened in his office was a secret. She said she had few friends at the school and was terrified of getting in trouble when she reported the person she confided in.

The girl, now 18, spoke with her mother and attorney present on the condition that only her first initial, J., be used to protect her privacy. The district’s report included notes from an interview with J.’s former principal, Lydia Olivo, who had left the school before J. and other students complained about Braff. Olivo said J. was “not always honest.” She described Braff as a reliable employee who had strong relationships with parents, staff and students. She “seemed surprised that any of this could have occurred while she had been Principal at McKevett,” the report said. Olivo did not respond to calls, emails and texts seeking comment.

The licensing agency appeared not to have attempted to reconcile the different accounts of Braff’s behavior. J. and her mother told KQED and ProPublica that no one from the state agency contacted them to verify the allegation or reconcile it with Olivo’s assessment, even though the state received the students’ contact information. At least one other student who accused Braff said the state did not contact her either.

“If a little girl comes up to you and says something, maybe you should not say that they’re a liar,” J. said. “They should interview students themselves and not just rely on the school district. Do a legit investigation,” J.’s mother said. Her mother did not know that the state had decided not to discipline Braff until the news outlets alerted her.

The commission did not answer questions about why J. was not interviewed or whether anyone else was. The Braff case had been assigned to a staffer whose job description did not include interviewing victims. A former agency investigator who reviewed the Santa Paula report for the news outlets said she believed the students’ accusations because multiple children made similar claims and Braff’s responses contained contradictions. She said sexual-misconduct cases should be assigned to investigators trained to interview victims.

“Any claim of sexual misconduct should go to somebody who’s going to be making phone calls,” the investigator said, speaking on condition of anonymity to protect her current job. “This man is grooming these children. There’s no other way to look at it to me. Their stories are credible.”

Under state law, agency staff assigned to investigate Braff’s case could not recommend discipline. That decision belonged to the seven-member Committee of Credentials, a volunteer panel that met monthly at the agency’s Sacramento headquarters. The panel weighed reports from investigators, school districts, police and other sources before making recommendations to the commission’s 19 commissioners, most of whom were appointed by the governor.

Commissioners could review a case only if the committee recommended discipline. If the panel found that no punishment was warranted, the commissioners did not review the case. State regulations gave the committee broad latitude in assessing allegations. The committee applied a “probable cause” standard before recommending a reprimand, a lower threshold than the “beyond a reasonable doubt” standard required for a criminal conviction.

Steve Thorpe, a retired law enforcement officer who served on the committee when Braff’s case was reviewed, described that threshold as the point at which “a normal and reasonable person would feel that there’s a chance that this person did this.” “It’s a low threshold. You know it when you see it,” he said. He declined to comment on Braff’s case specifically.

Over three days in December 2020, during the height of the COVID-19 pandemic, committee members met by phone to consider 198 cases involving educators accused of misconduct, including Braff. The volunteers included a retired law enforcement officer, a school board member, a school administrator, an education consultant and several teachers, all selected by the commissioners for two-year terms. By then, Braff had worked as a counselor at a different charter elementary school in Los Angeles for more than a year.

Several former agency staffers, including investigators, told the news outlets that the volunteer panel at times overlooked evidence of wrongdoing. “Sometimes, as investigators, we’ll see the recommendations, and we’ll be like, ‘What the hell happened here?’” one former investigator said. ProPublica and KQED reported that the perspective from staff members was supported by two former committee members and two people who had served on the state commission.

One former committee member, who spoke on condition of anonymity because of fear of repercussions from the teacher licensing agency, said she scrutinized district investigations because administrators can misrepresent the facts or ask children leading questions. Another former committee member who served during the Braff case said they were generally alarmed to hear some fellow members dismiss children as unreliable sources. Speaking on condition of anonymity because of fears of professional repercussions, the former member said, “I was consistently shocked,” adding: “To have committee members discuss serious allegations as, ‘Well, you know how girls can be. They say all kinds of things they don’t mean,’ is an unacceptable, antiquated point of view. What if we get this wrong? Who are we allowing in the classroom?”

The fact that Braff’s case was not forwarded to the commissioners, former committee members said, meant the majority of the seven-member panel did not see enough evidence in the district’s investigation and the state’s report to discipline Braff. By the time the committee decided against recommending punishment, Braff had worked at the different Los Angeles charter school for more than a year. After his 2024 arrest, a former student from that school told police that Braff had touched the boy’s thigh near his groin when they met in his office, according to a court transcript from his criminal case.

Commission spokesperson Anita Fitzhugh said the state automatically revokes teachers’ credentials when they are convicted of sexual criminal offenses. She wrote in a statement that “unless the applicant or educator is convicted of a mandatory criminal offense, there are no written criteria that set the level of adverse action for educator discipline matters.”

Fitzhugh said the state provides no guidelines for determining when misconduct warrants discipline or what punishment should result. She said the commission must balance “protecting students as well as the legal rights of educators who have been accused but not convicted.” She did not answer questions about the specific cases or details of the investigative process.

KQED and ProPublica identified at least two other educators who, like Braff, were reported by their school districts to the state for sexual misconduct but were allowed to keep teaching until they were criminally charged. Each drew additional allegations of sexual abuse. In both cases, the volunteer committee evaluated much of the same evidence as police, including complaints detailing students’ experiences, and investigators did not interview some victims.

A similar sequence occurred in the case of middle school math teacher Brashawn Washington. Romoland School District reported Washington to the state in the summer of 2020 after his 11-year-old student’s mother gave the district screenshots of messages he had sent through the video game Fortnite. She had provided the screenshots from her son’s phone to the school in May 2020, and the school promptly alerted police and began its own investigation, according to public records obtained from Romoland and district records.

The messages included a TikTok video showing another boy’s penis. Romoland also shared Google Hangouts chats asking the student if he worked out, calling him “guapo,” or handsome, and sending him kissing emojis. Washington told jurors at his trial that he did not send the video.

The district included the mother’s contact information in its report to the state, records show. Neither the student nor his mother was interviewed, the mother told KQED and ProPublica. Fitzhugh did not answer questions about how state investigators decided who, if anyone, to interview. A Romoland administrator who said the district shared the screenshots with the state as part of its report did not answer questions about the state’s response.

Washington resigned during the district’s investigation and began teaching at Pasadena High School in fall 2020. Teaching virtually at first during the pandemic, he continued messaging students through Fortnite despite a district policy prohibiting personal contact with students.

Seven months after Washington started at Pasadena High, the volunteer committee recommended a 21-day suspension in March. Commissioners did not approve the suspension until after school let out. State law did not require Washington to notify the Pasadena school district of the recommended suspension. Nor did the teacher licensing agency have to alert his new employer of any discipline if it did not know where he was employed.

Police arrested Washington in July 2021, just as his suspension was set to begin, and the state suspended his credentials indefinitely. The next year, while out on bail, he sent a Pasadena High student images of himself having oral sex with another man, among other messages, according to court records. At Washington’s 2024 criminal trial, that student testified that the teacher had also asked him to share naked pictures of himself when he was 16. Washington told jurors he did not know the student was a minor.

Washington was convicted of multiple sex offenses, including “annoying or molesting” the Romoland student by sending him the video, as well as producing and possessing child sexual abuse material involving minors who were not his students. He was sentenced to eight years and eight months in prison. Washington and his former attorney declined to comment. A Pasadena Unified spokesperson said the district conducts background checks but did not answer questions about Washington’s hiring or the recommended discipline.

A similar pattern unfolded with San Francisco Bay Area elementary teacher Matthew Shelton, who was convicted of sexually abusing four students last month. Shelton had first been arrested in 2007 on charges of sexually abusing students in Napa Valley, where he taught elementary school. Three third-graders told police he touched their buttocks or crotches, or made them touch his penis during class. Shelton was acquitted, but the licensing agency investigated the allegations. Shelton told state investigators that the students had made up the claims and that he had passed a polygraph test he obtained himself.

Two of those former students said state investigators never interviewed them. The agency did not respond to questions about why the students were not interviewed. In 2008, the licensing agency issued Shelton a confidential written warning that was expunged after three years, according to his trial testimony. The decision allowed Shelton to continue teaching for the long term, and he had already begun working in nearby Benicia.

Years later, in 2022, students began making new allegations against Shelton. The Benicia school district and a student’s parents reported him to the state commission in 2023. More students came forward and told police that Shelton had touched their crotch or butt over their clothing. One student said he made her touch his penis when she was in first grade during a school field trip. Shelton resigned but quickly obtained another job. The new school said it had no information about the ongoing investigation. It also had no knowledge of his earlier warning, which had been expunged more than a decade earlier.

The state did not alert the new school about any problems, but a parent did. In September, just weeks into the school year, Shelton’s new employer learned from a Benicia parent about the active police investigation as well as the earlier issues in Napa. Shelton was immediately put on leave. In February 2024, prosecutors charged him, requiring the licensing agency to automatically suspend his credentials. Unlike in Napa, the agency revoked his license in July 2024 without waiting for a conviction.

Shelton did not respond to requests for comment, and his attorney declined to comment. He is due to be sentenced in December. Benicia Superintendent Chris Calabrese declined to discuss Shelton’s hiring.

The cases expose a gap between a school district’s obligation to report misconduct and the state’s ability to act on the referral. California’s public educator database normally displays a warning beside a teacher’s name after the commission imposes discipline. It does not display such a warning when an educator resigns or is fired without state discipline, and confidential warnings such as Shelton’s do not produce a public flag.

That gap left potential employers without an indication of a problem. California lawmakers have tried to address the gap, but their efforts failed.

One of the students who accused Shelton in Napa told the news outlets that for years she wished she hadn’t reported him to anyone because she and her classmates were characterized as liars after “we poured out so much.” “It could’ve been stopped with us, but it wasn’t,” she said, adding, “They didn’t believe us.”

In 2022, nearly two years after the state closed Braff’s case, a Santa Paula administrator reported that one of the girls who had accused him continued to suffer severe anxiety and could not be in rooms alone with men. Another had attempted suicide multiple times and spent a year in residential treatment, the administrator wrote in an email obtained by KQED and ProPublica. In his response, an assistant superintendent said the district had previously sought revocation or suspension of Braff’s credential, but the state had declined. “At this point, I think pursing this matter legally is the best way to address these issues,” the assistant superintendent wrote, referring to following up with Santa Paula police.

J. told KQED and ProPublica that for years she avoided acknowledging what Braff did to her. “I couldn’t place where all these emotions were coming from. I was blaming everything else except the actual problem,” J. said, referring to the abuse by Braff. “I didn’t want to live anymore.”

J., now a high school senior, has been homeschooled by her mother for almost three years. She said she still has nightmares in which Braff yells at her for “telling on him.” A few years ago, a former classmate asked her, “Why would they let him go work somewhere else if he did that to you?”

Braff’s trial remains pending, Washington has been sentenced and Shelton is awaiting sentencing. Fitzhugh said California’s educator discipline system dates to the 1970s and said, “a deep and comprehensive review of the educator discipline system, prioritizing student safety in the schools, is in order.” She said, “We expect the Commission to take this up in the near future.”
