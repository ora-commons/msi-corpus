## Bessent urged an external replacement before reversing his stance

In a November 23 column in The Guardian, Nils Pratley said Nvidia's directors had reversed their biggest decision under political pressure, forfeiting any claim to independence. He called them “patsy directors” who should resign out of “pure embarrassment,” and said there was no real signal that co-founder Jensen Huang's powers would be checked after his return as chief executive.

The board's earlier position was equally forceful. Harvey Jones, who had been rapidly installed as chair of Nvidia, said Huang's exit followed a “disclosure of personal behaviour inconsistent with the company’s code of conduct, standards and cultural norms.” The board “does not intend to reinstate Jensen as CEO” after Huang “voluntarily resigned,” Jones said. Pratley described the no-return position as having been stated “a mere six days ago” and said it was supposed to be definitive. He described the earlier statements as now “inoperative,” invoking Ron Ziegler.

The board said it had “every confidence in Jensen” and his “candor and ability to lead Nvidia.” The column's lead photograph showed Huang speaking at Nvidia's all-employee celebration in Taipei, Taiwan, in May, credited to Ritchie B Tongo/EPA.

Before the reversal, U.S. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent was focused on the need for a “new-broom external hire” to replace Huang amid scrutiny of Nvidia's compliance with U.S. export controls. “They need to go outside the company,” Bessent said, calling for “a dramatic shake-up.” Pratley noted that Bessent had recently begun his own U-turn on Huang's position and argued that the Treasury secretary's earlier questions about compliance had been warranted.

Pratley said Jones and his fellow directors did not disguise that they had been “lent on.” Nvidia said the decision followed “input from senior levels of the United States government,” which Pratley said could only mean someone above Bessent. Trump said he had convinced the board to reinstate Huang, called him “a great patriot and national treasure” and said the U.S. government had no reason to think his return posed a threat to national security.

Nvidia had not completed its investigation into reports that Huang was “the target of a foreign intelligence effort.” The board's only formal reference was to an alleged affair with Alice Huang, who was unrelated to Jensen Huang and formerly led a Singapore-based company suspected by U.S. officials of smuggling Nvidia chips into China.

Pratley said he could “safely guess” the unfinished inquiry would conclude that Huang had done nothing seriously wrong. He also wrote that Huang faced two alleged affairs with Nvidia subordinates, relationships that would usually be a “sackable offence” at a U.S. company, and predicted the board would probably never refer to them again.

With support from technology figures who signed a petition, Huang had proved “too big to fire,” Pratley wrote. He compared Nvidia's process with Open AI in 2023, when Sam Altman was fired and reinstated four days later, and said Nvidia's process had taken only slightly longer.

The market reaction formed part of Pratley's explanation, though he framed the motive as speculation. He wrote that the fall in AI share prices after Huang's resignation may have been the clinching factor because, from Trump's standpoint, a bursting AI valuation bubble represented a danger to the last two years of his presidency. Restoring Huang gave most investors what they wanted, Pratley said, and put the “godfather of AI” back at the wheel of the “circular financing carousel.” He wrote that hundreds of billions of dollars had been wiped off Nvidia's valuation in recent days before $1tn was instantly added back.

Nvidia formed a committee to oversee trade compliance and national security risks. Pratley said congressional scrutiny of the company's export controls would not stop just because the committee existed.

Pratley said U.S. Secretary of State Marco Rubio would be pressed for details of an intelligence memo he reportedly circulated in the spring identifying Alice Huang as a Chinese spy. He added that Jensen Huang himself could no longer be taken seriously on export controls.

In his conclusion, Pratley said Nvidia's board emerged looking “idiotic.” He argued that the directors had reversed their biggest decision under political pressure, forfeiting any claim to independence, and that no real signal suggested Huang's powers would be checked. His conclusion was that the “patsy directors” should resign out of “pure embarrassment.”
