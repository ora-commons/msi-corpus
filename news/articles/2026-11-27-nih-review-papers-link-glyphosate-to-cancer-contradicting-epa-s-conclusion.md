## EPA reassessment could face new scientific and legal pressure

Three papers released by the National Institutes of Health link glyphosate, an active ingredient in Roundup and the world's most widely used pesticide, to cancer. The findings contradict the Environmental Protection Agency's conclusion that the pesticide is not carcinogenic as the agency conducts a court-ordered reassessment of glyphosate's cancer risks.

The papers could have significant political and legal fallout. A finding that glyphosate is linked to cancer would subject the pesticide to a modest increase in regulations, while another finding that it is not carcinogenic could complicate the legal terrain for both the EPA and Bayer.

The NIH did not conduct new studies. Instead, it systematically reviewed decades of scientific literature from around the world. The papers found an association between glyphosate and blood cancers in humans and reported that the pesticide caused several types of tumors in animals. Advocates said the work followed proper scientific protocol, also used by the World Health Organization's International Agency for Research on Cancer and other credible research organizations.

Nathan Donley, environmental health science director at the Center for Biological Diversity, said the EPA's pesticide division had reviewed most of the same studies several years earlier but reached a different conclusion because it deviated from its own methodology. In 2022, a court threw out the EPA's findings for failing to use proper protocol and ordered a new risk assessment. Donley said the EPA was expected to finish the reassessment early next year.

Donley said the EPA and NIH are often aligned on risk assessments for many chemicals, but he accused the EPA's pesticide division of chronically underestimating cancer risks because of flawed methodology. “This agency is completely incapable of doing a good, clear, robust cancer assessment, not just with glyphosate, but all pesticides, because they don’t follow their own methodology,” Donley said.

He said the studies raise further questions about chemical companies' influence on the EPA's pesticide division, which he said “parrots industry talking points” in its safety reviews. Donley also said Americans would be smart to heed the NIH findings rather than industry-influenced EPA claims and accused the pesticide office of making a mockery of pesticide science.

The EPA did not respond to a request for comment. Ruveon, a Bayer subsidiary that manages Roundup in the US, said in a statement: “The US EPA, like other leading health authorities globally, has previously and repeatedly concluded that glyphosate does not cause cancer.”

The Guardian characterized the papers as a major public-relations hit for Bayer, which has defended the herbicide and recorded $2.8 billion in sales for it in 2024.

The findings arrive amid criticism from across the political spectrum of the White House's support for Bayer. An executive order from Donald Trump aimed to protect glyphosate's production and provide immunity to its producers, while an amicus brief supporting Bayer in a US Supreme Court case partially shielded the company from lawsuits.

The administration's support for glyphosate has driven a wedge between pro-industry figures in the White House and the Make America Healthy Again movement spearheaded by Health Secretary Robert F Kennedy Jr. Kelly Ryerson, a Maha advocate who has met with top agency officials to advocate for stricter pesticide policies, said she was “extremely hopeful that glyphosate’s day of reckoning has arrived.”

Ryerson argued that the government-performed studies confirmed what independent scientists had claimed for decades and would force the EPA to change its carcinogenicity determination in the upcoming review. She noted that the NIH papers came in addition to a recent US Department of Health and Human Services-commissioned study that also reviewed literature on glyphosate's effects on hormones and found it likely reduced sperm counts and testosterone.

Donley disagreed. He said he was “99.9% certain the EPA is going to again find that glyphosate does not cause cancer.” He predicted the agency would then be sued and lose in court again, setting up a direct conflict with Ryerson's expectation that the reassessment will change the agency's determination.
