## China-US trade truce extended two months with about $60 billion in tariff cuts

China, Japan, Singapore and Malaysia on Thursday rejected the Trump administration's newly announced tariff increases, disputing the administration's determination that the targeted economies maintain excess manufacturing capacity. The duties, announced Wednesday and likely to take effect early next year, follow a monthslong Section 301 probe and are intended to help reinstate tariffs for most of the economies after the Supreme Court ruling in February struck down most of President Donald Trump's sweeping tariffs implemented last year.

U.S. Trade Representative Jamieson Greer said he determined that practices of the 14 economies relating to excess capacity and production in manufacturing are "unreasonable and burden or restrict U.S. commerce." The new import duties followed the monthslong Section 301 probe into the trade practices of those economies.

China, one of the biggest trading partners of the United States, faces a 20% tariff under the new package. Beijing called the notion of "overcapacity" a "false premise." "China opposes its use as a pretext for political maneuvering," Ministry of Foreign Affairs spokesman Guo Jiakun said at a regular press briefing. "Tariff wars and trade wars serve no one's interests."

The announcement comes weeks before the trade truce between the two countries is due to run out. The United States and China have extended by two months a trade truce that is set to expire on Jan. 10, 2027. The two countries also have agreed on tariff cuts on reciprocal lists of non-sensitive products worth about $60 billion.

Japan faces new U.S. tariffs of up to 15%, and the Japanese government said it protested to Washington. "Japan does not have any nonmarket policies or practices, or structural excess production capacity in the first place," Chief Cabinet Secretary Minoru Kihara told a regular news conference Thursday. "It is regrettable that the United States announced the proposed measures targeting Japan, and we have conveyed our position to the U.S. side."

Singapore, due to be hit by a 20% tariff rate, said it was "very disappointed" with the U.S. proposal. In a statement, its Ministry of Energy, Trade and Industry said "Singapore's economic policies do not protect our domestic market or subsidize manufacturing for export."

Malaysia said it "does not have excess capacity in its manufacturing sectors that distorts or disrupts global markets," adding that it will continue to engage and consult with the United States.

In Thailand, where the latest U.S. proposal caps tariffs at 18%, government spokesperson Lalida Persvivatana said Thursday that the proposal was "more favorable than expected." "The government will continue to pursue negotiations to secure competitive tariff levels and additional exemptions for Thai products, with the main goal of protecting Thailand's export markets," Persvivatana added.

Analysts say the 14 economies targeted by the Trump administration together account for roughly half of the global economy. Stephen Brown, a chief economist with Capital Economics, said the latest U.S. tariffs are not as alarming as they look because they are essentially broadly replacing, rather than adding to, earlier announced U.S. tariffs related to forced labor allegations announced in July.

Brown said in a commentary that the new tariffs will still likely help push prices higher and may "keep core goods inflation firm into next year."
