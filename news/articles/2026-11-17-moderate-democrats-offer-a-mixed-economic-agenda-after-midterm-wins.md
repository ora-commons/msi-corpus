## Denver summit featured natural gas and Medicare buy-ins; Gallego sought labor policies

In a Wall Street Journal economics newsletter, Greg Ip opened by arguing that moderates delivered Democrats’ midterm majorities. He wrote that they believed they, not the progressive acolytes of Sens. Bernie Sanders and Elizabeth Warren, ought to set the party’s agenda. His analysis focused on Majority Democrats, an umbrella group that promotes centrist candidates, at its Denver summit last week.

The group’s chair, Massachusetts Rep. Jake Auchincloss, delivered an address that would warm a supply-sider’s heart, Ip wrote. Auchincloss said Democrats needed to let go of bad ideas, “most urgently the ones that ignore supply and demand. Supply and demand is a reality, and the fact is when supply goes down, prices go up.” He favored natural gas and nuclear power to lower utility prices and opposed rent control and exclusionary zoning, which he said make housing expensive.

The gathering also heard from Texas senator-elect James Talarico, who called for “universal healthcare” in his speech at the conference by allowing anyone to buy into Medicare. Pete Buttigieg, a former transportation secretary and Denver headliner, ran for president in 2020 on a similar proposal. Ip said Talarico’s plan would entail “a costly expansion of government-funded healthcare” and wrote that “It appears that anything short of Medicare-for-all now qualifies as moderate.”

Separately, Arizona Sen. Ruben Gallego, whom Ip described as a progressive turned moderate, wants sectoral wage bargaining, double pay for overtime and a “New Deal-style public jobs program.” Ip compared that agenda with former President Joe Biden, who he said governed to the left of Bill Clinton and Barack Obama but did not go as far.

Ip said these Democrats had moderated most clearly on immigration, climate and culture. He wrote that they discuss a secure border rather than transgender rights and promise to meet voters where they are. Gallego put the approach this way: “Every Latino man wants a big-ass truck.”

Ip wrote that moving toward the center on culture “seems to have eased the way to moving left on economics.”

Ip then cited Gina Hinojosa, a progressive member of the Texas Legislature who was not in Denver. The Wall Street Journal reported that Hinojosa captured the Texas governor’s mansion by avoiding cultural issues. Citing The Washington Post, the newsletter said she plans to govern as a moderate, with freezing electricity rates, a data-center moratorium and $1,500 checks to everyone.

Ip also directed readers to his column on Rahm Emanuel, the former mayor of Chicago, whom he described as a radical centrist and a possible Democratic presidential candidate.

In Ip’s account, the proposals ranged from opposition to rent control to support for Medicare buy-ins, labor bargaining and public employment. He wrote that some candidates grouped under the label held positions that would not sound moderate to the median voter.

The newsletter also reported that Democrats will name Congress’s next budget watchdog at an anxious moment for the fiscal outlook. Phillip Swagel, the director of the Congressional Budget Office, is leaving the agency when his term ends in January. Ip’s colleague Richard Rubin said Swagel has run the agency since 2019 and is returning to his pre-CBO post at the University of Maryland. Rubin said Swagel has drawn praise from lawmakers in both parties, though Republicans have criticized the CBO as too pessimistic in forecasting policy-driven economic growth. Democrats will pick the CBO head for the first time since 2009, a decision likely led by Sen. Tim Kaine (D., Va.), who is in line to become chairman of the Senate Budget Committee.

Ukrainian drone strikes have taken out a reported 30% to 59% of Russian refining capacity, according to the newsletter. Former Russian deputy finance minister Sergey Aleksashenko challenged that implication, writing: “If those figures reflected real output losses, Russia would face a catastrophic fuel shortage. Yet we do not see this.”

Aleksashenko estimated that 40% of strikes between April 1 and Sept. 25 had no impact on sales volume, while only 10% produced stoppages lasting three or more weeks. Half of Russian diesel is exported, so diesel capacity affects Russians less than gasoline, where shortages have eased since the summer. The strikes “have hurt Russia but have not yet altered its capacity to wage war,” he said.

The newsletter said young people are more likely to gamble when long-run financial goals seem out of reach. In South Korea, young people who won a lottery for the right to buy price-regulated apartments cut their use of crypto-trading apps by 21%, Yale’s Costas Arkolakis and four co-authors found in a new report. Among people who had recently lost a job, crypto trading rose by 6%. The research suggested that young people are drawn to the YOLO (“you only live once”) economy of crypto, prediction markets and meme stocks when they see no clear path to financial milestones.

The newsletter also revisited a proposal from a research group led by former Biden economic adviser Lael Brainard to offer federally insured mortgages to first-time home buyers with low $1,000 down payments. South Carolina reader Woody Rash, in a letter edited for length and clarity, asked, “What could go wrong with only a $1,000 down payment on a government-insured mortgage?” and wrote, “Has she forgotten the mortgage debacle of 2005-2007 and the subsequent near collapse of the economy known as the great financial crisis? This is a disaster waiting to re-occur.” He said he witnessed firsthand how “all the policies promoting mortgages of low quality laid low firms such as Merrill Lynch, Bear Stearns and Lehman Brothers.”
