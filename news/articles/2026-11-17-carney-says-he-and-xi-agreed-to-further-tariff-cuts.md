## Union objects as Stellantis plans Brampton plant sale to Roshel

Canada’s automobile industry has become a central test of Ottawa’s effort to weather U.S. tariffs and chart a trade path. The past year and a half has been difficult for the sector since U.S. President Donald Trump imposed a 25% tariff on non-American-made vehicles, upending decades of free trade and automotive integration with Canada. Carney, visiting China for the APEC summit, is considering other markets as a way forward and has signaled that he is looking to China as part of the solution. That avenue is not without controversy and is once again drawing the ire of those close to Trump.

While in Cambodia earlier this week, Carney told reporters that Canada could turn to European and Asian carmakers to save its auto manufacturing sector if a trade deal with the United States could not be reached.

Carney described the potential shift in terms of making use of Canadian workers, markets and assets. “Look - great auto workers, important market, real assets in Canada,” he said. “If they're not fully utilised in a North American context, that provides other opportunities.” He added: “Canada's going to move forward either way.”

Carney met Xi in China on Tuesday for their second face-to-face meeting in less than a year. Afterward, the two emphasized the importance of developing their countries’ relations, and Carney said they had agreed to further tariff cuts. Neither side mentioned vehicles specifically, though analysts suggested the issue had been prominent in their minds.

Carney said in January that opening the door to Chinese electric vehicles could result in investment in Canadian-owned companies and the creation of manufacturing jobs. U.S. tariffs on imports of Canadian-made vehicles have led to thousands of job losses in Canada and anxiety about the sector’s future, while 90% of cars made in Canada are shipped to and sold in the United States. China has also viewed Canada as a potential entry point into the North American electric-vehicle market.

Earlier this year, Canada announced that it would admit 49,000 electric vehicles from China at a low tariff rate, with plans to raise the quota gradually to 70,000 by 2030. The move represented a significant break from U.S. policy, which had heavily tariffed Chinese electric vehicles since Joe Biden’s presidency to protect domestic manufacturing. Chinese manufacturers including BYD and Chery had lobbied the Canadian government for access.

Last week, Canada’s international trade minister Maninder Sidhu toured a Chery plant in China to discuss the company’s potential entry into the Canadian market. He told reporters that Canadians could expect affordable Chinese electric vehicles for sale in Canada by early 2027.

American car manufacturer Stellantis recently announced that it would sell its assembly plant in Brampton, Ontario, where it had made models including the Dodge Challenger and Chrysler 300. Carney characterized the move as a “betrayal” of the plant’s 2,200 workers.

The Brampton facility had been idle since 2023 for a planned retooling. What was supposed to be a temporary shutdown became permanent after Trump’s tariffs, and Stellantis shifted some manufacturing to Illinois. The company said it would sell part of the Brampton plant to Roshel, a Canadian defence contractor and manufacturer of armoured vehicles.

Canada’s auto workers’ union expressed concern about the sale, called the deal “underhanded” and warned that Canada would lose thousands of high-paying jobs as a result.

CTV News, citing anonymous sources, reported that Stellantis was planning to lease back 25% of the plant to assemble “affordable” electric vehicles. The report prompted speculation that a Chinese electric-vehicle manufacturer could move into the facility. Ontario Premier Doug Ford warned Carney in a letter against opening the door for Chinese vehicle kits to be assembled at Brampton.

China overwhelmingly builds its cars at home and ships them whole or to be assembled in kits overseas. Lynette Ong, director of the China Governance Lab at the University of Toronto, said introducing Chinese auto manufacturing in Canada would be “controversial” because of fears that Canadian jobs could be lost and the North American auto manufacturing industry could be eroded.

Ong said she wants assurances that Chinese manufacturers hire Canadian workers, help train Canadian workers and transfer technology. “We want to make sure they're hiring Canadian workers, that they are helping train Canadian workers and that they are transferring the technology over,” she said. She also urged caution about potential security breaches, foreign interference from China and the risk of drawing the ire of the Trump administration.

Peter Navarro, Trump’s senior counsel for trade and manufacturing, warned Carney against drawing closer to China in a post on X. “Are u really threatening to play China off the U.S.? This is offensive in the extreme,” Navarro wrote on Monday. “You should be far more worried China is going to eat Canada alive.” U.S. ambassador to Canada Pete Hoekstra reposted his comments.

Canadians’ views of China had grown more positive since Trump returned to the White House. An Angus Reid poll released Tuesday suggested many were open to Chinese investment in the electric-vehicle sector, with additional security conditions or restrictions. The Carney government has set terms for China’s entry to Canada’s auto market, including that any venture should be jointly owned by a Canadian company, that it must follow Canadian labour standards and that the cars should be built using Canadian-made parts.

Ahead of his meeting with Xi on Tuesday, Carney also noted there are “clear guardrails” in how Canada deals with China, including barring work on sensitive matters like artificial intelligence and critical minerals.

Canada was pursuing associate member status with the European Union and had signed a free trade deal with the Philippines earlier in the week. The extent of any embrace of Chinese manufacturing still depended on the outcome of ongoing U.S.-Canada trade talks. Carney recently said the negotiations had made progress but that differences remained, and it was unclear whether Trump and Carney would speak on the sidelines of the APEC summit later in the week.
