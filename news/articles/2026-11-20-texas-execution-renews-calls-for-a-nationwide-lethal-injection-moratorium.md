## Expert questions lethal-injection methods used in Dennes case

The Texas execution of Reinaldo Dennes on Thursday night renewed calls for a nationwide moratorium that would pause all lethal injections, as advocates challenged the method and the Texas Department of Criminal Justice called the procedure successful. Reprieve chief executive Maya Foa called for the pause. Department spokeswoman Amanda Hernandez said the agency followed all proper protocols and rejected the description of the execution as botched.

Foa said: “Lethal injection is torture – and Reinaldo Dennes was not sentenced to be tortured. Even the most ardent supporters of capital punishment must recognise that the dominant method of execution in the US is broken and cannot be allowed to continue.”

Robin Maher, executive director of the Death Penalty Information Center, said: “The world saw what happened to Christa Pike, and now to Mr Dennes. The question now is, how many more of these botched executions will be tolerated by the American people?”

The Texas Department of Criminal Justice insisted that the execution went fully according to plan. Hernandez told The New York Times that “as far as this agency is concerned, it is 100 percent not a botched execution. This was a successful execution, and we did follow all of the proper protocols to do it correctly.” Maher responded: “Americans are now seeing the harsh reality of the death penalty, and I just don’t know how much longer state officials think they can double down on a story that is false.”

A photograph caption in The Guardian's report said Dennes appeared in court in August. Dennes was convicted of capital murder in the 1996 death of Janos Szucs, a diamond dealer who was shot and killed in Houston. It took 63 minutes for Dennes, 70, to be proclaimed dead at the Huntsville penitentiary, and the execution team struggled to find a vein through which a lethal dose of the sedative pentobarbital could be administered. Hood, Dennes's spiritual adviser, stood beside the gurney with a hand on Dennes's shoulder. According to Hood's account, Dennes fought the pain for up to five minutes.

Hood wrote that Dennes “shifted on the gurney, back and forth, back and forth. He contorted. He grabbed hold of the leather straps and pulled against them until his body lifted. His shoulder drove up into my palm. Then it fell away. Then it drove up again. Twice he screamed, ‘It’s not working like it’s supposed to. Fuck. Fuck. Fuck. What is going on? Fuck. Fuck. Fuck.’” Hood also reported that Dennes shouted “The pain!” and “My leg, my leg, my leg,” said his leg was “on fire,” continued to pray throughout the ordeal and begged for an end to it. His account included: “Take me home, Father. I don’t think this is normal. Take me, Father. Don’t let me suffer like this. C’mon.”

The Guardian said Dennes's execution bore similarities to the failed Tennessee execution of Christa Pike in September. The newspaper said Pike had become the only person ever to survive a full dose of execution drugs. Both executions used pentobarbital, staff struggled to establish intravenous lines in both cases, and both condemned prisoners expressed excruciating pain and burning sensations.

Texas has been under scrutiny for years over how it obtains and stores stocks of lethal execution drugs. The Guardian said the state was careful to shroud those practices in secrecy to avoid public scrutiny. After an international boycott, the Texas prison service took to buying pentobarbital from compounding pharmacies that make the chemicals to order. Lawyers for condemned prisoners have argued that execution-drug supplies can be of variable quality and are often held by the state beyond expiration dates, running the risk of causing excruciating deaths.

The way Texas conducts executions is likely to come under fresh review given the political upheaval following the midterm elections, The Guardian said. Democrat Gina Hinojosa won the governor's office, Democrats flipped 20 state House seats and won every seat up for election on the Texas Supreme Court and Court of Criminal Appeals, which have enormous power over capital cases.

Jonathan Groner, an expert on execution methods and emeritus professor of clinical surgery at Ohio State University, said the Dennes execution illustrated why lethal injections were a “medical charade.” He said: “They imitate general anesthesia which is done in hospitals across the country thousands of times a day, by people with expertise, but when it is done in the death chamber without necessary equipment or skills, people suffer.”

Groner said the locations of two intravenous lines, one in Dennes's foot and one in his shoulder, indicated that the execution team was struggling. “Placing IVs in the neck is very painful and difficult, and a last resort; and the foot is not a top choice because there is a long way to the heart for the medication to be circulated.” He added: “It’s time for the medical profession to make a stand on executions. People are being tortured. We need to stop, and take a hard look at why we are still doing this.”
