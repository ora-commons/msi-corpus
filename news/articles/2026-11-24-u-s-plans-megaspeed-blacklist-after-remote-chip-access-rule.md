## Planned Huang testimony targets China chip exports and Trump intervention

The United States plans to add Megaspeed to the trade blacklist after the Commerce Department implemented a rule last week banning remote access to advanced AI chips and servers through data centers in third countries. Megaspeed is the data-center company led by Alice Huang and has allegedly diverted Nvidia chips to China. Other Republicans have called for Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick to testify before Congress and answer allegations that he slow-walked an investigation into Megaspeed.

Republican Sen. John Kennedy of Louisiana, who also serves on the banking committee, told NBC News on Sunday that he was less interested in Huang. “I’m less interested in talking to Mr Huang. You know he did what he did. I hope he had a good time. It was the most expensive sex since Helen of Troy,” Kennedy said. He wanted Lutnick and Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent before a committee under oath. Kennedy said he would ask them not to stab each other because he realized they did not like each other, and warned that “if Secretary Lutnick is giving our chips away, he needs to stop it.” Earlier this month, Bessent said he had pushed “for months” to sanction Megaspeed, which would prevent the firm from purchasing Nvidia chips. In a CNBC interview, Bessent appeared to blame Lutnick’s department for the delay and said: “Commerce has the pen, and you will have to ask them about their internal processes.”

Huang’s December 3 Senate Banking Committee appearance is one of several appearances on Capitol Hill next week, where skeptical lawmakers from both parties will grill him over his ties to Alice Huang, a woman whom some in the intelligence community have said was a spy for China. Huang will also sit for transcribed interviews with the House Foreign Affairs Committee, the Select Committee on the Chinese Communist Party and the House Intelligence Committee. Lawmakers from both parties are seeking answers about the CEO’s reinstatement and the administration’s handling of national-security risks.

Chris Van Hollen, the Maryland Democrat vying to become the next Senate majority leader, told reporters Monday that Huang’s reinstatement was “exhibit A” of the “political corruption and rot at the heart of the Trump administration’s big tech and AI policy.” Van Hollen said his questions would focus on Huang’s “efforts to increase and accelerate the export of high-end chips to China, how he’s been accomplishing that, how he’s been getting his way with the administration.” On domestic AI development, Van Hollen said: “It’s completely hypocritical for the Trump administration, on the one hand, to say the reason we have to take all the brakes off AI development here at home is because China will leap ahead if we don’t.” He added: “When in fact, it’s the Trump administration that’s helping China leap ahead.”

Nvidia’s board reinstated Huang on November 23, 11 days after his resignation, after Donald Trump intervened on his behalf and assured company leadership that he did not pose a national-security risk. The action came despite questions over Huang’s relationship with Alice Huang, a technology executive unrelated to him who has been accused of acting as a Chinese asset and routing advanced chips to Beijing via Megaspeed. In a Monday CNBC interview, Trump defended his involvement, arguing that Nvidia would face a “bigger threat” if it lost Huang’s “brain power” in the AI race. Trump also confirmed that he had provided the board with assurances regarding any national-security concerns and said: “I spoke to them, let me put it that way. And I convinced them.”

Van Hollen pointed to Huang’s ties to Trump and the Trump administration as evidence of Huang’s influence over administration policy on advanced chips. The list included Nvidia’s $25m donation to Trump’s White House ballroom project, Huang’s personal holdings of Nvidia stock, Huang’s contribution to Trump’s inauguration fund and his attendance at a $1m-a-plate dinner at Mar-a-Lago.

Several Democrats have demanded more information about Trump’s effort to reinstate Huang. Democratic Rep. Gregory Meeks of New York, the ranking member of the House Foreign Affairs Committee, called the decision “yet another example” of how “the wealthy and well connected are held to a different standard.” Sen. Elizabeth Warren, the top Democrat on the banking committee, said the administration “needs to explain to Congress what ‘input’ was given and by which senior officials in the United States government to support his reinstatement.” Warren argued that Huang’s return “only increases the urgency of passing bipartisan export control bills into law and making sure Nvidia does not lobby to kill that legislation and put our national security at risk.”

Rep. Robert Garcia, the incoming chair of the House oversight committee, said Trump’s actions must be investigated further. “No president who has invested in a private company should be involved in their board decisions. Congress must come together in swift bipartisan action and demand answers immediately,” Garcia said. Republican Sen. John Curtis of Utah said Huang was “right to step down” when news of his extramarital affair came to light. “Just 11 days after Nvidia announced his departure, the board brought him back while its own independent investigation was still under way. Last week, the board said it didn’t intend to reinstate him. I’d like to know what changed,” Curtis said.

Some Republicans directed scrutiny toward the White House and Huang in equal measure. Republican Sen. Tom Cotton, the chair of the intelligence committee, said in a statement: “I have warned the administration from the start about Communist China obtaining our most advanced chips through both smuggling and remote access.” Cotton called the controversy involving Nvidia and Megaspeed “just the tip of the iceberg” and said it was not resolved because Huang had his job back. He said Congress had much to investigate and “we will pass overwhelmingly bipartisan chip export-control bills, including my Chip Security Act, one way or the other.” Van Hollen called it a “good sign” that some Republican members of Congress had expressed alarm over Huang’s reinstatement. “Frankly, it’s hard not to,” he said. “But given the last couple of years, when Republican senators buried their heads in the sand regardless of what the Trump administration was doing, it does seem to be a good sign that they recognize this as a threat to our national security.” Van Hollen added: “The fact that this individual was able to use the president to secure his reinstatement at Nvidia is a problem that I think Republican senators can see as well.”

Matt Pearl, a director at the Center for Strategic and International Studies who specializes in national security, geopolitics and technology, said unanswered questions surrounding Huang’s conduct remained. “We haven’t had an investigation,” Pearl said in an interview, noting that such a probe would normally take “at least a year.” Pearl argued that the Nvidia board had relied on the “representations” of senior U.S. government officials, including Trump, and was “essentially outsourcing the investigation.” He said: “I suspect that we’ll find out a lot more information in the coming weeks and months as Congress investigates, but it just seems that we have no idea what sort of the risks and threats were in this case.”

Pearl said the episode could weaken how national-security concerns are raised and acted upon inside government. “It will make it difficult for individuals in the intelligence community to give honest assessments regarding security risks if it involves highly prominent individuals like [Huang],” Pearl said. He added: “It will make it difficult for senior officials, like Secretary [Marco] Rubio, to bring those to the president, given that they will be potentially sort of disregarded and not received well. The whole apparatus of the US government in terms of detecting and responding to these kinds of threats could potentially be put at risk.”

Pearl, who previously served as director of emerging technologies at the national security council during Joe Biden’s administration, said the episode raised another concern about succession planning for senior executives forced to step aside. Reinstating Huang, “almost regardless of what comes out,” means “it’s very difficult to act on that again,” Pearl said. “It’s hard to fire someone and then shortly after hire them and then fire them again. At some point you start looking foolish.”

If elected majority leader in next week’s closed-door Democratic leadership contest, Van Hollen said AI policy should rank among the new Congress’s top priorities during its first 100 days. He backed a Senate rules reform proposal that would allow “expedited consideration” of bipartisan AI legislation emerging from committees, and supported penalties for technology companies that “develop agents that can endanger the public.” Van Hollen also called for a joint congressional committee to examine “all of the different elements of how AI will impact our society and our world.”
