## White House declined a defensive briefing for Jensen Huang

The Guardian reported that Alice Huang, head of Singapore-based Megaspeed, bought $4.8 billion worth of Nvidia's highly sensitive AI chips that were then diverted to China. Julia Curlee, a former CIA officer, said the White House was warned about an emerging and eventually clear chip diversion network under Megaspeed and other Southeast Asian offshore data farms but failed to act. The warnings reportedly reached the White House, including through a classified report that Secretary of State Marco Rubio prepared last May. Trump said he did not believe U.S. national security was threatened, and the administration did not provide Jensen Huang with a defensive briefing. Nvidia reinstated co-founder Jensen Huang as chief executive on Monday after pressure from the White House and shareholders.

The Guardian reported that intelligence information described Jensen Huang's extramarital affair with Alice Huang, who was not related to him and was described as an alleged Chinese spy. Alice Huang headed Megaspeed, the company that bought the chips, and the purchases were diverted to China despite U.S. export restrictions barring such sales to a country Washington regards as a key strategic adversary. The Guardian said the affair threatened Washington's effort to prevail over China in the global artificial intelligence race. A Guardian photograph, credited to Leah Millis/Reuters, showed Jensen Huang with Donald Trump at an 'Investing in America' event in Washington on 30 April 2025.

The Guardian reported that Trump remained inactive after Rubio, whom it described as one of the administration's “China hawks,” repackaged the intelligence into the classified report. Julia Curlee, a former CIA officer who specialized in the Middle East and China and served on the White House National Security Council during Trump's first presidency and under Joe Biden, said: “It’s clear that the White House was warned, had adequate opportunities to take action against what was an emerging and then eventually very clear chip diversion network that was emerging under Megaspeed and other Southeast Asian offshore data farms – and it just failed to act.” Curlee said: “It’s the latest episode of the president ignoring or not believing the intelligence that’s being provided to him.”

The administration's decision not to give Huang a defensive briefing drew further scrutiny. No explanation was provided for the failure to warn him, which The Guardian said allowed the relationship to continue while Huang maintained his already close access to Trump. Curlee called the choice a “policy decision [rather than] an intelligence decision” and said it was unusually permissive not to protect a U.S. citizen targeted by what she described as a Chinese spy operation.

Curlee compared the Nvidia matter to Trump's handling of the war with Iran. She said he pressed ahead with military action despite being forewarned of potential complications. The Guardian reported that Trump's decision to go to war with Iran contradicted advice that Iran's government had the resilience to withstand attempts to topple it and was likely to retaliate by closing the Strait of Hormuz — predictions that were subsequently borne out. Curlee said: “The best example of this is the run-up to the war in Iran, where the administration just truly shut out the warnings which have been there and were provided for many years about what the Iranians would do in the case of a war.” She added: “I feel pretty strongly that the fault of the Iran war lies at this refusal to listen to the intelligence community.”

The Guardian noted that Trump's dismissal of intelligence warnings in both cases recalled his comments at a 2018 summit with Vladimir Putin, when he openly sided with the Russian president over U.S. intelligence agencies' assessment that Russia interfered in the 2016 presidential election. Curlee said Trump's administration stood apart from earlier administrations, including the George W. Bush administration, which was accused of politicizing intelligence. The Guardian said that accusation was leveled at Bush following the 2003 invasion of Iraq. Curlee said the current administration went further by firing officers publicly: last year, Lt. Gen. Jeffrey Kruse, head of the Defense Intelligence Agency, was dismissed after a report contradicted Trump's claim that U.S. strikes had “obliterated” Iran's nuclear sites in June 2025.

Curlee called the chip diversion an industrial-scale breach of a technology at the heart of the U.S. strategic competition with China. She compared its significance with that of Julius and Ethel Rosenberg, a U.S. couple executed after their conviction for passing atomic secrets to the Soviet Union in the early years of the Cold War. Curlee said official indifference to the discovery was therefore remarkable. She said the harm could not be undone, but the network's discovery and steps to stop it had occurred at least over a year earlier, leaving the intelligence community unable to act if a policymaker refused.

Fulton Armstrong, a former CIA analyst specializing in Latin America with experience in Taiwan, said that before public accusations and scandals, it had been accepted practice to pull an American target aside for a “man-to-man chat – to tell him to think with the head between his shoulders.” He said: “The chat might even include discussion of feeding the foreigner some disinformation.” Armstrong added: “It seems that four years of Trump first presidency, four years of do-nothingism with Biden, and now almost two years of Trump II have so deeply changed the intel community that practical solutions are no longer allowed.”

Speculation has swirled over whether Chinese intelligence ran a “honey trap” to lure Jensen Huang into a close business relationship that would lead to large Nvidia chip purchases for diversion to China. China's Ministry of State Security is believed to use the technique, though most Western intelligence agencies publicly insist they do not. They describe it as unethical and potentially counterproductive and say the technique often depends on blackmail.

Analysts doubt a straightforward honey trap occurred in this case. One theory is that Alice Huang disclosed the relationship to Chinese agents after it began because she was legally obliged under China's catch-all intelligence law to “cooperate” with security services on matters defined as “national security.” Chinese law uses a broader definition than in most Western countries and can cover commercial and economic development issues.

Frank Montoya, a retired FBI agent who specialized on China, said a straightforward honey trap was unlikely. He described a confluence of opportunities in which Alice Huang was ambitious and smart, had connections in the Chinese Communist Party and spoke with people who then passed the opportunity to the Ministry of State Security. Montoya said: “There’s no way the MSS alone can fund that.” He said someone higher in the party had to approve the multibillion-dollar purchase and provide the money. He described the operation as “whole of government” and lacking the “clandestinity” of traditional spy craft.

Curlee argued that Megaspeed's mass purchase of Nvidia chips and its rental of services from Singapore data centers to Chinese AI companies would draw U.S. intelligence attention. The Guardian reported that the relationship and links to Chinese intelligence were thought to have been discovered through phone surveillance, almost certainly carried out by the National Security Agency.

Trump dismissed the case's importance, saying he did not believe U.S. national security was threatened. He told Fox News: “We spy on China. They spy on us.” He later told reporters that he raised the matter only briefly when he met China's president, Xi Jinping, at last week's Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation gathering in Shenzhen.

Curlee said Trump's response showed disdain for intelligence work that requires tens of billions of dollars and risks lives to give leaders an advantage before crises. She asked: “If the president can’t be bothered to listen to his intelligence community then what is what is the point of it all?”
