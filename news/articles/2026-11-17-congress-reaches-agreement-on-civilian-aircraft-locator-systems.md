## Congress reaches agreement on civilian aircraft locator systems

**Subtype:** fact

- Congress agreed to require civilian aircraft to carry ADS-B In systems that help pilots locate nearby traffic.
- Lawmakers still must resolve the locator-system requirements that apply to military aircraft.
- The Army operated a Black Hawk helicopter without ADS-B Out during the fatal training flight because of security concerns.
- An American Airlines flight and an Army Black Hawk helicopter collided near Washington, D.C., on Jan. 29, 2025, killing 67 people.
- Lawmakers are crafting a compromise aviation-safety bill to address 50 National Transportation Safety Board recommendations.

## Military aircraft remain under unresolved requirements in the agreement

Congress agreed Tuesday to require airliners and other civilian aircraft to carry systems that help pilots locate nearby aircraft. The agreement does not yet resolve the provisions that apply to military aircraft. Under existing rules, aircraft flying in crowded airspace must carry systems that broadcast their location, but they are not required to carry complementary ADS-B In systems that show pilots where nearby aircraft are flying.

Senators Ted Cruz, a Texas Republican, and Maria Cantwell, a Democrat representing Washington state, announced the agreement at a demonstration on the lawn outside the Capitol building. A group representing families of the 67 people killed in the Jan. 29, 2025, collision organized the event. Cantwell said, “When military aircraft fly in the same airspace, they must meet the same standards as everyone else.”

The Army Black Hawk flew without the key ADS-B Out system on the night of the collision because of security concerns, even though it was only on a training flight. ADS-B Out broadcasts an aircraft’s location. An American Airlines jet flying from Wichita, Kansas, collided with the helicopter near Washington, D.C.

Everyone aboard the American Airlines jet and the helicopter died. The crash killed 67 people and was the deadliest plane crash on U.S. soil since 2001. The victims included 28 members of the figure skating community.

Capt. Jason Ambrosi, who leads the Air Line Pilots Association union, said Congress must require the locator systems on military and civilian aircraft because the Federal Aviation Administration had not required them. “Pilots can’t avoid what we can’t see,” Ambrosi said. “Everyone in shared airspace must be seen. Period.”

The Senate and House each passed competing aviation-safety reform bills, and lawmakers have worked since the spring to craft a compromise addressing 50 recommendations from the National Transportation Safety Board. The victims’ families and NTSB Chairwoman Jennifer Homendy have been pressing for the locator systems. The agreement announced Tuesday leaves unresolved the provisions that apply to military aircraft.

FAA Administrator Bryan Bedford assured the victims’ families that safety remains the agency’s top priority. The FAA has been in the spotlight since grounding all flights nationwide Friday because of a technical problem.

The FAA has already taken some steps to prevent helicopters and planes from sharing the same airspace around Reagan airport. Air traffic controllers are now prohibited from relying solely on pilots to see and avoid other aircraft.

For civilian aircraft, the agreement would require the complementary ADS-B In system that shows pilots where nearby aircraft are flying, rather than requiring only that aircraft broadcast its own location. The military provisions remain unresolved. Cruz said he believes Congress can pass the bill before the end of the year.
