## Leo calls for canonical rules to address spiritual abuse

Pope Leo XIV said Monday aboard the papal plane returning to Rome from Peru that his recent lung cancer surgery has not altered his agenda, outlook or sense of mortality. He projected a long pontificate focused on unity and healing within the Catholic Church. The flight capped a 12-day trip through three South American countries that left several members of the traveling Vatican delegation and press corps sick.

During the airborne news conference, Leo seemed full of health, energy and jokes. He said, “I’m not 100% but I’m pretty good,” and added, “Endurance is one of the gifts the Lord gave me.” Leo, 71, had the lower lobe of his right lung removed Oct. 13 because of a cancerous tumor.

The tumor was discovered by chance during an annual checkup and first appeared as a shadow on his lung. “The first news was a surprise. There were no symptoms. I felt fine,” Leo said. Once the cancer was confirmed, he decided to proceed immediately with its removal, saying he hoped his experience would help others not fear checkups or pursue preventive care.

Before surgery, Leo said he saw no need for a provisional letter of resignation setting out criteria for stepping down if he became medically impaired and unable to govern. His predecessors had left such instructions, and some legal experts believe they are necessary if a pope enters an irreversible coma and cannot articulate a resignation. “Eventually I will do it, I think, but I don’t see the need now,” Leo said. He said he never doubted he would recover well enough to make the South America trip, crediting good doctors, prayer and what he described as a strong determination to recover.

Leo also showed no haste to appoint new cardinals, who will eventually elect his successor. “I will eventually get around to that,” he said. He remained unsure which trips he would take next year, apart from a previously confirmed journey to South Korea to attend World Youth Day in August.

Leo made unity and healing his first priority amid longstanding polarization within the church that, in some ways, deepened during Pope Francis’s pontificate. Asked whether he would consider ordaining married men to ease the priest shortage in the Amazon, where remote Indigenous communities can go months or longer without a priest to celebrate the sacraments, Leo said the issue was too divisive. Amazonian bishops had asked Francis to allow the change, but he had declined.

Although ordaining married men is one solution to the priest shortage, Leo said “it’s a solution which in certain areas of the church can cause division because there is not universal, if you will, acceptance.” “One of my priorities is first to promote unity and communion among us as members in the church,” he said. He allowed: “It may be in the future that some things will change. However for right now, for what I believe, an important part of my ministry of the church is to heal, bring people together.”

Leo likewise said he had no plans to change Pope Francis’s decision restricting celebration of the old Latin Mass. Traditionalist Catholics had hoped he would be more sympathetic and reverse the restriction, but he said Francis’s decision already allowed exceptions and that the decision stands.

Leo said he met abuse survivors in Argentina and Peru and recognized “spiritual abuse” as a church problem that needed to be addressed. For over two years, a Vatican working group has studied “spiritual abuse,” given the increasing number of cases of people — often adult women — who accuse priests of using their spiritual authority to psychologically, physically or sexually abuse them. The Catholic Church has laws punishing priests who sexually abuse minors, but priests who use spiritual authority to take advantage of people under their care rarely face sanctions because there are no explicit laws criminalizing the practice.

Leo, a canon lawyer, said he was aware of the loophole in church law. “Spiritual abuse is a very serious topic in the church,” he said, adding that it needed to be addressed canonically. “In order to exercise justice, you have to have a codification or a juridical expression of how to apply that, and that’s a very vague concept still,” he said. Leo conceded that “we all probably could describe either specific cases or kinds of instances where that has happened.”

One recent case involving alleged spiritual abuse was expected to reach Leo’s desk soon. A Vatican-appointed tribunal was deciding the fate of the Rev. Marko Rupnik, a once-famous Jesuit mosaic artist accused by two dozen women of spiritual, psychological and sexual abuse. The women began complaining about his behavior in the 1990s, but nothing was done until they went public, amid suspicions that he had been protected by Jesuit superiors, up to and including Francis.
