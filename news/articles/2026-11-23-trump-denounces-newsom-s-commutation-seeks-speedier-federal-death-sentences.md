## Directive would cover child murders and killings by rapists and human traffickers

President Donald Trump on Monday denounced California Gov. Gavin Newsom's commutation of death sentences for more than 500 inmates, saying the state's action had taken away the only closure families of murder victims might ever have. Speaking in the Justice Department's Great Hall, Trump said he would sign a directive instructing the department to expedite federal death sentences and to seek the death penalty for murders of children as well as rapists and human traffickers who kill their victims. Newsom responded in a video that the commuted inmates will die in prison.

The commutation was the largest single one in U.S. history, The Wall Street Journal reported. It came in a state that has not carried out an execution in more than 20 years; Newsom placed a moratorium on death sentences shortly after taking office in 2019.

Trump said the directive would instruct the Justice Department to expedite federal death sentences and to seek capital punishment in murders of children and in killings by rapists and human traffickers. "Once jurors have spoken, capital punishment will be swift and certain," he said.

Trump used much of the speech to address relatives of murder victims, wearing a purple tie and flanked by family members of violent-crime victims. "Those families' lives will never ever be the same," Trump said. "Then, after the jurors delivered their verdicts and the justice system returned a much-deserved sentence of death, Newsom, a terrible governor in so many other ways, came along and stole away the only closure those families might ever have." Trump also offered his sympathies to families of victims who, he said, "were murdered by Newsom's monsters."

Newsom answered Trump in a video, saying the prisoners whose sentences he commuted will die in prison. "No one walks out," Newsom said. He criticized the pardons Trump has handed out and pointed to the prosecutions the Justice Department has sought against some of the president's perceived foes. "Donald Trump doesn't oppose mercy, he sells it. If you're poor, Black or brown, you get the needle. If you're rich and know Donald Trump, you get a pardon and a personal phone call," Newsom said.

Newsom, who is leaving office in January because of term limits, has been mentioned as a possible Democratic presidential candidate for 2028. He has been one of his party's most vocal critics of Trump, borrowing from the president's social-media tactics to position himself as a prominent foil to the White House.

Administration officials who attended included Attorney General Todd Blanche, Federal Bureau of Investigation director Kash Patel, Jeanine Pirro, the top federal prosecutor in Washington, and Associate Attorney General Stanley Woodward, the Justice Department's third-in-command. It was Trump's second speech in the Great Hall since the start of his second term. In March 2025, shortly after being sworn in, he used an address there to attack the agency after it had brought two criminal cases against him. After he left the stage Monday, the song "YMCA" played over the hall's speakers.

The speech came as the administration has worked to expand federal capital punishment. Trump rolled back many of the restrictions the Biden administration placed on the death penalty at the federal level shortly after taking office, restoring the use of lethal injection and approving firing squads. Even so, he reversed a decision by Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth earlier this year to livestream the execution of the Fort Hood shooter.

Attorney General Todd Blanche said the Justice Department would continue to seek the death penalty wherever appropriate. "That is what we have done since January 20th, and what we will continue to do from this day going forward," Blanche said.

Through tears, Sandy Friend, the mother of Michael Lyons, an 8-year-old boy murdered in 1996, condemned Newsom for erasing "with a swipe of the pen" the death sentence of her son's killer. Trump put his arm around her shoulder as she struggled through her remarks. "This is so very hard," she said.

Less than half of adult Americans are in favor of the death penalty, according to recent Gallup polling. Opponents of capital punishment have failed to remove it from the books in California, where voters rejected two state ballot referendums in 2012 and 2016 to abolish the death penalty.
