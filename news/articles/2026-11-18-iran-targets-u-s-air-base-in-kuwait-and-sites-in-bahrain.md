## Citrinowicz says Iran appears to pursue deliberate, calibrated escalation

Iran fired on the Ali Al Salem air base in Kuwait and on sites in Bahrain early Wednesday, widening attacks on Gulf neighbors that host U.S. facilities in what appeared to be retaliation for American strikes the day before. The early-morning attacks came after a U.S. fighter jet struck a Revolutionary Guard missile launch site on Qeshm Island in the Strait of Hormuz, a strike the United States made in retaliation for an Iranian attack on an American radar installation in Bahrain earlier Tuesday. Kuwait's military said its air defenses intercepted drone attacks, and Bahrain's military said it intercepted a number of attacks from Iran.

The round came after about two months without such attacks, and Iran has repeatedly targeted U.S. facilities in Bahrain in recent days. The small Persian Gulf island kingdom hosts the U.S. Navy's 5th Fleet. Iran said its recent attacks, including those on other U.S. facilities in Bahrain on Sunday and Friday, were in response to U.S. drone surveillance operations and an attack on Iranian communications towers.

Iran's state broadcaster reported that the Iranian military fired at air defense systems and drone and missile launch platforms at Ali Al Salem in the early Wednesday attack. Kuwait's military said its air defenses intercepted drone attacks and did not immediately report any damage. Kuwait's Foreign Ministry condemned the repeated Iranian attacks as "criminal," saying they threatened the country's security and stability.

Bahrain's Interior Ministry issued two alerts early Wednesday urging people to take shelter, followed by the "all clear" a short time later. The country's military later said it had intercepted a number of attacks from Iran but gave no information on damage or casualties. The U.S. military did not immediately respond to a request for comment on the latest attacks.

The Associated Press, using satellite imagery from multiple providers, found that Iran's strikes on more than two dozen U.S. bases in the Middle East focused on early-warning systems before hitting aircraft and buildings, causing billions of dollars in equipment damage. The AP also spoke to four regional officials who were briefed or had firsthand knowledge of what was hit, and found that through August, more than 115 drone or missile strikes claimed by Iran or Iranian-backed militias damaged or destroyed structures on 26 military bases and other sites with U.S. equipment.

Within the war, Ali Al Salem Air Base has been struck at least 30 times by Iran, damaging or destroying at least 15 aircraft hangars, numerous warehouses and 10 buildings near a residential area, according to an AP analysis. Under questioning before a U.S. Senate committee on Tuesday about damage to the Navy's regional headquarters in Bahrain, Acting Navy Secretary Hung Cao said he could not offer a full damage assessment there because "it's a nonpermissive area for us to go." He told a reporter months ago that Iran "blew the hell out of Bahrain."

The current round comes in a war that began when Israel and the United States attacked Iran on Feb. 28, now nearly nine months old. Since then Iran has regularly fired on American facilities and interests in neighboring Gulf countries, as well as energy infrastructure and other targets.

Beyond the military sites, Iran has continued to attack tankers as they attempt to transit the Strait of Hormuz, where American military escorts have helped restore the passage of oil tankers from the Gulf. With Iran's economy suffering under a U.S. naval blockade, experts say the recent attacks suggest Tehran may be growing weary of the status quo. The United Arab Emirates state-owned oil company ADNOC said Wednesday that one of its tanker crew members was missing after an attack in the strait on Tuesday, while 11 others were injured and were receiving medical care.

Two analyses posted on X describe the pattern in the Gulf. Hamidreza Azizi, a senior Iran analyst at the International Crisis Group think tank, wrote after the first reports of the attack on Kuwait: "Even if neither side intends to enter a broader confrontation, the accumulation of military incidents across multiple fronts increases the risk of miscalculation and escalation beyond what either side originally intended."

Danny Citrinowicz, a researcher at Israel's Institute for National Security Studies and a former Iran specialist in Israel's military and intelligence services, described an "increasingly clear" pattern of attacks by Iran on shipping and U.S. facilities in the Gulf region, and wrote that "Iran appears to be pursuing a strategy of deliberate, calibrated escalation." He said the objective is not necessarily to trigger an all-out war, but to steadily increase the military, economic and political costs of maintaining the blockade. Citrinowicz said Washington faces a dilemma in which a limited response may encourage Iran to push further, while a stronger response risks accelerating the escalation the United States is trying to avoid. "As long as the U.S. naval blockade remains in place and no agreement is reached, further escalation will become increasingly difficult to avoid," he said. "There are two clear paths to de-escalation: a negotiated agreement or a U.S. decision to lift the blockade."

The tit-for-tat attacks come as Adm. Brad Cooper, the commander of the U.S. military's Central Command, met Wednesday in Tel Aviv with Israel's military chief of staff, Eyal Zamir, and Roman Gofman, the director of Israel's intelligence agency, Mossad. The exchanges complicate negotiations for a diplomatic solution and raise the risk of a resumption of all-out conflict.

U.S. President Donald Trump has sent mixed messages on his next steps, saying new strikes are highly unlikely while also sending a third aircraft carrier and thousands more troops to the Middle East.

A separate front could keep the conflict running. Saudi officials have briefed allies that the conflict with the Iran-backed Houthis could last for another six months, according to a diplomat familiar with the matter who was not authorized to comment publicly and spoke on the condition of anonymity. The diplomat said the kingdom, in diplomatic talks, is making the case that the military alliance working to restore the internationally recognized government in Yemen must further set back the Houthis before it can begin credible talks with the rebel group.

The Iranian-backed Houthis control a significant swath of Yemen, including the capital of Sanaa, and have made territorial gains along the Red Sea coast. They have also rattled the kingdom with missile and drone attacks targeting Saudi infrastructure, including King Khalid International Airport in Riyadh and energy infrastructure. The kingdom continues to make the case to allies that U.S. military support could help change the course of the war in the Saudi-led coalition's favor, the diplomat said, but Trump has resisted the push from his ally for U.S. strikes on the Houthis.

The Associated Press's David Rising reported from Bangkok and Aamer Madhani from Washington, with Samy Magdy also reporting.
