## Plume could carry toxic lead dust over eastern Iowa and into Illinois

The U.S. Army delayed to Monday the detonation of five tonnes of degraded explosive material at the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant in Middletown, Iowa, after two environmental groups sued on Thursday to block the operation. Acting Secretary of the Army Adam Telle, who announced the postponement Thursday evening, said "public safety is the Army's top priority." The detonation was originally scheduled for noon local time (1800 BST) on Friday at the plant, in a community of about 360 people about four hours west of Chicago, Illinois. The plant produces medium- and large-calibre munitions for the U.S. Department of Defense.

According to his post on X, Telle said the new timing was chosen because it would be "the latest possible moment prior to a forecasted temperature drop, which would risk further destabilization of the material." The Army said the detonation was part of "a planned and controlled safety operation" to dispose of degraded lead azide.

The U.S. Environmental Protection Agency permitted the operation under conservation and recovery laws and granted an emergency permit to detonate 68 drums of the material. The EPA said it did so because the explosion risk "presented an imminent and substantial threat to human health and the environment."

The Army said the two controlled detonations would result in an air plume that could send toxic dust, containing lead particles, over eastern Iowa and into neighbouring Illinois, with the potential for contaminants to settle on farmland, playgrounds and other surfaces. The Army also said the two controlled explosions would be powerful enough to damage buildings and shatter windows nearby.

Middletown residents were asked to leave their homes for about six hours, from 08:00 to 14:00 local time, while the operation takes place and the dust settles. The Army advised "all humans" to avoid outdoor activities within affected areas. An advisory said residents would hear a loud noise, feel vibrations from two consecutive, rapid detonations and see a plume of dust.

According to CBS News, the BBC's US media partner, two environmental groups sued Thursday to block the detonation and challenged the validity of the EPA's emergency permit. The groups argued that the Army had failed to consider safer alternatives or provide adequate notice. Michael Schmidt, general counsel at the Iowa Environmental Council, said "The EPA itself admits that there is no safe level of lead exposure for humans." He said: "When our federal government makes decisions that result in a potentially toxic plume of lead dust that could cover much of southeastern Iowa, Iowans deserve to know that every available alternative was considered before choosing this method of disposal." No court ruling on the challenge had been reported, and the detonation remained set for Monday.

According to Iowa Public Radio, the plant found earlier this year that lead azide stored in underground concrete tanks was degrading and had become too unstable to move safely. Lead azide is a highly volatile compound used as a detonator. The Army said detonating the unstable material in place was the safest disposal option, while military and environmental officials determined that detonating it in place was the safest way to eliminate the explosive safety hazard.

Justine Barati, a public affairs specialist at Army Sustainment Command, said the substance had been stored at the plant for more than 20 years in earthen-covered containers. It sits in a bath of ethanol and water to keep the material stable, but a routine check earlier this year found that some of the water had evaporated, Barati told the Associated Press.

The Army said the lead azide barrels would be detonated inside earth-covered magazines made of concrete and surrounded with barriers full of sand. It said the soil above would be saturated with water in an effort to reduce lead in the air.
