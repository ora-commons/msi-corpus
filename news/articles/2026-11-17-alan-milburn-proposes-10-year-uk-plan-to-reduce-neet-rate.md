## Annual investment would reach £5.8bn by 2036-37, offset by savings and tax receipts

Five months after an earlier report detailed the scale of the problem, Alan Milburn has set out recommendations for a 10-year overhaul of policy for UK young people who are not in education, employment or training. The former minister's report spans more than 278 pages and covers employment regulation, education, health care and benefits. It presents recommendations rather than an enacted government package. In related coverage linked by The Guardian, Milburn urged Burnham to spend billions on young people.

Milburn argues that the issue reflects structural factors rather than a failure among younger people. The report says: “The young people I have met over the last year are not asking for an easier life. They are asking for a fair chance to build one,” Milburn writes. He says the response must be transformational rather than another initiative or round of incremental reform.

At the center of the proposed system is a youth opportunity service, or YOS, that would provide training and health support while expecting young people to participate. The report says about 13.5% of people aged 16 to 24 in the UK are classified as Neet, the highest proportion in Europe outside Romania. It notes that the rate has remained above 10% for most of the past 25 years.

The decade-long aim is to match countries including the Netherlands and Denmark. Milburn calls the eventual goal “Neet zero,” which he says means a consistent rate of about 5%. Separately, the report says its proposed policies would reduce the rate to about 6.8% by 2036.

Milburn notes that youth employment has declined even as overall employment has risen. He identifies limited access to secure work experience and first jobs, the cost of taking on staff and regulatory complexities as barriers affecting young people and employers. The report also says government support has been inadequate, citing spending on all-age hiring incentives and subsidies of about 1% of GDP in Denmark, compared with about 0.1% in the UK.

Among its employment recommendations, the report calls for doubling the salary threshold for employer national insurance for entry-level jobs for young people in retail and hospitality, possibly then expanded to other sectors. It would also extend the existing youth jobs grant, which provides up to £3,000 to employers taking on someone receiving universal credit for at least six months, to people who are not receiving benefits. The report proposes additional incentives for small employers and people facing barriers such as disabilities or being care leavers.

Milburn says moves that would bring young people's minimum wages closer to the adult rate should be judged “against the critical goal of protecting youth employment.” He also proposes a single digital gateway for company support schemes and clearer assistance for workers with disabilities. Under a proposed youth opportunity test, future tax or regulatory changes would be assessed for their effect on young workers, with the test set out in law.

The report treats Neet status as a long-term issue, saying indicators such as low attainment and poor attendance are clear during childhood. Milburn says countries with low Neet rates take vocational skills seriously, while in England “technical education has too often been regarded as for somebody else’s child.” His recommendations include more investment in early years provision, including family hubs, and individual support at school for students at “material risk of disengagement.” The report also calls for less use of school exclusion where possible, greater attention to preparedness for work alongside exams, more investment in further education and apprenticeships, and stronger financial incentives for employers that hire apprentices. It says the Netherlands' permanent exclusion rate is 73% lower than England's. University courses would also have to demonstrate clear value for learners, employers and the wider economy, with funding withdrawn from poorly performing examples.

Health is also central to the report's analysis. More than half of young people classified as Neet report a health condition, according to the document. Milburn attributes part of the problem to a health service “designed to diagnose and treat illness” rather than intervening earlier and helping people move toward work. He says diagnoses and the fit-note system can create a “binary” mindset.

The report recommends quicker access to physical and mental health assistance through YOS advisers before a formal diagnosis, with support potentially including talking therapies. It also proposes moving responsibility for fit notes away from GPs and toward occupational therapists or non-clinical employment advisers.

Milburn's report is highly critical of a benefits system it says can trap young people in dependence. It says: “It asks a young person what they cannot do before it asks them what, with the right support, they could. The welfare system for too many young people has become a trap when it ought to be a springboard.” The report argues that cutting benefit rates or entitlements crudely does not work, “fiscally nor morally.” It says: “If they leave young people destitute, then costs merely show up somewhere else on the state’s balance sheet in health, housing or criminal justice. Spending less is not the same as succeeding. The test is whether lives and trajectories change.”

The proposed opportunity guarantee would give every person aged 18 to 24 a place in education, training or work, while a support guarantee would cover the assistance needed to take it up. The report says the guarantees must be in place, backed by significant investment, before the wider changes begin. Options include a government-subsidized job, with employers receiving £3,000 for each young person they recruit and potentially more for smaller businesses or when the employee has additional needs; an FE place, or technical or university learning as appropriate; an apprenticeship; a work placement of up to 12 weeks; structured volunteering such as conservation, community gardening or a cadetship; and help establishing a business. The report's backstop would be a state-financed six-month job with an employer.

Other recommendations include a youth allowance set at the under-25 rate of universal credit and a health and disability fund operated by the local YOS to help with extra costs, which are or might be time-limited. The report also proposes an additional cost allowance replacing the personal independence payment for young people when their disability-related extra costs are “significant, established and ongoing.” Eligibility would more closely target people with demonstrable extra costs for functional needs that are long-term and enduring, with the aim of enabling independence and participation. The report also proposes a “severe and complex needs insurance,” a more generous long-term award without a qualifying period, for people with the most profound disabilities when it is clear they are unlikely ever to be able to work.

Participation would carry financial consequences. Young people could keep more earnings before benefits are reduced and receive extra money for undertaking civic service. Someone who persistently refused to engage would lose the youth allowance, but only after multiple attempts to re-engage and understand what had gone wrong.

The fiscal plan would begin with £500 million of investment in 2027-28, taken from existing spending, before ramping up. By 2036-37, annual investment would reach about £5.8bn to cover the guarantees, hiring incentives and changes to health, education and skills. The report projects that £3.6bn in savings and £2.4bn in increased tax receipts would offset that spending. Milburn says investment must precede savings over a longer period, rather than beginning with reduced benefit payments during the current parliament. “This is a 10-year plan, not a one-year sprint … Short-termism has been a constant feature of our country’s approach to this issue over the last 50 years,” he writes.
