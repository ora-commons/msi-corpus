## Lafortune says Ottawa is on track for NATO's 5% target

The U.S. Defense Department on Monday accused Canada of falling short as a credible partner in defending North America, saying Ottawa had failed to make the decisions needed to modernize its military and meet NATO commitments. In an unusually blunt fact sheet, the department said Canada lacked a credible, fully costed plan to reach NATO's 5% defense-spending target. The Pentagon also criticized Canada's review of a planned purchase of 88 American-made F-35 fighter jets.

The fact sheet said, "Canada's defense shortfalls speak for themselves." It added, "Canada must get serious and focused." The Pentagon said U.S. Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth was "laser focused" on modernizing Canada's military after decades of American taxpayers footing the bill for its defense.

Simon Lafortune, a spokesperson for Canada's defense minister, answered the criticism by saying Canada had committed 81.8 billion Canadian dollars ($56.9 billion) over five years to rebuild its armed forces and had reached NATO's 2% defense-spending benchmark five years ahead of schedule. Lafortune also said, "Canada is also on track to meet NATO's new commitment to invest 5% of GDP annually in defense by 2035 and is taking concrete steps to build the capabilities needed to meet that goal."

Canada reached NATO's previous target of spending 2% of gross domestic product on defense and joined allies pledging to reach 5% by 2035, including related security investments. Its latest budget projects combined defense and security spending of 4.1% of GDP by 2027-28, but only 2.6% of GDP would go toward core defense. The Pentagon said Ottawa was postponing the largest spending increases until after the 2029 federal election, while Canada's parliamentary budget watchdog also questioned how the government would meet the target.

The criticism followed a classified directive from Hegseth that Republican U.S. Sen. Mike Rounds said largely restricted U.S. military officials' participation in Canadian think tank and social events. President Donald Trump's trade war and threats to make Canada the 51st state had also battered relations between the two countries.

Wesley Wark, a Canadian national security expert and former government adviser, titled his Monday newsletter "Pentagon Pete strikes again" and called the criticism "insulting and infuriating." Wark suggested Washington was discounting Canada's NATO deployment in Latvia and Canada's support for Ukraine.

In May, Pentagon policy chief Elbridge Colby paused a U.S.-Canada defense advisory board established in 1940, accusing Ottawa of failing to make credible progress on its military commitments.

The Pentagon said only 41.9% of Canada's air force fleet and 53.9% of its naval fleet were serviceable in 2025-26, while army readiness stood at 48%.

The Pentagon warned that Canada's review of its planned purchase of 88 American-made F-35s could worsen personnel shortages if the country adopted a mixed fighter fleet. The department accused Canadian leaders of putting domestic economic interests ahead of military effectiveness, creating uncertainty for the North American Aerospace Defense Command, which Canada and the United States operate jointly. Prime Minister Mark Carney put the F-35 purchase under review as Trump threatened Canadian sovereignty and imposed tariffs. Canada has examined Swedish-made Gripen fighters, which could bring manufacturing jobs.

Carney has acknowledged readiness problems, saying only one of Canada's four submarines was seaworthy. His government plans to buy up to 12 replacements. In June, Carney said, "We should no longer send three-quarters of our defense capital spending to America."

Other NATO allies also remained short of the 5% target, but on Sunday Colby praised Germany's military buildup as a "tectonic shift" and called Poland a "true model ally."

The Trump administration had already barred Pentagon officials from attending the Halifax International Security Forum under restrictions imposed in 2025. The forum's president, Peter Van Praagh, previously served as a senior adviser to a Conservative government in Canada. Before the Nov. 3 midterm elections, Van Praagh warned that Trump might undermine the results if Republicans suffered major losses. If those efforts succeeded, he said, it would mean "the end of the United States as we know it."

At the forum on Saturday, Rounds said Hegseth's directive was not expected to affect joint military operations or intelligence sharing.
