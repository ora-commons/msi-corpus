## Democrats broaden abortion messaging as 2028 contenders test new appeals

The abortion fight is moving from the 2026 election results into the 2028 presidential primary, with both parties testing how to talk about the issue. Shefali Luthra and Grace Panetta of The 19th reported that Republicans are publicly moving away from national abortion bans, while Democrats are broadening their message after gains in Congress and state government. The change has prompted the anti-abortion movement to assess the limits of its alliance with the GOP and debate whether its work belongs in Congress or in state and local politics.

Abortion was not the top issue in a midterm cycle dominated by concerns about the economy, but Molly Murphy, a longtime pollster and president of Impact Research, said the organization’s research found abortion was a factor for more than half of voters in battleground congressional districts and in Maine’s Senate race. Murphy said, “So if it mattered in 2026, it matters more when they’re making decisions about who they want to be the president.” If Democrats win the presidency and control of Congress, they could have enough votes to pass a federal law enshrining abortion rights, a top priority party leaders have cited since the fall of Roe v. Wade.

Polling cited in the report pointed in the same direction. The 19th and YouGov found that a large majority of voters want abortion to be legal, while Impact Research, a Democrat-aligned firm, found that most voters in battleground states wanted lawmakers to work for federal protections. Anti-abortion ballot measures repeatedly failed to attract majority support, including in more conservative states such as Ohio, Kansas and Missouri.

The report found that the national ban many anti-abortion groups support was unpopular enough to dissuade many front-runners from endorsing major abortion restrictions. Anti-abortion strategists and policy analysts said the GOP, which has for decades backed abortion restrictions, is publicly shifting away from the national bans its members supported just a few years ago. The report compared that shift with President Donald Trump’s approach: in 2024 he said abortion should be left up to individual states, and he has frustrated anti-abortion activists by ignoring many of their requests for federal limits on abortion medication.

The shift is part of a larger Republican effort to find a message that may appeal to more voters than enacting a strict national ban. Patrick Brown, a scholar at the Ethics and Public Policy Center, a conservative think tank, said, “What we’re seeing is Republicans coming to some kind of grip with reality, when it comes to, OK, you can’t be a total no exceptions — you can’t be a hardliner and hope to have a future at the national level.” Democratic pollster and strategist Celinda Lake said, “They know they’re in a losing position, and they are trying to figure out — how do they waffle?”

Florida Gov. Ron DeSantis, who is reportedly exploring a presidential bid and helped enact a six-week abortion ban in his home state, said the national anti-abortion movement had been caught “flat-footed.” He suggested that an effective conservative message would approach the issue differently based on an individual state’s politics, saying, “Mississippi can have a different message than New Hampshire.” In remarks days after the midterm elections, DeSantis said, “I don’t really see much of a message at all on this,” and added, “I think the issue has lost a lot of salience, obviously partially because the left has won in most of these states at this point and the pro-life movement really hasn’t done much to fight that.”

It was not clear that avoiding abortion would prove successful for Republicans. Vice President JD Vance has spoken frequently about his opposition to abortion and was viewed by abortion opponents as a key White House ally and possible champion if elected president. Since the GOP’s midterm losses, Vance has not indicated how he would approach abortion if made the Republican nominee. His post-election remarks have focused instead on immigration policy and the ongoing military conflict in Iran. Secretary of State Marco Rubio, also viewed as a potential 2028 contender, backed national abortion restrictions as a Florida senator. In 2022, months after the fall of Roe v. Wade, he told a reporter that he supported a ban in Florida but believed the broader issue should be left to individual states.

Democrats won back both chambers of Congress and made large gains at the state level, flipping control of many governorships and state legislative chambers now responsible for setting abortion policy. The major national anti-abortion group Susan B. Anthony Pro-Life America blamed Republican leaders and consultants for shying away from the issue and depressing a key segment of the Republican base. In a post-election memo, the organization said, “The only tangible results of this strategy were turnout problems, missed persuasion opportunities, and hundreds of thousands of dead babies in the strongest pro-life states.”

Strategists, activists and analysts agreed that the Republican presidential primary represented an existential question for the future of the anti-abortion movement. Andrew Shirvell, founder and executive director at Florida Voice for the Unborn, said the anti-abortion movement as a whole has “soul-searching” to do. He argued that national anti-abortion groups had been overly focused on Congress and on trying to get an abortion ban passed at the federal level, which he said was infeasible. “The same movement leaders go back to the same nonsense,” Shirvell said. “I’m telling you this straight up. We’re never going to get a national abortion ban passed through Congress, no matter who the president is.”

Shirvell wants 2028 GOP presidential candidates who will be “a leader for a culture of life” and can restore morale among the grassroots base of the anti-abortion movement. He wants a candidate who will use the presidency’s bully pulpit to articulate the pro-life cause and motivate the movement’s difficult work at the state and local level, where he expects the movement to focus for the next 10 to 15 years. Brown concluded, “The pro-life movement is not in a good place, and I think we all know that.”

Kamala Harris’s unsuccessful 2024 presidential bid was defined by her embrace of abortion rights as “freedom,” but the 2028 approach is expected to take a bigger-tent approach. Central to that effort is connecting abortion to cost-of-living concerns and, in some cases, moving away from “culture war” issues or “identity politics.” The 2026 blue sweep also expanded the number of Democrats who identify as personally “pro-life,” while the party’s moderate wing encouraged voters to consider candidates who discuss abortion differently to compete in deep-red states.

Abortion was not a major topic at post-election Democratic gatherings including Crooked Media’s CrookedCon in Washington, D.C., and What’s Next, a conference of party strategists and 2028 hopefuls hosted in Denver by the center-left group Majority Democrats. Lis Smith, a veteran Democratic strategist and adviser to Majority Democrats, said on a panel at CrookedCon that signaling moderation on abortion had helped Democrats who support abortion rights win key midterm races in deep-red and purple states. Smith said the lesson was not to choose between left, right, moderate or populist labels. “There’s no one way to be a Democrat, and a big tent of voters requires a big tent of candidates,” Smith said. Smith said Democrats should stop trying from stages like CrookedCon to tell people that there is a single prescription for the party’s future and let people in different places show what it is.

Kansas Sen.-elect Adam Hamilton, a Methodist pastor, calls himself “pro-life with a heavy heart” and supports keeping abortion legal despite feeling “moral tension” around the issue. Mississippi Sen.-elect Scott Colom, who won a massive upset victory, says he is “personally pro-life” but believes in reproductive choice.

Reproductive Freedom for All President Mini Timmaraju wants 2028 Democratic contenders to campaign for expanded abortion access on the trail. She wants action “in the first 100 hours” of the new Congress to repeal the Hyde Amendment, which bans federal dollars from funding abortions in most cases, and wants 2028 Democratic presidential contenders to push for its abolishment. Timmaraju also wants clear commitments from Democrats across the board to restore abortion rights in all 50 states and remove discriminatory barriers to care, such as the Hyde Amendment. She said comprehensive plans addressing a right to contraception and IVF protections would earn bonus points.

Charleston-based political strategist Renee Harvey said many voters would expect to hear about abortion in some form when Democrats launch their campaigns in South Carolina, pointing to the state’s six-week ban. South Carolina is the first state scheduled to hold a Democratic primary and the only early primary state with an abortion ban. “We are the only early state with an abortion ban,” Harvey said. “The first time they talk to voters, they are going to be campaigning in a state where the consequences of Dobbs are not hypothetical.” She said campaigns would have to connect abortion with concerns such as the cost of living and health-care availability. “Reproductive freedom is not separate from those kitchen table issues,” she said, adding, “I don’t think anyone can claim to care about the cost of living while ignoring the cost of forcing women to give birth.”

Potential Democratic contenders have begun testing their own framing. At the end of October, Harris appeared on a Reproductive Freedom for All podcast and argued that restoring abortion rights would also require changing political institutions such as the Supreme Court and Electoral College. A spokesperson for Harris suggested the remarks were part of a broader focus on reproductive health and pointed to her September appearance at a birth center alongside Sen.-elect Abdul El-Sayed in Detroit. There, Harris criticized the Trump administration’s cuts to diversity, equity and inclusion programs and federal cuts to Medicaid, the health insurance program for low-income people.

Michigan Sen. Elissa Slotkin appeared on another episode of the same podcast and argued that voters perceive support for abortion bans as a sign of extremism. She also suggested that the right to end a pregnancy should be considered a cost-of-living issue.

Kentucky Gov. Andy Beshear devoted a chapter of his book, “Go and Do Likewise,” to campaigning for exceptions to abortion bans alongside Hadley Duvall, a young woman from Kentucky who had an abortion as a child after being raped by her stepfather. Beshear wrote, “In all the debate surrounding the question of abortion, almost all of it cast in religious terms, Hadley speaking out on behalf of others was the most Christlike action I had ever seen.”

Georgia Sen. Jon Ossoff, who cruised to reelection this fall, highlighted his state’s six-week abortion ban during his campaign, including in a speech where he described women “bleeding out in Georgia labor wards because they didn’t get prenatal care.” Multiple women’s deaths in Georgia have been attributed to the state’s ban.

Abortion is poised to help shape the 2028 presidential election, with both parties still testing the substance and tone of their messages. For Democrats, Lake said, “it’s a winning issue for candidates where there is a contrast.” Republicans have not settled on a shared position, while Democrats are still testing how to translate their state and congressional gains into a broader abortion-rights agenda.


[MSI previously reported that Democrats’ 2026 midterm gains fell short of full control over the state legislatures and governorships needed to dismantle many of the strictest abortion bans](/articles/2026-11-24-democratic-midterm-wins-leave-strict-abortion-bans-largely-intact/). The gains did not resolve the policy split now shaping both parties’ 2028 messages: Republicans are testing whether to move away from national bans, while the anti-abortion movement remains divided between federal restrictions and state-level work.
