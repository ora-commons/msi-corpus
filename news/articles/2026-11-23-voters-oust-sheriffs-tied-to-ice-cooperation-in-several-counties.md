## 287(g) partnerships accounted for about 15% of ICE arrests in July

Voters in several counties removed sheriffs this month after campaigns made their cooperation with U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement under 287(g) agreements a central issue. Shannon Long won in Alamance County, North Carolina, while Democrats Karl Bickel and Jason Rippl won in Frederick County, Maryland, and Winnebago County, Wisconsin.

Long, a pastor, business owner and former sheriff's deputy who worked in a neighboring North Carolina county, ran as an independent and defeated Republican Sheriff Terry Johnson. Local media reported that Long will be the county's first Black sheriff. Some activists, voters and local officials said the race turned partly on immigration enforcement and the treatment of Latino residents.

Alamance County is a longtime Republican stronghold of just under 200,000 residents between Greensboro and the Research Triangle metropolitan area that includes Raleigh, Durham and Chapel Hill. It has become more politically competitive as its population has grown increasingly diverse. The number of residents identifying as Hispanic rose from 750 in the 1990 census to nearly 25,000 in 2020.

The trust between law enforcement and the county's growing Latino community was so fractured, Long said, that his campaign van, marked with the word "sheriff," discouraged some people from speaking with him. Two weeks after defeating Johnson, Long told The Associated Press that some residents felt suppressed and oppressed, depressed and unsure that they belonged in the community. "They've been living in fear. They feel like they don't have a voice," Long said.

Activists and longtime residents said the Latino community, and to some extent the Black community, feared engaging with the sheriff's office. They described non-white residents as far more likely to be pulled over or detained after a traffic stop. Community organizer Leslie Arevalo said some people were afraid to call 911 or even go to work.

The county acquired the name "No Chance Alamance" because of a perceived history of unjust policing and a lack of political representation among Black and Latino residents, school board member Seneca Rogers said. Voter Renee Russell said the sheriff's cooperation with ICE created a false impression that Alamance was unwelcoming. She was excited to see fresh leadership.

Terry Johnson initially said immigration policy could have contributed to his defeat. In a later interview, he said it likely did not and blamed his long tenure and a national political environment he described as brutal for Republicans. He cited North Carolina, where Democratic former Gov. Roy Cooper flipped a U.S. Senate seat by nearly 20 percentage points. Johnson said he always followed the law and protected public safety.

Republican state Sen. Amy Galey, whose district includes Alamance County, defended Johnson's record and said he had improved training opportunities and mental health services. Although she did not think immigration played a major role in the race, she wondered whether hard-line ICE tactics elsewhere could have contributed to broader concerns about law enforcement.

Johnson was among the first sheriffs to allow his deputies to enforce federal immigration laws under a 287(g) agreement with U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement. Associated Press reporting described Johnson, who is white and was first elected in 2002, as a prominent and polarizing figure. He has said he welcomes lawful immigrants and has denied persistent allegations, including in a Justice Department lawsuit over a decade ago that ended with a settlement, that his office racially profiled minority residents. A sheriff's office spokesman confirmed that the office still participates in the 287(g) program, though it ended an agreement to house ICE detainees last year.

Frederick County, Maryland, northwest of Washington, D.C., was another longtime participant in the program. Republican Sheriff Chuck Jenkins said the county had detained and turned over 1,884 people to ICE since 2008. The program stopped this year after Democratic Gov. Wes Moore signed a law banning immigration-enforcement pacts with the federal government.

Jenkins said the program made the community safer but acknowledged that it likely cost him reelection. He was defeated by Karl Bickel, a retired U.S. Department of Justice employee who had lost four previous elections to Jenkins and pledged not to cooperate with ICE. Retired social worker Carol Antoniewicz, who distributed campaign emails, social media posts and literature for Bickel, said residents were fed up with Jenkins's negativity toward immigrants.

In Wisconsin's Winnebago County, ICE's arrest and detention of a schoolteacher weeks before Election Day sparked backlash against anyone who appeared supportive of immigration officers, including a Republican candidate seeking to replace a retiring sheriff. Local advocate and immigration lawyer Molly Smiltneek, who attended a vigil for the teacher, said, "Any kind of perceived alignment definitely is considered a liability at this point."

Winnebago County's sheriff's department had nothing to do with the teacher's arrest, but its 287(g) agreement became central to the campaign. The agreement helped boost Democrat Jason Rippl, who promised to end it.

ICE continues to rely on the partnerships to increase arrests despite a one-time $75 billion infusion from Congress and the hiring of about 12,000 officers. The Trump administration has more than 2,700 agreements with state and local agencies, up from 135 when Trump began his second term. The agreements are concentrated in the South and have deputized more than 29,000 local law officers.

Government data obtained by the Deportation Data Project and analyzed by the advocacy group fwd.us showed that 287(g) partnerships resulted in more than 6,600 arrests in July, about 15% of all ICE arrests that month.

The Associated Press reviewed about 250 elections held this month for sheriffs whose offices had 287(g) agreements. The review identified races in Indiana, Maryland, North Carolina, Wisconsin and elsewhere in which ICE pacts played a role in a candidate's defeat. Many sheriffs who ran were unopposed.

Although only a smattering of sheriffs were ousted, the Associated Press said the results could create trouble for other sheriffs seen as participants in the administration's immigration-enforcement efforts and potentially hinder agreements that have become a cornerstone of that strategy. The pacts could become an issue again in two years, when sheriffs in Florida, Texas and other states with large numbers of agreements are up for election.

Long said he will uphold the law and hold offenders accountable in his new role, but he will not participate in the 287(g) program. He has arranged meetings with the Latino community in hopes of building new relationships.

"I want them to see the sheriff's office in the community, creating a partnership, letting them know that they have a voice," Long said. "There's no need to operate in fear."
