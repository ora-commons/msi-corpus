## Cooper's May order blocked the closure plan

The Trump administration asked U.S. District Judge Christopher Cooper on Thursday to dissolve a preliminary injunction blocking the Kennedy Center’s plan to close its main building for two years while repairs are completed. The Washington hearing ended without an immediate ruling, and Cooper did not say when he would decide. Barack Obama nominated Cooper to the bench.

Cooper issued the injunction in May after the Kennedy Center board voted to close the facility. In that order, Cooper ruled that the board’s vote was “ill-informed and seemingly preordained” and reflected no regard for its legal obligations. He also ruled then that Trump’s name had been illegally added to the building.

Beatty, a Democratic member of Congress from Ohio and an ex officio member of the Kennedy Center board, sued last December to prevent the addition of Trump’s name. She later challenged the closure plans.

In August and September, the Kennedy Center board voted twice to close the main building for two years to complete planned repairs. Government lawyers said in a court filing that the center had become “dangerously dilapidated and decrepit,” with a ceiling partially collapsing in early September.

Justice Department attorney William Jankowski argued that Cooper’s review of the board’s decision must be “very, very deferential.” Jankowski told Cooper, “Plaintiff does not dispute the need for construction at the Kennedy Center,” and “The board’s decisions here were reasonable, and they must stand.”

Cooper asked an attorney for Beatty how keeping the injunction in place indefinitely would benefit the Kennedy Center and the public. Beatty’s attorney Nathaniel Zelinsky warned that lifting the order would “embolden future breaches and misbehavior” by the administration.

Zelinsky urged Cooper to appoint an independent monitor to protect the center if the judge dissolved the injunction. He also warned, “There’s a monumental breach of fiduciary duty that is ongoing.” When Cooper asked what authority he had to appoint such a monitor, Zelinsky replied, “It’s pretty standard trust law.”

Beatty’s attorneys urged Cooper to keep the injunction in place. In a court filing, the attorneys called the closure plan a “Band-Aid” designed to cover up officials’ mismanagement and to please Trump.

Congress appropriated roughly $257 million for maintenance of the main building last year, at the Kennedy Center’s request. Beatty’s lawyers argued that performing the work in phases, with partial closures spread across four years, would allow the center to remain open to patrons.

Government lawyers countered that a Kennedy Center official who consulted with Trump had concluded that a total two-year closure was the “most cost-effective and efficient path” to addressing the center’s infrastructure needs. Cooper ended Thursday’s hearing without announcing when he would rule on the government’s request.
