## Bipartisan bill would add up to 12 weeks of NICU leave for parents

**Subtype:** fact

- Rep. Brittany Pettersen, a Colorado Democrat, introduced a bill Monday that would entitle U.S. parents with newborns in intensive care to up to 12 more weeks of unpaid leave.
- The bill would amend the 1993 Family and Medical Leave Act to provide up to 12 weeks of dedicated NICU leave on top of the 12 weeks of parental leave already available.
- Rep. Mike Lawler of New York and Rep. Ryan Mackenzie of Pennsylvania, both Republicans, and Rep. Morgan McGarvey of Kentucky, a Democrat, signed on as co-sponsors.
- Colorado and Illinois became the first states to implement NICU leave policies earlier this year, and Walmart begins offering up to 12 extra weeks of NICU leave to full-time employees on Tuesday.

## Two GOP co-sponsors lost reelection bids in the midterms

Parents who exhaust their parental leave while their baby remains in a neonatal intensive care unit are the gap the bill targets. Many mothers and fathers end up working through their newborns' NICU stays to save their time off, according to the lawmakers who unveiled the proposal.

The bill, known as the "Stand By NICU Babies and Families Act," would amend the Family and Medical Leave Act of 1993 to offer up to 12 weeks of dedicated NICU leave to eligible workers, on top of the 12 weeks of parental leave already available under the law.

The United States has no federal law mandating paid family leave. The FMLA entitles eligible parents — those who have worked at least 12 months for a private employer with 50 or more employees — to job-protected unpaid leave to care for a newborn.

Pettersen introduced the bill with Republican co-sponsors Rep. Mike Lawler of New York and Rep. Ryan Mackenzie of Pennsylvania, along with Rep. Morgan McGarvey of Kentucky, a Democrat. Mackenzie and McGarvey have both advocated for other policies benefiting NICU babies and shared their own stories as fathers with newborns in intensive care.

"This isn't about party, it's about life experiences," Pettersen said in an interview with The Associated Press. "Where we want to go is paid family and medical leave, and we will continue to fight for it. This is a step forward building off the current policy that has bipartisan support."

Mackenzie said in a statement that "for parents with a newborn in the NICU, the last thing they should have to worry about is whether they'll have enough time off work to be there for their child." Lawler said in a statement that "every working parent deserves the chance to be there for their child, especially when that child is facing a serious medical challenge."

McGarvey said his now teenage twins spent 99 days in intensive care after they were born 14 weeks early. "When your baby's in the NICU, all you can focus on is getting them home," McGarvey said in a statement.

The campaign for NICU leave has gained momentum since Colorado and Illinois became the first states to implement such policies earlier this year. Colorado's paid leave passed mostly along party lines, while a shorter, unpaid leave adopted in Illinois had overwhelming bipartisan support. In both states, the policies were championed by legislators with their own experiences as NICU parents.

On Tuesday, Walmart will begin offering a maximum of 12 extra weeks of NICU leave to its full-time employees, joining a handful of other companies with similar policies, including Morgan Stanley, Pinterest and organic baby formula company Bobbie.

Nearly one out of 10 babies born in the U.S. are admitted to a NICU, according to the most recent figures from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. Advocates say parental presence in the NICU is important because skin-to-skin contact helps improve health outcomes, while time off is critical for mothers of premature babies who are at higher risk of postpartum depression.

A Better Balance, a nonprofit advocating for state and federal NICU leave policies, said most parents wouldn't need the full 12 weeks of extra leave since the majority of NICU stays are much shorter, but the longer cap will help families facing more critical health situations.

"This issue of leave for NICU parents seems to be something that people intuitively get, including people on the right and left and people in between," said Inimai Chettiar, president of A Better Balance.

While Republicans have generally opposed a federal mandate for paid leave, Pettersen said she is optimistic that the amendment to the FMLA will garner bipartisan support.

The bill could face a long road. It must be reintroduced after the new Congress is seated in January, and Pettersen will have to search for new Republican sponsors. Lawler and Mackenzie both lost their reelection bids in the midterm elections that gave Democrats control of both chambers of Congress.

Pettersen said she introduced the bill pro forma to put a stake in the ground as her newly empowered party readies its priorities for the next Congress.
