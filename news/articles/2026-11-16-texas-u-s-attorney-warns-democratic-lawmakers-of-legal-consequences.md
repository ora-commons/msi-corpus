## Reitz demands records from Blumenthal and Slotkin, posts warning letter to Warren

Aaron Reitz, the Houston-based U.S. attorney for the Southern District of Texas, has issued three written warnings in recent days to Democratic lawmakers, telling them they would face legal consequences if they "weaponize" their newly secured investigative power against President Trump and his allies. His office is more than 1,000 miles from Washington. Reitz is 39, a Marine veteran and former chief of staff for Sen. Ted Cruz (R., Texas).

Reitz sent letters last week to Sens. Richard Blumenthal of Connecticut and Elissa Slotkin of Michigan demanding that they preserve documents, communications and other records that he says could show criminal intent. Reitz argued that Democratic senators have touted oversight plans that would trample on the freedom of association rights of Trump supporters, including in his district, giving him jurisdiction to investigate.

On Sunday, Reitz posted a third letter, addressed to Sen. Elizabeth Warren (D., Mass.), citing her pledge to investigate companies that donated Trump priorities to receive favorable treatment. The letter accused Warren of preparing to "wield your future gavel like a tomahawk, hunting for the scalps of President Trump's private associates." Stephen Miller, the president's new chief of staff, reposted Reitz's message on social media.

In an interview, Reitz said, "We don't have to wait for them to commit a crime for us to begin investigating whether there's a basis to escalate or indict or prosecute." Democrats, he said, "are champing at the bit with bloodlust to punish those who associate with the president."

Reitz's letters to the senators cited federal laws that make it a crime to deprive people of civil rights and are more commonly used against officials such as police officers and jail guards who abuse their power. During the Biden administration, special counsel Jack Smith used one of the statutes to charge Trump related to his actions leading up to the Jan. 6, 2021, attack on the U.S. Capitol. Smith dropped the case when it was clear Trump would return to the White House.

Blumenthal, who is set to chair the Senate's main investigative panel and is himself a former U.S. attorney, responded to Reitz by ordering him to preserve all communications related to his decision to write the letter. Blumenthal has sent more than a dozen letters to donors for Trump's White House ballroom. "I will not be intimidated," Blumenthal said.

Slotkin has said she hopes congressional probes would hold people in Trump's orbit accountable for "enabling" the president and serve as a deterrent to college graduates considering whether to join the administration. Addressing Reitz's letter Friday, she said, "There's no better telltale sign of an authoritarian leader devolving than when they start trying to criminally indict people for the words they say against him." Warren didn't immediately respond to a request for comment.

The White House's top lawyer has warned lawmakers not to let congressional probes exceed their constitutional limits and veer into harassment and "blatant criminality." The administration has also warned that it may investigate the investigators, and Trump this month explicitly called on the Justice Department to investigate Slotkin.

Democrats are laying the groundwork for a flurry of investigations after their decisive win of both chambers in the midterms, including into Trump's business dealings, his ties to convicted sex offender Jeffrey Epstein, the activities of cabinet members and the administration's immigration enforcement.

Reitz said he welcomed the confrontation after encouraging conversations with senior Justice Department officials in Washington, who have more broadly urged prosecutors in the field to be on "wartime footing" during the last two years of the administration. He said that posture is especially critical in Texas, where Democrats won every statewide office and control of the statehouse. "I am going to be confronting what I anticipate to be a state government, local government liberal axis in opposition to federal law enforcement, and we haven't had that in Texas in a long time," Reitz said.

Reitz held a top Justice Department policy role for just three months last year before stepping down to mount a campaign for Texas attorney general, a position that opened up when his former boss, Ken Paxton, ran for Senate. Reitz lost in the Republican primary, finishing fourth. He became U.S. attorney in July.

Attorney General Todd Blanche has said Democratic rhetoric could be criminal if it turns threatening. At a Thursday event, Blanche said he was optimistic that the Justice Department's relationship with Congress will be "productive," and said lawmakers have important oversight functions. The department currently has "no ongoing criminal investigations into words that the incoming senators or congressmen have said." Asked if he personally signed off on Reitz's letters, Blanche said, "I'm the attorney general so I don't oversee every single thing that's done."

Legal experts said Reitz's approach was questionable. "This is an attempt to take that charge and flip it back on Congress," said Kimberly Hamm, a partner at law firm Morrison & Foerster who previously served as general counsel to former House Speaker Kevin McCarthy. Lawmakers enjoy sweeping protection for their legislative activity — including oversight — that could impede any Justice Department effort to prosecute, Hamm said. If a congressional inquiry is "truly intended to harass, that's a defense to cooperating with the investigation," Hamm said. "That's not a basis for a criminal charge."

Reitz has previously drawn criticism over his social media posts. Senators considering his confirmation to the earlier policy position questioned him on his deletion of thousands of social-media posts in which he suggested it was acceptable to defy a court order and expressed views on topics from abortion to the killing of George Floyd in 2020. On the first day of early voting in Texas, Reitz drew criticism from Democrats when he posted a video in which he said he would "hunt down" election lawbreakers.
