## Only the religiously unaffiliated are projected to grow primarily through switching

In NPR's Nov. 18, 2026, coverage of Pew Research Center's Future of World Religions report, religion correspondent Jason DeRose relayed Alan Cooperman's assessment of the projected shift in Christianity's global center of gravity. Cooperman, Pew's director of religion research, said it is “probably the most significant change pointed to by these numbers.” Cooperman also said, “The global change in what one might think of as the center of gravity of Christianity is really very interesting. The distribution of Christians around the world looks very different from the way it does today.” DeRose described the shift as Christianity's center of gravity continuing to migrate to the Global South.

Pew's researchers used national censuses, U.N. population data and other demographic research to produce the projections.

Conversion or religious switching does not account for most global religious growth, according to Pew's research. DeRose said the religiously unaffiliated, often called nones, are the only group projected to grow primarily through switching.

The report's explanation centers on fertility. Muslim women have an average of 3.1 children, while women in every other religious group have fewer, on average. DeRose said religious growth is mostly driven by births rather than conversion or what researchers call religious switching.

The analysis shows both Islam and Christianity continuing to grow worldwide, with Islam increasing faster. By 2035, nine years after the report, the projections say Islam will surpass Christianity as the largest religious group.

The report also projects a geographic shift in Christianity. By 2070, the Democratic Republic of the Congo is expected to have the world's largest Christian population, replacing the United States as the country with the most Christians. The change reflects growth in sub-Saharan Africa and a continued redistribution of Christians across regions.

Pew attributes the growth of Christianity in Africa primarily to higher birth rates rather than missionaries or conversions. DeRose said the geographic change could also affect the issues emphasized by Christians in the developing world, whose concerns differ from those of Christians in Europe and North America. He said the shift might bring greater emphasis on poverty or the environment.

In the United States, Christians currently make up about 62% of the population. Pew projects that their share will decline to less than half by 2070, when Christians will remain the largest group but will no longer constitute a majority.

By and large, people leaving Christianity in the United States are not becoming Muslim, Buddhist or members of another religion, according to Pew. Instead, those leaving Christianity are expected to identify as religiously unaffiliated. By 2070, the unaffiliated are projected to make up 41% of the U.S. population.

DeRose said the projected change alters the demographic context of debates over whether the United States is a Christian nation. He called the question one of the most salient issues in recent years and said he often hears discussion of Christian nationalism. He noted that the United States has no established official religion. Because Christians had been by far the majority for so long, DeRose said one could argue that the country is, at least descriptively, a Christian nation. Under the projection, the 2070 landscape would be different: Christians would remain the largest group but would no longer hold a majority. Many Christian conservatives already worry about what that might mean for the culture, DeRose said, adding that the coming decades would bring a decidedly different religious landscape.
