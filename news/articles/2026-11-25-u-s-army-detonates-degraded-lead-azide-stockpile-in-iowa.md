## Barriers and ballistic gels were among the safety preparations

The Associated Press reported that the U.S. Army detonated a dangerous, degraded stockpile of lead azide in Iowa on Wednesday. The decades-old supply had crystallized, posing a risk of spontaneous detonation. Officials and scientists are questioning how much lead may be in the air and nearby land and water after the detonation.

Lead azide is a highly sensitive chemical compound containing lead and nitrogen. It typically appears as a white or colorless crystalline powder or needlelike crystals. The compound has been used as an initiating explosive in munitions, and it was historically used in detonators to set off other explosives, such as TNT.

Its physical properties make it hazardous to handle. Lead azide may explode when dry and is sensitive to shock, heat, impact and friction. Temperature changes and the passage of time can cause lead azide to form larger, unstable crystals that are even more sensitive.

The Iowa material was decades old. The military stopped using lead azide in the 1980s because of its hazards, but the older supply remained in Iowa.

Lead is a toxic heavy metal that can harm the brain, nervous system, kidneys, heart and other organs. Lead exposure can also cause developmental damage.

Officials and researchers are asking what lead levels may be in the air and in nearby land and water after the detonation. The possible effects extend to residents, crops, cattle and wildlife, and the concern includes both immediate risks and longer-term health and environmental hazards.

The Army said its safety preparations included barriers around the blast zone to reduce the spread of the resulting plume. The preparations also included ballistic gels to absorb lead dust. The detonation does not settle how much lead was released or how long any contamination could last, leaving those questions for officials and researchers assessing possible levels in the air, land and water.
