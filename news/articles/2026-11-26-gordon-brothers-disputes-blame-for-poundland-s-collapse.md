## Potential buyers balked at terms tied to £22.5m shareholder loan

A BBC News analysis by Simon Jack examined the dispute over who bears responsibility for Poundland's collapse into administration. Gordon Brothers, the US-based owner, bought the discount chain for £1 and has taken nearly £20m out of the business. The firm stands to receive another £22.5m from its share of an existing £30m loan, which would rank among the first debts paid from the proceeds of any sale of the business in whole or in part.

Poundland's management team said its access to the retailer's own cash was restricted, leaving it unable to fully restock ahead of the crucial Christmas period. The team pointed to improving sales over October as proof the business had a future. Gordon Brothers disputes much of that characterization.

Members of the Business Committee accused the US firm of "predatory capitalism" and "asset stripping." The analysis said no one appeared to dispute that Gordon Brothers' insistence on being paid most of its £22.5m debt was a major stumbling block to a sale. Potential buyers balked at paying millions on a loan the firm inherited when it acquired Poundland for £1.

Martin Property Group offered £5m up front, with the balance deferred. Xyra Holdings offered £12.5m up front, although sources close to the deal believed Xyra could not actually pay.

Gordon Brothers said it lent Poundland £85m over 17 months to try to keep it alive and that, without it, the collapse would have happened in June last year. Most of the £20m removed since the acquisition consisted of interest on that loan.

Despite brief periods of sales growth compared with a year earlier, Poundland was not generating enough cash to sustain itself. Sales were forecast to be £115m lower than predicted when Gordon Brothers bought the business, more than offsetting a £100m cost-cutting programme. Like other retailers, Poundland was hit by rising labour costs, employment taxes and business rates. Gordon Brothers argued that none of the potential buyers could meet its terms and prove they had both the money and expertise to make a go of the business.

The chair of the Business Committee wants Gordon Brothers and Poundland's management to return before the committee to establish what happened. A bidder could still emerge in the coming hours and days to save the business as a whole, although most industry observers considered that unlikely.

Julie Palmer of insolvency specialists Begbies Traynor told the BBC: "This sort of direction of travel seemed somehow inevitable. Obviously, if one of those interested parties steps up and buys the whole business and assets, that's the best, quickest and cleanest outcome. I think realistically, though, there is a chance now that there will be a breakup."

The analysis drew a parallel with previous busts such as Woolworths, BHS and Wilko. Under a breakup, many stores could find new owners and would need local, experienced staff. Poundland's collapse puts 11,000 jobs at risk, leaving workers facing uncertainty a month before Christmas, when they should be at their busiest and most needed.

Gordon Brothers specializes in commercial distress and, when it takes on a distressed company, seeks to ensure the worst possible outcome still produces money. In this case, the worst outcome would mean receiving £22.5m, earning interest on a loan secured against company assets and collecting miscellaneous fees. The analysis said many observers believed that left the firm little incentive to strain to secure a buyer.

Gordon Brothers counters that it kept a business that would otherwise have collapsed in June trading for another 17 months, extending employment for thousands of workers. Some retail veterans told the BBC that Gordon Brothers never really intended to turn the business around. The BBC analysis challenged that claim by asking why previous owner PepCo would give Gordon Brothers a no-lose gamble and why PepCo did not instead do what Gordon Brothers was doing and profit with no risk. The analysis said the answer was that PepCo had other businesses that could have become entangled in Poundland's administration.
