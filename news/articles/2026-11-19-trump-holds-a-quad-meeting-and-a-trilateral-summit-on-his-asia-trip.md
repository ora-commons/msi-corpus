## Three-way statement pledges UN reform, increased petroleum supplies and no nuclear wars

Donald Trump held two diplomatic gatherings on the same Asia trip that placed longtime partners and longtime rivals in the same frame. On Tuesday in Manila, he met Japanese Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi, Indian Prime Minister Narendra Modi and Australian Prime Minister Anthony Albanese for a Quad meeting intended to strengthen a partnership meant to counter China. Two days later, in Shenzhen, he warmly greeted Chinese leader Xi Jinping and Russian President Vladimir Putin at an unprecedented trilateral summit of three countries long considered rivals. The trilateral summit convened to take on “new opportunities and approaches on a range of very important issues.”

The Quad session took place in a no-frills hotel conference room in a resort in the Philippine capital. Four long tables covered in blue cloth were arranged in a quadrilateral, each government taking one side, and the leaders were flanked by their Cabinet members. There were no decorations beyond a mandatory display of national flags. Before the meeting, the four leaders took a photo together, and Trump took questions from reporters and joked around with his counterparts.

Trump called the leaders “friends of mine,” turned to Albanese and patted him on the back, drawing a nod from the Australian prime minister. Throwing his thumb toward Modi, Trump joked, “And by the way, he’s a very tough man,” then pointed to Takaichi and said, “and she may be even tougher.” Modi did not crack a smile, though Takaichi chuckled. Trump turned back to Albanese: “And we? We’re OK, too,” which drew a laugh. Moments earlier, Modi, apparently impatient, had gestured to end the press conference to no avail while Trump kept taking questions.

At the meeting itself, Trump said “we’re all friends” and suggested that the leaders from Japan, Australia and India could reach him directly. “We talk a lot when they have a problem, when they have difficulty, when they want to tell me something, my phone is always open and the Oval Office is always open to you,” he said. Talking to reporters, Trump praised his fellow Quad leaders as “phenomenal people.” “And they’re friends of mine, so that helps,” the U.S. president said.

Two days later, the scene was much grander but far less spontaneous. Trump joined Xi and Putin at a massive ring-shaped table with a burst of pink flowers in the middle at a state-owned resort in Shenzhen, China. The spacious room had marble flooring, crystal chandeliers and jade sculptures. The three leaders sat alone at the table, some distance from one another: Xi read from a prepared script with hardly any expression on his face, Putin looked at the documents in front of him, and Trump sat back at the opposite end of the table with a slight smile.

Trump applied a similar vocabulary to his hosts in Shenzhen. On Thursday, he described Xi and Putin as his “friend.” At the airport in Shenzhen, Trump received a rare greeting there from Xi and even an unusual hug from the Chinese leader. At a welcoming ceremony for the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation grouping, Putin joined Xi and Trump on stage, where he and Trump shook hands and held each other briefly by the arm while Xi looked on and smiled.

At the trilateral meeting, Trump argued that differences among countries should not forestall talks. “Too often, past leaders avoided talking to nations with whom they had disagreements. And, uh, we feel the opposite,” he said.

The two meetings produced statements of markedly different texture. The Quad's joint press release called the group “a force for good and a partnership of action, committed to peace, stability, and prosperity” and laid out areas for cooperation including artificial intelligence, cybersecurity and maritime surveillance. The joint statement from China, the United States and Russia committed to reforming the United Nations, planning to increase petroleum supplies and affirming opposition to nuclear wars.

Edgard Kagan, a former U.S. ambassador to Malaysia now a senior advisor at the Center for Strategic and International Studies, said the Quad statement was substantive and reflective of the alignment of the four nations, while the trilateral statement was largely symbolic. He added that it still offered more detail than expected. With language about nuclear weapons and future dialogue, “it is a major concession by China,” Kagan said.

Ken Moriyasu, a senior fellow focused on Asia at the Hudson Institute, framed the contrast as a choice between two possible international orders. “The Quad, comprising four democracies that uphold the rule of law, represents the post-World War II international order,” he said. “The trilateral, meanwhile, raises the prospect of a very different order — one in which three great powers stand above the rest, recognize each other’s spheres of influence and refrain from interfering in each other’s core interests.”

The two gatherings put the varied strands of Trump's Asia diplomacy side by side at a time when the world order is shifting: an informal, joking session with Cabinet members around blue-clothed tables in Manila, then three leaders alone at a ring-shaped table in Shenzhen. In both rooms he described the leaders around him as friends, but the two sets of statements pointed in different directions — the Quad toward cooperation among four governments, and the three-way summit toward a shared pledge by China, the United States and Russia.
