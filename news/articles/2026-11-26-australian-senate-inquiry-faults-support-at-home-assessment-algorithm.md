## Inquiry recommends reinstating human overrides for Support at Home assessments

An interim report tabled on Thursday by an Australian Senate inquiry found evidence that older people were harmed because, in many cases, they were underassessed and left without adequate support. The inquiry, which received almost 300 submissions, recommended reinstating human overrides of algorithmic decisions. The findings also document long waits for funding and services, high costs for recipients and deaths among people awaiting funding or support.

Support at Home, the federal aged-care program, launched in November 2025 to help older people remain at home longer, give them greater control over their support and place a focus on human rights. The program uses an algorithm to assess support needs and allocate funding. According to the inquiry, the algorithm prioritizes physical mobility over cognitive decline, meaning people with severe dementia can be classified as “independent” and denied adequate funding. A single point difference in the decision tree can cost an applicant nearly $40,000 a year.

The report recommended restoring human override after evidence that older people were harmed because, in many cases, they were underassessed and left without adequate support. But assessors are barred from immediately correcting an incorrect algorithmic result. The inquiry heard that the appeal system can be lengthy and onerous and that some people were pushed into residential care before they were ready or into hospitals as their health deteriorated.

As of June, more than 106,000 Australians were on the official waiting list for a Support at Home place. In October, the health department reported that 6,436 people had died while waiting for their approved level of funding. The inquiry also heard that people who were healthy remained in hospitals for months while awaiting an assessment, funding allocation or access to support.

The inquiry heard the case of a woman with heart and renal failure who was discharged from hospital as a “medium priority” Support at Home case. She died four months later while waiting for funding to help her eat and administer critical medication.

Greens senator Penny Allman-Payne, the inquiry chair, said: “Labor has clearly botched the implementation of Support at Home and added baffling layers of bureaucracy and complexity, but fundamentally this is a problem of design.” She said: “Not only are providers jacking up their prices, the system rations and denies care, systematically underassessing need and priority and creating artificial scarcity by withholding packages.”

Guardian Australia’s September freedom-of-information requests revealed that health officials in several states and the Northern Territory were sufficiently alarmed by the algorithm’s effects to keep logs of hundreds of cases involving severely underassessed vulnerable people. The officials sent those cases to the federal government. Days before the algorithm was rolled out in November, the department told aged care assessment organisations that assessors would be barred from overriding incorrect algorithmic outcomes, despite assurances that they would have the final say.

The government has stopped short of allowing assessors to correct incorrect algorithm decisions immediately, despite growing calls from clinicians, advocacy groups and politicians throughout the inquiry. Instead, aged care minister Sam Rae said in July that assessors could escalate cases to a system governor. The government has also announced an independent review of the aged-care classification and prioritisation assessment.

The interim report found that “Many of the adjustments to date appear to be reactive in nature, driven by external pressures – including successive inquiries by this committee – rather than robust monitoring and improvement processes put in place by the government.”

The inquiry was due to finish in November but was extended for another year after receiving almost 300 submissions. Aged-care assessors have described elements of the reforms as “inhumane”. The program has also been plagued by complaints that it made care harder to access and more expensive.

The inquiry heard that funding was being eroded by exorbitant prices, with some services, including meals and cleaning, tripling in price. The program requires recipients to pay for services, and people can be required to contribute up to 80% of the cost. The inquiry heard that these co-contributions were causing financial distress and forcing some older people to cut back on essential support.

The report cited crutches that usually cost about $50 but rose to $1,800 after Support at Home began, as providers increased prices and administrative hurdles were added. Obtaining the crutches required a three-month wait. The report described the broader pattern in blunt terms: “older people go without, opt out and die without”. It said 4,812 older Australians had been approved for Support at Home but died before receiving a single service.

One witness told the inquiry: “My dad passed away on 29 April, two days short of turning 92, and his package arrived three weeks later in May.” Shadow health minister Anne Ruston said the committee found many problems “were either known, or predicted, prior to the rollout”. She said: “Older Australians and their families have spent a year telling this government that Support at Home is broken,” and “Labor dismissed them, and now a Senate committee has confirmed every word.”

The aged care royal commission’s final report in 2021 found that most older Australians wanted to remain at home and that supporting them safely could prevent premature decline and strain on hospitals. The inquiry’s interim findings document how assessment errors, delays, high prices and deaths have affected the Support at Home reforms’ stated aims of enabling longer time at home, greater control and a human-rights focus.
