## Parks Canada says visitors on natural ice must be able to self-rescue

A 70-year-old resident of Banff, Alberta, died Tuesday evening after falling through thin ice on Lake Louise while skating alone, the Royal Canadian Mounted Police said. The man, who has not been identified, was found two hours later by a rescue team and pronounced dead at a nearby hospital.

In a statement, the Royal Canadian Mounted Police offered condolences to the man's family and warned the public about the dangers of being on the ice. The fatal fall followed two other skaters falling into Lake Louise's frigid waters on Monday; both survived.

In a notice posted Monday before Tuesday's fatal fall, Parks Canada told visitors to "think twice" before stepping onto the ice in Banff National Park. The agency said, "Visitors must be able to self-rescue should they fall in the water." The notice also stated, "If you choose to skate on natural ice, you do so at your own risk."

Parks Canada said early-season ice can be unpredictable. The agency does not actively monitor natural ice surfaces for safety or mark potential hazards.

Lake Louise is a popular winter skating destination in Canada, though park authorities do not monitor natural ice for safety.

The incident came during warmer-than-average November temperatures in Alberta and other parts of Canada. Toronto, Canada's largest city, experienced unseasonable highs of 15C (59F) during the month.

In Alberta, temperatures have been about three degrees Celsius warmer than what has been historically observed for November, Alysa Pederson, a meteorologist with Environment Canada, told the BBC. Typically, temperatures would dip below -10C for a number of days in November, but the coldest November day this year was -6C.

"It's going to become a challenge to form ice when your temperatures aren't really getting that cold," Pederson said.

Environment Canada's seasonal forecast suggests that much of Canada, from Vancouver to Halifax, will likely have a warmer-than-average winter. The outlook is due partly to El Niño, which changes wind patterns over the Pacific Ocean, releases heat into the atmosphere and can drive major shifts in global weather patterns.

"We're currently in a very strong El Niño event, potentially record-breaking by the time it peaks here in the next couple of months," Pederson said.

She advised people eager to get outside during winter to remain vigilant because warmer conditions can bring thin ice and a higher risk of avalanches. Pederson said that although falling through ice in Banff is always a risk, the recent back-to-back incidents came as a surprise; she called the situation "unfortunate."
