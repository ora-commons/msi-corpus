## China’s 2022 security pact remains unresolved as Wale visits Australia

The Solomon Islands signed a treaty with Australia, a U.S. ally, on Wednesday that prohibits foreign military bases on the Pacific island nation’s soil. The agreement aims to calm U.S. and allied concerns that the Solomon Islands was slipping into China’s orbit and that Beijing could open a military outpost there.

More than 800,000 people live across about 1,000 islands northeast of Australia. A secretive 2022 security pact between the Solomon Islands and China raised fears among Western analysts that Beijing could begin docking warships in the country and eventually open a military base, threatening sea links between Australia and the U.S. in a potential future conflict in the Pacific.

“Solomon Islands has been for a number of years a source of uncertainty and doubt in the region,” the country’s prime minister, Matthew Wale, said Wednesday on his visit to Australia. “Those days are done.”

During a news conference with Australian Prime Minister Anthony Albanese, Wale declined to say whether the Solomon Islands would formally scrap the 2022 deal with China, noting that it contains a nondisclosure clause. “We have ongoing discussions with China,” he said.

China has denied that it is seeking to open a military base in the Solomon Islands. A spokesman for China’s foreign ministry said Beijing’s diplomacy in the Pacific was “not for geopolitical contest” and criticized efforts to thwart China’s regional engagement. The spokesman said, “The international community should help Pacific Island countries achieve sustainable development, respect their independence, put development first, and stick to openness and inclusiveness.”

The China accord prompted a flurry of activity by the U.S. and its allies to shore up relations with remote Pacific island states that had been important battlegrounds in the Allied victory over Japan during World War II.

The new Solomon Islands treaty follows similar agreements Australia signed with Pacific island states over the past two years. The Wall Street Journal reported that the agreements have curbed Beijing’s efforts to increase its influence in the region.

Last year, Australia signed a mutual-defense treaty with Papua New Guinea, its first military alliance since the ANZUS treaty with New Zealand and the U.S. in 1951. Pacts with Vanuatu and Fiji followed in June and July, respectively.

“Across the Pacific, we are taking our partnerships to a new level,” Albanese said. Under the new treaty, Albanese said Australia would continue to help the Solomon Islands strengthen its police force, create more opportunities for Solomon Islanders to work in Australia, and boost ties between the two countries through sport, culture and faith.

The treaty text, posted online by Australia’s foreign-affairs department, says the Solomon Islands committed to keeping sensitive infrastructure free of militarization. It also says the country will ensure that security cooperation comes from Pacific Islands Forum members. Australia is a member of the regional group, but China is not.

Wale, who took office in May, said he would seek legislation to “bed down the treaty,” which should make it harder for any pro-China successors to back out of the deal.

“Wale has sent a clear signal to Canberra and Beijing about who he wants to engage with on security moving forward,” said Connor Graham, a research fellow at the Lowy Institute think tank in Sydney.

China is the Solomon Islands’ largest trading partner, leaving the country vulnerable to potential retaliation from Beijing, Graham said. “The question now is can Australia deliver on whatever promises and commitments have been made to make this worth the blowback in the long term for Solomon Islands,” he said.
