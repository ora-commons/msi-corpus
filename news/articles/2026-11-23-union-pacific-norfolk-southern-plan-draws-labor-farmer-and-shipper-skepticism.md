## Union Pacific-Norfolk Southern plan draws labor, farmer and shipper skepticism

**Subtype:** fact

- Union Pacific and Norfolk Southern are seeking Surface Transportation Board approval for a merger that would create the nation's largest railroad and the first to serve both coasts.
- Union Pacific says the merger would eliminate rail interchanges, reduce shipping costs for consumers and strengthen the nation's supply chain.
- Elena Prager and Bob Granatelli warn that greater rail market power could raise prices, reduce service or quantity and worsen quality for captive shippers.
- The Stop The Rail Merger Coalition, which includes labor unions, shippers, farmers and transportation groups, opposes the merger over job losses, safety and the risk that other railroads will pursue further combinations.
- The Surface Transportation Board set Nov. 18 as the deadline for public comments and plans more federal-agency comments through winter and hearings in spring.

## Federal board weighs competition, jobs and safety as review advances

Union Pacific and Norfolk Southern are in the latest phase of a federal review of their proposed merger, which would create the nation's largest railroad and the first to serve both coasts. The Surface Transportation Board, which uses existing precedent on rail mergers to make its decisions, is considering the application; public comments were due Nov. 18, and the board plans to hear from other federal agencies through winter before additional hearings in spring. Union Pacific argues the combined system would eliminate interchanges and lower shipping costs, while the Stop The Rail Merger Coalition, which includes labor unions, shippers, farmers and transportation groups, disputes those claims and raises concerns about prices, jobs, safety and competition.

The proposed combination would link rail networks divided roughly by the Mississippi River. Chicago, Kansas City and St. Louis are among the Midwestern cities where goods move from one rail line to another, making those meeting points central to Union Pacific's efficiency argument. On Saturday, July 18, 2026, rows of freight cars filled a rail yard in North Platte, Nebraska, whose railroad roots date to 1866, when its first Union Pacific train arrived; mainline operations through the city began in 1867. Clark Ballew, communications director for the Brotherhood of Maintenance Way Employees, said, “If this goes through, they will be a mega-railroad.” He said: “Quite literally. Obviously the biggest and largest railroad that’s ever existed in the United States. Unforeseen to this point.” Despite promises from Union Pacific, workers across the Midwest where the railroads meet fear job losses. The Stop The Rail Merger Coalition also says reducing the number of major railroads from six to five could prompt the remaining companies to pursue additional mergers.

Union Pacific spokesperson Robynn Tysver said in an email, “The creation of America’s first single-service transcontinental railroad will eliminate time consuming rail interchanges that will improve service for shippers, reduce costs for consumers and strengthen the nation’s supply chain.” The company said a shift from road to rail would ease congestion, reduce taxpayer-funded road maintenance, cut emissions and deliver cargo faster and more reliably. Union Pacific declined requests to make a spokesperson available, instead providing a statement and additional information on its website. A spokesperson deferred to Norfolk Southern to speak for the merging partners.

Railroads are sometimes considered natural monopolies because the cost of creating a network far exceeds the cost of adding a train. Natural monopoly regulation monitors the company while ensuring it remains profitable. Elena Prager, an assistant professor of economics at the Simon Business School at the University of Rochester, said rising monopoly power typically brings higher prices, lower quantities or worse service and quality. “In general, you’re looking at higher prices, lower quantities and worse quality,” she said. Transportation consultant Bob Granatelli, who has been in the transportation business since the 1970s, said businesses with few rail options, known as captive shippers, are most at risk of paying more. “This is frightening for a captive shipper,” Granatelli said. “It will make captivity even stronger.”

The American Chemistry Council joined the Stop The Rail Merger Coalition because many of its members depend on a single railroad, particularly petrochemical companies along the Gulf Coast. Jeff Sloan, the council's senior director of regulatory affairs, said, “That exposes them to higher rates, less responsive customer service,” and, “Essentially, if you’re not afraid of losing your customer, you treat your customer differently.” Chemical manufacturers and the businesses they serve face a transportation disadvantage because their commodities are often shipped in tanks or hopper cars rather than the large standardized steel or aluminum boxes used as intermodal containers, and those commodities cannot easily move from rail to road.

Farmers face a similar concern. Ash Anderson, education director for the Brotherhood of Maintenance Way Employees, said, “If a grain elevator can only ship on one railroad now, and they set the pricing, that’s going to affect all of the farmers that get grain to that elevator, and so now those farmers may have to eat more costs.” Union members also fear that a larger railroad could prioritize some freight over others and that smaller rail lines could shut down or be absorbed by the dominant company. Union members also said the merger could be the first domino that ends in a consolidated market. Paul Bellows, vice chairman of the Unified System Division at the Brotherhood of Maintenance Way Employees, said, “The trickle-down effect is very evident in this merger because we’re talking about a transcontinental merger and basically a duopoly.” He added that “Every citizen in the United States is going to be affected, and possibly even, you know, people in Canada and Mexico.”

Norfolk Southern senior vice president and chief strategy officer Mike McClellan downplayed the inflationary concerns because the combined railroad would compete more effectively with trucking. The companies' merger application says improved service could reduce the number of truckloads on the road by some 2.1 million annually. “People use railroads because we’re cheap, right?” McClellan said. “Trucks will always present essentially the ceiling on what we can do with pricing.” The two companies are planning for a future where there are more autonomous trucks on the road, so they hope to keep rail viable for that future.

In general, rail is better suited to longer trips and to materials that are too large or dangerous for the nation's highways. McClellan said rail struggles compared with trucking in the area about 250 miles east and west of the Mississippi River because a nearby transfer point requires two trips: a short haul to the transfer and a theoretical longer haul to the destination. Removing that transfer, he said, could lead shippers to consider rail over trucks for shipments across the greater Midwest. “I will say that we are doing this to grow rail business, not to shrink it,” McClellan said. “You’re going to do it by improving your service and improving the competitiveness of rail.”

Opponents' skepticism increased this fall after a video circulated showing Union Pacific CEO Jim Vena discussing the merger with the Southwest Association of Rail Shippers. Vena said in the March meeting, “I’ll probably increase everybody's rates that said they didn’t want a seamless railroad by 3 or 4% just to teach you a lesson.” Union Pacific sought to keep the video private, but a federal judge ordered it released to the public in late October. The company released the video with additional context, stating, “Let us be clear: Union Pacific does not retaliate against customers.” Its statement said a customer’s position on the merger would not determine its rates or how it delivers service. It also said, “Customers should participate freely and candidly in the Surface Transportation Board’s (STB) review. We also welcome direct dialogue with our customers. Their perspectives matter to us.”

Safety concerns extend beyond rates and employment. In 2023, a Norfolk Southern train carrying hazardous materials derailed near East Palestine, Ohio. The cargo caught fire and sent a toxic cloud over the town, leading to evacuations. A lawsuit alleged the toxic fumes contributed to multiple deaths; that case has not been adjudicated. Nate Lee, a BNSF rail worker and local chairman for the Brotherhood of Maintenance Way Employees, said, “Across the country, you have the potential for an East Palestine-type incident pretty much every single day.” He added, “It’s up to us folks maintaining the railroads to make sure that that doesn’t happen. And unfortunately, we have our jobs cut out for us nowadays.”

On a Saturday afternoon in November, dozens of train cars passed beneath the West Haymarket Pedestrian Bridge in Lincoln. Empty freight cars headed west, likely toward Wyoming for a new load of coal, while tanker cars rumbled east, possibly carrying ethanol refined from Great Plains corn. A freight train traveled through Lincoln on Nov. 7; several rail tracks bisected the city, and most trains carried coal from Wyoming or ethanol from out of state.

Lee, whose BNSF lines make up most of the track near Lincoln, Nebraska, worries that the merger could reduce the number of workers repairing rail lines and that investor pressure could lead to job cuts. He said, “You can only run a person ragged for so long before it starts to wear on you,” adding, “And unfortunately, what happens when people get tired? People get worn out. Things that shouldn’t be missed may get missed.”

Union Pacific announced a “Jobs For Life” promise this spring for union workers on both sides of the merger. The International Association of Sheet Metal, Air, Rail and Transportation Workers Union supports the merger and negotiated the agreement with the company. Members of the Brotherhood of Maintenance Way Employees do not trust the promise and cite it as a reason for their opposition. Norfolk Southern employees would also have to adapt to Union Pacific's safety standards. Both railroads must follow Federal Railroad Administration requirements, but Lee and other union members said the transition to new safety operations could increase risk. Concerns about relocating managerial Union Pacific workers to Atlanta also arose in the Nebraska Legislature last session, when a bill passed that sought to preserve high-paying jobs through tax credits for large employers.

Lee said track maintenance can be unpredictable. October storms dropped nearly 8 inches of rain across southeast Nebraska, causing washouts in the region where he works, and his crew worked overtime to prevent derailments. Under normal conditions, Lee’s team welds rail lines back into place and levels the ballast, the small rocks that support the rail lines, to keep trains from overturning. Rail companies have also used longer trains in recent years to reduce labor costs and move goods faster. Those “super trains” place more stress on rail infrastructure and can block road access when a stopped train closes a crossing. “So when you had a train that was half a mile long, and now you have trains that are a mile and a half, two miles long,” Lee said. “It takes a long time for that train to stop.”

Existing legal precedent gives the federal government sole authority to regulate railroads, leaving local governments with little control over traffic or safety problems that follow from rail operations. Ballew said, “You’ll have a bunch of people at your meetings, and they will be angry and upset, and they will have valid reasons to be,” adding, “And if you’re a politician, there isn’t much you can do to help.”

Regardless of the Surface Transportation Board's ruling, opponents argue the merger could add to inflation after voters provided a national rebuke to the Trump administration's economic policies. Politics has played a role in the transaction, even though the board is independent and its members are sworn in by the Senate. Three of its five members are Republicans sworn in by President Donald Trump, a fourth seat is held by a Democrat, and one vacancy was created by the firing of Robert Primus. Primus was the only member to vote against the 2023 merger of Canadian Pacific and Kansas City Southern.

William C. Vantuono, editor-in-chief of Railway Age, said investors were motivated by the opportunity they saw after Trump's reelection. “I think the investment community, [the] Wall Street community, got excited because: Oh, you know maybe there’s going to be some regulations scuttled,” Vantuono said. “And this big transcontinental merger was announced last year.” In October 2025, months after the July announcement, Union Pacific donated $2.5 million for construction of the White House ballroom. Vena publicly denied the gift was intended to persuade Trump to support the merger. Vena's messaging also gained bipartisan attention in Congress, with Sens. Tammy Baldwin and Roger Marshall asking the board to scrutinize the plan.

The Surface Transportation Board will continue hearing comments from other federal agencies throughout the winter, with more hearings planned for the spring. Granatelli and Vantuono said the ruling will likely turn on the merger's effect on competition. Vantuono said he expects the board to follow existing precedent rather than bend to political pressure. “I know most of the Surface Transportation Board members personally,” he said. “They are the most ethical people you’d ever come across.” Until the board rules, the merger remains under review, with the companies' promised savings weighed against concerns about prices, employment, safety and rail-market power.
