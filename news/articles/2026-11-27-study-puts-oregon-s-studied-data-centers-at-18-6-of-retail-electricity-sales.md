## Environmental Integrity Project identifies 143 gigawatts of proposed data-center gas plants

Oregon's studied data centers used electricity equivalent to 18.6 percent of the state's retail electricity sales in 2024, according to a Harvard-led study published in Nature Sustainability. The study catalogued 403 warehouse-sized U.S. hyperscale facilities and estimated that they use about 82 terawatt-hours of electricity a year, equivalent to nearly 8 million homes.

That represents roughly 2 percent of U.S. electricity use, but the national figure masks high state-to-state variability, with many states having data-center electricity shares in the double digits. When viewed as a fraction of the retail electricity sales reported to the Energy Information Administration in 2024, the studied facilities in Virginia and Iowa represented 15.5 percent and 13.1 percent, respectively. The study also associated the facilities' electricity use with about 26 million metric tons of carbon dioxide emissions.

The computing campuses supporting the artificial-intelligence boom draw electricity for servers, chip cooling, networking and a smaller amount for ordinary lighting. Translating that demand into figures tied to individual sites is difficult because data-center operators are reluctant to disclose usage data. The researchers extracted locations and building footprints for as many U.S. hyperscale facilities as possible, then converted power capacity into theoretical electricity use. Because their inventory included only facilities they could verify, the authors said the estimates were likely undercounts.

Oregon's figures illustrate how quickly the state-level share can change. Analyses presented to the state by ECOnorthwest and the University of Virginia showed the data-center share of electricity use rising from about 3 percent in 2015 to about 23 percent in 2025. The group estimated data centers were associated with approximately 96 percent of Oregon's electricity-sales growth between 2020 and 2025.

The Nature Sustainability study's authors wrote that data centers can “no longer be understood as a marginal component” of Oregon's electricity demand. In its most recent biennial energy report, the Oregon Department of Energy said the state “currently lacks a policy mechanism to address emissions from data center load growth.”

Many states are grappling with the effects of recent data-center buildouts. In Virginia, the study included 142 facilities, together accounting for about 15 percent of the state's electricity sales. Dominion Energy, the state's largest electricity seller, estimated that data centers purchased 28 percent of the electricity its Virginia subsidiary sold last year. Electricity prices and utility planning also differ from state to state, so the national share does not capture the planning conditions facing each state.

Emissions estimates likewise depend on the energy mix of the grid serving each campus. Using federal power-plant data, researchers estimated average emissions for each confirmed data center, finding rates from 10 grams of carbon dioxide per kilowatt-hour in Washington's hydropower-heavy grid to 325 grams in Virginia's fossil-fuel-backed grid. The catalogued facilities together emit more annually than the carbon-dioxide equivalent of countries such as Jordan, Kenya and Guatemala, the study said. The estimates reflect both facility demand and the energy mix where the power is generated.

Grid averages fail to capture a growing part of the data-center power system: on-site power plants. Frequently gas-fired, the generators are built at or near hyperscale campuses and often operate behind the meter, meaning their electricity may never touch a public grid. As of June, about 2 gigawatts of gas generators powered four data centers around the country, including Elon Musk's xAI sites in Memphis, Tennessee. The Environmental Integrity Project, a watchdog group, said 143 gigawatts of gas plants currently have been proposed to power data centers nationwide.

Most of the proposed gas capacity could push the hyperscale buildout outside the Harvard study's map. West Virginia had exactly zero campuses confirmed in the Nature Sustainability study, yet ranked second only to Texas in proposed direct gas capacity, with more than 15 gigawatts tagged by the Environmental Integrity Project. If built, the projects could reshape the geography of U.S. data centers and make their electricity use less visible in utility-sales and grid-demand data because behind-the-meter power does not appear as utility purchases.

Many proposals may never break ground. The Environmental Integrity Project said only about two dozen of the 74 proposed plants it tagged were currently permitted for construction under the Clean Air Act.

“What we’re seeing is already striking, but it’s only a snapshot of a rapidly expanding industry,” lead author Gianluca Guidi said in a statement. Guidi said the team's electricity and emissions estimates “could already be substantially larger now in 2026.” The authors said the speed of hyperscale buildouts means the figures can change quickly. They also said the recent buildout suggested applications for new on-site facilities would continue to arrive.

There is currently no federal apparatus to track or check the figures. Surveys of utility sales generally label customers as residential, commercial, industrial or transportation-related, leaving data-center demand inside broader totals. In March, Senators Elizabeth Warren and Josh Hawley wrote to the Energy Information Administration, arguing: “Without this data, policymakers, utility companies, and local communities are operating in the dark.” The Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act, an infrastructure-permitting bill under consideration in the Senate, would direct the agency to explicitly measure data-center electricity usage.

State regulators are already weighing dedicated generation projects for data centers. The Public Utilities Commission of Nevada has moved to approve three methane gas power plants in the last three months for projects at and near the Tahoe Reno Industrial Center in Storey County. NV Energy told regulators that rapid data-center growth had outpaced available regional generation capacity. Tract Capital Management and its subsidiary Fleet Data Centers are pushing two behind-the-meter methane gas-powered plants, the Peru Ridge and South Valley power plants.

If approved, the Peru Ridge and South Valley plants would generate more than 360 megawatts of electricity with methane gas-fired engines, according to filings, enough to serve about 396,000 homes. Switch, a major data-center developer in Nevada, has proposed a 510-megawatt gas-fired plant. It would be one of a dozen new delivery points tied to a gas-pipeline expansion planned by the Great Basin Gas Transmission Company. More gas-fired plants are likely to be planned in northern Nevada as the region's methane gas pipeline expands.

The verified sample already suggests that the facilities use roughly 2 percent of U.S. electricity, but the study's map did not include every campus or dedicated generator. State shares vary widely, with many states in the double digits, and behind-the-meter power can stay outside utility-sales and grid-demand data. Oregon's energy agency says it lacks a policy mechanism for data-center emissions, a Senate bill would direct the EIA to measure the load, and Nevada regulators are considering three methane gas plants. Guidi's warning that the estimates may already be larger in 2026 leaves the study's total as a current snapshot rather than a final count.
