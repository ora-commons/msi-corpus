## FCC clears way for prison cellphone jamming requests

**Subtype:** fact

- Corrections agencies must prove that jamming technology works and will not affect communications outside a prison before receiving FCC approval.
- The FCC will issue annual certificates to confirm that approved jamming technology continues to work as planned.

## South Carolina awaits Federal Register publication before seeking FCC permission

At a Federal Communications Commission vote on November 19, commissioners approved a regulatory change allowing state prisons and local jails to seek permission to jam cellphone signals. The vote came a day after South Carolina’s top law enforcement officials called for the regulation to pass, following more than a decade of advocacy by state leaders. Chairman Brendan Carr and Commissioner Olivia Trusty voted for the change, while Commissioner Anna Gomez agreed in part and dissented in part.

Before a state prison or local jail may begin jamming cellphone signals, the agency must obtain FCC permission, prove the technology works and show that it will not affect communications outside the prison. Wireless Telecommunications Bureau attorney Andrew Ware said the FCC would then issue annual certificates to make sure the technology continues to work as planned.

State prisons director Joel Anderson said South Carolina was ready to apply as soon as the rule was officially added to the Federal Register. “South Carolina is ready to move forward and institute this crucial public safety technology,” Anderson said in a statement to the Daily Gazette on Thursday. “We will work methodically and strategically to make sure the public, our staff and inmates are safe.” Carr said adding the rule to the Federal Register could take up to a few weeks.

The Department of Corrections plans to deploy the technology one facility at a time, beginning with the Broad River Road complex, which includes five separate prisons, Anderson said. The department will need more state funding, according to spokeswoman Chrysti Shain, but the amount is unclear because it will depend on which companies can prove their jamming technology works.

Over the past two years, state legislators spent $18.5 million on technology that tracks cellphones in prisons, allowing officials to report illegal phones and get them turned off. Shain said the same technology should be able to block cellphone signals, but that aspect remains untested because jamming was previously illegal.

Commissioners agreed that contraband cellphones are a major public-safety issue. Officials have said inmates used the devices to have people killed, run scams, and traffic guns and drugs. In South Carolina, prisoners used cellphones to orchestrate a hit on contraband officer Capt. Robert Johnson in 2010; Johnson survived six bullet wounds at close range. The incident kicked off state leaders’ push to flush the phones out of prisons, and the U.S. Attorney’s Office said the largest crime ring in state history also relied on inmates with cellphones.

“While there is no dispute that this is a problem worth solving, one question has been how to effectively do so while protecting legitimate communications,” Trusty said. She and Carr said the regulation addresses that concern through specific requirements. The safeguards include notifying nearby communities, proving jammers will not interfere with other signals and alerting the FCC immediately if a jammer is taken off prison property.

Gomez said she was concerned that broad availability of jammers could put them in the hands of bad actors who might sell them for use outside prisons, leading to more crimes and other chaos. “I want to make sure that we don’t harm public safety in the name of helping public safety,” she said. Gomez said the regulation addressed many of her concerns through its requirements.

Gomez remained concerned that the regulation did not do enough to ensure prisoners can communicate with their families outside prison walls. She said the FCC’s primary job in prisons is to ensure access to communication, not block it. “While correctional facilities should have safety and security services like jamming to prevent criminal activity, it is not the responsibility of the families of incarcerated people to pay the costs of these measures,” Gomez said. Carr said inmates are not spending thousands of dollars on contraband cellphones to talk with their families when they can use prison-provided phones.

States including South Carolina have tried other ways of keeping phones out of prisons, but none have proved as effective as jamming, Carr said. He said prisoners will continue to obtain contraband and commit crimes without cellphones, but stopping the signals will make that more difficult. “This itself is not going to be a silver bullet, but I think it’s time that we stop denying this tool to state and local officials,” Carr said.
