## Pennsylvania reports nine deaths and about 340 hospitalizations this year

Pennsylvania health officials have reported more than 1,600 measles cases since April, nine measles-associated deaths and about 340 hospitalizations this year as they work to contain the nation’s worst measles outbreak. Weekly cases have declined since peaking in early September, but officials do not believe community spread is yet under control. The deaths include infants and adults.

The U.S. has reported more than 6,000 measles cases so far this year, the highest annual count in more than three decades, and lost its measles-elimination status earlier this month. Pennsylvania’s outbreak is the latest in a string of historically large outbreaks in Texas, Utah, Arizona, South Carolina and Wisconsin since early 2025. The disease has predominantly hit the state’s Amish and Plain communities, though outsiders have fallen ill, too. For a nation that had kept the vaccine-preventable disease at bay for decades, the outbreak and the effort to contain it represent a new reality.

Hundreds of state health employees are focused on ending the outbreak through vaccination and education campaigns, a toll-free hotline and tracing the close contacts of each reported case, according to Dr. Debra Bogen, Pennsylvania’s health secretary. “It’s all hands on deck,” she said. Since April, public-health officials have sought to engage thousands in Amish, Mennonite and other Plain communities scattered across more than 40 counties, many of whom have become increasingly wary of vaccines.

As measles cut a path through unvaccinated swaths of Pennsylvania this summer, Jonas Kurtz made sure all seven of his children had received the two shots needed for immunity. Since then, none has contracted the highly contagious disease. Several unvaccinated members of Kurtz’s extended family came down with the telltale rash and fever following a home wedding attended by hundreds. The host had apparently caught the virus at a horse auction in Lancaster County, the epicenter of the state’s outbreak. “That’s how it spreads in the Amish world,” said Kurtz, who grew up Amish outside Punxsutawney but left the church with his wife over a disagreement about their faith.

In Jefferson County, where Kurtz lives, reported cases rank third-highest in Pennsylvania. Statewide, about 340 people have been hospitalized this year, and officials announced a ninth measles-associated death on Monday; the dead include infants and adults. Large winter gatherings are the clearest concern for officials. Weddings are often held after the harvest and draw guests from other counties and states, which could fuel another surge.

Vaccination views remain mixed within Amish and other Plain communities. There are no religious prohibitions against vaccination, but many have preferred traditional remedies and developing natural immunity over medical interventions; others have routinely opted to get the shots. Their cultural beliefs also vary widely, as do the colors of their buggy tops: gray, yellow, white and black.

The Wall Street Journal reported that statements by Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. have fed some of that hesitancy. In 2021, he played down measles’ risks to a crowd in Lancaster; more recently, he said the MMR vaccine contains “aborted fetus debris” and questioned whether measles caused the state’s deaths. Last week, he called the vaccine the most effective way to prevent measles.

Dr. Jennifer Wallace, who oversees Pennsylvania’s 59 state health centers, turned to pop-up vaccination clinics to reach communities across different religious and cultural backgrounds. Since May, she has organized more than 290 clinics in fire halls, church basements and homes, administering 7,500 shots. More residents have come to accept the MMR vaccine as measles has spread and disrupted harvests and other manual work.

In southeastern Chester County, a firehouse clinic drew only a few dozen people in early August. An Amish man then called Dale Weiser, the county health department’s clinical-services supervisor, and asked her to bring vaccines to his home. Weiser and her colleagues vaccinated more than 100 people there over two days, she said. Word spread, and more than 50 households have since welcomed her in. “If you’re talking about a group of people who travel by horse and buggy,” Weiser said, “you have to go there.”

More cases are occurring among adults, a rarity for a disease long considered a childhood illness. Measles also is infecting unvaccinated pregnant women, raising risks of hospitalization, pregnancy loss and congenital measles, according to researchers from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention.

Earlier this week, at the Central Pennsylvania Clinic in Belleville, Dr. Katherine Mascia prepared to vaccinate two Amish families. Boxes of measles, mumps and rubella vaccines sat in a refrigerator in a barnlike, wood-timbered building decorated with quilts. A geneticist who never expected to see a measles case, Mascia has explained in clinic newsletters that vitamin A does not prevent measles and that natural infection carries serious risks, such as pneumonia and encephalitis.

Pennsylvania has reported more than 1,600 cases since April, including 209 in Mifflin County, home to the clinic and the state’s second-highest total. Mascia said actual local cases could be “a hundred times” higher. Amish families typically lack health insurance, and some must hire drivers to reach a medical center, so many treat sick relatives at home, leaving cases unreported.

The area, known as Big Valley, lies between two long Appalachian ridges, its patchwork of farmland far from any major city or interstate. Laundry hangs from lines, and yellow- and white-topped buggies clop down single-lane roads. Girls in dresses and boys in straw hats play tag outside schoolhouses. The Central Pennsylvania Clinic specializes in treating children with genetic disorders and has built trust partly by meeting with families for an hour at each visit. More families have accepted the MMR vaccine as the disease has spread and disrupted harvests and other manual work. After the measles-associated death of an 18-year-old woman in September, sign-ups for shots at the clinic jumped from 25 to 70.

Opinions still vary house by house over whether to get vaccinated or let the disease provide its own immunity. Korie Yoder, a produce farmer, chose not to vaccinate his family, and three of his children were inside a white farmhouse fighting off the disease. His four other children had already recovered; his wife had measles in 1988. Yoder suffered through a bout about three weeks earlier and traveled to a clinic for an IV. “I was down for a couple weeks,” he said. “The last are down with it right now.”

His neighbor, Sam Peachey, a florist with a pick-your-own flowers farm, said he, his wife and their five children have long been vaccinated for measles. “Churches encourage us to keep our opinions to ourselves,” he said. “We’re not scared of the vaccine, but a lot of people are.” Peachey doesn’t believe the outbreak is slowing. “Oh, it’s still happening,” he said. “It’s still wedding season, so a lot of people are being exposed.”

In Big Valley, many believe a single person brought the virus back from the regular horse auction in New Holland, in Lancaster County, the hub of a settlement of roughly 45,000 Amish. Taking a break at the machine-repair shop he runs behind his home, Allen Hoover, an Old Order Mennonite and a former administrator at the Parochial Medical Center in New Holland, said the pandemic undid much of the recent progress in vaccine acceptance. His family is vaccinated, but six children across the road got measles, with none of the cases reported. Three of their adult siblings vaccinated their own children after seeing the disease’s effects, but Hoover doesn’t think many people have changed their views. Locals said attendance at the horse auctions, where hundreds of bidders sit together for hours, hasn’t dropped. Cases still pop up locally, but Hoover thinks the worst has passed. “We had a wave that was vastly underreported,” he said. “There weren’t enough daughters to go around helping people.”

Pennsylvania health officials said community spread is not yet under control as winter weddings approach. The state’s response combines vaccination and education campaigns, a toll-free hotline and close-contact tracing, but officials said large gatherings could fuel another surge.
