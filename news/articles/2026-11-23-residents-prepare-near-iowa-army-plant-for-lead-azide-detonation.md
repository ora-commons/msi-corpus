## Public guidance urges sealed homes, indoor animals and cleanup afterward

Residential buildings stand near the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant in Middletown, Iowa, as residents make plans for the US Army's detonation of a degraded stockpile of lead azide. Iowans in the region were told to stay inside, keep windows and doors closed, bring pets and livestock indoors, and clean cars, windowsills and outdoor surfaces afterward.

Nearly 400 people live in Middletown, a small community about 160 miles (257km) south-east of Des Moines, and thousands more in surrounding areas could be affected by detonated material that could disperse in the air and land on the ground. The US Army plans to detonate several tons of the chemicals by Tuesday.

Government lawyers told the Eighth Circuit Court of Appeals in a filing late Saturday that the Army planned to invoke emergency provisions to proceed despite the court's permit hold, saying continued delays would make the material more dangerous as the weather turned colder. The lawyers wrote: “To protect its personnel and the surrounding communities, the Army needs to take swift action.” They added: “Protection of human health and the environment demand action now, not delay.”

The Eighth Circuit Court of Appeals granted the environmentalists' request on Thursday to put on hold a permit that had cleared the way for the detonation. The operation was initially planned for Friday, but the Army later moved it to Monday after an outpouring of public concern about potential health and environmental effects. The Army now plans to detonate by Tuesday.

Government lawyers said the Army does not need a permit to take emergency action against an immediate threat from explosive material. They said the standards of a federal law regulating hazardous waste do not apply in situations when an explosive or munitions emergency response specialist determines there is an immediate threat to health, safety or the environment.

Officials contend that the chemicals are unstable and that the risk of an uncontrolled explosion is growing, particularly as temperatures drop. They said there is a risk the chemicals may explode as temperatures are expected to fall below 30F (-1C) in the coming days. In March, staff at the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant found several dozen barrels of degraded lead azide, which is used in munitions production. Crystallization created what officials described as an unstable and dangerous situation, prompting the Army to assemble experts in munitions chemistry, engineering and environmental safety to develop a plan.

According to documents from the US Environmental Protection Agency, alternatives such as neutralizing and decomposing the drums' contents with other chemicals were considered. Army officials said handling the drums would increase the probability of an accidental, fatal detonation. The Iowa Environmental Council, Sierra Club and Earthjustice disagreed, arguing in their petition that other alternatives were not fully considered. They also criticized officials for not sharing more information with the public.

An advisory states, “The detonation will result in an air plume with some lead-containing particles which will disperse in the air and deposit on the ground.” The same advisory states, “Lead is toxic to humans and animals.”

The Iowa Environmental Council, Sierra Club and Earthjustice have called for a review of the emergency permit that the US Environmental Protection Agency approved earlier this month. In a Sunday statement, the three organizations questioned claims that all alternatives had received appropriate consideration. “The Army seemed more concerned over the weekend with finding a way to sidestep the judicial process than on meeting the public’s demands for greater transparency, notice to affected communities, and far stronger protection of people, property, and the environment from serious harm,” said Thien Chau, an attorney at Earthjustice.
