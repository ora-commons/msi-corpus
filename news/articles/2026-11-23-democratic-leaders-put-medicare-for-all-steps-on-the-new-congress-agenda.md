## A 56-seat Senate majority still leaves the 60-vote filibuster threshold ahead

Proposals that once drew little more than long-shot talk in Democratic politics now sit at the top of the party's agenda for the Congress that convenes in January. House Democratic leader Hakeem Jeffries of New York, on track to become House speaker, said the incoming majority intends to take swift action on them within its first 100 days, starting with a reversal of recent Medicaid cuts that threaten to leave millions of people without care and a revival of the tax breaks that help people who buy insurance through the Affordable Care Act.

The Democrats also have far more ambitious healthcare goals as they prepare to take charge in the House on Jan. 3, 2027, after an election that ousted Republicans. Bernie Sanders, a Vermont independent who caucuses with Democrats, is set to take over the chairmanship of the Senate health committee, where he unveiled his push for portions of Medicare for All.

Jeffries announced his intentions to take steps toward Medicare for All in a post-midterm election speech that stunned health advocates.

"We're going to begin very early on in the new Congress by working to reverse the Medicaid cuts and working to extend the Affordable Care Act tax credits," Jeffries said at a post-election conference of Democrats in Denver. "And then we've got to go further to make sure that we don't just fix what's broken but that we build upon what we need to do," he said, as a heckler tried to shout him down.

Jeffries went on to list what going further would mean. "I think we've got to lower the age of eligibility as it relates to Medicare. I think we've got to make sure that we cover hearing, vision and dental, and we got to make sure that every child in America can be able to see a doctor with high-quality, affordable healthcare." His list — lowering the Medicare eligibility age below 65, adding dental, vision and hearing aid benefits, and opening up healthcare to children — covers all aspects of Medicare for All, as part of the incoming majority's plans.

Those steps essentially outlined the first year of Medicare for All's multiyear phase-in plan, and progressives were thrilled. Rep. Pramila Jayapal, D-Wash., who has been in talks with Jeffries about moving the legislation forward and is a past chair of the Congressional Progressive Caucus, said the party's healthcare debate had been under way for months. "It's been an ongoing conversation over the last several months, about how we acknowledge the urgency of the moment on healthcare and that it can't just be going back to restoring what was," she said.

"We had a sense that he was positively inclined," Jayapal said, "but he just went ahead and did it."

Sanders, who has advocated for healthcare essentially since he arrived in Congress more than 30 years ago, is now seen as an elder statesman on the issue for a new generation of lawmakers, including Rep. Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez, D-N.Y. Jeffries, who had been an early co-sponsor of Medicare for All but backed away once he became House Democratic leader after the 2022 election, recently namechecked Sanders as the "O.G." whose committee will be among those tackling the issue.

Former President Barack Obama also encouraged newly elected Democrats to go beyond the ACA, also known as Obamacare, which was among his signature accomplishments in office. Speaking to incoming Democrats at the Capitol, according to a transcript of his remarks, Obama said: "We got the ACA done, which was wonderful." "But I was very explicit at the time. This is a starter home. It is not the answer," he said. "You should push further and get more," he said.

Democratic leaders are eager to use their incoming wide majorities in the House and the Senate to deliver on their campaign promises, particularly healthcare affordability. About 6 in 10 voters nationally said the federal government should play a bigger role in helping Americans get healthcare than it does now, according to The AP Voter Poll, a survey of more than 100,000 midterm voters. The same number said they are "very concerned" about the cost of healthcare, according to the survey.

The details are still taking shape, and there almost certainly will be pushback from moderate and centrist Democrats who helped win the majorities, especially regarding the costs of any healthcare expansion. Democratic leaders appear increasingly confident that proposals to expand healthcare are no longer far-flung liberal ideas but are basics that voters who sent them to Washington expect from their government.

President Donald Trump has promised to use the power of the presidential veto to swat back the Democratic agenda. But as he takes stock of his final two years in office with Democrats in charge of Congress, he has indicated the potential for dealmaking with Jeffries and Senate Democratic leader Chuck Schumer. At a news conference on Friday, his first after the midterm elections, Trump said about the fellow New Yorkers: "They're all smart." "They're going to send things my way, and I'm going to veto them," he said. "But I think after a month or so they're going to send things and I'm going to say, 'I'm going to veto — unless you want to do this and this and this,'" he said.

Trump suggested that after years of his own Republican Party's efforts on healthcare — largely attempts to repeal and replace Obamacare — perhaps he could find something better with the Democrats. "There's a real possibility that we'll get a lot of great things done, especially on healthcare," he said. "Now, can it be what they want? No, but it can be a combination of what we all want. And we could end up with great healthcare in our country." Democratic Sen.-elect Abdul El-Sayed of Michigan, who aligns with Sanders on this and other issues, recently posted imagery of "TrumpCare," a golden insurance card, in what appeared to be an attempt to entice the president to consider a universal healthcare system.

With their large House majority, Democrats should be able to quickly pass their initial bills to reverse the Medicaid cuts that threaten to leave millions of people without care and restore the ACA tax credits, but the outcome in the Senate is less certain. Democrats will have a 56-seat majority in the 100-member Senate, but most bills in the Senate require a 60-vote threshold to advance over a filibuster, if Republicans try to block the legislation. And Trump's veto threats loom.

Healthcare advocates are pushing Democrats to try anyway and see what happens. "Why bother? That's what members of Congress are elected to do — to try and try hard," said Brad Woodhouse, a seasoned strategist and executive director of the advocacy group Protect Our Care, "and if they fail, to try again."

What remains unsettled is what Democrats will put in a bill, what it will cost, and whether the Senate's arithmetic and the president's veto allow the party to go beyond restoring what was cut. The two steps party leaders have already committed to — reversing the Medicaid cuts that threaten to leave millions of people without care and extending the ACA tax credits — fall in the first 100 days. The expansion proposals Jeffries laid out are the ones that will determine whether Medicare for All moves from the agenda to a plan that survives both chambers and a president.
