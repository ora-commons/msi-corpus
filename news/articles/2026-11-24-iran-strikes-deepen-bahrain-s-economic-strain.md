## Bahrain's reserves fall as IMF forecasts 11.3% contraction

Iran has fired repeated rounds of missiles and drones at Bahrain, a Gulf Arab monarchy that hosts the U.S. Fifth Fleet. The bombardments, including one over the preceding weekend, have hit industrial sites and largely evacuated U.S. military facilities. The Wall Street Journal reported that Iran has also focused on Bahrain during lower-intensity phases of the war, striking it instead of more sensitive targets in Saudi Arabia or the United Arab Emirates that could invite heavy American retaliation.

Bahrain has little room to absorb further punishment. Shipping disruptions have choked exports, hits on the oil and aluminum industries have damaged crucial earners, and public debt has worsened with the cost of fending off the attacks. Families said they are cutting back on restaurant meals and renting out spare rooms to manage rising costs.

The International Monetary Fund forecasts Bahrain's nearly $50 billion gross domestic product will shrink by 11.3% this year. The IMF also forecasts government debt will surge to 163% of GDP, putting the burden fourth-highest globally, behind only Sudan, Japan and Singapore. Economists said a looming double-digit contraction could spiral into a full-blown currency and sovereign-debt crisis unless wealthier Gulf neighbors provide a fresh bailout.

Unlike Japan and Singapore, which borrow largely in their own currencies and hold vast domestic savings, Bahrain relies heavily on foreign financing and has lower reserves. Even before the war, interest payments consumed nearly 40% of government revenue.

Karen Young, a senior fellow at the Washington-based Middle East Institute, said Bahrain remained exposed to Iranian attacks, had little U.S. protection and no sign of a rebuild or significant future U.S. basing presence, and lacked the ability to accommodate Iran politically or financially before a return to war or settlement. “They are low-hanging fruit for Iranian aggression and essentially exposed,” Young said.

Capt. Tim Hawkins, a spokesman for U.S. Central Command, said American forces had helped Bahrain defend against Iranian missiles and drones. The Wall Street Journal, citing satellite imagery, social-media footage and interviews with current and former service members, reported that Iran had repeatedly targeted the U.S. Navy base in Bahrain throughout the war and caused extensive damage. The command headquarters and at least a dozen other buildings were hit hard, along with two satellite communications terminals. The Pentagon has not publicly acknowledged the extent of the damage.

American military facilities contributed an estimated $403 million to Bahrain's economy in 2021 through leases, contracts, employment and local spending, according to a U.S. Navy assessment. Attacks on U.S. facilities around the region have accelerated a re-evaluation of the American military's footprint in the Middle East. Hawkins said the military continually assesses its regional force presence and regularly makes adjustments to meet mission requirements.

The wider Gulf is also struggling with the fallout from a war its countries did not want and that shows no signs of ending. Tourism and conference businesses are suffering, foreign investment is threatened, some investment plans are on hold and others have been rolled back, and oil is getting out of the Gulf at a high cost. Gulf countries worry they could face a more aggressive Iran without the backing of an increasingly unreliable American ally.

H.A. Hellyer, a senior associate fellow at the Royal United Services Institute in London, said Bahrain shows what the war will mean for many of the development models Gulf countries planned for the coming decade. Those models depended on stability and on attracting investment, both financial and human. “There’s a lot of talk about resilience, and that’s an important factor. But the overall framework is in tremendous flux right now,” Hellyer said.

Bahrainis said the war had stoked inflation and raised the price of daily necessities such as food and transportation. Some said they want Americans gone and are quietly cheering Iran on. The country's Shia majority is ruled by a Sunni monarchy that has grown more nervous about Iranian influence during the war. Earlier this month, Bahrain sentenced dozens of Shia Muslim clerics and other religious figures to life in prison on terrorism charges. The government accused the defendants of colluding with Iran against the government.

Bahraini officials had hoped the war was already winding down, allowing the economy to pick back up, but they now face more lost months. Blockages in the Strait of Hormuz have dealt a particularly heavy blow by disrupting the waterway that carries roughly two-thirds of Bahrain's exports to markets outside the Gulf. According to OPEC data, Bahrain's average oil production fell 55% quarter-on-quarter to 46,000 barrels a day from April through June. Chinese imports from Bahrain fell 99% in September from a year earlier, according to Capital Economics.

State-owned Bapco Energies declared force majeure after its flagship refinery was struck in March. The refinery is operating at around 80% capacity and is not expected to reach full capacity before year-end. Aluminium Bahrain, one of the world's largest smelters and a pillar of the country's non-oil economy, idled three smelting lines. The company reported that production fell 41% in the first nine months of the year.

Bond investors are rapidly pricing the risk of a deeper financial crunch. The cost of insuring Bahraini sovereign debt against default through credit-default swaps rose 80% from the start of the year through early October, according to Allianz. The increases were 58% for Qatar and 26% for Kuwait. Central bank data also show that Bahrain's foreign-currency reserves halved to $2.4 billion in September from $5.2 billion in March, leaving little ammunition to defend the Bahraini dinar's longstanding peg to the U.S. dollar.

The U.A.E. provided a roughly $5.3 billion currency swap in April, and Justin Alexander, director of consultancy Khalij Economics, estimates two-thirds has already been used. Bahraini officials say the kingdom will be able to meet its debt commitments this year and are discussing a bailout package with Gulf states including the U.A.E. and Saudi Arabia. Potential creditors are pressing for austerity measures and asset sales, but the government is reluctant to pursue them because it fears stoking public unrest. “Without Gulf financing, Bahrain would probably be staring at a messy devaluation and sovereign default,” Jason Tuvey, deputy chief emerging-markets economist at Capital Economics, said. Bahrain did not reply to a request for comment.
