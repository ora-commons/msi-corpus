## CMS says H.R. 1 left the 1997 SSI safeguard unchanged

On Tuesday, five lawfully present immigrants who receive monthly Supplemental Security Income payments sued the Trump administration over Medicaid restrictions under H.R. 1. Two of the plaintiffs, A.W.M. and G.M., fled their homelands in Sudan and the Republic of Congo to escape war and settled in Providence. The proposed nationwide class action, filed in the U.S. District Court for the District of Rhode Island, challenges restrictions on some noncitizens’ Medicaid coverage that took effect Oct. 1 and asks for temporary and permanent relief blocking the policy nationwide.

The 37-page complaint was filed by lawyers from the National Health Law Program, the ACLU of Rhode Island and the Lawyers’ Committee for Rhode Island. All five plaintiffs are lawfully present in the United States and receive SSI payments each month. Two plaintiffs are from Rhode Island, with one each from Illinois, South Carolina and Missouri.

SSI is a means-tested federal program limited to older adults and people with disabilities who have smaller incomes and resources. Monthly payments are up to $994 for an individual and $1,491 for a couple. Those federal maximum rates are set to rise in January to $1,029 for an individual and $1,544 for a couple.

A.W.M., one of the two men who settled in Providence, is a 69-year-old man living with his wife and two minor children. According to the complaint, A.W.M. fled Sudan because of the ongoing civil war and stopped in Chad and Cameroon before arriving in the United States in February 2024. His green card application is pending, and he began qualifying for SSI payments in August 2024. He and his wife have a combined monthly income of less than $2,400 and pay $1,500 in rent.

Before A.W.M.’s Medicaid coverage ended on Oct. 1, he relied on the benefits to manage issues including knee pain, type 2 diabetes, high blood pressure, high cholesterol, and prostate and stomach pain. The complaint says A.W.M. missed a doctor’s appointment last week because it would have cost $985.65 without insurance, a bill that would have essentially consumed his entire $987 monthly SSI payment.

“He is scared of what will happen when he is no longer able to afford his medications because, if left untreated or unmedicated, he could get very sick or die,” the suit reads.

G.M., the other man who settled in Providence, is a 77-year-old refugee from the Republic of Congo. He fled war in his country with his family and arrived in the United States with his wife and son in April 2024. His only income source is a monthly SSI check under $500.

“Their monthly income covers their rent and utilities, but they already rely on food pantries for food, as they do not have enough money for food, let alone for medical care,” the complaint writes of G.M. and his family.

The filing says G.M. now takes his blood-pressure medication only two or three times a week instead of the prescribed once per day. He has also had to forgo regular optometrist visits for vision problems, including an Oct. 14 appointment he canceled because he could not pay the $150 cost. “If his vision problems remain undiagnosed and untreated, he could go completely blind,” the complaint states.

The complaint names the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services, the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services, and the agencies’ respective leaders, Robert F. Kennedy Jr. and Mehmet Oz, as defendants. The Department of Health and Human Services did not immediately respond to a request for comment Tuesday.

H.R. 1, a sweeping federal tax and spending law, became law in July 2025 and narrowed eligibility for federally funded Medicaid benefits based on immigration status. The complaint says the law did not repeal or amend a separate federal provision dating to 1997 that requires states to extend Medicaid eligibility to noncitizens receiving SSI, provided those noncitizens are subject to the same terms as citizen SSI recipients.

Fewer than 2,000 Rhode Islanders were deemed ineligible for Medicaid coverage under the Oct. 1 restrictions on noncitizens, Kerri White, a spokesperson for the Rhode Island Executive Office of Health and Human Services, said in an email Tuesday. The number of those individuals who also receive SSI was not immediately available.

The Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services issued guidance on April 8 saying H.R. 1 did not amend the SSI safeguard. The agency said it would, however, no longer supply matching funds for Medicaid coverage for certain noncitizen SSI recipients. CMS said the distinction matters because joint funding between states and the federal government is part of what constitutes Medicaid.

H.R. 1 “did not clearly and unambiguously alter the fundamental structure of the Medicaid program, whose ‘cornerstone’ is ‘financial contribution by both the Federal Government and the participating State,’” the CMS guidance reads, quoting an earlier court precedent. “[W]e would not consider health coverage that a state opts to continue to provide with 100 percent state-only funds to be Medicaid.”

The guidance also said there was nothing in the provision’s legislative history suggesting “a clear Congressional intent to require that states provide full Medicaid benefits to qualified noncitizens without receiving federal matching funds for that coverage.”

The lawsuit follows a similar legal challenge in Washington state that resulted in a Sept. 30 preliminary injunction protecting Medicaid coverage for more than 800 residents of that state. The plaintiffs seek a temporary and permanent nationwide block on the disputed policy, but the court must certify the proposed class before the case can proceed as a class action. The complaint says the Department of Health and Human Services “is in sole possession of the information regarding the total number of potential class members across the country.” U.S. District Judge Mary S. McElroy has been assigned the case.
