## Japan recorded 152 scramble responses from April through October

Russian military aircraft entered South Korea’s air-defense identification zone on Tuesday morning, triggering air-raid alerts. The aircraft came “right up to the brink of violating our airspace” near an island 80 miles east of the Korean Peninsula, South Korean Joint Chiefs of Staff spokesman Jang Do-young said. South Korea did not disclose how many aircraft were involved or their type.

South Korea summoned the Russian defense attaché in Seoul to lodge a formal complaint. The Russian Embassy in Seoul said the warplanes committed no violations of international law while conducting the flight near the South Korean island, according to state news outlet TASS.

The Wall Street Journal reported that the series of flights had prompted fighter scrambles and concerns about deepening military ties between Moscow and Beijing. The report said the uptick had added to Asian worries that Moscow was following China’s example by increasing pressure on U.S. partners and allies in the Western Pacific through an expanding range of military exercises.

The report described the recent episodes as a display of Moscow’s power before U.S. regional partners around the time Russian President Vladimir Putin took part in a trilateral meeting with Chinese leader Xi Jinping and President Trump in Shenzhen, China. On Nov. 17, Japan, South Korea and Taiwan each scrambled jet fighters when two unspecified Russian aircraft passed by. Japan said the aircraft violated its airspace and issued a “strong protest.”

The same day, two Russian Tu-95s — strategic bombers capable of carrying nuclear weapons — landed at an airport in Vietnam, a country that in recent years has forged closer ties with the U.S. On Thursday, Japan said two Russian bombers passed through the strait between Taiwan and Yonaguni Island in Japan’s southwest.

The latest flights were not the first appearance of Russian Tu-95s in the region. In December, Russian and Chinese bombers flew near Japan and South Korea in a joint patrol that Tokyo described as a show of force amid a diplomatic falling-out between Beijing and Tokyo.

The head of Taiwan’s National Security Council, Joseph Wu, said on X that it looked like Russia’s military collaboration with China was getting firmer. “Not good for the #1stIslandChain democracies,” Wu added, referring to the chain stretching from Japan through the Philippines that the report described as a key U.S. line of defense in any conflict in the Pacific.

Japan had 152 scramble responses to Russian aircraft this year from April through October, compared with 59 during the same period last year, according to defense ministry data.

James Brown, a Russian foreign policy expert at Temple University in Tokyo, said Russia carried out the latest flights with China in mind, as a way to “demonstrate to China that they can be useful, that they are a valuable partner and undiminished as a military power, despite their vast loss in Ukraine.”

Tensions between the Kremlin and Tokyo have been simmering in recent months. In August, Putin visited the southern Kuril Islands, and weeks later Russia held live-fire drills nearby. Last month, Russia detained a Japanese journalist on espionage charges, which Tokyo denies.

Earlier this month, Japan Tobacco lost $8.9 billion after Russia seized temporary management of its assets, and more than a third of the company is owned by the Japanese government. Japanese Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi said she raised the cases of the journalist and the Japanese companies with Putin when they met last week in Shenzhen at the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation summit.

Brown said Japan’s response has been muted. He suggested more forceful responses could include expelling Russian diplomats, closing Russian facilities and providing more aid for Ukraine such as air-defense systems. “They press where they see weakness, they back off where they find a firm response,” he said of Russia.
