## Roberts calls the midterm result a messaging and economic problem

Rob Schmitz said the midterms did not go the way President Trump hoped and that the result would shape The Heritage Foundation's and Republicans' plans. Roberts, appearing on NPR on Nov. 7, said the larger-scale Republican defeats did not amount to a wholesale rejection of Trump's agenda. In remarks to NPR's Mary Louise Kelly on election night, he described the result as "a complaint by voters about the economic consequences of the Iran conflict." Democrats held majorities in the House and Senate, a scale Roberts called "this week's major defeat."

Roberts conceded that he misjudged the outcome: "I actually thought the midterms would be OK." He added that even people more pessimistic than he was did not expect a defeat on that scale. The first step forward, he argued, was "to read reality truthfully," including acknowledging that "at least in terms of messaging, the message from the political right, from the administration, was insufficient." Roberts said the administration also had to manage what he called "lawfare" and the "nonsense" he expected from the incoming Democratic majorities while articulating a policy vision that returned to domestic policy and "kitchen-table economics issues."

## Roberts urges an Iran victory declaration despite lingering energy inflation

Although he remained supportive of the Iran war, Roberts called it "a major problem" politically. He said he had urged the administration since April and May to declare victory because the conflict's economic consequences had aggravated existing inflationary concerns that Trump inherited. Speaking with Heritage's national-security colleagues, Roberts said President Trump "could have declared victory given the military achievements as early as May or June." Had he done so, Roberts thought the war's diminished economic disadvantages could have appeared in time to affect the election positively from Heritage's perspective.

Roberts said the declaration remained important soon because Heritage's economist colleagues expected it to take several months for the inflationary pressure brought on by energy prices to come down. His recommended posture was to leave "some naval and air superiority in Iran, maybe not to the extent of the deployment now" and, when the Iranians misbehave, "zing them with cruise missile attacks." He paired that recommendation with what he called "a really good economic strangulation campaign" by Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent.

Roberts said the Iran conflict had not by itself caused all of the country's economic difficulties but had aggravated longstanding problems. He called it "objectively true" that the Trump administration "inherited very high inflation from the previous administration" and that economic measures "were improving the week before the war in Iran." Heritage's chief economist, E.J. Antoni, had predicted "there would have been a Trump economic boom had he not started the war in Iran," Roberts said. He added that Iran, particularly by increasing energy prices, had aggravated inflationary pressures.

## Heritage supports tariffs while seeking a narrower policy

Schmitz described the election as largely understood as a referendum on the economy and the cost of living for average Americans. He said gas prices had been a problem because of the war with Iran and President Trump's tariffs, which Roberts had initially opposed before embracing. Roberts rejected moving away from tariffs and said he and Heritage were "very supportive" of "a true reciprocal tariff regime."

Roberts said Heritage had advocated since the "so-called Liberation Day" for tariff policy to focus on the "5 to 10 worst transgressors" of "free trade." Had the administration done so, he thought the American people might have embraced the policy, "maybe even a bipartisan way." He still saw "a future for that policy," though it would have to be "more circumscribed than it's been." Roberts also said Heritage would "continue to preach for a more restrained national security posture," which the Trump administration had done "a very good job" of until the Iran War.

## Heritage makes affordability a priority for 2028

Schmitz framed Republicans' post-midterm question as where to go next. He said Heritage had long supplied answers, dating to the "mandate for leadership" the think tank wrote for President Reagan in 1981, and that the foundation had informed many Trump administration priorities through Project 2025. Roberts, a political strategist and key architect of Project 2025, has led Heritage as president since 2021.

Schmitz described the right's former agenda as clear — smaller government, lower taxes and free trade — and called Project 2025 a huge effort to define a new post-Reagan conservative movement. He said, "But you've had challenges, I think, keeping everyone aligned," and added, "It seems there's, like, sort of a generational civil war happening in the party," as influencers and other right-wing think tanks tried to define the party. His 2028 focus was Heritage's core vision and how the organization could regain voter trust.

Roberts agreed there were tensions on the right. With President Trump "leaving the stage in early 2029," he said conservative policy leaders were trying to articulate a vision for the future. Heritage had two priorities. The first was "to actually provide the policy solutions for Americans' legitimate worries about affordability." The second was confronting openness to socialist policies. Roberts said Heritage was "gratified that still a slight majority of Americans think that socialist policies are bad" but worried that somewhere between 40- and 45% of Americans were willing to try them.

Conservatives had to explain why the policies of "legitimate socialists" were problematic rather than simply "pointing to socialists, pointing to neo-Marxists and just labeling them as that," Roberts said. "The American people actually want answers." Heritage was trying to keep its message focused on the essentials, he added: "At Heritage, we're trying to keep things simple. We're trying to address the kitchen-table issues."

## Personnel, corruption and the limits of 2028 endorsements

Schmitz said there had been personnel news the previous day: Susie Wiles was out as President Trump's chief of staff and Stephen Miller was in. He framed the change as a signal about the administration's path. Roberts praised Miller as "a great promotion of a great patriot, Stephen Miller" and said it signaled to the "radical-left majority coming into Congress" and the political right that President Trump and his administration were "not rolling over." Roberts noted that people on the political left "disagree maybe with Stephen Miller with everything" and probably disagree with him and Heritage "with everything."

Roberts described Miller as "an extremely competent, politically conservative, very intellectual policymaker." For the political right charting its way forward after the defeat, he said, "I think Stephen's a perfect choice." Roberts also said Trump was signaling a new era and needed Miller's acumen, particularly his ability "to be on offense even with a large Democrat majority in the House and Senate."

Schmitz said corruption was also among the top voter concerns going into the midterms. He had been in Hungary earlier in the year, where he said corruption played a big role in Viktor Orbán's defeat. He said, "People see the president and his family benefiting from cryptocurrency, other investments," and framed the issue as damage to Republicans at the polls. Roberts said exit polls indicated that voters viewed corruption as a concern and called the Hungary comparison "very accurate." He said "the Orbán government's policies were very good," but noted that the government had been in power for 16 years and "there clearly was corruption that was a problem."

Roberts said the administration, Republican allies in Congress and the incoming Democratic majority needed to remember that "this has been a very anti-power election." Americans were saying they were tired, he said, and the problem was not confined to either party: "It isn't just a problem on the right. It isn't just a problem on the left." Roberts said Americans were paying attention and wanted leaders "who are not engaged."

Heritage does not endorse candidates, Roberts said, rejecting any suggestion that the organization would back Marco Rubio or JD Vance in 2028. "Nice try, but that's not something we do or can do at Heritage," he said. "We don't get into endorsements." What mattered, he said, was that whoever became the conservative standard-bearer in 2028 and whichever conservative officials ran that year should discuss issues addressing "affordability and economic concerns."
