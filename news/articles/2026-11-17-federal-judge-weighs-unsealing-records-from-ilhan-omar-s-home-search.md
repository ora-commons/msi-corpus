## Redacted filings could be released as soon as next Monday

More than a month after Department of Homeland Security agents searched Rep. Ilhan Omar's Washington, D.C., home, a federal judge heard arguments Tuesday on requests to unseal records from the search. More than a dozen news organizations, including The Associated Press, maintain that the public has a First Amendment right to the search warrant and other court filings that led to the Oct. 12 search.

Omar has denied wrongdoing and says the underlying immigration-fraud investigation is politically motivated. Chief Judge James Boasberg said during the hearing that it was premature to unseal the 67-page search warrant affidavit. The document contains information about witnesses and other details of the government’s investigation, and Boasberg said it would remain under wraps for now.

The judge said he would privately review other sealed court filings for possible public release as soon as next week. He gave the government until Friday to provide proposed redactions to filings that could shed light on some of the underlying legal arguments. Boasberg said redacted versions of those filings could be released as soon as next Monday.

In a court filing last month, the government argued that unsealing the court records before a charging decision would jeopardize the investigation. Attorneys for the news organizations argue that the government’s privacy concerns are outweighed by the “enormous public interest in and historic significance of a search warrant executed at the home of a sitting member of Congress.”

The Associated Press has reported that Omar, a naturalized U.S. citizen born in Somalia, “has been a target of anti-immigrant rhetoric by President Donald Trump and many Republican allies.” She denied wrongdoing and said in a social media post that the search was an “attempt to target political enemies and weaponize federal law enforcement.”

Federal authorities are investigating potential fraud in her family’s immigration paperwork and have issued grand jury subpoenas to some relatives, according to a person briefed on the investigation who was not authorized to discuss the ongoing inquiry.

Agents conducted the court-authorized search less than a month before the Nov. 3 midterm elections, when Omar won her reelection bid. The Justice Department historically has avoided public investigative actions involving a candidate within 60 days of an election.

Omar said Department of Homeland Security agents seized 17 items during the search, including phones, notebooks, a Somali-language children’s book and a vintage camera that her husband had received as a gift.

Boasberg, who was nominated to the bench by Democratic President Barack Obama, has been a frequent target of Trump’s ire. Trump called for Boasberg’s impeachment last year after the judge blocked his administration from deporting planeloads of Venezuelan migrants to El Salvador under the Alien Enemies Act.

Justice Department attorney Alessandra Serano said the government is not trying to keep the affidavit under seal permanently. “It’s not our position. It’s never been our position,” Serano told the judge.

Boasberg noted that Vice President JD Vance and Department of Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin had already publicly commented on the contours of the investigation. Serano said she could not control what others in the Trump administration said about the case.

“It seems to be a common refrain,” Boasberg replied.

The next procedural steps are for the Justice Department to provide proposed redactions by Friday and for Boasberg to review the other filings privately. Redacted versions could then become public as soon as next Monday, while the search warrant affidavit remains sealed.
