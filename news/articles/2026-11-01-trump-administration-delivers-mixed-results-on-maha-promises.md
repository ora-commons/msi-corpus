## EPA rollbacks and corporate interests draw criticism from supporters

Donald Trump and Robert F. Kennedy Jr. joined forces just over two years ago to build Make America Healthy Again, or MAHA, around promises to reduce chronic disease rates, reform the food system and clean up harmful chemicals. NPR health correspondent Will Stone described the administration's record as mixed. He identified new dietary guidelines as an obvious victory and said they represented a real departure from previous guidance.

The guidelines encourage people to eat more saturated fat and avoid processed foods, and they raised daily protein goals. Although the guidance promotes more saturated fat, the administration did not change existing saturated-fat limits, Stone noted. The response among MAHA supporters remained mixed overall, he said, particularly as the coalition pursued food, water and air concerns tied to toxic chemicals.

Stone said some supporters rate the administration's track record poorly under MAHA's own rubric. The Environmental Protection Agency is central to that policy because the agency shapes much of it. Kelly Ryerson, a MAHA activist focused on pesticides who runs the nonprofit American Regeneration, said: “I cannot think of a single good thing that the EPA has done during this administration.”

Ryerson said the EPA rolled back protections covering forever chemicals in drinking water and approved pesticides that she said had no business being approved.

Stone said some chemicals identified in the administration's own MAHA report as possibly harmful for children's health were the same ones the EPA had moved to weaken protections around. He called the contrast between the report and the agency's actions striking.

Stone used glyphosate, the main ingredient in the weed killer Roundup, as a major example. Many in MAHA wanted meaningful action on glyphosate because of cancer concerns. Instead, the Trump administration backed the company that makes Roundup in a high-profile Supreme Court case, which the company ultimately won. Trump also signed an executive order encouraging domestic production of the pesticide. Stone said the move disenchanted many MAHA supporters.

In November, after the 2024 election, Kennedy told NPR that Trump expected measurable progress in reducing chronic diseases in two years. Barry Popkin, a professor of nutrition at the University of North Carolina Gillings School of Public Health, said he did not see any evidence that this had happened.

Popkin said the administration had raised important issues about pesticides and ultra-processed foods, but he also described some of its actions as dangerous. He said it had unleashed people in the country who would not get vaccinated and cited major measles outbreaks. Stone also pointed to large foodborne illness outbreaks that affected thousands of Americans in the last year or two.

Tension over who speaks for the movement beyond Kennedy surfaced at a recent high-profile MAHA summit in D.C. Stone said the summit was stacked with figures from corporate America. Ryerson said she was mortified and said: “I don't even recognize this iteration of MAHA.” She said grassroots participants want corporations out of the movement.

Stone said Ryerson believes the MAHA brand may need to evolve in 2027, depending on how things shake out in the midterms. At the two-year mark, the administration's record includes new dietary guidelines that Stone called an obvious victory, Popkin's assessment that no measurable progress was evident, EPA disputes from within the movement and a corporate summit that left one activist questioning the movement's direction.
