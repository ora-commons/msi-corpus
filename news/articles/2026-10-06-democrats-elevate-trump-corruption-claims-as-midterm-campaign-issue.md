## Trump memecoin dinner offer for top investors intensifies ethics scrutiny

The cryptocurrency company behind President Donald Trump's memecoin, $Trump, invited the 185 largest holders of the token to what it called "the most exclusive dinner in the world" at the president's members-only Trump National club in Washington, the Guardian reported. The top 29 buyers were promised an invitation to an additional "VIP reception with your favourite president and one of the legends," and the top four donors were promised a Trump 18-karat gold watch. The aim appeared to be to boost sales of the coin over the next two months. Trump personally profits from the coin, and disclosures show he made about $636 million from it last year.

The dinner invitation arrived a day after Trump told supporters at a Hispanic heritage month event at the White House that he was "the only president that donated his salary," explaining that his $550,000 annual presidential pay had been directed to "fixing the White House" in his second term.

Senate Minority Leader Chuck Schumer, the Democrats' leader in the US Senate, posted on social media that "Donald Trump is vying to become the most corrupt person to ever walk the face of the earth." A 30-second advertisement from Chris Pappas, a New Hampshire representative running for re-election, reflects a more widespread mood in favor of putting corruption center stage. With control of both chambers of Congress at stake on November 3, Democrats have seized on the dinner invitation as a political gift as they seek to convert concerns over Trump's perceived corruption into a midterm wave.

The White House rejected the corruption allegations. In a statement, spokesman Davis Ingle called the accusations "irresponsible" and likely to "reinforce the public's distrust in what they read." Ingle said "President Trump only acts in the best interests of the American public" and that "There are no conflicts of interest," citing pharmaceutical pricing negotiations, the TrumpRx.gov drug discount site, trade policy and the schedule F reclassification of federal employees as evidence Trump "is delivering on his promise to empower the forgotten men and women of our country."

Trump's personal financial disclosures, according to the Guardian, show he earned $2.2 billion from his various business dealings in his first year back in the White House — including $1.4 billion from World Liberty Financial, his family's cryptocurrency enterprise. Observers identified three main areas of potential abuse: crypto, a venture Trump once denounced as a "fraud" five years ago; stock trading by government officials, including the president; and the predictions markets, represented by companies such as Kalshi and Polymarket, both of which have links to Donald Trump Jr and have raised concerns about well-connected figures profiting through bets on government decisions and the content of official speeches.

Beyond those sectors, the Guardian identified concerns about "pay to play" practices in which contributors have been granted special access and favorable decisions — including dropped criminal investigations — in exchange for donations to Trump's campaign and political Super Pac. Presidential pardons and sentence commutations have also been directed to contributors to the president's re-election effort, according to the source.

Public concern about government corruption has reached historic levels. A Gallup poll published in September recorded 89 percent of American adults saying the problem was widespread — a level exceeded globally in the past three years only by Lebanon, Peru, Ghana and Nigeria.

"We're facing an ethics crisis which starts and ends with a president who is constantly using his office to find new ways to personally profit," Jordan Libowitz, communications vice-president at Citizens for Responsibility and Ethics in Washington, a non-partisan watchdog, told the Guardian. "With a new story every day about Donald Trump using the presidency to line his pockets, it's no surprise that Americans are losing faith in their government."

Brendan Fischer, director of strategic investigation at the Campaign Legal Center, said the public has been overwhelmed and "numbed" by a "fire hose" of corruption from Trump's second term. "It can become overwhelming trying to keep up with each of the latest examples," Fischer said. "I think the Trump's crypto dealings are incredibly concerning. His investments in the crypto industry open up new avenues for wealthy special interests to channel money to the president. But It's potentially harder for the public to grasp. So it hasn't broken through in the way that it that it probably should."

Dylan Hedtler-Gaudette, director of government affairs at the Project on Government Oversight, said the complexity of the allegations makes their electoral impact hard to disentangle from the cost-of-living concerns driving the broader erosion of Trump's standing. "Support for the Trump administration and for President Trump himself is declining pretty rapidly," he said. "But it's hard to disentangle. Is it about the corruption? Or is it about the affordability crisis continuing and in some ways getting worse?" Hedtler-Gaudette added: "Corruption tends to be in the eye of the beholder. And if you ask different people about the word corruption, they say, 'Yeah, it's a problem.' But if you ask them to define what they mean by corruption, it can diverge wildly depending on how they affiliate and how they identify in terms of their partisanship and their political identity."

Other episodes have been easier to grasp. Trump has said he will use a $400 million plane received from Qatar in place of the existing Air Force One. "It's easy to just throw your hands up and say, 'Everybody's corrupt, nobody's representing my interests, and my life is just not getting any better,'" Fischer said. "That is a dangerous situation for democracy. We need voters to cast their ballots and then remain engaged in the political process to make sure our elected officials are representing the interests of voters and not just their own or the interests of their donors."

Richard Painter, a former White House ethics lawyer under George W Bush now a corporate law professor at the University of Minnesota, said the unpopular war with Iran has created new corruption opportunities. Citing reports that both Donald Trump Jr and his brother, Eric Trump, have invested in drone manufacturers that have secured government contracts, Painter said: "Whenever there's a war, there's an enormous amount of money to be made. People say there are those who die in a war, and those who become millionaires, or maybe in this case billionaires."

"This is what President Eisenhower talked about with the military-industrial complex back in 1961, but we've never had a president whose family actually made money from the military-industrial complex," Painter added. Painter predicted conditions were ripe for "an enormous public backlash" but said Democrats must adopt a credible anti-corruption message after losing public confidence by failing to speak out against Hunter Biden's attempts to leverage his White House access when his father Joe Biden was president. "If they want to win races not only in 2026, but in 2028, they'd better get their act together and focus on ethics," Painter said. One historical model, he suggested, was Jimmy Carter's 1976 anti-corruption campaign, which ran amid lingering anger at Richard Nixon over the Watergate scandal and the Vietnam War.

Democrats have also invoked a more recent inspiration: Hungary's recently elected prime minister, Peter Magyar, who mobilized rising resentment over government corruption in April to unseat the incumbent, Viktor Orbán — a close Trump ally — in an election landslide. Steven Levitsky, a political scientist at Harvard University and co-author, with Daniel Ziblatt, of "How Democracies Die," said the parallel lies in corruption further undermining an already-failing government. "Orbán was successful in part because the economy was pretty good for much of his period in office," Levitsky said. "But eventually it slowed down, and corruption became a major factor." He added: "It's sad but true that in many, many cases in democracies around the world, if incumbents are perceived to be delivering – if the economy is going well, if they brought down inflation, etc – voters will turn a blind eye to corruption. It's only when you combine poor performance with corruption that it becomes salient." And: "Unfortunately, in the United States, one of the things that's benefited Trump is the perception that all of politics is corrupt, mainly because of the extraordinarily outsized role of money in politics." "There are a lot of reasons why Trump is unpopular now," Levitsky added. "Corruption is one of them but I don't think it's the principal one."
