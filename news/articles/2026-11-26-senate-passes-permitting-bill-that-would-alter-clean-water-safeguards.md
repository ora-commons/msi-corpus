## Senate passes permitting bill that would alter clean-water safeguards

**Subtype:** fact

- The US Senate passed the Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act of 2026 by a 62-33 vote, advancing the permitting measure to the House.
- The bill would make it more difficult for states to put protections in place against water pollution.
- The bill would extend the EPA’s mandatory water-pollution permit reviews from five years to 10 years.
- More than 50 groups in communities affected by PFAS pollution opposed the measure.
- The American Chemistry Council and American Petroleum Institute support the bill.

## House debate centers on state authority, permit reviews and PFAS limits

The US Senate passed the Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act of 2026 by a 62-33 vote, and the House is now debating the permitting measure. Also known as Baaja, the bill is intended to reduce the time federal agencies take to approve major energy and infrastructure projects, including power lines, fossil fuel pipelines, natural gas plants and datacenters. The Senate vote advanced a package that combines permitting changes with provisions governing water pollution.

The bill’s water provisions would make it more difficult for states to put protections in place against water pollution, double the interval between automatic reviews of water-pollution permits and reduce transparency about the types and levels of pollution. Another provision would allow industry to sue to undo Environmental Protection Agency guidance for states on water-pollution discharges.

The measure drew wide industry support, including from the American Chemistry Council and American Petroleum Institute. Its supporters said the trade-off would be worthwhile because it would allow energy projects to be built faster and hasten the transition to clean energy. Opponents said industry was using the clean-energy transition as cover to push unnecessary changes to water laws that do nothing to improve permitting. Opposition came from virtually every major US environmental group; those groups said the bill would eliminate some foundational environmental protections.

The Clean Water Action advocacy group, which is lobbying against the bill, said in a statement: “BAAJA is a handout to the oil and gas industry, while doing very little to solve the permitting problems being used to justify those changes.” The group also said, “Congress is trading away protections for states, tribes, wetlands and streams even though those protections are largely unrelated to the years-long delays at the center of the permitting debate.”

More than 50 groups in communities affected by PFAS “forever chemical” pollution opposed the measure. They warned that communities would suffer further if the legislation were approved because it would weaken some of the few mechanisms in US law that regulate chemical discharges. Emily Donovan, co-founder of the Clean Cape Fear advocacy group in Wilmington, North Carolina, said discharge from a nearby PFAS factory has polluted drinking, surface and ground water in the area. The Chemours Company’s Fayetteville Works plant in North Carolina produces GenX, one of the main PFAS compounds polluting the state’s waterways.

Donovan said the law would “put us in more danger.” She said, “We already see how hard it is for the [Environmental Protection Agency (EPA)] to [regulate] Pfas,” and added, “What frustrates us is we don’t need to make it harder.” Donovan argued that the bill would weaken some of the few legal mechanisms available to regulate chemical discharges.

The EPA currently develops criteria that states use to limit discharges into waterways without issuing a formal rule, and those criteria cannot easily be challenged in court. Under the bill, a formal rulemaking process would slow the issuance of clean-water criteria by several years and open the process to lawsuits. States use the criteria to develop limits on PFAS pollution, and Donovan said the change would shift more responsibility onto states that are largely under-resourced and unable to meet the challenge.

The bill would also change the EPA’s mandatory review of water-pollution permits from five years to 10 years. The agency uses the review mechanism to require polluters to track PFAS and other water pollution, and Donovan said the longer interval would prolong public exposure to the chemicals. Advocates fear that a company issued a water permit in 2020 could continue to pollute at higher levels for much longer.

North Carolina regulators set a health advisory limit of 140 ppt for GenX in 2019, while the EPA established a health advisory of 10 ppt in 2022. Donovan warned about the consequences of preventing permit limits from changing as the science changes: “Imagine not being able to make a change for 10 years despite that the science is showing that a chemical is more toxic than originally thought – that’s what terrifies and frustrates us,” Donovan said.

She added that the transition to clean energy did not need to sacrifice clean water. “We can get clean energy without having to cause cancer,” Donovan said.

As the House debates the measure, the disagreement remains over whether faster approvals for power lines, pipelines, natural gas plants and datacenters justify changes to state water-pollution authority, permit reviews and pollution transparency. The Senate’s 62-33 vote put those provisions before the House without resolving the competing claims about permitting speed and environmental protection.
