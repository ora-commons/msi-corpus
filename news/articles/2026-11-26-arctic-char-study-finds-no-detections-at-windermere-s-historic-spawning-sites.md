## Conservationists ask Andy Burnham to halt sewage discharges into Windermere

A Freshwater Biological Association study published on Thursday found that arctic char appears to have disappeared from Windermere’s southern basin and was not detected at the lake’s historic spawning sites. Experts said the species remains in the 11-mile-long lake but is in serious decline and faces extinction without urgent action. Prime Minister Andy Burnham was urged to halt sewage discharges into England’s largest natural freshwater lake.

Funded by Natural England, the study found that the char’s spawning habitats had degraded significantly since the last observations in 2017. It recorded increased sediment and algal accumulation linked to sewage. Researchers also said sewage and other substances had eroded the fish’s habitats, while the survey detected no arctic char at the historic spawning sites.

Widespread raw sewage and bacterial pollution have affected Windermere, an 11-mile-long lake in Cumbria’s Lake District. Discharges from United Utilities, 1,800 private septic tanks and private sewage treatment works have been linked to the pollution. In March, the mother of a seven-year-old boy who kayaked on the lake said the boy nearly died after contracting a dangerous strain of E coli from contaminated water.

Arctic char has survived in Windermere for more than 11,000 years. Experts said it exists nowhere else on Earth and has formed four distinct populations since the last ice age. The orange-bellied fish inhabit the coldest depths of the 67-metre-deep lake, leading some to describe the species as a “mythical creature” or a “ghost.”

The first known written record of people catching arctic char dates to 1223, when Henry III was king of England. For thousands of years, the fish was a prized delicacy served to the royal courts of Tudor kings and local restaurants, and it became a key part of the Lake District’s economy.

Conservationists erected a 1.2-metre-high ice sculpture outside No 10 North in Manchester on Thursday, with a depiction of Burnham between two blocks of sculpted arctic char. Comedian Paul Whitehouse, who co-stars with Bob Mortimer in the BBC’s Mortimer and Whitehouse: Gone Fishing and is also a co-star of The Fast Show, unveiled the structure. Whitehouse called the “imminent extinction” of the fish “absolutely symbolic” of pollution in England’s waterways. Conservationists urged Burnham to prevent the loss of what they called a “unique” species.

Whitehouse said: “We need something to really highlight exactly what is happening, you know Windermere, the jewel in the crown of England’s freshwater resources – and it’s free, it’s available for everyone but it’s a basket case at the moment or it will be soon.” He said Burnham had made “some positive noises about taking back control of our water companies” but argued that stopping sewage discharges could be his legacy: “This government and Andy Burnham could say, look, we cleaned up the water companies. What a legacy that would be.”

Matt Staniek, a zoologist who founded Save Windermere, called the char the “canary in the coalmine” warning of the lake’s rapidly deteriorating health. He urged Burnham to step in and halt all sewage discharges into Windermere. Save Windermere also released a documentary about the fish’s demise, The Hunt for an Ice Age Survivor, on Wednesday.

Staniek said: “It is the epitome of the failure of government to protect our waterways from sewage pollution. If it’s happening to England’s largest natural freshwater lake in a Unesco world heritage site at a national park, then no river is safe from this level of pollution.”

Whitehouse described the char as “elusive and ephemeral and extraordinarily beautiful” and said he had only ever seen one in Iceland, where “the waters are still pristine and they don’t have water companies polluting their beautiful waterways”. He added: “Bob Mortimer, with all his angling skills, will never catch one – certainly not with the rate we’re going.”

Simon Johnson, executive director of the Freshwater Biological Association, said Windermere without arctic char would be “like a classic Lakeland woodland without red squirrels in it”. Johnson said the fish were less able to capture the public imagination because they were hidden, and asked: “If we lose arctic char, what’s next?”
