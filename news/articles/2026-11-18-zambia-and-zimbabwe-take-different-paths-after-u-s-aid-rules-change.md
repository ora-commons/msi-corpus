## New HIV infections fell 76% in Zambia and 80% in Zimbabwe

The Trump administration's move toward government-to-government aid agreements has left Zambia and Zimbabwe on different paths. After dismantling the U.S. Agency for International Development without warning in 2025, the administration worked toward separate government-to-government agreements with the two countries. Zambia's agreement provides less annual U.S. health aid than the country previously received, while Zimbabwe received no U.S. health aid this fall after its talks collapsed.

The stakes follow major gains against HIV/AIDS in the neighboring southern African countries. According to data from the Joint United Nations Programme on HIV/AIDS, new infections dropped 76% in Zambia and 80% in Zimbabwe between 2000 and 2025. In both countries, more than 95% of infected people were identified, and 95% of those identified were on treatment. NPR said much of that progress was due, in large part, to billions of dollars in U.S. aid over the previous two decades and described both countries as being on the cusp of controlling the virus.

Health workers in both countries said the aid transition could weaken treatment, outreach and public-health monitoring. The Zambia worker worried about losing the community networks that reached vulnerable populations and about records that might not be adequate. The Zimbabwe worker said medications were dwindling and questioned whether future HIV data could be verified or trusted.

ZNBC — Zambia National Broadcasting Corporation — announced Zambia's agreement on October 8 as "a new chapter" in which U.S. aid would flow primarily through the government and help build the national health system. The deal was signed last month, according to NPR, but it provides far less annual health aid than Zambia received in the past.

Asia Russell, executive director of the HIV/AIDS advocacy organization Health GAP, described the negotiations as secretive and said they became contentious. Russell said there was a "massive scandal" because activists were able to show that the agreement was planning to condition access to U.S. global health funding on access to Zambia's copper and rare earth minerals. Her organization helped expose the issue. The discussion of mineral rights was ultimately separated from the health agreement.

The Zambian health worker said she felt "a bit relieved" but added, "the work now begins." She asked NPR not to use her name because she helped make the mineral-rights issue public and feared government retribution. She said the government was ill equipped to face the epidemic's obstacles. Because homosexuality is illegal in Zambia, she said, people afraid of arrest at public health facilities may stay home rather than seek treatment, leaving them without care and allowing transmission through unprotected sexual relations, drug activity and other routes.

Under the new agreement, international and local nongovernmental groups are sidelined, even though the previous system sent substantial U.S. aid through organizations with staff trained to reach vulnerable populations. The U.S. strategy says the agreement aims to build the government's health system rather than a parallel system. The health worker feared that officials could report success without the records, infrastructure and capacity needed to sustain the response. She said the government might claim it had employed 5,000 community health workers, then asked: "What is the quality? What capacity do they have? Who will they be serving? Which district?" Many medical workers, she said, had not received answers about the future health system.

She said, "It feels like a big mess, honestly. It's scary." She added, "It feels like doing it right is not the priority, but people's lives are at risk." Zambia's Ministry of Health did not respond to NPR's request for comment. At the agreement announcement, Health Minister Roma Chilengi said the ministry planned to recruit up to 40,000 health care workers, including 15,000 front-line workers such as doctors, nurses, midwives, scientists, pharmacists and community health workers.

Despite her apprehension, she said that "what's happening in Zambia is still better than the situation in neighboring Zimbabwe."

Zimbabwe's negotiations collapsed in February. Zimbabwean officials said the proposed agreement was lopsided because the country would have been obligated to provide pathogen data and biological samples for research without a guarantee of access to treatments resulting from that information. The U.S. Embassy in Zimbabwe said in a press release that the breakdown was regrettable. The country went from receiving well over $100 million in U.S. health aid each year to having none this fall.

A Zimbabwe health worker who asked NPR not to identify him because he feared government retribution said HIV medications were dwindling in his community. He predicted, "Within a period of six months or so, you will start to see a very huge impact." He also questioned how the United States could discard 20 years of support after describing it as a huge investment.

He was also concerned the world might never know the real impact. He said the U.S. had provided the best data collection and monitoring system for tracking Zimbabwe's HIV epidemic and response, and he doubted future data from the country would be easy to verify or trust: "We may not even know…if it's true or not."

Zimbabwe's Ministry of Health and Child Care did not respond to NPR's request for comment. During the previous summer, Health Minister Dr. Douglas Mombeshora pledged to expand the health workforce and infrastructure. Asked about aid to Zambia and Zimbabwe, the U.S. State Department did not address the workers' concerns but said it remained committed to working "with partner governments to define and advance shared global health priorities."

Public reaction has been muted despite anger in the communities, according to the two health workers. The Zambia health worker said, "People are trying to not lose more than they've already lost," explaining that anger and frustration existed without becoming highly visible. The Zimbabwe health worker said people who spoke publicly knew there would be consequences. Russell said many people she works with fear retaliation because they lived under authoritarian regimes: "The problem is retaliation."

Jennifer Kates, senior vice president and director of global and public health policy at KFF, said her team was scrutinizing all available information about Zambia's agreement and its implementation. She called Zimbabwe "an important one to watch" because the country had made substantial progress while relying heavily on U.S. support. She said the future was uncertain. Zambia is entering implementation with a government-centered agreement and less annual money; Zimbabwe is entering the same period without U.S. health aid, with medications dwindling and questions about whether future HIV data can be verified or trusted.
