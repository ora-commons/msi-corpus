## Insured patients face higher deductibles, coinsurance and prescription costs

U.S. medical debt is expected to grow as stubbornly high inflation and healthcare costs strain household budgets and more people lose access to insurance, experts said. Kathy Hempstead, a senior policy advisor at the Robert Wood Johnson Foundation, said medical debt is often seen "as a symptom of being uninsured," but "it's increasingly a risk for people who have insurance." "All the factors that lead to debt are rising," Hempstead said.

The burden is also rising among people with employer-sponsored coverage. Faced with rising employer-provided health insurance premiums, Hempstead said, people may have more difficulty paying higher deductibles and co-insurance for visits or procedures and more expensive prescription drugs. "People's ability to pay that sort of cost-sharing that's expected of them in employer insurance has really deteriorated because people's out-of-pocket responsibilities are rising," Hempstead said. "I think that's one of the things that makes people feel really crazy," she said. "They're paying a lot every month for premiums for health insurance, but then they feel like they can't really use it to stay healthy because the exposure to cost is so high."

The expiration of enhanced Affordable Care Act premium tax credits at the end of 2025 marked a sharper break. Around 2.6 million people lost health coverage after the credits expired, Hempstead said. Others likely ended up with plans that cover less, which exposes them to more debt, she said.

Changes to Medicaid in President Donald Trump's tax and spending bill add work requirements, disenroll some immigrants and require more frequent verification. Millions of people are expected to lose health coverage entirely as a result, Hempstead said. "That's going to create a new cohort of uninsured people who are not going to be able to pay for care when they need it," she said.

Coverage losses can have downstream consequences for providers and patients. With more uninsured people, providers may have to provide more uncompensated care, which could then lead to higher prices for patients who pay, Catherine Ettman, a professor at the Johns Hopkins Bloomberg School of Public Health, said. The result could be more people with insurance having difficulty covering their expenses. Maanasa Kona, an associate research professor at Georgetown University's Center on Health Insurance Reforms, said medical care affordability also is tied to households' ability to pay for gas, housing and other essentials as inflation remains stubbornly high.

Kona also said payment transparency can play a role. Providers sometimes contract billing to revenue cycle management companies that sometimes use automated systems. "We don't exactly know what these entities are doing and how aggressive they're being in collections, even though they're working on behalf of the hospitals," she said. She described hospital payment processing as "a black box." A lack of transparency can mean actors "jumping in at various points in the supply chain" and extracting value from the system, she said. She called medical debt the natural consequence of those combined factors: "This is just how the system was created, and this was the obvious end result of it."

Medical debt remains difficult to measure. Ettman said, "Medical debt is unfortunately a common feature of the U.S. healthcare system." Current data on medical debt are sparse and can vary widely depending on how debt is defined. An Urban Institute analysis based on a 2025 survey of self-reported responses found 27% of U.S. adults reported that they or a family member had medical debt, defined as owing money for medical or dental bills they were unable to pay in full. A 2022 KFF survey found that 41% of adults reported some kind of debt related to medical or dental bills, including 21% who said they were directly repaying their provider and 17% who said they owed a bank, lender or collection agency.

Medical debt is more common among people with lower incomes, women, Black and Hispanic Americans and people with poorer health. A KFF survey from October 2026 found that 39% of people without insurance had problems paying or were unable to pay medical bills, compared with 24% of people with insurance.

Insurance does not eliminate the risk. Ettman said people with coverage can end up with medical debt when they have high out-of-pocket maximums, receive care out of network or have inadequate insurance. Unexpected health events, including accidents and trauma, often leave patients unable to pay medical bills, she said. "If you have a health event tonight, and an ambulance comes to pick you up to bring you to a health system, you might not have a say in whether that ambulance is in-network. You might not have a say in where that ambulance brings you. You might not have a say in the care that was delivered to you to save your life, if it was delivered in a place that was not covered by your insurance," Ettman said.

Among privately insured adults with medical debt, 46% owed $2,000 or more, according to the Commonwealth Fund's 2025 Affordability Survey. Nearly 1 in 5 said they owed $5,000 or more. Sixty-four percent of privately insured adults with medical debt accrued it because of inpatient or outpatient hospital care, the Commonwealth Fund found. A quarter said their debt came from dental costs. Six percent said they owed money from using an ambulance.

Cancer diagnoses are also major drivers of medical debt, Hempstead said. A cancer diagnosis can be "economically devastating," she said, in part because cancer patients may need to miss work. A 2024 American Cancer Society survey found that Forty-seven percent of cancer survivors and patients incurred medical debt, despite nearly all having healthcare coverage when they started accruing debt. Of those with debt, about a third had been carrying it for more than three years, and more than half had gone to collections.

The consequences extend beyond finances. Ettman's research has found that people with medical debt are more likely to defer or forgo medical care, sometimes when their illness is more severe and care is costlier. "I'm worried about the populations that will lose access to health insurance and the downstream consequences that will have not only on their health, but also on the broader health system," Ettman said. Her research also has shown that people with medical debt are more likely to later report housing instability. Debt collectors can sometimes take extraordinary actions against people who are delinquent, Ettman said, resulting in jail time in some extreme cases.

Financially, Ettman said patients with medical debt may find it "challenging to cover the cost of other essentials" and may be more likely to accrue other types of debt. People may cut back on food, rent or utilities to pay medical bills, and money diverted to medical debt means they cannot keep the income they earn. "Then they're not going to be able to accrue wealth over time, which is really important for financial stability and health," Ettman said. The broader effect of high healthcare costs may be greater than medical-debt figures capture, she said: "If we take a step back and think holistically about how high healthcare costs may be influencing Americans, it is likely much greater than what we are capturing by this concept of medical debt."

After midterm elections in which affordability was a major concern for voters, Hempstead said the new Congress might have different marching orders to repair some federal policies and make healthcare more affordable. "If we don't make any policy changes in the short- to medium-run, this problem is going to get a ton worse. We're going to have millions more people with no insurance, and that's going to make providers even more desperate for any kind of income that they can get," she said. Even when insurers do not pay a bill, providers still have to collect it, Hempstead said.

Kona called the issue bipartisan and said she was optimistic about momentum, particularly at the state level. States without medical debt protections lack what she calls the "safety net underneath the safety net." "They're supposed to catch the people that are falling through the system. Right now there are too many people falling through the system, but either way, we've got to have something there," she said. Ideally, nobody would need to rely on medical debt protections, she said. But more states instituting minimum protections could help remove some of the worst effects.
