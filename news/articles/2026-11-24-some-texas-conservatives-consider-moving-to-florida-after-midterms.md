## Some Texas conservatives consider moving to Florida after midterms

**Subtype:** fact

- Reigen Lox and Marie Bailey are considering moving to Florida after the election, according to interviews reported by Meghan Bowman.
- Florida Republicans retained all branches of state government after the midterms despite Democratic gains in the state legislature.
- Michael Fusella said conservative arrivals from Democratic-leaning northern states helped build Florida's Republican strength.
- University of South Florida professor Kirsten England said the election results alone were unlikely to cause a large influx of moves to Florida.

## England says election results alone are unlikely to trigger a large influx

Some conservative Texans were considering Florida as a destination after the midterms. Democrats won a few more seats in the state legislature, but Republicans retained all branches of Florida's government, making Florida the largest state in the country where the GOP was still in charge. Meghan Bowman reported from Tampa for member station WUSF that the migration trend could continue.

Republican voter Michael Fusella moved to Tampa from New Jersey about four years ago and credits conservative arrivals with building the state's Republican strength. Fusella said Florida Republicans have a lot to thank conservatives like him for and should be grateful that they moved there. “So many conservatives, like myself, fled these Democrat states, especially from up north, and came down here and helped change Florida into the conservative stronghold that it is today,” he said. Bowman said the influx was especially important during the COVID years. Fusella said people like him helped Florida Republicans hold the line on election night. He compared the outcome to a blue tsunami and a red wildfire, saying wildfires can rejuvenate soil and grow back stronger.

In Austin, people cheered when Democratic Senator-elect James Talarico won in the state. Reigen Lox, a conservative voter in suburban Dallas, said, “I had an inkling that something could change, but I didn't expect it on this large of a scale. And I think that is concerning for a lot of people in Texas.” Lox said he was considering moving to Florida based on the midterm results.

Marie Bailey, a conservative realtor in the Dallas-Fort Worth area, started a Facebook group for people leaving California and moving to Texas. She was also considering moving herself. Bailey said everyone was shocked by the election and that many people were rethinking where to move.

Bailey said Florida's beaches, amusement parks and cruise ports are major draws. Bowman said that with the midterm results, conservatives may be eyeing Florida for its values. “And we all know that Florida's a destination for people moving, but I think even more so now that Texas turned blue,” she said.

Kirsten England, a political science professor at the University of South Florida, said a large influx of people relocating solely because of the midterm results was unlikely. “If they were saying, oh, I'm going to move to Florida, well, then they already were thinking of it, and that's not the same thing,” she said.

Sean Freeder, a political science professor at the University of North Florida, said age is what mostly brings people to Florida. “Florida has a unique role in the country where it acts as kind of an absorption sink for retirees and older folks,” he said. He said the influx refreshes Florida's conservative population in ways it does not in other states.

Freeder said his data show that older retirees moving from California and New York to Florida are Republicans, not lefties. “Older retirees - we find in our data, it's not lefties moving from California and New York to Florida. It's Republicans,” he said.

Tim Lyons, a Sarasota mortgage broker, co-manages a Facebook group that helps people relocate to Florida. The group has more than 32,000 members. Lyons said a recently passed amendment that will drastically lower property taxes could be another draw for people considering the move.

Lyons dismissed the idea that the midterms alone would bring more people to Florida. “It's not about blue or red, necessarily. It just seems to be the same old things - weather, just the timing of their life,” he said. Florida, he said, remains a destination regardless of politics.

The interviews separated interest in a destination from an actual decision to move. Election results could make Florida more prominent for some conservatives. Freeder cited age, Lyons cited weather and lower property taxes, and Bailey cited Florida's beaches, amusement parks and cruise ports as more concrete reasons to relocate than the election result. Neither England nor Lyons said the midterm interest would by itself produce a large influx.

The new reporting develops that result by examining whether conservative relocation could shape Florida's Republican electorate.
