## Deployment milestones run through 2038 as technology remains under development

The Federal Communications Commission said in a Wednesday statement that it had approved Elon Musk’s SpaceX request to deploy up to 1 million Starmind satellites, or compute modules, for processing data in space. The commission said the authorization was the first of its kind in US history and would “support America’s booming super intelligence economy.”

The FCC set staged milestones rather than a single date for the full 1 million satellites. Under the license, SpaceX must begin deployment no later than November 2033, deploy 10% of the maximum number, or 100,000 satellites, no later than November 2035, and deploy 50% by November 2038.

Supporters said space datacenters are essential as the AI industry grows and that terrestrial facilities carry high costs for the power grid and water supply. The proposal would place computing capacity in orbit rather than adding it entirely to infrastructure on Earth.

In its filing to the FCC, SpaceX said, “By directly harnessing near-constant solar power with little operating or maintenance costs, these satellites will achieve transformative cost and energy efficiency while significantly reducing the environmental impact associated with terrestrial datacenters.”

The FCC framed the authorization within a long-term vision of computing in space. The commission said the authorization “promises to bring the human race closer to a Kardashev II civilization – one that can harness the full power of the sun.” FCC chair Brendan Carr said, “America is leading the world into the era of super intelligence.”

Critics said the technology for space datacenters is still being developed and that deployment remains a long way off. Amazon’s rival satellite unit, Leo, and others said SpaceX was unlikely to deploy all the satellites and that doing so could take centuries.

Carr had rejected that criticism in March. “Amazon should focus on getting Amazon’s house in order with their own launches and their own satellite constellation, rather than worrying about other people that are actually out there launching satellites at the pace and cadence that SpaceX is,” he said.

The authorized scale is far beyond the current population in space. The Guardian reported that only 17,000 satellites currently exist, while the Starmind license sets a maximum of 1 million. The report said it remained unclear whether SpaceX would put all 1 million satellites in space and noted that operators sometimes request approval for more satellites than they intend to deploy to retain design flexibility.

SpaceX sought approval for 42,000 Starlink satellites before beginning deployment of that system. The growing Starlink network currently has just over 11,000 satellites in space.

The Starmind request also relies heavily on reduced costs from Starship, SpaceX’s next-generation reusable rocket, which remains under development. The company is betting that lower launch costs will help it deploy the proposed compute satellites.

The FCC also said it was approving SpaceX requests for additional communications to back up the company’s optical network.

The decision authorizes the development of a space-based compute constellation far larger than the current satellite population, but it leaves the scale and schedule unsettled. SpaceX’s first required step is a November 2033 start on deployment, followed by 100,000 satellites by November 2035 and 50% of the maximum by November 2038.
