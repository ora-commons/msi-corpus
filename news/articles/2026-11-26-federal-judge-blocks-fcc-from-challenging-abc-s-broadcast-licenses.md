## Justice Department attorney said FCC could subpoena or fine Disney

A federal judge on Wednesday blocked President Trump and his administration from taking action to challenge ABC's broadcast licenses, ruling that the accelerated license reviews amount to unconstitutional retaliation for satire, commentary and news coverage the administration dislikes. U.S. District Court Judge Loren AliKhan issued the preliminary injunction in the case ABC and its corporate parent, the Walt Disney Co., brought against the Federal Communications Commission, and she also rejected the administration's motion to dismiss the lawsuit.

AliKhan based her finding largely on public statements and social media postings in which Trump attacked ABC and Disney, along with posts by Trump's pick as FCC chair, Brendan Carr. The perceived offenses she identified included late-night host Jimmy Kimmel's jabs at the president, the critical remarks of hosts on the chat show The View, and the moderation of a presidential debate in September 2024.

"Given the extreme, indeed existential, nature of the possible penalty Plaintiffs face -- loss of their broadcast licenses -- the court concludes that early license renewal proceedings create a risk sufficient to chill a broadcaster of ordinary firmness," AliKhan wrote. ABC and Disney serve six of the 10 largest markets in the U.S., including New York, Los Angeles and Houston, and their stations contribute significantly to the network's bottom line.

AliKhan also rejected arguments from the FCC that the court could not act because the agency had not yet rejected any license renewal, writing that the very act of premature review for retaliatory reasons is unconstitutional and unlawful, and hard to reverse. Broadcast licenses typically last eight years, come up for renewal on a staggered basis and are rarely taken away.

In her order, the judge wrote that "What started in 2020 as questions about how ABC 'can keep getting a license' if it 'report[s] fake news,' ... has crescendoed into repeated calls for the Commission to strip ABC's broadcast licenses for their editorial decisions," and she added, "The court counts eleven such calls made by President Trump over the past two years -- the most recent being in July 2026." AliKhan also cited the administration's "tangible steps toward enforcing President Trump and Commissioner Carr's repeated threats to strip the Plaintiff Stations of their licenses," and wrote that "Chairman Carr has taken President Trump's rhetoric as his marching orders." Carr did not respond to NPR's request for comment Wednesday evening.

ABC called the ruling "an important victory for the First Amendment" in a statement. The network's lawyers told the court that ABC's decision not to carry Trump's July 2026 Oval Office address was one of several examples of newsroom decisions made under duress. ABC did not broadcast the address on its television network, though the network streamed it on its digital news service and, the network's legal team said, would not have done so absent the political pressure from the FCC. Jimmy Kimmel's interview with then-Democratic U.S. Senate nominee James Talarico of Texas was uploaded to YouTube instead of being aired on ABC's late-night television show, and The View stopped hosting candidates for political office in 2026 in the months ahead of the fall elections. Talarico won election to the U.S. Senate earlier this month.

Anna M. Gomez, the lone Democratic commissioner on the FCC, said: "For nearly two years, the agency has weaponized its licensing authority to retaliate against broadcasters for exercising their right to free expression, and now a federal court has stepped in to put a check on that conduct. Every broadcaster facing this pressure should take note and choose courage over capitulation because, as I've said from the beginning, the First Amendment is on their side."

The ruling comes six weeks after a parallel ruling in a case brought by CNN, MS NOW and Politico against the Trump administration for restricting access. White House officials barred journalists from the three outlets after Trump lambasted them for "purposefully negative" news coverage. U.S. District Court Judge Timothy Kelly issued a preliminary injunction, writing, "It is not hard to conclude that President Trump likely banned Plaintiffs from the White House because he believes that their reporting is generally inaccurate and biased against him." Kelly described that as a form of unlawful retaliation. The administration is appealing, and the White House continues to limit the three news outlets' access to the president.

In both cases the media companies argued that the Trump administration had violated their constitutional rights of free speech and due process, while Justice Department lawyers pointed to other priorities and prerogatives: national security in the White House case, and the need to pursue an investigation into diversity practices in the FCC case. In both cases, the judges expressed incredulity or skepticism that the administration had acted in good faith. NPR wrote that the Trump administration was pressing to expand its authority and powers, including beyond matters involving the media. NPR said the White House access case would effectively require a reversal of decades of precedent on closely related cases. NPR also characterized the administration's position in the ABC case as seeking the ability to "trip up broadcasters who put things on the air that offend the president," citing the government's regulatory authority over broadcast technology. Separately, Justice Department attorney Dimitar Georgiev-Remmel defended the license review as a useful "tool" the FCC could use to secure more complete information for the diversity investigation.

Disney's decision to litigate marks a change in strategy. In December 2024, just a few weeks before Trump became president for the second time, Disney agreed to settle a lawsuit Trump had brought against ABC News by paying $16 million, including legal fees. Many outside legal experts believed Disney would have had the upper hand if it had stuck it out in court. Now under new leadership, Disney has drawn the line at what it contends is a threat to its broadcast licenses, and profits, over its editorial independence.

"We're very principled on this," Disney CEO Josh D'Amaro told CNBC in August, just days before the company filed its lawsuit. "We're going to stand up to what we believe is journalistic integrity, and we're not going to be told how to run that side of our business." D'Amaro became chief executive in March.

The FCC had already opened an investigation of Disney's diversity practices when it announced the license review. Carr has said the agency initiated the review of Disney's broadcast licenses over concerns that the company engaged in racial discrimination through its diversity, equity and inclusion actions, and he told NPR in August: "Obviously, the Disney lawsuit is without merit. From our perspective, we're going to continue to follow the facts and the law wherever they may lead." AliKhan wrote in her order that Disney had been responsive to every query the FCC had made in the diversity investigation. Carr said in a statement to a congresswoman that he found the responses to be "deficient, nonresponsive, and disingenuous."

The speed and scope of the review are themselves unusual. The FCC generally reviews broadcast licenses once every eight years, and reviews of a company's licenses are usually staggered rather than taken all at once; some of Disney's licenses are not due for renewal for another several years. The decision to examine Disney's licenses now is considered an essentially unprecedented act for the FCC to take, and at an October court hearing Disney's legal team argued it was suffering irreparable harm to its First Amendment freedoms.

Lead Disney attorney Beth Wilkinson compared the reviews to a "guillotine blade" hanging above the company, ready to fall at any moment; the judge thanked her for not comparing it to the Sword of Damocles of Greek legend. U.S. Justice Department attorney Dimitar Georgiev-Remmel shrugged off that claim, contending that the review was a useful "tool" the FCC was deploying to secure more complete information for the diversity investigation because the agency was having trouble eliciting necessary information from Disney. Wilkinson seized on the claim, asserting that it proved the license review was unnecessary, and she noted that the FCC informed Disney it was dissatisfied with the information provided about its diversity practices only 17 days after the reviews had been announced. That gave Disney little time to stave off the process by fulfilling the FCC's requests.

AliKhan asked Georgiev-Remmel what other tools the agency had at its disposal, and the Justice Department trial lawyers representing the FCC appeared to be caught off guard. Georgiev-Remmel initially said he wasn't sure. Some minutes later, after conferring with colleagues, he said the FCC could have subpoenaed the information, fined Disney, or taken other measures, and could set the reviews aside should Disney provide sufficient information. The judge appeared immediately skeptical that the instant license reviews were a necessary or even reasonable step to achieve that.

Even during the litigation, the Justice Department initiated an antitrust investigation into the five networks, including ABC and CNN, that make up the White House video pool sharing footage and duties to other outlets. The networks have been collaborating since their inception. What drew the administration's attention was that the networks consulted before deciding not to substitute another channel in for CNN on the increasing number of days when the president's aides blocked it from serving as the pool representative covering Trump's public appearances in small spaces that cannot accommodate the large contingent of reporters covering the White House.

The order is preliminary, and the FCC's stated reason for the reviews -- Disney's diversity, equity and inclusion practices -- remains the subject of an agency investigation. For now, the court has barred the government from turning a licensing review into a penalty for a broadcaster's editorial decisions.
