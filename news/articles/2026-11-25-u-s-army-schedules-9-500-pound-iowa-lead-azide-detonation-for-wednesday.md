## Army projects plume could carry lead particles more than 100 miles into Missouri

The U.S. Army is preparing to detonate 9,500 pounds of degraded lead azide at the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant on Wednesday around 10 a.m. ET, Army officials said. The plan has prompted an outpouring of public concern about the spread of toxic lead dust and an intervention from President Trump.

The lead azide is an explosive used in military munitions. Officials said the material had been stored at the ammunition plant in Middletown, Iowa — near the state border with Illinois and roughly 160 miles southeast of Des Moines — for more than a decade. Inspectors in March found the lead azide had partially crystallized, raising the chances that the highly sensitive explosive would detonate on its own.

The Army originally said it would carry out a controlled demolition of the 9,500 pounds of lead azide last week. It later moved the detonation to Wednesday amid weather concerns and a lawsuit from environmental groups seeking to halt the blast.

Officials said a controlled detonation was the safest path forward, arguing that falling temperatures could destabilize the material and trigger an accidental explosion. They said other options for disposing of the material were not possible because of how volatile the lead azide had become.

“Inaction is no longer an option,” Maj. Gen. Eric Shirley, the commanding general of the U.S. Army Sustainment Command, said Tuesday.

The decision followed several days of uncertainty over whether the controlled detonation would occur as scheduled. The U.S. Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit placed a temporary hold on the maneuver last week after environmental groups sued, alleging officials had not done enough to notify and protect residents from possible lead exposure.

The Army said it planned to move forward anyway under a different legal authority. President Trump then said he had ordered the Army to wait for approval from the court and from Iowa Gov.-elect Rob Sand. The court lifted the hold Monday night, after which Army officials said they would proceed as planned.

The Wall Street Journal reported that lead exposure poses severe health risks, particularly for children and pregnant women, including potential damage to the brain and nervous system.

The Army said it had implemented several safeguards to limit the spread of lead dust. Those measures include sand barriers, gel blocks to limit particle dispersion and foam barriers to absorb thermal energy.

Federal officials on Tuesday recommended temporary relocation for all Middletown residents and those in the immediate area. They set a 5-mile evacuation cone extending southwest from the ammunition plant, and residents near the evacuation zone were encouraged to shelter in place.

The Army’s projections released Tuesday said the plume could carry measurable quantities of lead particles more than 100 miles to the southwest, stretching into northern Missouri. The Army advised people in the expected path to consider avoiding the area or remaining indoors with windows and doors closed until the Army gives the all-clear.
