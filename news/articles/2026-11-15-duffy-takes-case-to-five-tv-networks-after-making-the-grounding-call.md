## Ground-stop cause still unclear as Himes calls it a prioritization problem

Sean Duffy was in the air on Friday when he made the call to ground thousands of flights nationwide, according to The Wall Street Journal. Air-traffic-control disruptions that day had sparked safety concerns that, the Journal reported, weighed heavily on the 55-year-old U.S. Transportation Secretary, who had been extremely sensitive to the risks in part because of the deadly January 2025 midair collision near Washington — one of the worst U.S. aviation disasters in decades — that unfolded the day after he was sworn into office.

In the hours after the ground stop started, Duffy raced to the White House to brief President Trump and later argue that Congress needs to deliver $50 billion by the end of this year or else there could be more disruptions to America's aviation industry, or worse. He made the case over the weekend, appearing on five TV networks and holding two news conferences. His point: There has been massive underinvestment in telecommunications equipment and more problems are likely. As of Sunday, he was still unable to pinpoint precisely what happened or who was at fault.

"It happened on my watch," Duffy said on NBC's "Meet the Press." "But I can't apologize for what was passed off to us. And I can't blame Republicans or Democrats because it's all of them. It goes back 25, 30 years of a lack of investment."

Duffy has tried to use the crisis, which disrupted thousands of flights and snarled weekend travel, to demand Congress give the Federal Aviation Administration $20 billion more than what he had previously said was needed for upgrades.

His pleas have been met with some skepticism on Capitol Hill, as the lame duck Congress is in the midst of a massive change in control. Rep. Jim Himes (D., Conn.) said on CBS's "Face the Nation" that the issue was "not immediately a spending problem. It is a prioritization problem." Some Democrats have questioned where money allocated by Congress has already been spent.

Over the weekend, Duffy said it was unclear whether "incompetence" was to blame for the communications outage or if it was caused by faulty equipment. Without an air-traffic system overhaul, Duffy has said outages could happen again — just as there were in the Northeast in September and around Newark Liberty International Airport last year. "You will have more of these," he warned.

A crisis has never been far away during Duffy's almost two years on the job. The first was the midair collision between an American Airlines regional jet and an Army helicopter near Reagan National Airport in Washington that killed 67. An accident earlier this year at New York's LaGuardia Airport resulted in the deaths of a captain and first officer when their Air Canada Express regional jet crashed into an emergency truck crossing the runway. Those deaths, along with numerous other aviation close calls in recent years, put a spotlight on air-traffic control staffing shortages and technology gaps.

The Washington crash has shaped Duffy's approach to a massive, multiyear effort to try to fix the nation's aviation system. Congress last year approved a $12.5 billion down payment to modernize air-traffic systems. Large amounts of copper wiring have already been replaced with fiber-optic cables, while new runway-safety technology has been added at dozens of airports. Staffing in air-traffic control has also improved after Duffy in 2025 released a slate of incentives to attract and retain controllers, including financial rewards for those who reach certain training milestones.

Steve Scheibner, a retired military and commercial pilot with 1.3 million YouTube subscribers, said Duffy inherited a system that has been stressed ever since President Ronald Reagan fired more than 11,000 striking air-traffic controllers in 1981. "He's inherited a 40-year-old-plus problem," Scheibner said in an interview. "It's not as simple as saying it's this person's fault or that administration's fault."

Duffy will need skills he honed as a former small-town prosecutor and Wisconsin congressman to sell lawmakers, even though he has strong relationships with the nation's top airline executives and industry groups. Before joining the Trump administration, Duffy had previously lobbied Congress, including for the aviation industry. The Journal reported that he quickly got industry leaders on his side following the crash near Reagan. At a meeting of CEOs convened by the U.S. Travel Association last year, Duffy handed out his personal cell number, executives said. The Journal also reported that Duffy's business-friendly approach has won over airline leaders who clashed with his predecessor, Pete Buttigieg, a Democrat who took a more consumer-focused approach that carriers viewed as adversarial.

Trump was happy with Duffy's TV performances, people familiar said, even though they got off to an awkward start on Saturday with a microphone malfunction. Duffy, a father of nine children, cited family demands when he left Congress in 2019, pivoting back to television, where, according to the Journal, he became one of Trump's most vocal defenders and co-hosted "The Bottom Line" on Fox Business before joining the administration. Like the president who appointed him, Duffy appeared on reality television: his television career began in the 1990s when he appeared on "The Real World: Boston" and "Road Rules: All Stars."

Duffy, also once a competitive lumberjack athlete, participated earlier this year in "The Great American Road Trip." The video series featured his family traveling throughout the U.S. to celebrate the country's 250th birthday, with costs covered by a nonprofit backed by some companies his agency oversees, including United Airlines and Boeing.
