## Quota also covers Japan, Turkey, South Korea and the UK

The European Commission will impose a temporary quota on tariff-free Chinese hybrid vehicle imports into the EU from 1 December, limiting the flow to about 191,000 units over 200 days. The measure forms part of a first-year limit of 350,000 Chinese vehicles, which the EU said is about half of the hybrids expected to enter the bloc from China over the next 12 months.

Under World Trade Organization rules, the limits are temporary and will run for 200 days from 1 December during an investigation into imports of plug-in and battery-powered hybrid vehicles. The quota follows an agreement between the EU and Beijing in October that was followed by a rise in exports to the bloc.

The official journal published the EU's reasoning on Tuesday. The journal said: "In view of the sensitivity of the hybrids sector, its strategic importance to the union economy in terms of innovation, electrification and employment, and the existence of sufficient evidence showing that imports of hybrids threaten to cause serious injury to the union industry." To head off "the likelihood of substantially increased imports into the union," the EU is imposing the quota.

The limits are not confined to China. First-year quotas are set at 310,000 for Japan, 230,000 for Turkey, 105,000 for South Korea and 190,000 for the UK. The UK's allocation sits above the 166,000 hybrids the UK exported to the EU last year.

The volume limits are "safeguard" measures, which must apply to all big exporters of hybrid cars to the EU. The journal named South Korea, Mexico, Morocco, Serbia and the US among other countries covered, but the limits are most restrictive for China.

Chinese electric vehicle imports had risen from 21,000 units in 2020 to 438,000 in 2024, the year EU tariffs were imposed. After the tariffs took effect, electric vehicle imports began to "flatten out," the official journal said, with a "clear shift" from electric vehicles to hybrids from China.

Chinese hybrid exports worldwide rose from 191,000 vehicles in 2023 to 1.124 million in 2025, the EU said. The EU said the measures are intended to help cut the bloc's huge trade deficit with China.

The European Commission said imports from China came not only at the expense of EU production but were also taking market share from other exporters such as Japan and South Korea.

Industry reports say non-EU countries including the UK have questioned the allocations. At a WTO goods council meeting last week, South Korea questioned whether the investigation was fair and transparent. Japan, the UK, Switzerland and Turkey pressed for transparency and due process.

China's commerce ministry said it hoped the EU would abide by the consensus the two sides had reached, and said it reserved all rights to firmly safeguard the legitimate interests of Chinese industries.

The UK is separately fighting a 10% tariff on all electric vehicle exports to China, due to take effect on 1 January under the 2020 Brexit trade and cooperation deal. Andy Burnham's attempts to have the UK classified as a European manufacturer in the EU's still-to-be-finalised "Made in Europe" scheme have been linked to that dispute; the scheme is intended to protect against cheap Chinese imports.
