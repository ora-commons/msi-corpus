## DDIA analyzed more than 2,200 Spanish-language posts and articles about outage claims

The Federal Emergency Management Agency's simulated emergency alert ran for roughly one minute on Tuesday before ending without the dire predictions circulating around it coming true. The test, scheduled every three years to identify issues in the alert system, has drawn conspiracy claims before, and this year's version drew even more false claims.

The drill followed a week of genuine disruptions. Service outages disabled Outlook and other Microsoft programs, grounded thousands of flights, blocked access to JPMorgan Chase and halted internal communications at the National Security Agency. Researchers said the real failures gave false claims about a wider collapse a factual foothold.

Online, false and misleading claims questioned the cause of the outages, or connected them to threats of international blackouts caused by rogue or mysterious actors. The narratives spread across borders and languages, with particular implications and cultural connections tied to Spanish-language claims that circulated throughout global Latino communities. Although the misinformation was not limited to Latino or Spanish-language communities, experts warned that these online conversations showed how growing distrust in elites and institutions uniquely influences Latino media consumption.

"This sense of general distrust within many communities, but especially including the Latino community in the United States, will then feed into and be amplified by the online discourse around it," said Dr. Arthur Soto-Vásquez, who teaches ethnic and equity studies at the University of Nevada, Las Vegas.

In the days before the test, conspiracy theories wove real events into false ones. The timing of the previous service outages was cited as supposed evidence of a larger international catastrophe that people needed to prepare for.

Social media posts questioned whether the drill was part of broader preparedness efforts as concern about climate change and reliance on technology grew, and referenced programs including Fortress America, a Defense Department initiative to heighten preparedness on U.S. military bases, and the United Kingdom's "Be Ready" campaign, which urges the public to stock supplies for 72 hours.

"The success in virality of these rumors is because it's anchored in things that are real," said Rafael Olavarría, a research consultant with the Digital Democracy Institute of the Americas.

The institute analyzed more than 2,200 Spanish-language posts and articles uploaded from Nov. 11 to Nov. 17 to track claims forecasting a "worldwide outage." The content amassed more than 95 million views and 6 million interactions, the institute said, and it traveled furthest when pushed by "mainstream and clickbait-style outlets" using "alarmist headlines" that were often debunked inside the outlets' own articles.

The institute's analysis separated the claims by language. Misinformation in English centered mostly on the possibility of prolonged nationwide blackouts, and some of it originated with a fabricated video of President Donald Trump warning that "a 90-day blackout is going to happen before the end of the year." In Spanish, erroneous claims cited real events occurring abroad and distorted them as proof of rumored blackouts in their own countries.

Olavarría said the data showed a trigger for the surge. "The rumors, according to the data we gathered, exploded online after what we saw with airports and banks going offline," he told the Associated Press. "Misinformation, when it gets a grain of truth, has a way of becoming more effective."

Roberta Braga, founder and executive director of the institute, said what set the Spanish-language misinformation apart was the motivations of bad actors to leverage "levels of mistrust among Latinos about elites and institutions." The institute's own research found that most Latinos do not trust technology company chief executives, AI companies, politicians or government institutions to keep them safe as technology develops.

Braga tied that distrust to the infrastructure people rely on. "I think this all gets at a discomfort that people feel about the digitalization of their lives," she said. "A lot of aspects of their day to day and security are dependent on digital systems."

The context is a lived one in parts of the hemisphere. In his track "El Apagón," Bad Bunny laments "Maldita sea, otro apagón" — "damn, another blackout." The track captures what the report described as some Latinos' sentiments that widespread power outages are more than just viral threats. Blackouts, or "apagones," have become an increasingly common hardship from the islands of the Caribbean to nations across Central and South America, the Associated Press reported.

These outages are rarely ever seen as conspiracies, though. Their increased frequency stems from rising temperatures and catastrophic climate change, which burden grids that in some countries have been neglected for years because of economic constraints or government inefficiency and corruption, the report said. The report also said that, for populations that "grew up distrusting people in power," this vulnerability becomes something misinformation campaigns are able to exploit when targeting global Latino communities and their distrust of institutions.

Soto-Vásquez said the fear and mobilization surrounding the claims draw on prior experience. "The fear and the mobilization of what's happening is maybe based off of the distrust of previous experiences with other forms of panic or fear," he said. "The consistent moderating factor is just this general sense of distrust."

No widespread apagón accompanied Tuesday's test alert. That left the false narratives with an opportunity for people to examine the sources of the information they use, Olavarría said on Tuesday: "If the apagon doesn't happen today, now you know that that influencer, that account, shared something that didn't happen."

Associated Press writer Fabianna Rincon reported the story, and AP writer Melissa Goldin in New York contributed to this report. Rincon has participated in the Latinos Media and Democracy Program at the Digital Democracy Institute of the Americas.
