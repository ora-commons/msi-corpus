## US homelessness falls more than 6% in 2025, largest drop in over a decade

**Subtype:** fact

- The U.S. homeless population fell more than 6% in 2025 — roughly 49,000 fewer people — in the largest annual drop in more than a decade, according to the Department of Housing and Urban Development's annual count.
- HUD Secretary Scott Turner credited President Trump's border and immigration actions with what he called a historic decrease in homelessness.

## HUD and an independent analysis credit different factors for the decline

The number of people homeless in the United States fell more than 6% in 2025 — roughly 49,000 fewer people without housing — after years of increases to record-high levels, according to an annual count conducted in communities around the country and announced by the Department of Housing and Urban Development. It was the second consecutive annual decline, following a smaller drop in 2024.

Housing and Urban Development Secretary Scott Turner announced the results in a press release. "President Trump's actions to secure the border, get criminal illegals off our streets, and restore safety to our communities drove a historic decrease in homelessness," Turner said. The agency had not yet posted a detailed report.

A second independent analysis, based on the same count but commissioned by the advocacy group National Alliance to End Homelessness, found a similar decline and credited different reasons for it. "It's good news that more people are getting housed, and that the programs that are funded by Congress are working," said CEO Ann Oliva. Both analyses found there are still nearly 700,000 people in the United States living in shelters or on the streets — significantly more than before the COVID-19 pandemic.

On the causes, the administration has cited its changes in immigration policy as a key factor in the decline. During the Biden administration, New York, Chicago, Boston and Denver all saw a surge in asylum seekers who turned up at homeless shelters. "That's also the four cities that have driven half of the drop most recently," said Devon Kurtz, public safety policy director at the Cicero Institute, an administration ally on homelessness policy.

Kurtz also pointed to declines in cities like San Francisco, which is moving to support more sober-living options — housing that bans drugs and alcohol. Critics say that can send more people back to the street if they relapse, which is common. The Trump administration argues that the requirement to be drug-free would help more people achieve recovery and wants to expand such programs.

The administration also wants to overhaul several billion dollars in homelessness spending and shift it toward short-term housing with mandated addiction and mental health treatment. Courts have so far blocked that proposal. The administration says the longstanding federal policy of getting people into permanent housing first, then offering voluntary treatment, has failed and is to blame for the pandemic spike in homelessness.

Oliva, with the homelessness alliance, said programs that get people off the street without preconditions were a big part of the latest declines, especially among veterans. "Investments in programs like that are much closer to the scale of need for veterans than for any other population experiencing homelessness," she said. Homelessness among U.S. veterans was down more than 7% last year, and has declined nearly 60% since 2010.

By contrast, Oliva said more permanent housing is needed for people who are chronically homeless and more likely to suffer from mental health problems and addiction. Unlike other groups that saw a decline last year, there was a small uptick in people who have been homeless for a long time.

HUD also credited enforcement of public camping bans, after the Supreme Court said in 2024 that cities can ban people from sleeping and camping in public places. Oliva said the analysis her group commissioned showed that cities with such bans "actually did worse than the national average."

Oliva noted last year's decline in homelessness was broad and likely due to a number of factors, including softening rental markets in some places. Rents remain high, but they are down from record peaks during the pandemic — a shift that could stem the flow of newly unhoused people.

Declines also showed up in places that saw major weather disasters, like western North Carolina and Hawaii. When HUD and other groups move in to help those displaced, unhoused people also get assistance they did not have before.
