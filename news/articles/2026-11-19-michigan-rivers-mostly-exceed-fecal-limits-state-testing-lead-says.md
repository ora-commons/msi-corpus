## State has one full-time employee monitoring river E. coli

Michigan's testing program has reached roughly half of the state's rivers and tributaries. Yet based on results so far, Rippke estimates that 75% of Michigan's rivers and streams likely exceed state limits for fecal contamination. Despite federal mandates to ease pollution, roughly three in four waterways likely fall short of water-quality standards intended to protect swimmers and anglers. Manure runoff from large-scale farms and leaks from tens of thousands of home septic systems fuel the contamination. Legislators have done little for years as the problem worsened and have focused little attention on solutions in recent years. The pollution violates the Clean Water Act and likely sickens thousands of swimmers, boaters and anglers each year through exposure to bacteria, viruses and parasites.

E. coli is a gut bacterium regulated under the Clean Water Act as a proxy for fecal contamination. The vast majority of tested sites exceeded levels deemed safe for swimming, and in more than half, levels are often too high even for partial body contact activities such as paddling or wading. That does not mean a waterway is always polluted: water quality fluctuates over time, and most waterways are not routinely sampled. Rippke said the numbers did “not look pretty.”

Rachel Cuschieri-Murray, executive director of the Eaton Conservation District, calls the Grand River a “precious gift.” The state's longest river travels 252 miles from Hillsdale County, past farm fields and the state Capitol, before emptying into Lake Michigan. She would not swim in the stretch that runs by the Capitol and the halls of power where lawmakers decide Michigan's most important issues.

“You shouldn’t even put your hands in,” Cuschieri-Murray said. “It’s that dirty.”

Michigan's 76,000 miles of rivers and streams connect communities, sustain wildlife and feed the Great Lakes. Pollution reaches from small creeks to sections of the Boardman and Pere Marquette rivers in northern Michigan, the Tobacco River in mid-Michigan, the Kalamazoo River in southwest Michigan and the Rouge River in metro Detroit. The contamination also extends into the state's lakes, causing bacteria-related closures or advisories at dozens of beaches every summer.

The dirtiest waterways tend to be small tributaries in the southern half of the Mitten, where livestock farms, failing septic systems and sewage dumping create widespread problems. The source identifies the White River in the Thumb, the north branch of the Salt River near Coleman, the Red Run drain in Warren and various tributaries of the Flint, Grand and Raisin rivers. Waste is diluted as those tributaries enter larger rivers, but not always enough to eliminate health risks. Goose droppings, dog waste and sewer overflows add to the contamination.

Tiffany Minder, Flint's environmental compliance supervisor, said, “We don’t have a whole lot of choice other than be reactionary,” describing the city's struggles with sewer backups during rainstorms. “Our infrastructure is 100 years old.”

Accidentally swallowing contaminated water can expose a person to pathogens including E. coli, cryptosporidia, norovirus, giardia and hepatitis. Fever, diarrhea and vomiting are the most common symptoms, while some infections lead to severe illness or lasting organ damage. When met, Michigan's water-quality standards are supposed to limit the number of people sickened to a handful out of every 1,000 swimmers; the greater the exceedance, the greater the risk.

Dr. James M Fleckenstein, a Washington University School of Medicine professor who studies E. coli, said, “We’re really just beginning to break the ice on what the long-term consequences of those infections are.” He added, “But generally, we know that contaminated water is not a good thing.”

State statistics show that at least 12.4 million people spend time on Michigan's rivers and 30.7 million swim at the state's beaches each year, but nobody tracks how many are sickened by fecal pathogens during those activities. People usually fight off the resulting stomach bugs at home, Fleckenstein said, perhaps wrongly blaming food poisoning or a seasonal illness. Estimates from the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention suggest the nationwide toll could top a million. A separate study by researchers at the University of Illinois and Indiana University estimated 50 million annual gastrointestinal illnesses associated with lakes, rivers and ocean beaches.

As the 10th most populous state and a place known for its water, Michigan likely has a higher percentage of that toll, said Matthew Sims, a physician researcher specializing in infectious diseases at Corewell Health. Sims and his colleagues see patients with signs of such illnesses “all the time.” He said, “If somebody says we went camping, if somebody says we drank stream water, river water, whatever: We know there’s potential issues.”

Fecal pollution can also overload water with nutrients that cause toxic algae blooms and oxygen-depleted dead zones where fish cannot survive. Its costs include missed workdays, medical bills, reduced tourism and depressed real-estate values. One Michigan-based study found that waterborne E. coli outbreaks diminished home prices by an average of $13,000 when a sale coincided with an outbreak.

Joan Rose, a Michigan State University microbiologist who has extensively studied the problem, said, “Ultimately, a polluted environment is not going to allow for economic growth and it’s not going to allow for health and wellness.” Megan Tinsley, the Michigan Environmental Council's water policy director, asked, “Why would people want to continue to come here if over half of our waters are out of compliance for swimming?”

Direct comparisons with neighboring states are difficult because states vary in their monitoring and in the bacteria levels they consider unsafe. Even so, fecal pollution is widespread. In Indiana, 74% of assessed river miles exceed regulatory limits; in Illinois, the figure is 83%. A recent Environment America study found elevated bacteria levels at 69% of monitoring locations across 17 states.

AJ MacArthur, a Kalamazoo resident who founded the paddling club Michigan River Rats, blames a culture of environmental neglect that also fuels the PFAS crisis and littering. “It’s just beyond me,” MacArthur said. “We’re supposed to be known for our water.” To limit his exposure, MacArthur does not swim, wears rubber boots when launching or landing, keeps his hands away from his mouth and showers immediately after returning home. MacArthur said, “There are ways around it,” then added, “But it’s unfortunate that we have to even think about that.”

Legislative leaders in both parties call the pollution unacceptable for a state so rich in water resources. But they are split on how to address it and have focused little attention on doing so in recent years. Megan Tinsley said, “It’s just wild to me that this would be OK,” adding, “And it seems to be OK with a great many of our decision-makers.”

Rep. Mike McFall, D-Hazel Park, blamed the lack of urgency on polluting industries that see tighter regulations as a threat to their profits and have the money to sway policy through lobbying and campaign donations. “They’re out for their own self-interests,” McFall said. “That plays a role in a lot of the legislation in Lansing.”

Sen. Roger Hauck, R-Mt. Pleasant, attributed the inaction on Michigan's pollution issues to a fear of tightening regulations without providing money to help people comply. Supporting bigger fixes for septics, sewers or farm pollution would cost money the state cannot afford, he said. “Unfortunately, we’re broke.”

Environmental groups argue that state manure-pollution regulations are too lax, and the livestock industry has sued to block their enforcement. Money remains a persistent obstacle, preventing cities from repairing leaky sewers and stopping lawmakers from requiring people to fix failing septic systems. The inaction threatens to undermine investments taxpayers have made to reclaim Michigan's rivers, including billions spent to dredge contaminated sediment, restore habitat and replace derelict factories with waterfront parks.

Lacking more authority to prevent pollution proactively, the state environment department is left to hunt down violators one by one. It is a large job, and the state has just one full-time employee monitoring river E. coli. Local conservation groups therefore promote a different approach. They encourage people to pick up dog waste, promote septic-system maintenance and help farmers buy fencing to keep livestock out of streams. Those groups receive little state support: $40,000 apiece in annual operating cash, plus whatever grants they manage to secure.

Cuschieri-Murray has broad plans for the Grand River but limited ability to pursue them. Given the choice between cleaning up water through regulations and providing adequate support for voluntary fixes, Michigan has chosen neither. “It should be nonpartisan, right?” she said. “We all need water to survive. We all need our natural resources to be as healthy as possible, so that we can be healthy.”

At the current rate of progress, Rippke does not expect Michigan to solve its problems with feces-tainted rivers in her lifetime. “More people are going to get sick,” she said.
