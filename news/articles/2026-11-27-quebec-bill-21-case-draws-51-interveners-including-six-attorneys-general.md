## Wagner’s majority opinion cites Quebec’s historical commitment to laicity

Canada’s top court held four days of hearings in Quebec’s Bill 21 case, and 51 interveners participated, including six attorneys general — also a record number. The court described the hearings as one of the longest in its 151-year history. Its website crashed Friday morning when the judgment was released.

The Guardian called the law controversial and described the closely watched decision as exposing tensions between minority rights and the power of government to override those rights. The 6-1 ruling released Friday morning permitted Quebec to shield most of Bill 21 from court challenges alleging violations of Canada’s Charter of Rights and Freedoms. Chief Justice Richard Wagner wrote in the majority opinion: “Bill 21 is rooted in Quebec’s legitimate and historical commitment to ensuring the laicity of the state in a free and democratic society.”

Passed in 2019, Bill 21 bars workers, including lawyers, judges, police and teachers, from wearing conspicuous religious symbols such as a hijab, turban, kippah or cross while on the job.

The notwithstanding clause was the central issue, and The Guardian said provincial governments across the country were closely watching the result. The Guardian explained that the mechanism arose from a political compromise in the 1980s, when the federal government set out to replace the British North America Act with a new constitution. Provinces feared that courts would become too powerful under the new system, leading to a provision that allows provincial or federal governments to invoke the clause when they believe courts have overstepped.

Under that arrangement, governments can suspend protections for religious freedom, equality and speech, while voting and language rights remain untouchable. A government can invoke the clause for five years and must face voters before renewing it.

Quebec’s use of the clause was the first pre-emptive invocation intended to shield a law from court challenges. Quebec also used the approach with Bill 96, which sought to strengthen French-language protections. People affected by those laws can still challenge them. Many teachers and religious and civil-liberties groups argued that the laws violated constitutional rights and reflected government overreach; Quebec’s government defended the law as constitutionally valid.

The court rejected most arguments that Bill 21 violated Charter guarantees outside the clause’s reach, including equality between men and women and education rights for Quebec’s English-speaking minority. In its 520-page judgment, the 6-1 court did find that one element infringed democratic rights: it prevented elected members of Quebec’s National Assembly who wear face coverings for religious reasons from participating in the legislative process.

In a 4-3 decision, the court also endorsed lower courts’ authority to issue “judicial declarations” on laws protected by the clause. A declaration can state that a law violates the Charter even though the law remains in force.

Earlier courts had upheld Bill 21 on the same constitutional ground. In April 2021, a Quebec superior court judge described the effects of the law as “cruel” and said it “dehumanises” the people it targets, but conceded that the statute could not be struck down because the notwithstanding clause protected it. Quebec’s court of appeal upheld the law in February 2024 on the same grounds.

On the same day, an appeal was scheduled for a superior court decision requiring members of Quebec’s National Assembly to swear allegiance to King Charles to sit in the Salon Bleu, the chamber of the assembly. Lawmakers from a number of provincial parties had recently refused to take the oath.

The Guardian wrote that the governing Parti Québécois “has looked for wedges with the federal government and institutions like the supreme court.” Premier Paul St-Pierre Plamondon had previously said Quebec’s right to decide its own laws was at risk. The Guardian also said Canada had only recently fended off a threat to national unity after voters in Alberta rejected the initial stages of a separatist push. The Guardian wrote that Quebec’s newly elected government “has shown an eagerness to clash with the prime minister, Mark Carney.”

Some people in Quebec celebrated the judgment as a vindication of the province’s fight, while religious civil-liberties groups swiftly criticized it. Stephen Brown, chief executive of the National Council of Canadian Muslims, told reporters at a press conference: “We cannot exist in a country as free people, where governments can literally go to the electorate and say, ‘I’m gonna take away the rights of a certain group of minorities’, because it’s popular and to play wedge politics with the charter rights of Canadians,” Brown said. “If that is the case, we don’t have a charter of rights and freedoms. We have a charter of suggestions and permissions.”
