## Early intervention may be best before native ants come under fire

Little fire ants can reverse a disadvantage against red imported fire ants by going motionless when outnumbered, then growing their ranks in warmer weather before launching lethal attacks. A study led by ecologist Defu Chen of the South China Agricultural University documented the strategy while examining native ant communities in Southeast China. The study appeared in Proceedings of the Royal Society B.

Ants disperse seeds, scavenge, turn soil and serve as a food source in their ecosystems, according to Corrie Moreau, dean of collections at the American Museum of Natural History. "They're a fundamental piece of the fabric of any ecosystem," Moreau said. She also said invasive ants often displace native ant species. When local ants are displaced by invasive ants from elsewhere, the effects are widely felt.

The study focused on two invasive species that Ari Daniel described as wreaking havoc globally: the red imported fire ant and a honey-colored insect called the little fire ant. Chen described red imported fire ants as highly aggressive and said they attack immediately. Daniel said both species pose a serious threat to native ants. Chen said little fire ants hurt and kill native ants.

The practical question motivating the research was how multiple invasive species would interact and how those dynamics might evolve through time. Moreau said, "If we're trying to make conservation decisions or we're trying to do eradication programs, we need to try to understand what's going to happen through time."

Chen and his colleagues studied the ants in laboratories and outdoors in agricultural and urban settings. They initially found that both types of invasive fire ants battled one another. That conflict insulated native ants from more severe harm.

The pattern changed when little fire ants were outnumbered. Rather than continue fighting, they went motionless. Chen described the behavior as "Playing dead." Red imported fire ants stopped assailing them.

In warmer weather, little fire ants grew their ranks more quickly. Once little fire ants numbered about four times as many as the red imported fire ants, they launched devastating and lethal attacks. Chen said, "The two may coexist in the short term, but ultimately, one side will defeat the other."

The little fire ants then turned their attention to native ants, which they displaced or wiped out. Chen said the results suggest it may be best to intervene early and counteract the invasive species before native ants come under fire.

Entomologist Corrie Moreau, who was not involved in the study, said the findings had value but management lessons might not apply across all landscapes. The research was conducted in a city, she said, while conservation efforts are often most needed in places less touched by humans.

Moreau nevertheless saw broader value in examining the complex and changing interactions between multiple species over time. She said predictions about the best solutions for protecting biodiversity should be made thoughtfully.
