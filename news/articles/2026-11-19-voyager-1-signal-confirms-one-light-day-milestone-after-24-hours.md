## NASA investigates halted science and engineering data

Flight controllers at NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory in California received Voyager 1's carrier signal on Thursday, exactly 24 hours after the spacecraft sent it. The signal traveled more than 16 billion miles (26 billion kilometers) at the speed of light, and its arrival, together with flight-path calculations, confirmed the spacecraft's one-light-day distance from Earth.

NASA's heliophysics division director Joe Westlake said in a statement: “Traveling a light-day is a singular feat for a spacecraft, but Voyager is so much more than a distant traveler.”

NASA could not confirm the newest distance record until the signal reached the control team at the Jet Propulsion Laboratory. The agency had marked the occasion on social media the day before, wishing people a happy Voyager light day.

JPL director Dave Gallagher said in a statement: “Voyager 1 crossing the light-day threshold is yet another historic feat on an extraordinary voyage of discovery.”

Voyager 1 launched in 1977 and swept past Jupiter in 1979 and Saturn in 1980. In 1990, it snapped the most distant photograph ever taken of Earth's “Pale Blue Dot.” The spacecraft became the most distant human-made object in 1998 and crossed from the sun's sphere of influence into the vast void between stars in 2012.

The crossing made Voyager 1 humanity's first emissary to travel that far in interstellar space. Voyager 2 launched two weeks earlier, but its route past Jupiter and Saturn and on to Uranus and Neptune was slower and longer. It did not reach interstellar space until 2018 and will not reach the one-light-day mark until 2035.

Voyager 1 and its twin, Voyager 2, are NASA's longest-operating space missions. Both remain in operation despite having powered down most of their instruments to keep exploring.

Both spacecraft are doing well at 49 years of age and are moving at 38,000 mph (61,000 kph), using plutonium for power. Their picture-taking ended long ago, but they typically send back small quantities of data almost every day from environments they have never before explored.

No science or engineering data had been received from Voyager 1 since Saturday, according to NASA. The carrier signal that normally conveys that data kept arriving, and flight-path calculations allowed the team to confirm the light-day milestone. Controllers are still trying to determine why the flow of science and engineering data stopped.

Each spacecraft carries a Golden Record filled with sounds of Earth. The late Cornell University astronomer Carl Sagan led the project and coined the phrase “Pale Blue Dot” for Voyager 1's farewell glimpse of Earth. NASA expects the gold-plated phonographic records, conceived as time capsules for anyone or anything that might encounter them, to last more than 1 billion years.
