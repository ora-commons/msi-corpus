## Democratic leaders weigh oversight and legislation after 2026 midterms

**Subtype:** fact

- Democratic leaders said they are weighing oversight and legislation after the 2026 midterms gave their party control of Congress.
- Hakeem Jeffries, set to become House speaker, named restoring Affordable Care Act premium tax credits, reversing Republican Medicaid cuts, undoing Trump's tariffs and ending the conflict with Iran as legislative priorities.

## Trump's veto and Senate filibuster limit the new Democratic majorities

The 2026 midterm elections returned Democrats to power in Congress after nearly two years of criticism aimed at Donald Trump over costs, government performance and corruption. Republicans lost Senate seats they had held for decades and House seats drawn specifically to elect only Republicans, creating what The Guardian described as the biggest midterm defeat for an incumbent party in more than 50 years. The result gave Democrats new leverage, but party leaders said their exact strategy remains under discussion.

## Institutional constraints define the new congressional majorities

The election did not give Democrats unlimited governing power. Trump is set to remain in the White House until January 2029 and can veto bills that do not pass with overwhelming support. The Senate filibuster also remains in place. Democrats used it during the first half of Trump's second term to block many bills Republicans sought to pass along party lines, while Republicans now can use it to stop incoming Democratic legislation that lacks 60 votes in a chamber.

Ballot counting was still ongoing, and if the party's candidates won the two outstanding Senate races, Democrats and their allies would have 56 seats. The 2028 presidential election remains a long and unpredictable two years away. The Guardian described the Democratic Party brand as remaining “deeply unpopular” despite the party's seat gains across the country.

## Casar proposes weekly votes for the 2028 campaign

Greg Casar, chair of the Congressional Progressive Caucus, told reporters Wednesday that presidential candidates should be asked about broader legislative priorities rather than plastic-straw bans. “We are going to make sure that those candidates running for president of the United States aren’t getting asked about plastic-straw bans. Instead, we should be talking about all of the legislation that we pass out of the House, pass out of the Senate, and force to Donald Trump’s desk,” he said.

Casar envisioned “passing bold legislation that people called for, every single week, off of the floor of the House and the Senate,” pointing to bans on price gouging, Super PACs and stock trading by lawmakers. “If [Trump] vetoes it, then that is the platform upon which candidates can run in 2028, and then we can get signed into law in 2029,” he said. The weekly schedule was Casar's proposal, while party leaders said the broader strategy remained under discussion.

## Jeffries and Schumer outline legislative priorities

Hakeem Jeffries, who is set to become speaker of the House of Representatives when the new Congress convenes after the new year, said: “We made a promise to the country that we would fight for an affordable America, and we intend to keep it.” He named reimplementing the premium tax credits for Affordable Care Act health plans and undoing Medicaid cuts approved by Republicans last year. He also promised legislative action to make Trump undo his tariffs and end the conflict with Iran.

Despite the procedural obstacles, incoming congressional leaders said they would try to enact legislative solutions to the problems they campaigned on fixing. Chuck Schumer, expected to become the next Senate majority leader, spoke at the Center for American Progress, a liberal thinktank. “Everything we do maps onto three promises, the very same three we ran on, turning from a critique into a to-do list: lower costs, make your government work for you, clean up the corruption,” he said.

Schumer also warned: “Trump has the ability to block our efforts to lower costs for American families, root out corruption in Washington, end the chaos in our streets and overseas.” He added: “But if he does that, I have no doubt the American people will rebuke him and his party just like they did in the election.”

## Oversight plans cover contracting, trading and Epstein files

Robert Garcia, who will lead the House oversight panel, publicly warned potential targets to “lawyer up” and told reporters to expect inquiries related to allegations of corruption in Department of Homeland Security contracting, stock trading by lawmakers, corporate profiteering and the government's release of files related to Jeffrey Epstein.

Jamie Raskin, expected to lead the House judiciary committee, said he had sent more than 200 demands for information to the Trump administration and others. Discussing whom he would investigate next year, he told reporters: “go back and read my letters.” His letters carried no legal force while Democrats remained in the congressional minority; once they take committee gavels next year, the chairs can issue subpoenas.

## Impeachment appears outside the near-term agenda

The Guardian's assessment was that a third impeachment of Trump did not appear to be on the table. Trump was impeached twice during his first term — first on charges of leveraging aid to undermine his political rivals, and again after his supporters attacked the Capitol on January 6 — and the Senate did not convict him either time. The report also said Democrats had accused Trump of regularly violating the law and the Constitution during his second term while appearing to recognize Republicans' willingness to force the president from office.

Jeffries offered a personal account of the party's deliberations: “I haven't had a single member of the House Democratic Caucus talk to me about impeachment,” he said. “We've been relentlessly focused on affordability and on accountability.”

## Affordability was a major voter issue

Affordability was a major issue for voters in the elections. The Guardian reported that Democrats' demands to extend Affordable Care Act subsidies led to the record-long government shutdown last year, and their candidates made warnings about impending Medicaid cuts prominent in their campaigns. The outgoing Republican congressional majorities had so far rejected attempts to address either issue.

Democrats had some success coaxing the Republican majority to vote for war powers resolutions intended to end the conflict with Iran. One measure passed, but Trump had ignored it thus far. Trump, who cannot run again in 2028, has said he would be willing to work with Democrats and has also posted on social media an image of a red cap with the word “Veto” written on it.

## Primaries tested party divisions despite larger majorities

The midterm victories followed primaries that underscored divisions between the party's left and center, including over aid to Israel. After the wins, leaders of the party's ideological factions emphasized unity and cooperation. The Guardian said that shift could be aided by Democratic majorities in the House and Senate expected to be so large that intraparty fights could be less impactful.

## Healthcare debate could test party unity

Kristen McDonald Rivet, who leads freshman lawmakers on the centrist New Democrat Coalition, called healthcare “a marquee issue for everyone in the party” and said “people want big change.” “Some people's approach to that is Medicare for all. Others are talking about Medicare for all who want it,” McDonald Rivet told reporters.

She said healthcare did not need to become a source of combat within the caucus and expected debate over what major change to pursue: “There’s all kinds of ways to solve the solution, but I actually do not see that as a place of combat with other members of the caucus. It’s actually an incredibly hopeful thing that we are going to debate what big thing we can do around healthcare.”
