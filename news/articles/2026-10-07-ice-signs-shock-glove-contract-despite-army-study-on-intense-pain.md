## 16 senators press ICE for answers on shock-glove purchase

US Immigration and Customs Enforcement signed a $16.7 million contract in August to purchase 6,000 pairs of electric-shock gloves, despite a previously unreleased army study showing the devices caused "intense pain" in military subjects. The findings, which ICE leaders were aware of before the contract was signed, came to light through records obtained by the watchdog group American Oversight and shared with the Associated Press. The purchase is being made under the second Trump administration.

The study was conducted in October 2024 at the Fort Leavenworth military prison in Kansas, during the final months of the Biden administration. Army employees played the role of combative prisoners during the exercise, which assessed whether the gloves could be used to forcibly remove prisoners from cells, respond to disturbances in common areas, and transport uncooperative inmates. Role-playing employees conducted several scenarios with and without the gloves to test their impact, including violent encounters in which inmates were to fight as hard as they could.

The study found that using the gloves "increased the efficiency" of teams carrying out forced cell extractions and responding to disturbances by reducing the length of physical encounters, keeping guards from becoming exhausted and preventing injuries. The gloves did not "significantly change" the time it took to control inmates during escorts but required less effort, the study found.

"GLOVE provides a boost in confidence to smaller or less trained members," the study found. "Inmate compliance was immediate" compared with other methods, it added.

Several participants, some of them large-statured men, stopped resisting within seconds of officers applying shocks by touching the gloves to their skin. Afterward, the participants were left with "fatigue, soreness and excessive apprehensiveness to being shocked again," the study found. "After experiencing the effects of the GLOVE … participants stated just seeing the GLOVE was enough to make them want to comply," the study's authors wrote.

The gloves carry the brand name G.L.O.V.E., which stands for Generated Low Output Voltage Emitter, and are manufactured by Compliant Technologies, a company owned by military veterans and based in Lexington, Kentucky. Unlike Tasers, which cause the body to freeze, the electric jolt "works by overloading the peripheral nerve endings" of the skin to cause pain, the study said. Participants who were wet or had oil on their skin described even more powerful shocks, with one reporting discomfort for 30 minutes afterward.

ICE has called the gloves "distraction and de-escalation devices" that the agency said will be safer than other non-lethal options for subduing targets for arrest, deportation, and protesters who create civil disturbances. The agency has argued the rapid compliance they cause will keep officers safer and prevent incidents from requiring potentially lethal force. Critics have warned the gloves will give ICE another weapon to intimidate and brutalize people even as the agency is under fire for excessive force that has left a trail of deaths and injuries across the nation.

"The military assessment in ICE's own files shows that these gloves can cause 'intense pain' and force compliance. It does not establish that they are safe," said Chioma Chukwu, executive director of American Oversight. "Everyone should be alarmed that ICE is preparing to deploy a tool designed to inflict, at best, intense pain, without showing that it is safe for the people who will be subjected to it."

A 43-year-old man died after allegedly being shocked 40 times by officers using a combination of the gloves and a Taser in a county jail in Kentucky, and his widow is now suing authorities. The case is among several high-profile lawsuits over the use of such devices in local correctional facilities.

ICE released the study and other documents to American Oversight as part of the group's Freedom of Information Act lawsuit against the agency seeking records related to its use of force. The agency's contract requires the manufacturer to deliver the gloves within six months. The company owner, Jeff Niklaus, declined to make himself available for an interview.

White House border czar Tom Homan said it could "be a while" before the gloves are in the field because ICE is still working out training and policy issues. A statement released Tuesday by ICE did not address questions about the study and the status of the rollout, but said "officers are highly trained in de-escalation tactics."

Senator Catherine Cortez Masto of Nevada said ICE had not responded to the August 27 letter she and 15 other Democratic Senate colleagues sent calling for the agency to abandon the purchase or explain how the devices would be used and managed to avoid abuse. "Without ICE's responses to legitimate questions from 16 United States senators, the American public is being left in the dark about how these electric-shock gloves are being deployed and what, if any, training the agents who use them have received," she said.
