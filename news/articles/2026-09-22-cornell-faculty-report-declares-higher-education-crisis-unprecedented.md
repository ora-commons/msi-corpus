## Committee urges 'broader, more human-centered judgment' for AI era

A more-than-200-page report from a Cornell University faculty committee declared an "unprecedented crisis" facing American universities on Tuesday, attributing the moment to disrupted federal funding, an erosion in public trust and rapid AI-led technological change.

The committee's recommendations included diversifying funding sources, embracing a range of ideologies and developing a new tuition model. Threaded through those proposals was a central call for universities to graduate students with "broader, more human-centered judgment" suited to the AI age.

The document follows similar reflections from Yale University and Vanderbilt University, which the Wall Street Journal said "criticized institutional missteps and blind spots." The Journal characterized Cornell's report as offering "a gentler approach and a more capacious central recommendation" than those issued by Yale and Vanderbilt.

"Judgement is the skill that learning produces," the committee wrote, "and it is the university's most important contribution to a modern democracy."

In many instances, the committee recommended a return to the basics. That included more project-based learning and explicit instruction in time management strategies such as "navigating competing priorities, assessing workload, anticipating expectations, and adapting to personal and academic circumstances."

The committee described what it called a "risk-averse student body" anxious about the cost of college and finding a good job. Employers, the committee wrote, hold what it called "jarring" skepticism about the current generation's ability to handle workplace demands, solve problems without clear answers and recover from setbacks.

On cost, the committee found that Cornell students who pay full tuition spend nearly $400,000 on an undergraduate degree and identified expense along with opaque pricing as sources of public distrust. The committee advised against shortening the standard degree path to three years or leaning into online education, recommending instead that a task force develop a new tuition model.

For the university's own finances, the report urged school leaders to push for robust federal and state funding following what it described as "the Trump administration's attacks on research dollars." On research itself, the committee recommended examining "publish-or-perish incentives that emphasize quantity over quality."

In an interview, Provost Bala said universities need a vision for the AI era. "Universities really need to develop a vision of what we do, what we teach, how we teach it, what research we do, how we do it," Bala said. "AI could be incredibly positively transformative and deeply harmful both at the same time."

The committee framed the moment as one requiring difficult choices. "Higher education finds itself at a crossroads that calls for more than just surviving a crisis," the report said. "It calls for making difficult choices."
