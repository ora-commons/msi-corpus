## The recommended evacuation covers a 5-mile cone southwest of the plant

At a Tuesday press conference at the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant, officials from the U.S. Army, the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, the Department of Health and Human Services, the Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, Firearms and Explosives, the Department of Agriculture and other agencies said the planned disposal of nearly 5 tons of degraded lead azide now includes added safeguards and emergency assistance. The detonation is set for about 9 a.m. Wednesday at the plant in Middletown in southeast Iowa, following delays over the past week stemming from Army scheduling, a lawsuit, presidential intervention and widespread public concern about health and environmental effects. The response has prompted emergency responses in multiple states and what the Army calls “unprecedented” coordination across federal agencies.

Acting Secretary of the Army Adam Telle said the government's priority was the “health and safety of the citizens of this region.” He said officials sought advice from “every expert” inside and outside the federal government and considered all viable options and data before deciding to proceed. “There is consensus across the experts across the country that a controlled, mitigated detonation of the lead azide here at Iowa Army Ammunition Plant, is far preferable to an uncontrolled spontaneous detonation of that substance,” Telle said.

Telle said the added mitigation includes sand around the structure holding the barrels, two walls of four 16,000-gallon water tanks, a plan to “flood” the exterior of the storage area with aqueous foam to help absorb particulate matter from the blast and modeling to forecast affected areas based on prevailing weather conditions. The water walls and foam were not part of the Army's originally released detonation plans. Telle said federal agencies had worked “24 hours a day” and had “continuously added” mitigation measures.

Telle said, “There has been unprecedented collaboration across the federal government.” Maj. Gen. Eric Shirley, commander of the Army Sustainment Command, said the work had involved multiple agencies since the degraded lead azide was discovered in March. “This is not simply an Army problem set, nor will it be an Army solution. It is a whole of government response,” Shirley said. Telle said “every reliable bit of information” the government had over the next 24 hours would be made available to the public.

Army lead-deposition maps released Tuesday morning showed that lead released by the detonation could disperse through the air as far as 100 miles southwest of Middletown and cross into Missouri. An additional map showed there was a potential for lead-bearing surface dust to settle as far as 50 miles southwest of the detonation site. FEMA Administrator Cameron Hamilton said the maps represented “some of the worst case scenarios.” The Army plans to release maps adjusted for the latest weather conditions six hours before the detonation and every hour leading up to it.

The Army has recommended, and FEMA is supporting, the evacuation of Iowans in Middletown and the immediate area — a 5-mile cone to the southwest of the detonation site — during the detonation period. When the Army initially announced the operation, it recommended that individuals in a selected Middletown neighborhood evacuate because the blast could shatter windows. The Lee County Sheriff's Office said U.S. Army soldiers would begin going door to door to “notify residents” about the recommended evacuations.

Department of Health and Human Services official Sean Kaufman said the highest risk was for pregnant individuals, children and people with chronic illnesses who live or plan to be in areas the Army forecasts could be affected. “You definitely want to be out of that area,” Kaufman said. “I would evacuate that area that’s signaled in the model.”

FEMA has a hotline at 1-800-621-3362 and has sent emergency alerts to cellphones in the area. The agency said additional assistance can be found online. FEMA is offering transportation, food and relocation assistance to those in impacted areas, and the Army release said short-term lodging will be provided to eligible Iowans through Friday, Nov. 27. Hamilton said the White House had made it “very very clear that we are to spare no expense to ensure that Americans are kept safe.” He said communities southwest of the plant had been told, “if they choose to relocate, we will offer services.” “We care deeply about the American people,” Hamilton said. “What you see here is not red, not blue, it’s red, white, and blue. This is the very best of the American’s team.”

Iowa Gov. Kim Reynolds issued a disaster proclamation Tuesday for Des Moines, Lee and Van Buren counties for the planned detonation, activating the response plans for the counties and state agencies. According to the governor's release, the Iowa Department of Health and Human Services is working with federal partners and other state agencies, evaluating potential public impacts, providing precautionary guidance and informing local public health partners. The Iowa Department of Natural Resources is assessing Army, EPA and other federal plans for pre- and post-detonation monitoring of air, land and water quality. As many as 100 Iowa National Guard soldiers will be available to aid local, state and federal agencies in their public-safety plans and help provide guidance and assistance to around 150 households with temporary evacuations.

“Today’s briefing by the U.S. Army highlighted the responsible planning and coordination that have gone into the upcoming detonation. I greatly appreciate their due diligence, responsible approach, and focus on the public health and safety of Iowans in nearby communities,” Reynolds said in the release. She added that Iowa agencies were engaged with federal and local partners to ensure communities had information and resources before, during and after the operation.

Missouri Gov. Mike Kehoe launched a state emergency center, while Illinois Gov. J.B. Pritzker put state agencies on alert even though plume models did not currently show that Illinois would be affected. “While risk to Missourians remains minimal, our State Emergency Operations Center is activated and our state agencies continue to be in constant contact with federal partners to take the necessary actions to protect the health and safety of our citizens, livestock, and environment,” Kehoe said in a statement.

Thomas Croci, with the EPA's Office of National Security and Operations Coordination, said the agency had a “blanket” of sampling teams in the region collecting before-detonation samples and preparing to collect samples after the detonation. Croci said more than 150 teams were sampling areas in three states and four mobile laboratories had been deployed to test air toxins and “anything that comes off the combustion.” “No one should feel like they will not get a sample,” Croci said. “If you want your home sampled, your property sampled, your school sampled, you will get that sample and the EPA will assist with that sampling.” Army soldiers, environmental teams and other federal officials also are helping sample soils and alert residents.

Richard Fordyce, an under secretary at USDA, said the department estimated 350 farms in Iowa and Missouri could be affected by the detonation. USDA said on X that it was working with Iowa and Missouri officials to “protect livestock, support producers and safeguard our food supply.” The department recommended that farmers shelter or move livestock from the region and contact veterinarians if they suspected animals had been exposed to lead. The Iowa Department of Agriculture and Land Stewardship published new guidance for livestock producers within 20 miles of Middletown based on the most recent weather information.

Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, Firearms and Explosives director Rob Cekada described lead azide as a “sensitive” primary explosive that the government had phased out around 15 years ago. Cekada said nearly 10,000 pounds of the nation's stockpile moved to the Iowa plant in 2009, with some of the material “already 60 plus years old.” Government filings said the barrels were visually checked monthly and annually refilled with a solution of water and ethanol. The Army detected crystallization in March, preventing it from administering the solution that year because of the risk of setting off the explosives. Cekada said the lower liquid level in the barrels made the material more susceptible to approaching freezing temperatures. The stockpile was intended to remain at the Iowa plant in case the U.S. military needed to deploy lead azide as a primary explosive again.

Army Explosive Ordnance Disposal commander Col. Aaron Teller said the last major disposal of lead azide occurred in Colorado in 1977. He said that detonation had “zero mitigation” in place and only 25% of the lead material was released into the atmosphere. “So we believe we can significantly reduce that last 25% with the mitigation strategy that we have in place,” Teller said.

After nearly a week of nationwide public outcry and political calls for greater protections of the surrounding area, politicians, Iowans and environmental groups have repeatedly called for greater transparency from the Army and its associated federal partners. Sen.-elect Josh Turek called Tuesday's briefing “long-overdue” and said transparency and clear communication had been “sorely lacking up to this point.” He said Iowans' health and safety must be the top priority in the operation's execution.

Iowa Secretary of Agriculture Mike Naig told reporters Tuesday in Ames that it was “unfortunate” that the situation in Middletown had reached the point where detonation is the only option. He said questions would need to be answered after the detonation about how things got to this point and where communication broke down. He said the late notice to the state caused a lot of work over a short number of days and “could have been handled differently,” but added that Iowans' questions needed answers and farmers needed support through the process.

With harvests essentially complete for the year, Naig said the Iowa Department of Agriculture was focusing on helping livestock producers prepare for the detonation and record what they can before and after the operation for testing and compensation if necessary. He said a lot would need to be determined after the impact and the levels observed, with monitoring continuing throughout the area.


The environmental groups’ request for another pause, detailed in [Environmental groups seek another pause before Iowa lead azide detonation](/articles/2026-11-23-environmental-groups-seek-another-pause-before-iowa-lead-azide-detonation/), was followed by the Army’s addition of water walls, foam and expanded evacuation support.
