## Alberta to vote Monday on path to binding separation referendum

**Subtype:** fact

- Alberta Premier Danielle Smith has scheduled a Monday vote on whether the province should begin the legal process for a binding independence referendum from Canada.
- The vote follows a citizen petition signed by more than 300,000 Albertans that an Alberta court blocked earlier this year over insufficient consultation with Indigenous First Nations.
- First Nations in Alberta have called Smith's decision to proceed with the referendum "undemocratic" and "authoritarian."

## Polls show 70% of Albertans favor remaining in Canada

Alberta voters will go to the polls Monday in the first major test of national unity in decades, deciding whether the province should begin the legal process required to hold a binding referendum on separating from Canada.

The ballot question asks: "Should Alberta remain a province of Canada or should the Government of Alberta commence the legal process required under the Canadian Constitution to hold a binding provincial referendum on whether or not Alberta should separate from Canada?" Voters will check one of two boxes: option A to remain in Canada, or option B to begin the legal process for a binding separation referendum.

The vote follows a citizen petition signed by more than 300,000 Albertans that sought an independence referendum. An Alberta court blocked that petition earlier this year, ruling that the province had failed to consult Indigenous First Nations whose land would be affected should Alberta become independent. Smith's government has appealed the decision. Meanwhile, former Alberta deputy premier Thomas Lukaszuk organized a separate petition called Forever Canadian that gathered more than 400,000 signatures against separation. Alberta's population is slightly more than five million.

First Nations in Alberta have also criticised Smith, calling her decision to hold a referendum despite the court ruling "undemocratic" and "authoritarian."

While Monday's vote centers on independence, voters will also weigh nine other questions on immigration and constitutional matters. These include whether Alberta should have greater control over immigration to bring it to "more sustainable levels," whether to require proof of citizenship to vote in Alberta provincial elections, and access to social services such as healthcare for non-permanent residents. The constitutional questions include proposals to allow provinces to push for the power to elect provincial judges and senators; Canadian senators are currently appointed by the governor general on the advice of the prime minister.

The separatist movement is led by Mitch Sylvestre, a gun shop owner from Bonnyville, and Jeffrey Rath, a Calgary-based lawyer, both members of the Alberta Prosperity Project. The group argues that the province's economic growth has been hindered by years of Liberal Party rule in Ottawa and is particularly frustrated with environmental policies it says have stood in the way of building pipelines and unlocking resources from the oil-rich province. Separatists also argue that Alberta contributes more to Canada than it receives and that Ottawa has a disproportionate say in its internal affairs.

The sentiment is rooted in "western alienation" — a decades-old term describing a sense among some residents of Canada's western provinces that they are overlooked and underrepresented by federal politicians in Ottawa. The movement is not uniform: at separatist town halls last year, some attendees told the BBC they wanted to use the threat of independence as a bargaining chip with Ottawa, while others said they would pursue a full provincial divorce. A few said they would be open to Alberta joining the United States.

Rath has told the BBC that he believes Alberta has more in common culturally with parts of the US than with the rest of Canada. He has travelled to Washington several times on what he described as a "fact-finding" mission, meeting Trump administration officials to gauge whether the US government would support an independent Alberta with a line of credit. Rath has not publicly named which Trump administration officials the group has met with.

Smith, who announced the vote in May, supports a unified Canada and has said she will vote to remain. She said she was "deeply troubled" by the court decision and would not let "a single judge silence the voices of hundreds of thousands of Albertans." She added that "kicking the can down the road only prolongs a very emotional and important debate." She has faced pressure from separatist Albertans, some within her own United Conservative Party, to hold a referendum regardless of the legal decision.

Prime Minister Mark Carney, raised in Edmonton, has pressed for the province to remain, arguing that Alberta is central to his ambition of making Canada an "energy superpower." "We're renovating the country as we go, and Alberta being at the centre of that is essential," Carney said in May. Opposition Conservative leader Pierre Poilievre, who enjoys significant support in Alberta and was raised in Calgary, has said his party will advocate for unity. The launch of the referendum triggered a five-month campaign period for interested parties on both sides of the issue.

Polling suggests a strong majority of Albertans favor remaining in Canada. A Leger poll published earlier this month found 70% want the province to stay as is, just over 20% would vote to begin the legal process for a future binding referendum, and 7% were undecided. A September poll by Janet Brown Opinion Research for broadcaster CBC showed 72% support for the remain side and 23% for the leave side, with slightly higher leave support among male and rural voters. Despite the lead for remaining, there are concerns of a Brexit-style upset, particularly if voters use the ballot to "send a message" to Ottawa — though that scenario is seen as unlikely.

If Albertans vote to begin the legal process, Smith has said that could lead to a binding referendum as soon as 2027. The premier has committed to accepting Monday's result and campaigning for the remain position. "I will accept the result of this referendum," she said. "That's why I'll be campaigning hard to try to convince Albertans of my position, which is to remain." Rath has expressed disappointment in the question's wording, arguing it should have been a direct "stay or leave" option, and has threatened to organize a leadership challenge against Smith within her party. "To hell with 301,620 Albertans who were promised a vote on their question," he wrote on social media following her announcement. "Danielle Smith just lost her base!"

The ballots — one color-coded sheet per question — will be counted by hand starting at 20:00 local time Monday. Election officials have 48 hours to complete the count, with the independence question (Question 10) to be tallied first given high public interest. Results for that question are expected in the early hours of Tuesday.
