## Draft calls for firing museum director, new exhibits and curriculum changes

A draft titled “Smithsonian Institution Renewal Plan” surfaced on the laptop of a White House associate policy adviser aboard a Washington, D.C., Metro train in late October, ProPublica reported. The 25-item proposal calls for firing the Smithsonian National Museum of American History’s director and other senior staff, adding exhibits and revising K-12 materials. The White House did not respond to ProPublica’s query, and the Smithsonian declined to comment.

The document came into view during the last week of October, as political Washington focused on the coming midterm elections. A commuter traveling from the Northern Virginia suburbs on the Orange Line noticed a man in a gray tartan suit with the plan displayed on his laptop. The commuter took phone pictures over the man’s shoulder and sent them to ProPublica.

The laptop displayed the initials B.S. or S.B. ProPublica reporters checked White House staffing records, returned to the Orange Line and found a likely match carrying the same style of brown leather satchel and using the same type of phone. The man left the train in Washington and walked toward a White House employee checkpoint. He was Sam Bock, a 27-year-old associate policy adviser with the Domestic Policy Council, the White House office that produced the July report. Bock previously worked on the staff of Rep. Jim Jordan, the Ohio Republican elected the previous week as House Minority Leader.

The 25-item list called for firing museum director Anthea Hartig and a dozen other top administrators and curators. It also proposed a permanent exhibit called “The Riches of Our Western Civilization Inheritance,” an exhibit on “Great Families of American History,” and a “Colossus Statue” of George Washington for the museum’s entrance. The American history museum employs several hundred people, houses several million artifacts and draws close to 2 million visitors per year. ProPublica said that implementing the proposal in anything like its written form would represent an unprecedented assertion of White House control over the Smithsonian, which is structured to be independent.

Other items would issue new interpretive guidelines and end the museum’s reliance on a diversity style guide. The draft would review the Smithsonian’s strategy for traveling exhibitions, including halting the exhibition underway, “The Bias Inside Us.” It would also review Smithsonian financial grants to organizations the plan called “woke third parties,” including the American Alliance of Museums.

For the museum’s online K-12 curriculum, the draft calls for changes to “realign educational materials with patriotism, not ideology.” The plan would drop the “Becoming US” module on immigration and launch a founders-focused curriculum with possible titles “Architects of Liberty: The Heroic Journey of America’s Founders OR Profiles in Liberty: Washington and America’s Heroic Men of Courage.”

The document appeared unfinished. The staffer’s handwritten notes in view proposed “announce exciting cool exhibit on D-Day” and “no X rated exhibitions policy.” ProPublica said the latter likely referred to a gay history exhibit whose two-piece chrome and rubber crotch harness, designed for sadomasochism, drew criticism in the July report and two congressional hearings.

The proposal came after months of White House criticism and other actions involving the museum. This summer, the Trump administration criticized the Smithsonian for promoting “radical activist ideology,” and the pressure helped prompt Smithsonian Secretary Lonnie Bunch’s departure. Before the draft appeared, the White House’s next move on this front had remained unclear. A 162-page White House critique released July 4 was followed by back-to-back congressional hearings and a Trump executive order requiring warning signs outside the museum about “ideological capture.” The Smithsonian had bid farewell to Bunch at a board meeting the previous month. The signs had not gone up, and the precise changes the White House sought from the report remained unclear.

The White House report said the museum should not whitewash slavery or Jim Crow but argued that it did not explain how the country developed. It stated, “It should tell the whole story of a nation,” including “the good and the bad, so Museum visitors can understand the development that led our nation to where it is today.” The report was released with symbolic fanfare on the 250th anniversary of the nation’s founding.

ProPublica also placed the draft in the museum’s longer history, which it had recounted in September. Founded in 1964, the American history museum had struggled with its mission of telling the story of a vast and variegated nation and faced another round of criticism in 2001 that led a blue-ribbon commission to call for a more unified national story with fewer nods to political correctness. After the murder of George Floyd in Minneapolis in 2020, Bunch and other museum leaders led an institution-wide self-reckoning. By Trump’s return to office in 2025, the July report cited a recent exhibit on Ben Franklin that speculated about whether he had used enslaved members of his household as subjects of his experiments with electric shocks. It also chastised the museum for having trained staff using the “Museums as a Site for Social Action Toolkit,” a 200-plus-page tract on how to “truly transform the museum space.” The report said the toolkit was linked with “white supremacy, hetero-patriarchy, abuse of labor, colonization, imperialist theft of art and artifacts, destruction or absencing of alternative ways of interpreting or representing art and artifacts, structural racism and other oppressions.”

The Smithsonian encompasses 20 museums beyond the American history museum and has a 17-member board led by the chief justice of the Supreme Court. The board includes the U.S. vice president, three members of Congress from each party and nine private citizens nominated by other board members. The institution receives a majority of its funding from the federal government, more than $1 billion per year. It is seeking a replacement for Bunch, while the board has vacancies as private-citizen terms expire without replacements.

Until President Donald Trump attempted to fire the director of the National Portrait Gallery the previous year, no president had attempted to remove a Smithsonian executive. The Smithsonian resisted that effort, but National Portrait Gallery Director Kim Sajet decided to leave.

Bock did not respond to messages, and the Smithsonian declined to comment. The White House also did not answer ProPublica’s query about the draft, which was described as apparently still a work in progress. The warning signs ordered by the executive order had not gone up. ProPublica described the way the plan surfaced as an inadvertent reveal and a soft rollout on the Orange Line.
