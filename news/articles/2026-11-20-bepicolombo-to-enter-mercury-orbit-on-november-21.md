## Planetary flybys slowed BepiColombo before its Mercury arrival

The joint European-Japanese BepiColombo mission is scheduled to fire its main engine on November 21 to enter orbit around Mercury. After more than eight years and 6 billion miles in space, the spacecraft is approaching the solar system's innermost planet with a series of orbital adjustments ahead.

To reach Mercury, BepiColombo had to slow down so it would not fall directly into the Sun. It flew by Earth once, Venus twice and Mercury itself six times, using the gravity of those planets to reduce its speed.

Clerigo said BepiColombo was approaching Mercury at about 670 miles per hour, a crawl by interplanetary standards. The slow speed makes his life a little less stressful as orbit entry approaches, he said. The mission also retains a chance to try again: “If something does not work as expected, we still have a chance to come back and to try a second time.”

Over the coming weeks, Clerigo said BepiColombo will fire its engines a total of 16 times to adjust its orbit so it can fulfill its scientific mission.

Clerigo said BepiColombo should be the first mission to take a very close look at the southern part of Mercury.

Anne Pommier, a scientist on the BepiColombo mission at the Carnegie Institution, said the spacecraft should provide some answers. The questions include why Mercury has an unusual chemistry, a weak magnetic field and a large metallic core, and why the planet appears to be shrinking.

Pommier said the mission's instruments will help detect ice found in some craters on Mercury. She said scientists were surprised that ice could form on a planet so close to the Sun.

Pommier said a better understanding of Mercury should help scientists figure out how planets around other stars formed. She also said that understanding exoplanets in other solar systems requires understanding how the solar system works first.

Joe Palca said the scientific results should start rolling in next spring.
