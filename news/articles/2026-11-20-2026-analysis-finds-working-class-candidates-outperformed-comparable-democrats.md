## College graduates favored Democrats by 26 points; non-degree voters by 4

In a Guardian commentary published November 20, 2026, Dustin Guastella and Jared Abbott said their district-adjusted analysis showed candidates with working-class backgrounds outperforming comparable Democrats in the 2026 midterms. The authors compared candidates with other Democrats in districts with similar political lean and demographic makeup. To estimate how a typical Democrat would have performed in the same district, Guastella and Abbott used its 2024 presidential vote, demographics and whether an incumbent was running. They called the statistic “wins above replacement,” or War. Their central finding was that candidates with working-class backgrounds performed better than professional-class progressives and centrists backed by the New Democrat Coalition. The authors said the former had “a bold economic vision for working people” and knew how “to talk to workers.”

Guastella and Abbott said Democrats still needed to improve with working-class voters. They described voters without a four-year degree as the largest group in the electorate, at 57% of 2026 voters, and as a swing group. Voters without a college degree moved seven points toward Trump from a six-point Republican margin in 2020 to a 13-point margin in 2024, then moved back by 17 points in 2026. Democrats won college graduates by 26 points and voters without degrees by four points in 2026, leaving a 22-point education gap. They also lost white voters without degrees by 19 points. Guastella and Abbott said Democrats would need to improve with these voters to win what they called a “commanding and durable majority,” capable both of implementing their progressive economic plans and “finally delivering for working Americans.” The authors framed the remaining question as what kind of Democrat could improve the party’s performance with those voters.

Guastella and Abbott began with Democrats’ strong performance on November 3, 2026. The party ran an average of about 15 points ahead of Kamala Harris’s 2024 margins, picked up seats Donald Trump had carried by double digits two years earlier, and saw Democrats win elections across the party in places considered unlikely. They said the size and scope of the results made specific lessons difficult to draw because commentators would interpret the outcome through their prior beliefs. The authors wrote that “all sorts of Democrats won big in 2026 thanks to a backlash against Trump,” but warned it would be a big mistake to assume that any Democrat would do in 2028. They said a decade of experience, including 2026, showed that “I’m not Trump” was not the slam dunk Democrats wanted. Democrats still needed a program and political pitch for 2028, the authors said.

The candidate-level results did not bear out, “at least not in a straightforward way,” the longstanding argument that progressives were best suited to winning back the working class, Guastella and Abbott said. Although most liberal candidates performed well overall, the most liberal generally fared worse than their centrist counterparts relative to the estimate for a typical Democrat. Nominees backed by Justice Democrats ran about one point behind the estimate, while Bernie Sanders’s Senate endorsees ran about six-and-a-half points behind. Centrists had argued on social media that their politics was more suited to building majorities, which Guastella and Abbott said could overstep, especially on social class. Joe Weisenthal of Bloomberg’s Odd Lots podcast tweeted, “The whole theory that to expand the map that Dems had to nominate working classoids who dress in Carhartts just seems totally obliterated.” Guastella and Abbott responded, “Is that true? Hardly.”

Guastella and Abbott said the comparison favored a slate of union-backed working-class candidates who ran as the Blue Collar Brigade: Brian Poindexter, a union ironworker in Ohio; Randy Villegas, a community college professor from California’s Central Valley; Sam Forstag, a former smokejumper in Montana; and Bob Brooks, a firefighter in Pennsylvania. As a group, the four averaged nearly +8 in War and ran about eight points ahead of comparable Democrats on average. No other progressive or centrist group’s nominees averaged better than about +3. Villegas ran about 14 points ahead, although his race was still being counted.

Guastella and Abbott said individual results also favored four candidates backed by both Bernie Sanders and the moderate Blue Dog Democrats: Rebecca Cooke, a waitress in Wisconsin; Sam Forstag; Bob Brooks; and Brian Poindexter. Cooke ran about four points above the estimate, Forstag about five, Brooks about two and Poindexter almost 11. Their average was about +5, compared with about +2 for nominees backed only by the Blue Dogs and about even for those backed only by Sanders.

Guastella and Abbott said Abdul El-Sayed, a doctor, and Peggy Flanagan, a nonprofit executive turned lieutenant governor, were professional-class progressives backed by Bernie Sanders and progressive PACs. El-Sayed ran five points behind the estimate, while Flanagan ran six points behind. Tom Suozzi and Jared Moskowitz, centrists backed by the New Democrat Coalition, trailed by one-and-a-half and six points, respectively.

Guastella and Abbott identified the combination they called the “Bernie-dog” candidate as the apparent sweet spot: someone who leans left on wages, jobs and the economy, discusses those issues in plain language and steers toward the center on immigration and criminal justice. “These are candidates who are justifiably frustrated with trade deals that shipped their communities’ jobs overseas, who value patriotism and tradition while sticking up for the working class against the economic giants that are grinding them into dust,” the authors wrote.

The authors argued that candidate class remained important and said it mattered when candidates came from the popular classes. Professional-class progressives such as El-Sayed received more media attention, while moderates such as Jon Ossoff could rely on donors, Guastella and Abbott said. They said candidates with working-class backgrounds remained underappreciated despite their ability to break through. Guastella and Abbott wrote, “Social class remains a major issue unto itself and blue-collar populists are uniquely able to tap into the frustrations of blue-collar voters.”

Guastella and Abbott cited Troy Jackson, the soon-to-be senator from Maine, and his defeat of Susan Collins. A late-campaign attack ad questioned Jackson’s competence because of his background, while Collins’s “cord of wood” ad implied that he was better suited to logging than legislating. The authors wrote, “That kind of visceral class condescension makes the Gilded Age economy much more real for voters and could have shifted his margins in a favorable direction.” Jackson succeeded in unseating Susan Collins, whom they described as supposedly untouchable.

The authors said it was not clear what direction the Democratic Party would take, but maintained that candidates with working-class backgrounds were “still the most underrated current in the party.” They urged Democrats seeking to win back working-class voters to invest in candidates with working-class backgrounds before 2028. The commentary’s subheading had described “Bernie-Dog” candidates as “the most underrated success stories in US politics.”

Guastella is a research associate at the Center for Working Class Politics and the director of operations for Teamsters Local 623. Abbott is the director of the Center for Working-Class Politics.
