## Officials deny eight alleged open meetings violations

Three south-central Louisiana residents have filed a lawsuit against Louisiana and Vermilion Parish officials, alleging the officials violated the state's open meetings law eight times while facilitating an agreement that gave SpaceX access to 125,000 acres (50,600 hectares) of coastal marsh formerly owned by ExxonMobil. The suit follows Louisiana and Vermilion Parish officials' approval of an estimated record $27.5bn tax incentive package for SpaceX's Starbase Louisiana project. The package was announced over the summer. Officials deny the allegations and say they followed the law.

The lawsuit alleges that the parish board was manipulated to allow the agreement to go through, the ExxonMobil settlement agreement was hidden from the public, and officials violated the state's open meetings law eight times in attempts to “keep the public in the dark about what they are doing for SpaceX.” After officials ended a lawsuit over ExxonMobil's damage to wetlands, Louisiana officials moved quickly to approve the marsh agreement. Louisiana Gov. Jeff Landry announced the agreement to build a new spaceport in Abbeville on August 25, 2026.

Before its work on the SpaceX tax abatement, the Vermilion Parish industrial development board had not been active for nearly three decades. In July 2026, the Vermilion Parish police jury appointed seven members to the board without advance notice at the same meeting where it approved the ExxonMobil settlement, which was not on the agenda, according to the lawsuit.

Paul Moresi, the police jury's legal counsel, said in an interview with WWL Louisiana that the officials deny the lawsuit's allegations and followed the requirements of the state's open meetings law.

The governor's office then collected all copies of the ExxonMobil settlement agreement, leaving its details unclear, said New Orleans attorney William Most. “The Vermilion parish government has been violating transparency laws in totally new and unprecedented ways including using code names in public agendas and making sure they don’t keep copies of the contracts they sign so their constituents can’t read them,” Most said. He added that “Transparency is so important because it helps prevent the state from making the same mistakes of the past and letting out-of-state corporations take our resources and hurt our citizens.”

Jackson Voss, the government affairs and policy coordinator for the Alliance for Affordable Energy, said there was still much unknown about the deals, making it harder to know what the state would receive in return for lost tax revenue and potentially further wetland damage. “I think that what we’ve done with this deal is subsidise both ExxonMobil and SpaceX. We’re helping two of the biggest corporations in the world to make even more money than they’re already making,” he said.

SpaceX told local residents that 30 rockets per day could be launched from the spaceport's 10 launch pads, or about 10,000 launches per year.

In a separate agreement reached last month, the University of Louisiana System Board approved the lease of five acres of UL Lafayette property to the nonprofit Ragin' Cajun Facilities for $1 per year, along with a sublease to Space Exploration Technologies, or SpaceX. The arrangement would allow SpaceX to expand the footprint of its 125,000-acre spaceport in Vermilion Parish's coastal marshlands.

The lease does not say SpaceX will launch rockets from the five-acre site. It includes a wide range of permitted uses, such as training and workforce development, warehousing and assembly, and “testing and light industrial operations”. In exchange, SpaceX agreed to invest $10m in planned improvements to the site, which was donated to the university the previous year for a wildlife and coastal preservation center.

Local residents have raised concerns that the launch site could disrupt migratory bird habitat on a cheniere surrounded by fast-eroding marshland. A lawsuit against ExxonMobil that was settled this summer alleged that decades of drilling made the company partly responsible for severe land loss in the parish.

Louisiana is experiencing some of the highest rates of relative sea level rise in the world, while wetland loss is estimated at a football field every 100 minutes. Leveeing the lower Mississippi River contributed to much of Louisiana's land loss, but wetland loss in Vermilion Parish was largely caused by oil and gas activities, said coastal researcher Alex Kolker, the director of the Coastal Climates Institute. “It’s a little different from other parts of Louisiana because you never had much of a river input,” Kolker said. “Changes in hydrology of the Mississippi River weren’t as impactful.”

Voss said the university and those responsible for the SpaceX tax deal were making a lot of bets without any real guarantees. “We’re going out of our way to make sure that whatever deal is reached is the best possible for industry no matter how much it costs everyone else,” he said. “It really is like a fire sale: get what you can before it’s too late.”
