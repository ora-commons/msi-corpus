## Texas Democrats win every statewide office; Cruz is lone GOP holder

Osita Nwanevu, a Guardian US columnist, wrote on Nov. 16, 2026 that Americans have reached a real point of decision in the aftermath of the 2026 midterms — not on election day itself, but in the uncertain weeks after it. The Democratic party, Nwanevu argued, now faces a critical crossroad: its task is to finally advance an agenda that both indicts Trump and looks beyond him.

Until 2026, the most important midterm year of the last quarter-century was very probably 2010, Nwanevu wrote, and the column arrives as Democrats absorb a set of victories measured against that benchmark. In Mississippi, quixotic-seeming investments aimed at harnessing anger over the decade's regression in voting rights paid off with the election of Scott Colom, who will be the first Democratic senator to serve in that state since the 1980s. In Texas, Democrats captured a long-sought-after Senate seat and every statewide position on the ballot, including the governorship. In the Senate alone, a slate of freshmen — from Iowa moderate Josh Turek to Michigan's Abdul El-Sayed — will be positioned to help the party shed its gerontocratic image, Nwanevu wrote.

Nwanevu cautioned that the size of the impact will take a lifetime to size up. "We will, in all probability, be sussing out the impact of what happened in the 2026 midterms for the rest of our lives," he wrote, "this is as likely if Democrats squander the stunning victories they've achieved and offer Republicans a path back to power as it is if the election winds up marking the beginning of the end of the modern Republican party." Both outcomes, and many in between them, seem plausible, Nwanevu wrote; what is certain is that the American political landscape, for the next few years at an absolute minimum, has fundamentally changed.

Ted Cruz described his own position in a solemn post-election video to his followers. "As we sit here today, I am the only statewide elected Republican who remains in Texas. That's not something I ever thought I would say," Cruz said.

Nwanevu cautioned that the scale of the victories should not be read as an endorsement. The Democratic wins, he wrote, were products less of confidence in the party and its vision than of the electorate's pique toward the present administration and its policies, particularly a war with Iran that, like the president's tariff regime before it, has raised prices. Going into election day, Nwanevu noted, Republicans were barely more unpopular than the Democrats.

The columnist drew an explicit parallel to 2010, when Barack Obama famously called the elections a "shellacking" for the Democratic party and, Nwanevu wrote, he was right. Thanks to a sluggish economic recovery from the Great Recession and backlash to the passage of the Affordable Care Act, Republicans took control of the House with a 63-seat gain — the largest House sweep since the 1940s — and won six seats in the Senate. Nwanevu noted that the 2010 midterms were significant well beyond those numbers: it was the first election shaped by the rise of the Tea Party and its blend of rightwing economic populism and xenophobia. Those aligned with the movement lost nearly twice the number of races they won, but the presence of an energized hard-right contingent among Republicans in Congress shaped legislative outcomes for the remainder of Obama's term and beyond, Nwanevu wrote.

A cadre of figures the Tea Party successfully backed that year — Marco Rubio, Rand Paul, Mike Lee, Ron Johnson, Mick Mulvaney and Kristi Noem — have become key or leading figures in the Republican party in the time since. Just over a month after the new Congress was sworn in that cycle, Donald Trump delivered an address to conservative activists and operatives at the Conservative Political Action Conference, where he aligned himself with the stances of Tea Party voters on taxes, trade and Obamacare, and voiced agreement with rightwing conspiracy theories about Obama's origins that had gained currency within the movement. "If we'd been paying closer attention, it would have portended much more," Nwanevu wrote.

In Nwanevu's telling, what has happened since the 2026 election has already strained the Democrats' initial response. Stephen Miller will now be running the White House as chief of staff, and the ideas the administration has floated since that announcement have included going after non-profit groups for supposed terrorist ties, banning pregnant women from entering the country and naming the UFC's Dana White as press secretary — a joke, Trump claimed. Those developments, Nwanevu wrote, have rendered the remarkable post-election unity Democrats displayed awkward: establishment leaders and progressive stalwarts such as Bernie Sanders signaled a sincere willingness to work with the administration on affordability legislation, on top of messaging bills destined for an expected veto.

Nwanevu argued that the impeachment of Pete Hegseth alone will not, and should not, satisfy the Democratic base, and that Democrats will have to spend more time than most in the party seem to want litigating the outrages to come. At the same time, he wrote, the instincts of most Democrats that the party needs to keep a focus on material politics are right. Cory Booker said that the new Congress will have to draw out the "real connection between corruption and costs". Greg Casar, leader of the House's progressives, has promised to introduce a group of 10 bills "that focuses on costs and healthcare and corruption".

The prescription Nwanevu offered is an agenda that carries a message that Trump's material and moral failures sit atop systemic failures and tendencies within Republican politics that will follow whoever Trump's successor as leader of the party happens to be. Because Trump is unlikely to enact measures that meaningfully constrain him or hold him accountable for his own malfeasance, Nwanevu wrote, the Democrats' task is to finally advance an agenda that both indicts him and looks beyond him. That messaging hasn't quite coalesced yet, he wrote, but the factions vying for control of the Democratic party seem to have reached temporary agreement on a loose policy platform for the near to medium term: a higher minimum wage and taxes on the very wealthy, more attempts at major healthcare reform and immigration reform, new regulations to rein in tech and AI, and restrictions on foreign aid to rein in Israel.

Nwanevu credited the left's organization and advocacy over the last decade with bringing Democrats to broad agreement on those priorities, and wrote that the party will have to lean into them all the harder now that Trump — the argument for Democratic governance the party has come to rely upon — is shuffling off the political scene. Whether he actually will, the columnist added, remains open: fears of a third term now seem especially implausible, but Trump will almost certainly remain a figure of influence on the right after he leaves office, with the extent of that influence depending on how much more damage he manages to do over the next two years and on the political calculus of Republican business interests, media executives and donors, who may well decide that rightwing populism has outlasted its usefulness.

For those actors and for the American people alike, Nwanevu concluded, the real point of decision is not election day — even one like the 2026 midterms — but the heady, uncertain moment after it.
