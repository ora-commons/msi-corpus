## Federal funding cuts leave food banks buying more supplies

At Cross-Lines Community Outreach in Kansas City, Kansas, the line at check-in had grown two days before Thanksgiving as more people came to stock their cupboards. Andreas Leal, a 75-year-old retiree, and his wife perused a dwindling meat freezer and a hodgepodge of donated items on the toiletries shelves. Leal said every bit helps as he tries to keep up with rising prices. “It’s very expensive,” he said. “Everything is expensive right now.”

Leal said his monthly Social Security benefit cannot keep pace with rising costs. Although the federal program increased benefits by 2.8% this year, he said his residential property-tax bill will jump 30%, costing him about $600. To conserve gas, the couple forgoes trips to visit their son, who lives about 45 minutes away.

Cross-Lines’ Community Market, a food pantry resembling a grocery store, serves about 1,900 families per month, a 35% increase over last year. “Daily, we have to turn people away,” Executive Director Susila Jones said. The market once aimed to provide every family with a couple pounds of frozen ground beef each month. Higher costs have pushed Cross-Lines to buy ground turkey or chicken when those prices are low enough.

The pantry serves Wyandotte County, one of Kansas’s most populous counties, where the poverty rate exceeds the state and national averages. In recent years, Cross-Lines has relaxed its income requirements and welcomed people who seek aid from multiple organizations. Executive Director Susila Jones said, “This is not what people want to do and so we really just let people come to us when they need to come to us.” She added, “Because we know even if you are working, if you’ve had steady income, just one paycheck can put you over the edge.”

At the same time, charitable providers are seeing an influx of people who have lost access to the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program, or SNAP, the nation’s largest food assistance program. Stateline reported that about 5.5 million Americans have lost benefits since the One Big Beautiful Bill Act passed last July. The law cut taxes and federal safety-net programs, changed SNAP eligibility rules, ended certain work-requirement exemptions and shifted new costs to states that administer the federal program.

More people are expected to lose assistance as the legislation is fully implemented in coming years. Earlier this month, the Trump administration proposed enrollment rule changes that would cut eligibility for another 6.3 million people. Without congressional action, few experts see much relief ahead, adding pressure to the patchwork of churches, food banks and nonprofits that feed people. Gina Plata-Nino, SNAP director at the anti-hunger nonprofit Food Research and Action Center, said, “Unfortunately, it has become a big part of people’s lives because they keep cutting off SNAP benefits.” She added, “…And you have these volunteers at food banks trying to do their best, but they are suffering in terms of being able to keep up.”

In Detroit’s East Side, a line of cars waiting to pick up ingredients for a full Thanksgiving dinner stretched a mile last week. The Capuchin Soup Kitchen closed its line a few hours after opening when it became clear that the annual giveaway would quickly exhaust 500 turkey meal kits. Brother Fred Cabras, director of social services at the nearly century-old Catholic nonprofit, said, “If we had 750, we still would have to shut the line down.” He added, “I imagine that whatever number we set, we will probably hit every time because there is a huge demand.”

Capuchin serves about 1,000 hot meals each day across two locations and operates a food pantry for thousands of families. Food pantry visits have been surging in recent years. The pantry served about 15,000 families in 2022, and Cabras expects that figure to reach 34,000 to 35,000 by the end of this year. “This is definitely worse than the pandemic,” Cabras said.

Demand and food prices caused Capuchin to spend about $20,000 more on food last month than in September, although private donations remained strong. Cabras said the coronavirus pandemic and last year’s unprecedented disruption to SNAP benefits because of the federal government shutdown heightened public awareness and compassion about hunger. “People also realize it because they’re spending more money on groceries,” he said. “And they’re like, ‘Well if I have a decent job, and I’m struggling, I can’t even imagine for the folks who are not getting great wages how much they’re struggling.’”

In Louisville, Kentucky, United Crescent Hill Ministries serves about 50 people per day at its food pantry. That is quadruple the number of people it served last year, Associate Director Courtney Goff said. The nonprofit is supported by local churches, businesses and individuals. Goff said federal cuts to food pantry aid and rising consumer prices had prompted the group to purchase more food and seek more cash and food donations.

People tend to donate more generously around the holiday season, but high prices have also squeezed the budgets of many regular donors to charitable food groups. Still, the food disappears nearly as quickly as it’s stocked because so many people are struggling to afford the basics. “Everybody’s struggling. People who were not struggling a year ago are in a different space, just because of the rising costs,” Goff said. She described households choosing among rent, groceries and gas: “You have people who maybe they make enough money to pay their rent, but rent is so high that that means they don’t have money for groceries or they don’t have money for gas,” she said. “And so they’re picking what is the most vital in that moment, and then trying to scramble together for the rest of it.”

St. Mary’s Food Bank in Phoenix, Arizona, which calls itself the World’s First Food Bank, has drawn down savings this year to meet higher diesel and fuel costs. “We bought more food than we ever have before,” said Jerry Brown, the food bank’s director of public relations. “And just like everybody else who goes to the grocery store, that food is costing more.” The food bank serves hundreds of communities across Arizona.

Arizona has experienced the nation’s sharpest decline in SNAP benefits. Between July 2025 and July 2026, about 450,000 Arizonans lost benefits, a decrease of more than 50%. Brown said St. Mary’s has seen an influx of people who lost SNAP and more visits from retirees unable to keep up with rising prices. “They thought they had enough money to retire on, and then the price of everything skyrocketed, while the amount of money that they receive remains the same,” he said.

In Texas, some food banks have rationed food because demand exceeds what they can provide. The pressure comes from households losing SNAP and from separate reductions in federal food assistance. Last year, the Trump administration cut hundreds of millions of dollars from a local food program serving food banks and school meals, and the U.S. Department of Agriculture slashed $500 million already allocated to food aid nationwide through the Emergency Food Assistance Program.

Those changes have pushed more food banks to purchase food their communities need, said Celia Cole, CEO of Feeding Texas, a statewide network of 20 food banks. “And that’s a real concern because it’s not really sustainable,” Cole said. She foresaw no immediate relief from high food prices but said politicians were increasingly recognizing the growing threat of hunger across the country. Cole was also hopeful that Congress would make changes to last year’s federal legislation: “I think everyone recognizes the importance of having a well-nourished population and sees how these changes to SNAP are leading to people losing access to what’s really the backbone defense against hunger in our country,” she said. “So I see a light at the end of the tunnel there because I think there is a lot of bipartisan concern.”
