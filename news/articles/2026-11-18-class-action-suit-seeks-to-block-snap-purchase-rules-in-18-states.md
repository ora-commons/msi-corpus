## Plaintiffs say USDA lacks authority for broad SNAP changes

A class-action lawsuit filed Wednesday in federal court in the District of Columbia seeks to block restrictions on what Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program recipients may buy. Led by the National Center for Law and Economic Justice, the case targets state waivers limiting products including candy and soda in 18 states. The plaintiffs argue that the U.S. Department of Agriculture lacks legal authority to use the waivers for a large-scale change to SNAP's structure.

SNAP has traditionally allowed recipients broad autonomy to select their own groceries, while the Trump administration has encouraged states to seek federal waivers that limit what recipients can buy. Those waivers target a range of products but generally focus on sugary beverages and candies.

“These unlawful waivers serve as a backdoor method to restrict access to SNAP,” Katharine Deabler-Meadows, senior attorney at the National Center for Law and Economic Justice, said in a Wednesday news release. “Our lawsuit seeks to hold the Trump administration accountable for violating the law and causing unprecedented chaos for SNAP recipients and retailers alike.”

The lawsuit names Arkansas, Florida, Hawaii, Idaho, Indiana, Kansas, Louisiana, Missouri, Montana, Nevada, North Dakota, Ohio, Oklahoma, South Carolina, Texas, Utah, Virginia and Wyoming. In earlier litigation, a judge blocked state and federal efforts to use waivers to restrict SNAP purchases in Colorado, Iowa, Nebraska, Tennessee and West Virginia.

The waivers vary by state. Hawaii's waiver restricts only purchases of soft drinks, according to the U.S. Department of Agriculture. Florida's waiver restricts purchases of soda, energy drinks, candy and prepared desserts.

The federal government argues that the restrictions divert taxpayer dollars away from food purchases the administration says fuel the nation's epidemics of diabetes and other chronic diseases. In a statement last year, U.S. Health and Human Services Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. said, “These waivers help put real food back at the center of the program and empower states to lead the charge in protecting public health.”

Critics say the changes could harm people in food deserts, where healthy options are limited, and take away recipients' power to make their own food choices. In the lawsuit, plaintiffs argue that the changes have harmed recipients' health.

One Idaho plaintiff said she has multiple chronic health conditions, including autoimmune conditions that make it difficult to regulate her blood pressure, heart rate and blood sugar. She relies on electrolyte-infused drinks and easy-to-eat nutrition and granola bars that are now restricted in Idaho. The lawsuit said she has frequently been dehydrated since the waiver took effect and that her kidney function, digestion and autonomic function have worsened. It said her autonomic function affects her heart and her ability to remain upright.

Another plaintiff in Oklahoma said she relies on easy-to-prepare foods because she lives in an extended-stay hotel without a kitchen. She said upper gastrointestinal issues cause discomfort and that she drinks soda to relieve gas trapped in her esophagus. The lawsuit said those beverages are now restricted under Oklahoma's waiver.

At issue is whether USDA may use the waiver process to alter the structure of a federal food-assistance program at this scale. The class-action complaint asks the court to strike down the restrictions and argues that the department lacks the legal authority to do so.
