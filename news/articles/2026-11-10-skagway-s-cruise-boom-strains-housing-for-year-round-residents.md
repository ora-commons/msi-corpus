## Seasonal workers moved into crowded homes after Skagway camping ban

Skagway counted nearly 2 million cruise-ship visitors and crew in 2025, according to the most recent figures available. More than 1.35 million were tourists and more than half a million were crew members; the passenger count had risen from 820,000 in 2013, and the numbers were growing each year. Skagway is now one of the world’s most popular cruise-ship destinations. It had about 1,100 permanent residents, while housing, services and infrastructure designed for a town of about 1,000 people had to accommodate thousands of workers arriving in late winter and staying until early autumn.

Like many tourist towns, Skagway has a housing shortage. A report commissioned by the Skagway Development Corporation in 2023 found 644 housing units in town. More have been added since, but not enough for the town to accommodate the cruise-ship industry and the businesses that support it, its permanent residents, seasonal employees, and the workers and families who keep essential services running. Skagway is squeezed into a narrow valley at the northern end of Alaska’s Inside Passage, a 500-mile maritime route that hugs the coast along Alaska and British Columbia. The route is known for rugged mountains, forests, glaciers and abundant wildlife. Skagway has no realistic commuting options and no room to expand; its only road leads to Canada.

Skagway’s scale has changed sharply before. At the height of the Klondike gold rush, it was Alaska’s largest town, with about 10,000 people. It became Alaska’s first official city in 1900, but by 1910, after the gold rush subsided, its population had dwindled to about 600. The historical contraction took a decade. The modern cruise trade can produce a sharp seasonal shift in a single day, when thousands of passengers arrive and leave as ships call. The historic White Pass & Yukon Route railroad depot is in Skagway, and a “Gateway to the Klondike” mural adorns a town building. Part of the town’s appeal is its Klondike gold-rush heritage.

In October, as the 2026 cruise season wound down, shops that had been busy throughout the tourist season were reducing their opening hours or beginning to close. The crowds of cruise passengers who had filled the streets since April, wearing lanyards emblazoned with the names of their ships, dwindled to a trickle, while employees who had slept in overcrowded bunkhouses were leaving or had already left. The Guardian reported that the remaining cruise passengers would soon all be gone. Snow was visible on nearby mountains. After the season ended and the last ship disappeared from sight, what remained was a year-round community of about 1,100 people — on paper at least — and concern that life would become more difficult as demands from the cruise industry grew.

Accommodation has expanded beyond conventional housing. Warehouses, single-family homes, tents, recreational vehicles and motorhomes are accommodating increasing numbers of seasonal workers each year. Al Wall, director of the town’s Dahl Memorial Clinic, said, “There’s a lot of congregate living and couch surfing.” He added, “There are quite a few people that sleep in hammocks in the woods. And frankly, some would prefer to sleep in a hammock in a tree than stay in a crowded place with a bunch of people.”

In earlier years, many seasonal workers slept in camp sites. A change in Skagway’s law forbidding season-long camping pushed them into traditional homes that previously housed the town’s established population. Janilyn Heger, a longtime Skagwegian, owns the town’s distillery, Skagway Spirits. She and her husband Gary, who owns a construction company, once managed the town-owned camp sites. Heger said, “The city made the decision for whatever reason to abandon that type of housing, and that created a domino effect that has been a disaster for the community.” She also said, “Employers started buying buildings and displacing year-round housing and so now we’re at an absolute standstill. There’s none to be had.”

Skagway’s housing shortage has become so severe that many of the town’s limited hotels have been converted into accommodation for seasonal employees. Longtime resident Mike Healy bought Sergeant Preston’s hotel from friends when he was unable to find land to build enough employee housing to meet his businesses’ needs. He explained, “I didn’t want to purchase Sergeant Preston’s … I approached the city and said, hey, Municipality of Skagway, I am going to purchase the only year-round hotel in this community if you don’t free up some land for housing.” After receiving no answer, he converted the hotel into seasonal housing. His portfolio now provides off-season accommodation to visiting workers and contractors.

Town assembly member Jonathan Hillis, who owns the Mountain Shop outdoor gear store, said that pattern was playing out across town. “We have very few places now for independent travellers to stay, which is fostering this kind of cycle of more and more cruise ship tourists and fewer independent travellers.”

Two local restaurants remain open with limited hours outside the cruise season, but the Sweet Tooth cafe, once a longstanding year-round diner, is gone. It is now a storefront for Diamonds International, a tax-free jewellery store that partners with cruise lines for onboard retail and shops in ports that open seasonally. Mayor Orion Hanson said, “The year-round economy, the year-round population is one of my bigger concerns as someone who lives here.” He asked, “What does Skagway do without a coffee shop to congregate in or without a restaurant open all the time? We don’t have a diner any more.”

Mayor Orion Hanson said the boom-and-lull cycle leaves Skagway with little revenue not directly tied to cruise ships. “Part of the complication of Skagway is that you have such a boom in the summer and such a lull in the winter,” Hanson said. “In terms of revenue that’s not directly related to cruise ships there is not a lot.” He also said, “As we saw during the pandemic, we’re at risk,” adding, “If all your eggs are in one basket, it leaves you very vulnerable.”

An Oregon State University survey found a mixed assessment of the industry’s effects in Skagway. Just over 40% of people who responded said tourism’s benefits outweighed its costs, compared with 25% who put it the other way round. Another 33% viewed the relationship as roughly balanced. For a town economically dependent on one industry for its livelihood, options are limited.

“There’s only so much we can do, even if we wanted to,” Hanson said. “We need to be incredibly careful about not rocking the boat.”

The scale of cruise tourism is a frequent topic of debate in towns throughout the Inside Passage. As the 2026 season winds down, those towns are consulting docking schedules for 2027 and 2028, noting projected visitor increases and asking how much they can realistically handle.

One hundred miles south of Skagway in Alaska’s capital, Juneau has seen repeated attempts to limit cruise-ship tourism. A ballot measure for “ship-free Saturdays” failed in 2024, after which the city introduced a voluntary limit on the number of ships that could dock daily. Farther south in the Inside Passage, Ketchikan has reported more ships as cruises adhered to Juneau’s limitations, while Sitka, also on the cruise-ship destination trail, is reviewing its agreements with the industry after an anticipated increase in visitor numbers in 2027.
