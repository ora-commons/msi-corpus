## Carrie Kahn recounts covering Brazil's election upset and Pope Leo's Peru visit

**Subtype:** fact

- NPR South America correspondent Carrie Kahn said covering Brazil's runoff election and Pope Leo XIV's visit to Peru within weeks of each other was "exhausting" and "incredibly amazing."
- Kahn said she did not foresee Lula's comeback in the runoff and had been in Brasilia on election night, among hundreds of reporters waiting for Jair Bolsonaro to give a victory speech.

## Pope's Peru trip came weeks after lung surgery, correspondent says

Rob Schmitz opened the segment by noting how uncommon it is for one correspondent to witness two historic events on the same beat just weeks apart. He recounted past examples from NPR's archive — a correspondent describing the breaching of the Berlin Wall in 1989, and another reporting from Nelson Mandela's inauguration in 1994 — before turning to Kahn's month in South America.

Kahn did not dispute the framing. "It's been exhausting. But it's also incredibly amazing," she said. "I just had this front seat to two very historic events on the continent." Watching millions of people mobilize across two very different public events, she said, was "a career high point."

The first of the two was Brazil's election. Schmitz noted that President Luiz Inacio Lula da Silva came from behind to win a runoff, and that no one in modern Brazilian history had come back to win a second round after coming in second place in the first.

Kahn was on air on October 4, the day the first round finished, and her assessment at that point was pessimistic for Lula. "Lula will have to find a way to close the gap in this runoff," she said then. "He's not going to really be able to win over the 8% of voters who went for the other candidates." Those voters, she said, were far-right and right, and would fall in line with Bolsonaro rather than with Lula.

Asked whether the shift in the three weeks that followed was visible at the time, Kahn declined to claim foresight. "I would totally be lying if I told you that I perceived this changing landscape or a huge political shift. I did not," she said. She recalled thinking Bolsonaro had won: she spent election night in Brasilia at Bolsonaro's headquarters with hundreds of other reporters, waiting for him to deliver a victory speech.

The second event was Pope Leo XIV's first visit to Latin America as pope, beginning in Peru — a country, Schmitz noted, that the pope called home for 20 years. Kahn's reporting on the day of his arrival captured the mood. "It's a huge party. This country is so happy the Pope is here," she said on air.

She described the scene around the visit in detail: bands, performers and local choral groups along the route, long stretches of waiting, and celebrations breaking out in the crowd. "There was a lot of emotion in this visit," she said, recalling a group of young people singing in the streets.

The trip followed a serious health episode. Kahn said it came just weeks after a portion of the pope's lung was removed following a cancer diagnosis. That shaped the early days. "The beginning was a little stiffer," she said, describing him as "kind of standoffish" and noting that visitors wait for the pope to extend his hand before touching him. "But then they were hugging, and it was just - it was emotional all around."

What struck her most, she said, was the pope's demeanor. "This was this very plain, simple man," Kahn said, adding that he "touched a lot of hearts with his simplicity and his very simple message" — that "everybody needs to work for the common good, the bien comun, the good of everyone." She said the message echoed that of his mentor, Francis: that "individualism, vanity and hypertechnology is not what humanity needs now. It needs community and civility."

Schmitz asked Kahn to compare the visit with Pope Francis' 2016 trip to Mexico, which she also covered. "It was just really different. It was a different time then," she said. "We hadn't seen so much polarization." Francis, she said, came from a more liberal wing of the church while Mexico's church is and remains very conservative. "He wasn't as warmly embraced in Mexico as Leo was welcomed in Peru," Kahn said, adding that during Leo's trip, "people, Catholic or not, really just wanted to feel hopeful."

On the practical side of the beat, Kahn described a continent that resists easy coverage: "The continent is huge. I can travel a full day by plane and still be in Brazil." Reaching the Amazon can take two or three flights that do not run all the time, she said, and the trips are costly and time-consuming. The working question, she said, is always "will this story interest Americans?"

U.S. policy has pushed that question to the fore, in her account. Under both Trump administrations, she said, NPR spends considerable time covering what the president does in Latin America, and in the second term "his administration has flexed so much U.S. power in the region that we're not - we haven't seen in decades." She pointed to "seizing Nicolás Maduro in Venezuela and the U.S. now pretty much running the country," and said visas to report there are now obtainable when previously "you couldn't get visas before."

Alongside that, she cited "rising gang violence and organized crime infiltrations all over the continent" as a standing subject, and credited local journalists in every country as partners — including a reporter in Uruguay she had not worked with before and her regular producer in Peru, with whom she spent time brainstorming future stories.

Asked whether she gets homesick, Kahn laughed and asked where home is. She has not lived in the United States for 20 years, she said; she grew up in Southern California and lived much of her life in Northern California, and her kids are there. "My home is also in Mexico, where I have a lot of family," she said. "I feel very comfortable in Latin America, and it feels like home."
