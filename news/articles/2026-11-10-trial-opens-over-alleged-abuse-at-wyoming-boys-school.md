## Attorneys dispute whether isolation amounted to solitary confinement

The first trial concerning allegations of abuse at the Wyoming Boys’ School opened Monday in U.S. District Court in Casper. The case centers on Blaise Chivers-King, a former resident who alleges that staff isolated him, used excessive force and violated his due-process rights under the 14th Amendment. Attorneys for Chivers-King and the three defendant staffers presented opposing accounts of his time in isolation.

The litigation has lasted more than two years. Chivers-King is one of six former residents who lodged complaints against the facility in 2024. The 38-acre residential school near Worland detains delinquent boys ages 12 to 21 and is overseen by the Wyoming Department of Family Services.

Chivers-King spent time at the school between 2020 and 2022. The defendants are Shaffer, Nelson and Weber, who is the school’s superintendent. All three sat in the courtroom Monday.

Facing eight jurors, Ciara Boerman, one of the attorneys representing Chivers-King, described him as a child who struggled with anxiety, anger and other emotions that he did not always know how to handle. She called him “a difficult child, but still a child.”

“Instead of helping him manage his emotions, they locked him away,” Boerman said of defendants Nelson and Weber. At some points, Chivers-King no longer wanted to live, she said.

Defense attorney Catherine Young asserted that the proceeding was “about difficult decisions made in difficult circumstances.” She said the defendants “responded to safety concerns, and did their best to keep everyone, including Blaise, safe.” Young also said the defendants did not violate Chivers-King’s constitutional rights.

Staffers isolated Chivers-King eight times, with one stay lasting 15 days. Boerman referred to the isolation as “solitary confinement,” though the defendants disputed that label. She showed the jury images of the room where Chivers-King was isolated. It had one window in the door, which staffers sometimes covered with a magnet, according to Boerman. When Chivers-King tried to look under the door, a staff member covered the crack with a mattress, she said.

Boerman told the jury that staff kept Chivers-King isolated even after he had calmed down. At times, he hurt himself to obtain human interaction, she said. She displayed photographs showing an injury to his head after he struck it against the wall in the isolation room and said staff responded by making him clean his blood from the wall.

Young said staffers attended to Chivers-King while he was isolated. His detention status required more individual attention than usual, she said. “You’ll see that it was not solitary confinement,” she told the jury.

Boerman also played a video showing Shaffer holding a riot shield as he and several other staffers entered Chivers-King’s isolation room. Chivers-King backed up as Shaffer pushed him against the wall and the group restrained him. Chivers-King has accused Shaffer of using excessive force.

Young said the encounter followed repeated problematic behavior that staffers were trying to stop. Shaffer had entered the detention room with a riot shield to keep himself and Chivers-King safe, and she said it was safer for multiple staff members to participate. Nursing staff checked Chivers-King afterward. “And he was OK,” she said. Young also noted that Chivers-King was over 6 feet tall and weighed about 270 pounds. “You’ll hear that he was capable of hurting people, and sometimes did,” she said.

Boerman concluded that Chivers-King continues to struggle with mental-health issues related to his time at the school. “Blaise left the boys’ school, but the boys’ school has not left him,” she said. Boerman told the jury that Chivers-King had become more reclusive and hardly leaves his room. She said a doctor diagnosed him with post-traumatic stress disorder and concluded, in the medical professional’s opinion, that it was directly related to his treatment at the school.

Young countered that Chivers-King’s mental-health struggles, including post-traumatic stress disorder, predated his placements at the school. She described his early childhood as marked by repeated abuse and neglect. “In short, for much of Blaise’s childhood, chaos was his normal experience,” she said.

Young contended that the school did good for Chivers-King and that he developed relationships with staffers, including the defendants. She showed the jury a photograph of Chivers-King walking a dog outside and said staff arranged for him to visit an animal shelter off campus. Being with animals kept him calm and helped deescalate his behavior, she said.

Attorneys spent the morning choosing jurors before opening statements, and the trial is expected to last five to seven days. Chivers-King is expected to testify Tuesday. The central dispute is whether the isolation and restraint at issue violated Chivers-King’s constitutional rights or instead reflected staff responses to problematic behavior and safety concerns.
