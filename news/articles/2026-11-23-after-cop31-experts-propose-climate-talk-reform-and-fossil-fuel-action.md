## Six November daily readings exceeded 2 degrees Celsius above pre-industrial levels

The United Nations’ COP31 climate talks in Turkey ended Sunday without the broad agreement sought, leaving negotiators divided and pointing fingers at one another. The principal obstacle was disagreement over whether the final agreement should mention a need to transition away from fossil fuels, which the report identifies as the chief cause of global warming. The conference was intended to advance efforts to reduce heat-trapping gas emissions from burning fossil fuels, but the talks are set to reconvene next year in Ethiopia without a new overarching deal.

Experts said the collapse in Turkey did not mean hope for rescuing an ever-warming world was gone. The setback prompted a warning that the route to limiting warming will be tougher, will require changes to negotiations and may depend more on individual action by countries and companies than on another joint agreement. U.N. climate chief Simon Stiell said, “The world cannot afford a repeat of this moment,” and, “The fight continues. We will never give up.” Most of the experts found positives from the collapse, mostly in how it appears to be galvanizing many countries to act on phasing out coal, oil and natural gas.

Johan Rockström, director of the Potsdam Institute for Climate Impacts Research in Germany, called the outcome a climate and political crisis. Rockström nevertheless said the collapse might prompt countries to take COP reform more seriously and to become “truly getting serious” in delivering the 2015 Paris Agreement. That pact requires countries to strengthen their plans to cut carbon emissions and aims to keep global average temperatures out of the danger zone. The agreement sets long-term goals of holding warming well below 2 degrees Celsius (3.6 degrees Fahrenheit) and preferably below 1.5 degrees Celsius (2.7 degrees Fahrenheit) above pre-industrial levels.

Earlier in the year, the United Nations conceded that it was inevitable the world would pass the 1.5-degree mark, probably within a year or two. Rockström warned that exceeding 1.5 degrees Celsius over the long term meant “danger in terms of billions of people suffering from droughts, floods, fire, heat waves, disease and human-reinforced storms.”

The European climate service Copernicus reported that the average global daily temperature was more than 2 degrees Celsius above pre-industrial levels six times in November. Heat-trapping gases keep increasing in the air, and the planet will keep getting hotter. Zeke Hausfather, a Berkeley Earth climate scientist, said the November temperature spikes show that the world is not only passing the 1.5 mark over the long term but is not too far away from the 2-degree threshold. Hausfather said, “This is yet another sign that the climate doesn’t really care about our politics — either in the U.S. or COP.”

Michael Mann, a University of Pennsylvania climate scientist, cautioned against making too much of the November heat spikes because the El Niño weather pattern, which is separate from climate change, influenced them.

Climate advocates were more severe in their judgment. Mohamed Adow of Power Shift Africa and Tasneem Essop of Climate Action Network International called COP31 “the worst COP ever,” pointing to the need to address suffering from increasingly severe climate events, including the deadly Nepal flood.

Joanna DePledge, a climate-negotiations historian at the University of Cambridge, rejected that description. She said COP31 was not quite the worst climate-talk failure because expectations were low and no major agreement was needed; she said there was no need for an overarching final agreement because the purpose of this round of talks was more about implementing past goals. DePledge said insisting on something large and then failing to deliver it was a mistake. She pointed to the 2000 talks in The Hague, which had to continue six months later in Germany, and the 2009 Copenhagen summit, where a hoped-for deal collapsed.

DePledge, like many experts, blamed the joint Turkey-Australia leadership of COP31 for handling the negotiations poorly. Six years after Copenhagen’s failure, countries reached the landmark Paris pact. Bill Hare, CEO of Climate Analytics, said that earlier collapse taught countries how important cooperation was. Hare said the latest failure would remind them to “stop running after the fossil fuel producers and companies and begin to deal seriously with the climate change problem.”

Many outside experts have called for changes to the broader COP process, described as a two-week climate “Super Bowl” that attracts 100,000 people to a closed-in campus. Their leading proposal was to replace near-unanimous consensus for major final agreements with decisions by majority or supermajority rule.

Mann also favored financial penalties, including embargoes and tariffs, against countries that fail to fulfill climate commitments. He objected to holding the talks in fossil-fuel-exporting states, noting that four of the last five host countries fit that description. Mann said, “We can no longer continue with this charade where a small number of bad petrostate actors stand in the way of planetary sustainability. The U.N. must change the rules now.”

Attention turned to side deals that did not require consensus and were thriving. About 80 countries signed Turkey’s plan for electricity to supply 35% of the world’s energy use, including transportation such as cars and planes. Fatih Birol, chief of the International Energy Agency, said the plan could reduce emissions by at least 8 billion metric tons.

Brazil separately offered countries road maps for transitioning away from fossil fuels before the conference began, and several countries had already adopted them. Nearly 60 countries were participating in a separate conference devoted entirely to phasing out fossil fuels.

U.N. Secretary-General António Guterres said, “The true test of climate action will be what happens beyond the negotiating halls.” He urged young people, scientists and people vulnerable to climate disasters to “keep demanding action. Keep turning up the heat,” and to “Hold governments and companies to account.”
