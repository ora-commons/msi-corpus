## Work requirements and refugee eligibility changes narrow Medicaid coverage in Kentucky

Federal Medicaid cuts enacted by the Trump administration could directly affect as many as 250,000 Kentucky residents. About 35 rural Kentucky hospitals are believed to be at risk of closing, more than in any other state. The Guardian reported that the changes could threaten both insurance coverage and the facilities that provide care.

Kentucky's eastern coalfields fueled America's economic rise in the early 20th century, but the state has since fallen behind. The Guardian linked a lack of investment in rural areas to health and socioeconomic problems that have persisted for generations. Kentucky has the country's third-highest cancer and drug overdose death rates and ranks among the five worst-affected states in several other health measures. About 1.4 million Kentuckians receive Medicaid support.

A photograph accompanying The Guardian's report, credited to Rick Bowmer/AP, shows protesters blocking a street during a demonstration on 27 June, 2017 against a Republican bill in the U.S. Senate.

Dustin Pugel, policy director at the Kentucky Center for Economic Policy, a research organization, said: “These hospitals are very dependent on Medicaid; it makes up a really significant part of the economy, particularly in eastern Kentucky. If you look at just Medicaid payments to providers in Kentucky’s 5th congressional district [a region encompassing much of Appalachian Kentucky], those payments make up 13.5% of the GDP there.” He added: “That’s an enormous percent[age] of a regional economy. If that money starts to dry up either because people aren’t covered any more or through cuts to provider rates, that’s going to create enormous economic harm in those areas.”

Almost 36% of residents in Perry County in southeastern Kentucky, including more than half of all children, relied on Medicaid in 2023, according to reports cited by The Guardian. The rate was 16% higher than the national level. At more than $10,600 per capita in 2023, Kentucky had some of the highest Medicaid expenses in the country.

Many Medicaid recipients will have to document at least 80 hours of work or volunteering each month under a federal work requirement that takes effect in January. Recipients must submit records of hours worked or school attendance every six months, and those who cannot do so will lose access from 2028. Reports cited by The Guardian suggested that Kentucky was set to lose $28bn in federal Medicaid funding over the next decade.

The Guardian said Kentucky's most vulnerable residents were set to be especially affected. Local health centers, urgent care facilities and substance-use-disorder clinics that rely on federal funding to remain open could face what The Guardian described as an untenable future. Rural hospitals also face a capacity problem. Zack Hall, chair of the Perry County Democratic party, said: “When you have five hospitals [in Appalachian Kentucky] that are at risk of closing, you have to take patients from an additional nine or ten counties. It’s going to overwhelm the hospital that’s here.” He added: “You already have a situation in eastern Kentucky where most counties don’t have a hospital.”

Hall said the two areas in Appalachian Kentucky that need a higher rate of health care have issues with cancer and black lung, which are harder on older residents. “If you are having to take an elderly patient and bring them to the hospital and having to drive two, two-and-a-half hours to get them to a hospital, that makes it that much harder on them,” he said. The access problem becomes more difficult when local facilities close.

In counties where more than two-thirds of residents are covered by Medicaid, losing coverage could create a significant economic headwind, Pugel said. He noted that limited jobs, difficulty meeting the work requirement and a lack of broadband could make documentation harder. Pugel warned: “That will certainly result in layoffs, may result in hospital closures and will definitely hurt people’s health.”

Immigration has also been central to Kentucky's recent population and community economics. The Guardian reported that before the Trump administration shut down the refugee resettlement program to all but white South Africans last year (2025), Kentucky ranked fourth nationally in refugee resettlements per capita, according to the Immigration Research Initiative. Immigration numbered nearly 82,000 people between 2020 and 2025, and without it Kentucky's population would have declined. Refugees and asylees have also played an important role in revitalizing small Kentucky communities in recent years.

Since October, refugees and asylees have lost Medicaid access because of the One Big Beautiful Bill. In Covington, a city of about 42,000 people immediately south of Cincinnati, Wilson Mejia of Kentucky Refugee Ministries has worked with about 840 refugees since opening the branch in 2021. Kentucky Refugee Ministries is a nonprofit with sites in three cities.

The nonprofit previously received funding from the Office of Refugee Resettlement, a federal agency, but that arrangement changed under the Trump administration. The funding had provided wraparound services for refugees for up to five years after arrival, including help from case managers obtaining social services. Mejia said changes under the One Big Beautiful Bill also left clients without green cards unable to receive SNAP food services.

Mejia said: “As of 1 October, clients that have not received their green cards can no longer access Medicaid. We are trying to work with the community to see where we can send these folks. But we are way off seeing the full impact of Medicaid changes. We anticipate it’s going to be a huge loss.”

The Guardian reported that, for immigrants without green cards who are responsible for supporting family members, the health insurance marketplace was likely the only available route to coverage. The report also said the Trump administration had ended subsidies from the Obama administration's Affordable Care Act, and that 2027 marketplace rates were set to increase by about 42% compared with 2025. Doctors and medical experts in Covington were volunteering to help refugees.

Mejia said: “That income could have gone to rent, towards food. Now, it’s going towards medical coverage.” He added that around 40 to 50 clients who had received letters about the end of their Medicaid coverage had sought help.

Some immigrants who hold green cards may also find it nearly impossible to meet the 80-hour monthly work requirement necessary to qualify for Medicaid. Mejia said: “Some clients can’t do this because they have a disability.”
