## Vatican says 1.5 million attended the final Mass in Lima

Pope Leo XIV ended his Peru visit on Monday with an “hasta luego” after a 12-day South American tour that tested his stamina after lung cancer surgery. During the traditional popemobile ride before his final Mass in Lima, an apparent rosary thrown toward the pope seemed to hit him in the face. Leo winced, touched his temple and continued; the Mass began as planned. The Vatican said some 1.5 million people attended.

For the Chicago-born pope, Peru was his beloved second homeland. Leo spent two decades there as a missionary priest and bishop, and the trip was an emotional homecoming to a place he had credited with teaching him how to be a shepherd. Upon arrival, he declared Peru his “patria,” or homeland, and sang the national anthem alongside President Keiko Fujimori. Leo holds American and Peruvian citizenship.

The faithful often tossed rosaries, stuffed animals, sports jerseys and other items toward the popemobile, and Leo had often caught them with quick reflexes. His bodyguards had lamented that such objects were among their chief concerns when protecting him in public. At the end of Mass, as many people knelt on the dirt ground to receive his final blessing, Leo said, “It was here that you taught me how to be a bishop, and this time I learned a little more about serving as the Successor of Peter.” He added, “I have felt at home, and you have reminded me that the Church truly is a family.”

Leo’s ministry in Peru and now for the universal Catholic Church was modeled on St. Turibius of Mogrovejo, the first archbishop of Lima. St. Turibius lived from 1538 to 1606 and is revered for denouncing abuses against Indigenous and enslaved people by Spanish colonizers. He is also remembered for crisscrossing his vast diocese on foot and horseback to minister to his flock, learning Quechua and Aymara to better preach to Indigenous members and overseeing the translation of church teachings so they could understand.

Following that example, Leo traveled across Peru to communities where the Catholic hierarchy rarely goes, going high into the Andes, deep into the Amazon rainforest and to points in between. On his final full day in Peru on Sunday, Leo went from his former diocese of Chiclayo on the north Pacific coast to the southeastern Andean city of Cusco, then to the northeastern Amazonian city of Pucallpa and back to Lima on the coast.

The climatic and altitude changes alone were exhausting. Leo went from hot, humid Chiclayo to chilly and rainy Cusco, at an oxygen-challenging altitude of 3,400 meters, or about 11,150 feet, then to the steaming heat of the Pucallpa rainforest and back to the gray humidity of Lima.

The trip had been in doubt after Leo underwent lung cancer surgery in Rome on Oct. 13, and some in the Vatican had questioned whether he would have to change the course of his pontificate given his lung cancer diagnosis. As the visit began, Leo said he had “no doubt” he would make it. On Monday, he proceeded with his program as planned and made clear he was not saying “adiós,” or goodbye forever, to Peru.

Speaking to the crowd outside the Vatican residence, Leo thanked Peruvians for accompanying him on his trip. “Have a good day, thank you very much, and until later!” he said. At a home for the elderly in Callao, where Leo visited with residents, he bid farewell again, saying “hasta pronto, hasta luego,” another way of saying “see you soon.”

Callao Mayor César Pérez told The Associated Press that Father Roberto, as he was known to the Peruvian people, would drive approximately 12 hours from Chiclayo in northern Peru to Callao during the pandemic because he wanted to ensure the well-being of older residents. Leo delivered food and other supplies to the home but did not enter the facility to protect the residents from the virus, Pérez said.

The Peru stop capped a visit that began on Nov. 6 in Uruguay and proceeded to Argentina, the homeland of Leo’s predecessor, Pope Francis, who never returned during his pontificate.

Rosa María Flores, who attended the final Mass, said she was glad Leo had made the visit. She said the most important thing about the pope’s visit was that he brought faith to the people of his homeland of Peru. “We have so much crime, so many deaths; we’re in great need of peace, reconciliation and faith,” she said. “I know that he’s pouring blessings on Peru and that things will get better for all of us.”

Some people at the Mass complained they could not hear the pope because the giant TV screens did not work. Others begged firefighters to spray them with water to cool off, and the firefighters obliged.

One of Leo’s most meaningful visits was to the sanctuary of Mogrovejo in the city of Zaña, within the diocese of Chiclayo. Leo revealed that he had gone there the night before his ordination as a bishop to pray because the saint was important to him. St. Turibius died there after falling ill during one of Turibius’s own missionary visits. “This is a kneeling place for Robert Prevost,” the Rev. David Farfan, the parish pastor, told The Associated Press. Many of the pope’s actions, Farfan said, “helping people in the world, as a peacemaker, he did them while also kneeling in this sacred place because a saint passed away here.”
