## SNAP enrollment losses and USDA cancellations deepen pressure on Texas food banks

Texas food banks are heading into the holiday season with the highest demand they have seen since the 2020 pandemic, and less to give away. High food prices, a dearth of federal funding and fewer donations have left many pantries short of the holiday foods families look for, and Houston Food Bank President and CEO Brian Greene said the agency bought no turkeys this year. Turkey, ham, cranberry sauce and dressing are among the traditional foods millions of Texas families who rely on food banks will not find on the table this season.

"This is the first year we bought zero turkeys. They're just too expensive," Greene said. Greene said the Houston Food Bank has seen a 20% increase in requests for food assistance and a 10% increase in people being served through the organization's network of charities in the Houston area. "Part of the phenomenon we are seeing is that the pantries are having to spread the peanut butter thinner, leaving families to see who else can help them," Greene said.

Texas' safety net for food insecurity includes federal nutrition assistance programs such as the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program, or SNAP, as well as food banks and school programs. A food bank is a regional nonprofit that sources, stores and distributes food to local programs, including pantries and school programs. Twenty food banks serve more than 4 million Texans annually through a network of more than 4,000 partners. The food bank system was not designed to address all food insecurity in Texas because most of its resources come from philanthropy, but it is increasingly being asked to do so as federal assistance declines.

Statewide, the strain shows up in referral data. United Ways of Texas reported that calls seeking food assistance through the Texas Health and Human Service's 211 assistance hotline rose 28% from February to June 2026, while referrals to food banks and food pantries climbed nearly 37%, from 4,230 to 5,785. Texas food banks currently distribute more than 700 million pounds of food annually through more than 5,000 community partners. For every meal food banks provide, SNAP provides nine. Celia Cole, CEO of Feeding Texas, a statewide network of food banks, said, "We're struggling to make up for that."

Rural food banks face particular pressure because families have migrated from urban areas into their regions. They cannot keep up with demand, much less afford high prices for turkey, ham and other proteins. The High Plains Food Bank is having the worst food crisis in its 40-year history, said Zack Wilson, its executive director. "We are serving over 14,000 households for a month now, which on average is, is a record number of households. We are serving the most we've ever served, but with probably the least amount of inventory," Wilson said. "We bought some turkeys early, but they went fast. Once the inventory is gone, it's gone," he said.

Sari Vatske, president and CEO of Central Texas Food Bank, said urban-to-rural migration in Texas is putting particular pressure on rural food banks because the banks are often the nearest food source for families. "The need has increased since the beginning of the year as the holidays are upon us and the cost of living remains high," she said. Greene said, "The worst-hit regions are in the Panhandle, Lipscomb, Hemphill, and Sherman counties, as well as parts of West and North Texas, but this is a problem statewide," and added, "It's scary." Urban regions were no better in sheer numbers, as overall demand pressured Texas' entire food-insecurity system.

The holiday season adds pressure because children do not receive free or reduced-price school lunches while schools are closed, and the number of food-insecure families is growing faster than the available food supply, leading to rationing or changes to holiday meals. Food insecurity is defined as a household lacking the money or resources to reliably access adequate food. Feeding America's 2026 Map the Meal Gap study, which uses 2024 data, found that 19.4% of Texans, or nearly 1 in 5, were experiencing food insecurity. The study also found that 26% of Texas children — 1 in 4, or nearly 2 million — were experiencing food insecurity. A significant portion of people experiencing food insecurity may not qualify for federal nutrition assistance programs such as SNAP, leaving them dependent on local food banks and community meal programs.

The High Plains Food Bank has served families in the Texas Panhandle for decades and has seen several crises, including the COVID-19 pandemic and the 2010s recession. Past crises brought surges of emergency help from federal and state governments or large donations, while this crisis comes as all measures of assistance are shrinking. "It's just been a decrease in all of our food channels, from donated foods to delays and cancellations of USDA deliveries, and a decrease in monetary donations in order to purchase food," Wilson said. "It has been a unique situation for us, with all of those channels showing a decrease, really over the past nine to 10 months." Wilson said the combination of issues has led to a shortage of nonperishable food items as demand for food assistance grows in the region.

For many years, rural food banks relied on federal programs to complement their donor bases with funding and food commodities. Support dwindled over the past two years, and the lack of support is catching up as need reaches its highest level. Increasing holiday demand comes as food-bank funding and supply are at their lowest. In 2025, the U.S. Department of Agriculture canceled two programs that supplied $1 billion in products directly to food bank warehouses. The USDA justified the decision by saying the former Joe Biden administration had created "unsustainable" expectations by spending beyond standard levels. The drop in federal commodities has been drastic for Texas food banks, with many losing millions of dollars in funding or food over the span of a year and handing out fewer meals per family. "Our USDA program right now makes up about 18% of our total food inventory. It was as high as about 25% back in 2024, but our biggest issue has been some of the cancellations or delays we have seen this year from them," Wilson said. Local pantries in schools and neighborhoods, especially in rural areas, are also overwhelmed by the number of food-insecure families, putting pressure on larger food banks to assist.

High grocery prices also prevented generous donations from reaching food banks, especially during a time of economic uncertainty. Greene said much of the Houston Food Bank's supply comes from private donations and food-industry surplus, often produce from farmers. Food banks still have to pay to transport donated food, which can cost about $16,000 to rent a tractor-trailer, plus additional money for gas as fuel costs rise.

Federal nutrition assistance has shrunk as well. State data show nearly 733,000 fewer eligible Texans participated in SNAP in October than in September 2025, amid work requirements and immigration fears. Food advocates said every one of those households still needs to eat, and much of that burden is landing on food banks. Greene said SNAP work requirements are pushing many rural families off the program because jobs may not be readily available, forcing under-resourced remote food banks to meet growing demand.

Cole described the pressures converging on food banks heading into the holiday season. "People are struggling to put groceries on the table, gas in the car, pay their rent, and a lot of people are losing access to SNAP benefits," she said. "That means more people in our lines, our partners wanting more food from us, and at the same time federal commodities have gone down, and it's costing us more money to get food in and out of the door." "It's just not a great place to be going into the holiday season," Cole said.

Wilson said inflation and rising costs for food and fuel are the top drivers. "Our fuel bill alone is up 220% this month compared to January so everybody is feeling it," he said. He also cited utilities during one of the hottest summers the Texas Panhandle has ever had, along with natural disasters "from fires to flooding to tornadoes" over the past two years. Wilson described the strain reaching households with steady work: "I was talking to a donor who told us she had a family member who had a good job, a good wage, and yet couldn't provide for his family at times due to the increasing cost of insurance and fuel," he said.

Advocates are pressing the federal government to reverse course. Greene said, "It's time for the federal government to step up." Cole said many candidates who won on Election Night earlier this month platformed on affordability, and one of the main things the federal government can do is stop the nationwide decline in SNAP enrollment. "Hunger is a problem we can't afford to ignore, but we can afford to solve," she said. Reversing cuts to federal commodities is another option because the change has had one of the most significant impacts on food banks this year. One food bank executive said, "It's very frustrating because I just saw recent polling that showed that a strong majority of Americans think that the federal government should be helping food banks more, not less."

At the state level, a new crop of lawmakers is due to gather for the 90th legislative session next year, and advocates see an opening for food-insecurity legislation. "Our requests for the state remain the same, with a bit more optimism for support," Vatske said. "The state House and Senate are now controlled by different parties, so we are reminded that collaboration and bipartisan legislation are impactful approaches to good legislation. Hunger is a nonpartisan issue and requires both sides of the aisle working together," she said. Vatske said food banks "are already strained and cannot continue to absorb government funding cuts while operating costs continue to rise and more people are in need of assistance."

One of food advocates' main priorities next year is addressing growing issues with SNAP. Texas faces a potential $826 million financial hit by 2027 from federal penalties and administrative cost shifts tied to SNAP payment errors, fueling fears that the state is scrutinizing each application to reduce the error rate and keep more people who need benefits out of the program. A SNAP error rate does not reflect fraud; it is based on unintentional mistakes by the agency or clients that result in underpayments or overpayments. States were given very little time to improve payment accuracy, and most mistakes are processing errors, not fraud, but thousands are still being removed anyway. Many food banks across the nation are asking Congress to give states a two-year extension to meet the new accuracy standards. If Congress does not grant the extension, advocates hope Texas lawmakers will fully fund SNAP next year despite rising costs because removing more families would put more pressure on food banks.

Food advocates also asked lawmakers to consider a Summer EBT program, which would let children receiving free or reduced-price school lunches eat outside the school year and relieve pressure on food banks, along with Medicaid expansion. "We are one of the few states that have not embraced Medicaid expansion, and we have the highest rates of uninsured people," Greene said. "That is a major cause of food insecurity in the state." Food banks requested more funding for the Surplus Agricultural Products Grant Program, managed by the Texas Department of Agriculture, which helps food banks and charitable networks collect and distribute surplus farm goods to low-income residents. "It's one of the greatest tools we have; it's how we get the money to pay the farmer to pick and bag the surplus produce and the transportation costs to bring it in," Greene said. Without support from the federal and state governments, the food bank situation is expected to worsen during next year's holiday season. "If the number of households that we're serving continues to rise in the longer term, it's not sustainable without help," Wilson said.
