## Energy shocks have so far produced limited knock-on price effects

At a two-hour hearing of the cross-party Treasury select committee, Bank of England Deputy Governor Clare Lombardelli said food price inflation was running below what the central bank and the food industry had expected. She was questioned alongside other members of the Bank's Monetary Policy Committee as policymakers weighed the effects of higher energy costs on consumer prices.

“Food price inflation is coming in under what we expected [it] to be, and in fact under what the industry expected it to be,” Lombardelli said. The Bank nevertheless expected food inflation to rise sharply from a current level of about 1.3%. “We think that … it will go up to 3 to 5% in the middle of next year. I mean, obviously that’s quite uncertain, but that pressure is there,” Lombardelli said.

Annual UK inflation hit 3.6% last month as surging oil and gas prices pushed petrol and diesel costs more than 33% higher over the year. The knock-on effects beyond energy have so far been limited, and food inflation remains well short of the above-17% peak in 2023, when Russia's invasion of Ukraine exacerbated a supply squeeze as the global economy emerged from the Covid-19 pandemic.

The Monetary Policy Committee raised rates by a quarter-point to 4% at its meeting earlier this month to tackle above-target inflation and suggested further action could be necessary.

Governor Andrew Bailey said the committee was monitoring the potential effect of this year's unusually large El Niño weather event on future food prices. “There is uncertainty around this. You know, we still don’t know what the impact of this El Niño effect is going to be, for instance, because it will take time for it to manifest,” he said.

External MPC member Catherine Mann, who voted for a half-point increase this month, said fierce competition in the food retail sector had constrained price increases. “We all go shopping ourselves. I can say that Aldi price match in my stores is apparent. Now, it wasn’t apparent four years ago. So that’s, I think, important,” she said.

Oil prices have fallen sharply since the Democratic Party's sweep in the US midterms brought hopes of an end to the Iran conflict, and alternative sources of supply have started to come onstream. Bailey said if current prices stayed at their present level, the Bank's projection of UK inflation peaking at almost 5% next year would be lower. “The prices are volatile … if these current prices were to stay what they are, the peak of inflation mechanically would be lower,” he said.

Bailey and other MPC members said some pressure for next year was already built into the forecast. They pointed to the 26% rise in the price cap for household energy bills due in January.

The hearing exposed divisions within the committee. Swati Dhingra suggested this month's quarter-point rise was a one-off “insurance” measure, while Mann said she feared the MPC had undermined public confidence by failing to move more aggressively.

Dhingra compared the muted effect of rising oil and gas prices this year with the surge in 2022 after Russia invaded Ukraine. At a similar point in 2022, prices of 70% of goods included in the consumer price index had risen, she said, while just 20% of prices had reacted so far this year.

Mann noted that inflation had remained consistently above the Bank's 2% target for five years. “If we let inflation expectations drift away from 2% in the longer term, then monetary policy is going to have to work harder, meaning a higher rate for longer,” she said, adding: “The optimal response is to hike more and sooner.”

The MPC's next rate-setting meeting is scheduled for 17 December. Lombardelli and Bailey both qualified their projections: Lombardelli called the 3% to 5% path uncertain, while Bailey said energy prices were volatile and any El Niño effect would take time to manifest.
