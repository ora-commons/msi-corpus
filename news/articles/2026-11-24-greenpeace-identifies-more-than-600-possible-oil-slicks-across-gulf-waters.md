## Umm Al Quwain's coast remains closed after a tanker spill

Greenpeace's satellite monitoring has identified more than 600 possible oil slicks across Gulf waters, with the affected area stretching some 8,000 square miles. Greenpeace said the slicks came from tanker attacks and the war. The possible slicks threaten marine life in the Strait of Hormuz and across the wider Gulf.

The U.N. has tracked around 150 attacks on ships since the start of the war. NPR reported that Iran's Revolutionary Guard Corps was attacking oil tankers at a record pace. The IRGC says it is hitting several tankers a day despite U.S. military efforts to guide ships through the Strait of Hormuz.

NPR's Aya Batrawy reported from Khor Fakkan in the United Arab Emirates, where she saw oil washing ashore. She described a bright, sunny day with families on an open beach and clear water. Several stretches of beach in the country had been closed because of oil spills as Iran continued attacks on tankers trying to enter or leave the Strait.

Greenpeace Middle East and North Africa campaigner Inesaf Benzaki said, “It is really the repeated aspect of it in such a small area that compounds the scale of the damage.” She said long-term environmental monitoring is essential and recovery will need a lot of time.

Benzaki said the repeated spills are harming marine life and sensitive ecosystems such as mangroves, along with the food chain and drinking-water supplies the Gulf relies on.

One of Iran's attacks on October 2 struck a ship carrying 2 million barrels of crude. The oil washed onto the UAE's shores a couple of weeks later. The entire coast of the emirate of Umm Al Quwain has been closed to the public ever since. The sand has dark brown hues, and the smell of oil permeates the air.

Batrawy saw a cleanup crew with men in hazard outfits working along the coast. She also described a generator running and a hose apparently being used to draw oil from the water.

To draw attention to the environmental costs of the war, British endurance swimmer Lewis Pugh swam in the Strait of Hormuz this month. He told The Associated Press, “I heard two very loud explosions. They literally rocked the sea.” Pugh said he then saw an oil tanker about 4 kilometers away had been hit.

Pugh described seeing dolphins following the boats, turtles and big ospreys. “It's literally one of the treasure troves of the natural world,” he said. Batrawy said the same waters are also a lifeline for the Gulf and are being harmed every day the war continues.

The current findings document the environmental consequences of the continuing tanker attacks, including possible slicks across Gulf waters and a prolonged closure along the UAE coast.
