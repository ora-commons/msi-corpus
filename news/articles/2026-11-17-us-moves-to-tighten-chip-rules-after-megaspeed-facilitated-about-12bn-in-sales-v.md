## Huang’s exit raises succession questions as Nvidia reports earnings Tuesday

Montgomery described the cross-border sales system at the center of the dispute. Nvidia was barred from selling advanced chips to China. Megaspeed would buy Nvidia chips from a base in south-east Asia, install them in datacenters in Singapore, Malaysia and Indonesia, then lease or sell them to Chinese clients. Montgomery said Megaspeed facilitated about $12bn in chip sales in contravention of US export controls. He predicted the scandal would result in harsher controls on China and said the Trump administration was already moving to tighten them on Monday morning.

The dispute inside the Trump administration pits Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent against Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick. Bessent accused Lutnick of failing to curb Nvidia sales that contravened export controls. Montgomery wrote that Lutnick had pushed to allow more trade with China, particularly where his family business, Cantor Fitzgerald, was concerned. Montgomery also wrote that Bessent argued for a more hawkish posture and more restrictions. Both secretaries were traveling with Donald Trump to China that week, and Montgomery said Trump’s remarks could indicate which policy path the administration would take. A separate linked Guardian item reported that Donald Trump bought Nvidia stock while helping the chipmaker change an export rule.

The corporate consequences ran alongside the policy fight. The Guardian described Nvidia as what once was the world’s most valuable company. Jensen Huang, the chipmaker’s CEO and co-founder, resigned on Thursday after reports that he had at least three affairs, including one with a Megaspeed executive and others with his own employees. The Guardian said his departure had already cost Nvidia hundreds of billions in stock-market value. Montgomery said it might cost the world economy much more. Nvidia is central to the AI boom both as a chipmaker and lender. Interim CEO Colette Kress will face investor questions about the post-Huang era when the company reports quarterly earnings Tuesday.

US intelligence investigated the Megaspeed executive for spying for China, The Guardian said. The newsletter identified the executive as Alice Huang and described her as an alleged Chinese spy who worked at the firm.

Montgomery asked whether Huang had amassed too much influence before his downfall and wrote that the Nvidia founder seemed indispensable to the company’s success until the moment he left. Elon Musk and a host of other Silicon Valley power players argued over the weekend that Huang was irreplaceable and that he should be reinstated. Trump said the same thing on Monday: “He could return. There’s a big group of ‌people that would like him to return … I would certainly not mind seeing that.” Montgomery said it would be difficult to find another businessman like him, though the possibility of Huang’s return might be remote. The Financial Times reported on Sunday that Huang had discussed a return to Nvidia with allies; Montgomery compared the discussions with Sam Altman’s firing and rehiring at OpenAI. The next day, the Financial Times reported that Nvidia’s board of directors was not considering reinstating Huang.

Critics of Silicon saw Huang’s departure as a repudiation of the myth of the all-knowing, all-important company founder. “This week is seeing a long overdue reckoning for the genius-founder business model,” said Alison Taylor, an associate professor at New York University’s business school. Gil Durán, described by the newsletter as a strident critic of Silicon Valley, made a similar point during a panel organized in response to the news, as did Carissa Véliz, a philosophy professor at the Oxford Institute for Ethics in AI. Taylor separately warned: “Relying on the judgment and ethics of one person, no matter how brilliant, is always a catastrophically bad idea, and powerful CEOs are particularly vulnerable because they tend to be surrounded by people that only say yes to them.”

Montgomery described Nvidia’s fate as the highest-profile example he had ever seen of “key man risk.” Companies heavily reliant on the brilliance of one person — usually a man — or a small group of executives often notify investors that the business will suffer if that vital person goes out of commission. Companies with more diffuse responsibility hedge against that possibility but can become more bureaucratic, leading many Silicon Valley companies to operate with as few employees as necessary for as long as possible.

Huang had made no succession plan to speak of, Montgomery wrote, though that may have reflected a failure of Nvidia’s board as much as his own. Huang reportedly managed about 60 people, meaning there were not likely successors who had ascended through the ranks. Judging by Nvidia’s stock price, Montgomery said, the concentrated management approach had worked until Huang’s crisis.

Cast another way, Montgomery said Huang’s tenure looked like the best case of a founder assuming the role of longtime CEO. Huang presided over Nvidia for more than 30 years and shepherded it from a gaming-chip company to a vital cryptocurrency supplier to the “keystone of the AI boom.” The newsletter said Nvidia’s wares were considered the “cream of the crop,” so incomparable that they were the target of international espionage operations and so valuable that the company forecast $100bn in quarterly revenue.

The same TechScape issue paired the export-control dispute with a series of critical-system failures. A Microsoft Outlook outage that began late the previous week grew into millions having no access to email or other critical services. Jails could not release prisoners, hospitals turned to paper records, schools lost contact with parents, and police departments could not receive crime reports. Microsoft said it had resolved the issue and restored service by Saturday and issued an apology. Its initial report did not attribute the blackout to a cybersecurity breach, instead citing a combination of a months-old bug, malfunctioning internal connections and insufficient server capacity.

The day after the Microsoft outage began, the US Federal Aviation Administration grounded all flights across the country because of a malfunctioning communication system. Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy attributed the stoppage to “a contractor doing routine maintenance to switch telecommunication lines from old copper wire … to fiber.” Duffy said on Saturday that the FAA was still working to determine whether the fault was “incompetence” by the contractor, or “a faulty router.” The newsletter invoked William Gibson’s observation, “The future is already here – it’s just not evenly distributed.”

Montgomery also listed other disruptions. An oil tanker collided with a passenger cruise on the Mississippi River on Friday, though no one was killed. Australian students could not take their final exams on Sunday because of a technology failure. On Monday, leading US banks reported major outages, primarily at JPMorgan Chase. Customers could not access online services such as Zelle, which the bank attributed to an unspecified technical issue. United Airlines and American Airlines also reported problems that delayed flights across the country, though the problems were resolved within hours of being announced.

Montgomery said the available evidence did not connect the outages, and it was not known whether cyber-attacks or rogue AI caused them. He raised the possibility that old systems were struggling with the transition to new ones, while also asking whether an onslaught of cyber-attacks had been brought on by AI models’ advanced hacking capabilities. Montgomery said he did not know what was causing the mayhem, said the lack of a unified explanation was maddening and said readers would have to wait for answers. He offered Stephen King’s speculation: “The aliens have landed. That's the REAL reason for the ground stop. They don't want to start a worldwide panic.”
