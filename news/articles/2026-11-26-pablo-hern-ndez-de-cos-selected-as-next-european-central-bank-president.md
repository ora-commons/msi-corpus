## Eurozone inflation stands at 4% as government debt strains persist

Eurozone leaders chose Pablo Hernández de Cos to succeed Christine Lagarde as president of the European Central Bank, which decides borrowing costs for the 359 million people in the eurozone. The selection at a summit in Brussels came as the bank confronted inflation driven by the war in Iran, high government debt in several European countries and rising borrowing costs.

Leaders from the 21 European member countries that use the euro also filled two other top positions at the ECB. An EU diplomat tasked with communicating for the Irish Presidency said agreement on all three positions had been reached at the summit. The diplomat, who was not authorized to be named, spoke on condition of anonymity, and French President Emmanuel Macron confirmed the result on X.

De Cos's nomination still requires review by the European Parliament and the ECB's governing council; those steps were described as formalities. He is currently general manager of the Bank for International Settlements, an international organization of central banks in Basel, Switzerland.

The appointment puts an experienced central banker with a reputation for backing lower interest rates and monetary stimulus in charge at a time when several European governments are struggling with heavy debt loads and rising borrowing costs. De Cos, now 55, served on the ECB's rate-setting council from 2018 to 2024 as head of Spain's central bank. A review of his speeches by Commerzbank analysts “suggests that de Cos is among the monetary policy doves,” meaning policymakers who lean toward lower rates to support growth. De Cos prevailed over another main contender, former Netherlands central bank head Klaas Knot, who was regarded as a hawk favoring higher rates to fight inflation.

In 2020, de Cos joined a council majority under Lagarde in backing 1.85 trillion euros ($2 trillion) in pandemic stimulus to keep rates low and support the economy during widespread closures and travel restrictions. De Cos praised the massive pandemic stimulus in a speech that year, saying it “has been clearly successful in curbing financial market deterioration in all euro area countries” and had lowered borrowing costs for eurozone governments and corporations.

Commerzbank analyst Marco Wagner said that “Especially against the backdrop of rising government debt in numerous countries ... many governments are likely to prefer a dovish ECB president.” France, in particular, is struggling with high debt and burdensome interest costs. With inflation at 4%, above the bank's 2% target, the ECB could still raise rates at its Dec. 17 meeting, which Lagarde will preside over.

In some circumstances, the ECB can intervene to lower government borrowing costs when markets are in turmoil that it views as unjustified. Analysts said the central bank is unlikely to bail out French politicians who cannot agree on how to reduce their deficit.

Lagarde, 70, said on Oct. 30 that she would resign on Feb. 7, nine months before the end of her eight-year term. She said she decided to resign early so she could speak more freely about matters not related to monetary policy. The timing of her departure both allowed EU leaders to choose a successor while Emmanuel Macron remained in office and guarded against the possibility that Marine Le Pen, a presidential candidate, might win the election and influence the choice.

Leaders also chose German economist Markus Brunnermeier, a professor at Princeton University, to replace Philip Lane on the bank's six-member executive board. Lane's term ends in May; as chief economist, he proposed rate moves for the full governing council to consider and decide upon. Leaders selected Laurence Boone, head of corporate and investment banking in France for Spanish bank Santander, for the seat being vacated by Germany's Isabel Schnabel, who is resigning in January.

The executive board runs the ECB's day-to-day operations. The bank sets interest benchmarks for the 21 countries that use the euro, influencing overnight loans between banks, credit cards, home mortgages and government bonds. Lower rates can spur a slumping economy, while higher rates restrain inflation when it is above the bank's 2% annual target.

The ECB's ability to act quickly, in contrast to drawn-out negotiations among the 27 European Union national governments, has given it a leading role in combating Europe-wide crises such as the pandemic and the peak of the eurozone debt crisis in 2010-12. The president leads meetings of the rate-setting council at the bank's headquarters in Frankfurt. Lagarde is regarded as having steered a pragmatic middle course based on building consensus among the national bank heads who sit on the rate-setting council. She resisted being labeled a hawk or a dove, saying she was an “owl,” the bird associated with wisdom in Greek and Roman mythology.
