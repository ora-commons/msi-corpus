## White paper next year may reshape payments for other disability claimants

Ministers have pledged faster Personal Independence Payment (Pip) claims for adults in England and Wales whose conditions are severe or unlikely to change. The Department for Work and Pensions said on Tuesday that the move would create a fast-track route for people with severe, well-evidenced conditions and those nearing the end of life. A formal government response to the Timms review is expected next month, followed by a white paper in the spring next year.

The immediate commitment covers two elements of Stephen Timms's review: fewer award reviews for people whose health conditions are lifelong, progressive or unlikely to improve, and a fast-track route for people with severe, well-evidenced conditions and those nearing the end of life. The report proposes that reviews for people with lifelong or degenerative conditions focus only on what has changed rather than start from scratch. For other claimants, the report calls for an assessment of how a condition affects daily life to determine the cash payment received. It also recommends more government spending on face-to-face assessments.

Timms, who led the review, said: “The Timms review’s interim report was clear that while Pip is valued, the system is no longer fit for purpose.” He also said: “These changes are a first step to building a system which gives greater security for those with the most severe conditions.”

The Department for Work and Pensions said the new system would “help disabled people achieve better health, higher living standards and greater independence”. Campaigners and disability charities broadly welcomed the recommendations, saying the review drew on consultation with disabled people and provided a basis for a potentially fairer and more compassionate system.

The Guardian reported widespread nervousness that some claimants would fail to qualify for cash payments under a new “impact assessment” of needs. Campaigners said they would seek assurances that vulnerable people were not left without a financial lifeline. Claimants outside the fast-track route would have their conditions assessed for effects on daily life to decide what cash payment they receive, rather than all receiving the current set rates of up to £194.60 a week. Alexa Knight of the Mental Health Foundation said: “No one receiving Pip should be pushed into deeper poverty as a result of these changes, even in the short term. The consequences for individuals and their families can be devastating if support is removed before suitable alternatives are in place.”

Ministers are working on a formal government response, expected next month before a white paper in the spring next year. The white paper will include broader proposals and could mark the first step in what is likely the biggest shake-up of the welfare system in over a decade, The Guardian said. The Guardian reported that the broader response was likely to include cuts to cash payments for people who did not meet the new fast-track threshold.

The review began last year after Keir Starmer's government dropped a plan to cut the Pip system by £5bn in the face of a backlash from backbench MPs. Timms defended the proposed cuts as a minister at the time, warning that the welfare bill would become unsustainable without spending reductions. The Office for Budget Responsibility forecast that, without changes, spending on Pips would rise from around £16bn in 2019-20 to just over £41bn by the end of the decade.

Timms's final report was written with disability rights advocates Sharon Brennan and Clenton Farquharson. It does not identify specific areas where the overall bill could be cut. Instead, the report recommends easier use for people with serious conditions and more tailored treatment for claimants with less severe needs.

Timms said cash payments should remain at the heart of the system. In a separate report on youth joblessness, Alan Milburn recommended that under-25s should no longer be able to claim Pip and that the money saved should instead fund schemes to help young people enter the workplace.

If ministers followed the proposals made by Milburn and Timms, The Guardian said, the result would be two separate welfare systems in which adults aged 25 and over could claim disability benefits but younger adults could not. Timms's report leaves space for ministers to save money from Pip by reducing cash payments for many current recipients.

The Guardian said it had been told that ministers were working on a Pip system combining extra work and training support with cash cuts for people of all ages who did not meet the new fast-track threshold. Those broader changes remain proposals, while the immediate commitment is limited to faster processing and fewer reviews.
