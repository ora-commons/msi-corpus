## Iowa schools close Friday as U.S. Army updates lead-drift maps

**Subtype:** fact

- Danville Community School District will close Friday because Superintendent Pat Wallace said the district did not know exactly what conditions would look like during the Army's planned detonation.
- Mediapolis schools and public library will close Friday.
- The Army's updated map no longer expects Mediapolis, a city of nearly 2,000 residents, to be affected.
- The Army will continue updating maps based on weather to show potential lead-dust drift north.
- Mediapolis officials learned Sunday that an initial map placed the city in the center of the dust-exposure area.
- Mediapolis fire officials plan to hose playgrounds that could be covered in lead particles.

## Updated map no longer expects Mediapolis to be affected

Danville Community School District, just a few miles northwest of the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant, will close Friday, with Superintendent Pat Wallace saying enough uncertainties made cancellation “the most responsible decision.” Wallace said his initial intention was to keep schools open. In an announcement Tuesday, he wrote, “I am not suggesting that the detonation will result in unsafe conditions” and “The reality is that we simply do not know exactly what conditions will look like Friday afternoon.”

Less than 20 miles (32 kilometers) northeast, Mediapolis schools and public library will close Friday, and the local fire department plans to hose down playgrounds that could be covered in lead particles. Mediapolis officials learned Sunday that the community could be exposed when an initial map placed it in the center of the dust-exposure area, after officials approached the city late last month about allowing soil, water and air testing. An updated map no longer expects the city of nearly 2,000 residents to be affected.

Deputy City Clerk Amy Stucker said Wednesday, “This is probably a first for me. I believe it's a first for a lot of our citizens.” She added, “The biggest issue we're having is the lack of information.”

The U.S. Army will continue updating maps based on weather. The maps show the potential for dust to drift north, and some schools in the region have already canceled classes. Nearly 400 people live in Middletown, but thousands more could be affected.

Residents in southeast Iowa are worried that a cloud of lead dust could cause environmental contamination and health problems. The detonation at the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant is set for Friday and involves about 5 tons of bomb-making chemicals that have become unusable and might explode if moved. Officials recommend that some residents in nearby Middletown begin vacating the area Friday morning. Residents who cannot leave should stay inside and keep windows and doors closed.

The Army's advisory states, “The detonation will result in an air plume with some lead-containing particles which will disperse in the air and deposit on the ground.” It adds, “Lead is toxic to humans and animals. The goal of this advisory is to provide the public with actionable information out of an abundance of caution to minimize lead exposure.”

While the Army recommends cleaning cars, windowsills and outdoor surfaces, residents have taken to social media to voice concern and question whether those precautions will be enough. Officials said the vibrations from the two blasts would be quick but could break old windows in Middletown. The resulting dust was expected to linger for no more than one hour, depending on wind speed.

Middletown residents learned in March that the Army needed to dispose of several dozen barrels containing lead azide after the ammunition plant paused operations as part of a review and the degraded material was discovered. At the time, Lt. Col. Daniel Nosse, the plant's commander, said, “The safety of our people and our neighbors is non‑negotiable.” He acknowledged the volatility of the chemical compound used in munitions production. In October, the Army shared more details at a public meeting in Middletown and said a detonation was planned for an unspecified date in November.

The Environmental Protection Agency approved the Army's emergency permit on Nov. 13, paving the way for Friday's detonation. The permit required the Army to model the pathway for specific amounts of lead that could disperse in the air and deposit on the ground, and to notify the public five days in advance. That requirement prompted Sunday's advisory to communities as far as 100 miles (160 kilometers) away.

Iowa Gov. Kim Reynolds' office was first briefed Monday on the public advisory, her office said. News of the planned detonations also drew attention from environmental and public health officials in Illinois, who urged the U.S. military to conduct comprehensive sampling in the state after the detonations.

Public affairs specialist Justine Barati at the Army Sustainment Command said the barrels of lead azide had been stored at the ammunition plant for more than 20 years in earthen-covered containers, sitting in a bath of ethanol and water to keep the material stable. During a routine check earlier this year, officials found that some of the water had evaporated, causing the lead azide to crystallize. That makes the material more sensitive to friction and shock, creating “a highly unstable and dangerous situation,” according to the emergency permit approved last week by the EPA.

Officials said moving or relocating the material posed an unacceptable risk of accidental detonation, and Barati said that possibility increases in cold weather. “We're really concerned about the weather situation,” Barati said on moving forward with Friday's detonation. “We're really concerned that it will detonate on its own.”

The Army said it assembled a panel of experts in munitions chemistry, engineering and environmental safety to develop the detonation plan. The barrels are surrounded above, below and on all sides with concrete, which is buried under soil, and sand barriers stacked two stories high surround the soil. During the detonation, the soil will be sprayed with water and the air will be misted to try to keep the majority of lead on Army property, Barati said.

Patrick O'Shaughnessy, a professor at the University of Iowa's Department of Occupational and Environmental Health, said it was encouraging to see the Army and the EPA doing all they could to minimize the dust. He said it remained unknown how much lead would reach the atmosphere and nearby surfaces. O'Shaughnessy said lead is a neurological toxicant that can decrease brain activity and development, especially in young children. He was particularly concerned about toddlers at the age of putting everything in their mouths.

According to EPA documents, alternatives such as neutralizing and decomposing the contents of the drums with other chemicals were considered. Officials said any plan involving handling the drums would increase the probability of an accidental, fatal detonation.
