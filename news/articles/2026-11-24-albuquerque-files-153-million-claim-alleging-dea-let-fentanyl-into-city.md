## DEA rejects AP account citing court-authorized Title III investigations

Last week, Albuquerque submitted a $153 million claim against the DEA, alleging that the agency allowed fentanyl into the city so agents could build larger criminal cases. Known as “walking,” the law-enforcement practice allows some drugs to proceed into the country in the expectation of arresting figures higher up the smuggling chain.

New Mexico Attorney General Raúl Torrez sued the Justice Department three months before Albuquerque submitted its claim, seeking DEA records he said were necessary for the New Mexico Department of Justice’s investigation. That investigation focuses on allegations that DEA agents knowingly allowed large quantities of fentanyl to be distributed in New Mexico.

Albuquerque City Attorney Joshua Boone said the city planned to hold the government accountable. “Albuquerque is fighting an uphill battle that we didn’t know about for the longest time. Now that we know about it, we can do something about,” Boone said.

The DEA initially denied ever knowingly allowing the trafficking of a synthetic opioid. Federal and local authorities then opened inquiries into the alleged practice, including by the office of New Mexico Attorney General Raúl Torrez, the U.S. Department of Justice Office of Inspector General and congressional committees.

The DEA challenged the AP reporting, saying in a statement to The Guardian that “public descriptions suggesting that DEA knowingly permitted fentanyl to reach communities are false and fundamentally mischaracterize the facts.” It said the cases involved complex, court-authorized Title III investigations in which agents and prosecutors conducted real-time surveillance, intelligence gathering and operational analysis targeting larger drug-trafficking organizations.

The agency said it was mandated to coordinate investigative decisions with leadership from U.S. attorney’s offices to ensure investigative steps were carefully coordinated to prevent harm to the public. It said its decisions “were lawful, reasonable under the circumstances, and consistent with department guidance.”

Tessa DuBerry, a U.S. attorney’s office spokesperson, wrote in an emailed statement to AP: “The Department of Justice is committed to using every lawful tool available to identify, disrupt and dismantle the organizations responsible for trafficking fentanyl into our communities.”

Michelle Lujan Grisham, the outgoing New Mexico governor, said allowing fentanyl to be walked was “like trying to prevent my house from burning down by negotiating with an arsonist.” She also said the DEA was “fully engaged in the cover-up because they know they went too far and it’s wrong.”

DEA whistleblower David Howell supplied much of the information behind the Associated Press report. In June, Howell claimed the DEA “poisoned our community to make cases. Through our own willful blindness, we get to say: ‘We don’t really know what happened to the drugs.’ But we 100% got people killed.”

New Mexico Department of Health data put the state’s projected overdose death rate for 2025 at 46.4 per 100,000 residents, more than twice the national rate, as the fentanyl crisis begins to subside in most areas.

The Albuquerque Journal reported last month on claims that the whistleblowers at the center of the controversy were subject to ongoing retaliation by supervisors.

An Associated Press investigation published by The Guardian alleged that DEA agents allowed 1.4 million fentanyl pills to enter the country during a New Mexico sting operation between August 2024 and April 2025. The Guardian said the operation had been billed as the largest drug bust in U.S. history. The new figure was far above the 74,000 fentanyl pills previously reported.

AP said DEA agents watched from a surveillance plane as dealers trafficked large packages of pills in plastic bins and duffel bags through highly transited hotels, apartment complexes, Airbnb rentals and self-storage facilities in six states. A photograph published with the report showed fentanyl seized by the DEA at an Albuquerque self-storage facility on April 28, 2025. The image was credited to the U.S. Department of Justice and AP.

The report said agents also allowed as many as 100,000 pills at a hotel in Albuquerque after the DEA surrounded the building in a stakeout, more than the 74,000 previously reported. AP said the figures, combined with the lethality of fentanyl pills often disguised as less potent pharmaceutical pills, raised questions about the legality and morality of walking. “Everybody wants the big fish, but the big fish isn’t guaranteed,” Brian Besser, a former DEA senior executive who oversaw the agency’s Rocky Mountain division, told AP. “When you’re dealing with a substance that can take this much human life, you can’t gamble like that.”
