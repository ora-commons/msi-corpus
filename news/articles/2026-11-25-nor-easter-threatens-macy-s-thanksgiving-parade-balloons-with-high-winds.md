## National Weather Service warns of possible 60 mph gusts

A potent Nor'easter is approaching New York City, where strong winds threaten to disrupt Thursday's Macy's Thanksgiving Day Parade for the first time since World War Two. The 100th parade faces a National Weather Service high-wind warning for the city, Long Island and north-east New Jersey. The storm could bring sustained winds and gusts close to the limits for full-size balloons, while heavy rain, high tides and coastal flooding create additional risks for Thursday's Thanksgiving travel.

The parade is scheduled to run from 08:30 local time until around 12:00 and includes marching bands, street performers, floats and 100 brightly colored inflatable balloons moving through New York City streets. New York City regulations prohibit full-size balloons from flying if sustained winds exceed 23 mph or gusts exceed 35 mph. Thursday's forecast could approach those limits.

The warning takes effect at 10:00 local time Thursday and remains in force until 01:00 Friday. Gusts could reach 60 mph, although the strongest winds are likely after the parade is due to end around 12:00. Because the warning starts 90 minutes after the parade's 08:30 start, only the parade's final portion overlaps it, and winds could approach the threshold that would keep the balloons from flying even as the forecast calls for the greatest gusts later in the day.

Past parades have continued despite wind and snow problems. Record wind gusts made the 1997 route a no-fly zone, but the giant character balloons were still featured in the parade and tethered to golf carts. In 1989, a major snowstorm kept balloon handlers from reaching the route, and the cheer and dance teams stepped in to pull the giant balloons along.

The event began as Macy's Christmas Parade, organized by employees at the department store to boost holiday sales. The first event took place on Thanksgiving Day in 1924. The parade was put on hiatus between 1942 and 1944 during World War Two, the only period in which it was canceled, making this year's event the 100th parade.

Over the last few days, a cluster of showers and thunderstorms has been swarming in the Gulf of Mexico. The disturbed weather is moving north toward the East Coast and deepening into a potent area of low pressure. The system is called a Nor'easter because it delivers strong north-easterly winds that drive into the East Coast.

Up to 3 inches of rain could fall with the Nor'easter. Flooding risk could be exacerbated along the coast as high tides arrive Thursday evening, while large waves and high sea levels are expected to bring the threat of erosion and damage along the shoreline. Strong to damaging gusts could also affect trees and power lines and cause significant travel disruption.

Other eastern parts of the United States will also receive heavy rain and strong winds, with snow possible in some inland areas.
