## Huang agreed to appear before three House committees on 1 December

Robert Reich, a former US secretary of labor, a professor of public policy emeritus at the University of California, Berkeley and a Guardian US columnist, built his November 19 commentary around Nvidia's response to Jensen Huang's resignation. Reich argued that Nvidia depended on Huang, the AI industry depended on Nvidia and the US economy depended on the AI industry. On that chain of leverage, he concluded that Huang could be too big to fail.

Huang said he would return to Nvidia as its CEO if its board requested it. “I’ve seen an outpouring of calls from Nvidia’s employees, investors, business partners and customers for me to return as CEO,” Huang said. “If the board of directors decides that my return is in the best interest of our shareholders, our employees, the company and America, I would be honored to answer these calls and return as CEO.”

The column reported that Huang had resigned last week and had admitted being unfaithful to his wife. It said US intelligence agencies had identified one of his alleged lovers as a spy for China. Reich framed the possible chip-transfer consequence conditionally: if that identification was true, Nvidia's most advanced AI chips may have found their way there.

Reich reported that more than 177,000 people had signed a “Stand with Jensen” open letter demanding that Nvidia's board of directors “bring Jensen back while an independent investigation establishes the facts.” Signers included Elon Musk, Y Combinator president Garry Tan, Epic Games CEO Tim Sweeney and thousands of self-reported Nvidia employees.

Huang was discussing a possible return, though the chair of Nvidia's board had said the company was not considering the move. One Huang ally told the Financial Times that a “war room” of Silicon Valley executives was being organized to lobby for the former Nvidia CEO's return and garner support from Trump. “Everyone that matters in tech outside the frontier labs loves Jensen,” the ally said, adding that the effort had Huang's support. Trump told reporters on Monday that he “would not mind” if Huang were reinstated as Nvidia CEO, adding that he thought Huang had “learned a big lesson.”

Reich asked what “big lesson” Trump had in mind, writing: “That it’s not a good look for the CEO of the US’s most valuable corporation to have a romantic relationship out of wedlock?” He then asked: “Or that such a CEO shouldn’t break the law by supplying America’s most advanced AI chips to China through a Chinese spy?”

On Tuesday, it was announced that Huang had agreed to appear on 1 December before the House foreign affairs committee, the select committee on the Chinese Communist party and the House intelligence committee in transcribed interviews. Reich closed his column by asking, “Even in an authoritarian economy, no CEO is above the law. Or is he?”

Reich cited the immediate market reaction: Hours after Huang's resignation last Thursday, Nvidia stock dropped 10.3% in pre-market trading, erasing approximately $490 billion of Nvidia's value by the time the market opened. Since the leadership controversy at Nvidia began on November 8, the company had lost about $800 billion in market value, according to the column.

Reich put Nvidia's share of the global advanced AI-chip market at about 90%. He said the company had become the major financier for the entire AI industry, with some people calling Nvidia the “central bank of AI.” Before November 8, Nvidia's $5 trillion market value had equaled about 8% of the total value of the S&P 500.

Reich then linked the AI industry to the wider US economy. He wrote that in the first quarter of 2025, buildouts of datacenters using Nvidia chips accounted for 92% of US economic growth, and that over the past two years, AI had been responsible for roughly half of America's overall growth. He also relayed analysts' fear that Nvidia's collapse could cause the AI bubble to burst, triggering a failure that could threaten the entire US, if not also the world, economy.

Reich said most Americans' 401(k)s were invested in giant index funds tied to the Nasdaq and S&P 500. He said those indexes were based largely on 10 huge corporations, all connected to Nvidia, which together comprised about 37% of the value of the entire US stock market.

Reich placed economic power on the same democratic-authoritarian continuum he used for government. He said that, for the last 22 months, the United States had witnessed what happened when the US government turned authoritarian, and he described the US economy as far more authoritarian. Throughout most of the last century, the US economy had been fairly democratic: Decisions about what was produced and at what price reflected the judgments of tens of thousands of corporate executives, managers and small-business owners, all of whom were trying to respond to consumers and investors. Through labor unions, workers also had some say.

In recent decades, Reich said, the US economy became concentrated in the hands of a few giant corporations. He said economies of scale had enabled those corporations to spread their fixed costs over many goods and services, while the US government had nearly abandoned antitrust (anti-monopoly) enforcement. Labor unions had shrunk from representing over a third of private-sector workers in the 1950s to just 6% today.

Over the last five years, especially in big tech, Reich said a handful of men had come to control the US economy. They had become among the richest people in the world, gained significant political clout and gained control over the direction and pace of technologies on which the US and the world depend. Among the men he named were Elon Musk, Jeff Bezos, Mark Zuckerberg, Larry Ellison, Tim Cook and Jensen Huang.

Reich described the group as “the authoritarians of the new economy.” He wrote: “They have unparalleled power over every aspect of the lives of Americans and millions of others around the world. And they have leveraged themselves to be, or seem, too big to fail.” His application to Nvidia was direct: Nvidia's value depends on Huang, he said, and Huang may be too big to fail.
