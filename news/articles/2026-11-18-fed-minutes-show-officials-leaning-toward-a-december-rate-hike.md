## Waller backs a half-point increase as inflation shows signs of broadening beyond energy and tariffs

The minutes from the Federal Reserve's Oct. 27-28 meeting, released three weeks after the gathering, showed a committee broadly united on the direction of rates for the December meeting and divided on the size of any increase. Nearly all officials expect the central bank to lift its key interest rate by a quarter point, according to the minutes, while some supported a hike in October and two floated the possibility of a half-point increase next month.

The minutes also said a "vast majority" of the 19 members of the Fed's policymaking committee worried that inflation could persist even if gas prices kept declining and the impact of tariffs and the buildout of AI infrastructure faded.

The release came three weeks after the central bank held its key rate unchanged at 3.9% in October, a decision that followed a rate increase for the first time in three years in September. At a news conference on Oct. 28, Chairman Kevin Warsh gave little guidance on the central bank's next moves. The statement issued after the Fed's meeting acknowledged that "inflation remains elevated."

The minutes are expected to solidify expectations for a rate hike in December and likely early next year as well. Wall Street investors already assign the December increase odds of nearly 95%, according to CME Fedwatch. When the Fed lifts its key rate, it can, over time, raise borrowing costs for mortgages, auto loans and credit cards. Home sales have withered as mortgage rates have topped 7% in recent months.

Some policymakers pressed for higher rates at the October meeting. The minutes said "some" policymakers supported lifting rates in October, and two of them — Fed governor Lisa Cook and Beth Hammack, president of the Federal Reserve Bank of Cleveland — formally dissented in favor of higher rates. President Donald Trump has sought to fire Cook, but the Supreme Court ruled in June she could keep her position while the case is litigated.

Inflation accelerated this fall and has shown signs of broadening beyond industries directly affected by higher oil and gas prices or tariffs, a trend that has raised alarm among many policymakers. Several officials have spoken in favor of more rate hikes in the past week.

Governor Christopher Waller said in a CNBC interview early Wednesday that inflation is "headed in the wrong direction and it's spreading out" into more goods and services. Waller is one of the Fed's seven governors and a member of its rate-setting committee.

"If you really want to get it down, you've got to start taking more aggressive actions," Waller said. He reiterated his support for a half-point rate increase at the Fed's December meeting, double the usual hike, and said several more hikes — not just the two that many economists forecast — might be needed to tame inflation.

The minutes suggested that one other official also agreed a half-point increase should be considered in December, though it was not clear whether that official is a voting member. Twelve of the 19 committee members vote on rate decisions, though all participate in the debates, and the minutes do not identify which officials took which positions.

Waller said core inflation, which excludes the volatile food and energy categories, has run at about a 3.5% annual rate over the past three months, far above the Fed's target of 2%. Businesses increasingly appear willing to pass on their own rising costs and also believe their customers can handle price increases of about 3%, he added.

"That's the kind of psychology I think we need to start breaking," Waller said.

Waller also argued that one reason he supports a half-point hike is that without greater communication, financial markets will not know whether a quarter-point increase in December will be followed by a similar move in January. If investors have a sense of further hikes in the pipeline, he said, they will push up market rates in anticipation and help the Fed cool inflation. Warsh has steadily refrained from providing such guidance, which he has said locks the Fed to a predetermined path.

John Williams, president of the New York Fed and vice chair of the Fed's rate-setting committee, has pushed back against the potential for a half-point increase. He told Axios on Tuesday, "I don't think the situation calls for dramatic action."

Other Fed officials have suggested many more hikes are likely necessary. Alberto Musalem, president of the St. Louis Fed, said in a speech in Buenos Aires on Tuesday that "the economy will likely require several interest rate increases before the spring of 2027" to bring inflation down. Musalem said the economy is expanding at a healthy pace and interest rates are not high enough to restrain borrowing and spending sufficiently to cool inflation. He does not have a vote on rates this year.

Mary Daly, president of the San Francisco Fed, said last week that she is leaning toward supporting further rate hikes, and added that she also worries that companies are increasingly willing to pass higher costs on to consumers.

"That's the inflationary impulse that we want to avoid," Daly said.
