## UK's Burnham only G7 leader to attend Cop31 in Turkey

The Guardian's editorial board said the Cop31 climate talks had acquired "a little political breathing space" that their delegates scarcely expected, pointing to the comeback by Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva in the second round of Brazil's presidential race and to Democratic success in the US midterms. The editorial, published Nov. 15, treated both results as climate outcomes: victory for Lula's opponent, Flávio Bolsonaro, "would have marked a dangerous retreat from democracy", and the editorial wrote that his commitment to environmental deregulation "would have spelled disaster for the Amazon". On the United States, the editorial said Donald Trump's grip has been loosened and argued that if Democrats win back the White House in 2028, the US should rejoin the UN process in time for Cop34.

The Guardian's board argued the diplomatic picture is less bleak than it might have been while the threat level remains high and rising. António Guterres used his last Cop as UN secretary general to warn that "it's make or bake time". The editorial cited the latest analysis pointing to heating of 2.7C by the end of the century, far beyond the 1.5C target in the Paris agreement, and said governments face another crunch moment in which pivoting away from fossil fuels can still avert the most dangerous impacts.

Attendance at the talks was thin among major economies, according to the editorial. The UK's Andy Burnham was the only G7 leader to make the trip to Turkey, while the French and German leaders chose to attend an EU summit in Dublin instead. Burnham's appointment of a climate security adviser, Madeleine Alessandri, should focus minds in the UK, the editorial said.

Both host countries' leaders prompted anger last week, The Guardian wrote. The Turkish president, Recep Tayyip Erdoğan, publicly extolled his country's oil reserves, while Australia's Anthony Albanese failed to mention fossil fuels in his speech. The editorial also pointed to warnings of mounting risks, unprecedented heat and disasters such as August's glacier collapse on the Nepal-Tibet border, which it said can engender a sense of paralysis or unreality.

Even so, the board wrote that for all its flaws the UN climate process remains crucial because there is no other mechanism to broker a global green transition. The editorial singled out UK domestic politics, writing that Kemi Badenoch's rejection of net zero has undermined an important cross-party consensus. UK ministers, who must soon make a decision on Rosebank oil and gas field, must realise that economic damage caused by the carbon pollution from the oil and gas produced would dwarf any benefits, it said. Governments of countries far poorer than the UK cannot be expected to turn away from coal and oil if rich countries keep investing, the editorial added.

On the negotiations themselves, the board said a resolution to derive 35% of energy worldwide from electricity by 2035 will be agreed this week if objections from fossil fuel producers can be overcome. It wrote that attempts to undermine the work of the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change must also be resisted, because the panel provides the science on which the process depends, and that increased accountability on climate finance is urgently needed, since poor countries cannot be expected to put up with endless delays.

The editorial closed on public opinion, writing that a large majority of the global public recognises the threat from global heating and wants action by governments. It acknowledged that this takes courage and leadership, because people may back a green transition in the abstract but be more resistant to changes that affect their lives. Those pushing for a faster shift away from fossil fuels, at Cop31 and at a national level, should be emboldened by the way public awareness and support have grown, the board wrote that "they are on the side of the future, while their opponents are stuck in the past."
