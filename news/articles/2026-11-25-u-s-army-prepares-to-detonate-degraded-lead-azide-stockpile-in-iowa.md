## Army adds barriers, foams and water controls near Middletown

The U.S. Army plans to conduct a controlled detonation of 68 barrels of degraded lead azide at 9 a.m. local time Wednesday at the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant in Middletown, Iowa. The operation would remove the nation’s entire stockpile of material used in munitions production after Army officials warned that the deteriorating chemicals could explode at any time.

Maj. Gen. Eric Shirley, the Army’s leader of the operation, said, “Inaction is no longer an option,” as the Army warned that the degraded chemicals could otherwise explode. The Army assembled experts to formulate a disposal plan, and the experts found that alternative solutions such as thermal treatment and neutralizing the drums’ contents were not as safe as a controlled detonation.

Robert Cekada, director of the Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, Firearms and Explosives, said the stockpile was brought to the Iowa plant from bases around the country in 2009. The lead azide was stored in barrels in case the military needed to use it again.

During a routine monitoring inspection in March, plant staff found that the lead azide had partially crystallized. The Army said crystallization made the material sensitive to friction and cold temperatures and created a risk of spontaneous detonation.

The Army has set up additional “blast barriers” around the bunkers where the 68 barrels will be detonated. The materials will be surrounded by concrete and sand barriers two stories high, while ballistic gels and foams will absorb lead dust. The Army also plans to douse the materials with thousands of gallons of water to tamp down the plume. Acting Secretary of the Army Adam Telle said the public should not panic.

Officials overseeing the operation said Wednesday’s wind is expected to carry the plume southwest toward Missouri and away from Burlington, a city of just under 25,000 people along the Mississippi River. Government officials encouraged residents within 5 miles of the site to evacuate to avoid lead dust in the air, while assuring residents that the barriers should limit much of the plume.

Environmental groups filed a lawsuit seeking to halt the operation. On Monday, a federal appeals court found that the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency had adequately incorporated safeguards to minimize the dispersal of lead dust. The ruling put the detonation back on track.

The blast was originally planned for last Friday. The Army changed the detonation date three times amid public backlash and to allow more time for safety measures and favorable wind, according to court documents.

Residents expressed concern about both the operation and its timing. Some said the timing could have been better because it was the day before Thanksgiving, while acknowledging the issue needed to be addressed. Others expressed concern about the effects on water, farms, animals and their homes.

“I don’t think lead danger goes away any time soon,” said Lucy Giehl, who lives nearby in West Burlington. “It seems someone might have thought about all of this a little sooner before it came to this.”

Ahead of the blast, schools near Middletown shut down, businesses closed and some residents sealed their windows and doors as a precaution. The preparations followed public concerns about potential health and environmental impacts from lead dust and frustration over the lack of information about the operation.
