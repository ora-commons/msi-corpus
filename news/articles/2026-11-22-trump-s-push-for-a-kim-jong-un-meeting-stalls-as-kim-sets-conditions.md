## North Korea's nuclear arsenal has grown to an estimated 60 warheads

President Trump's renewed effort to sit down with North Korean leader Kim Jong Un hit a wall during his trip to Asia this week. The two sides explored the possibility of face-to-face talks in a last-minute scramble, but Kim set conditions the United States could not accept, according to people familiar with the discussions.

A Trump administration official said Saturday there have been no efforts to broker a meeting with Kim. North Korea's mission to the United Nations could not be reached for comment.

The failed opening follows a stretch in which the White House could not get the North Korean leader on the phone at all. Trump tried reaching Kim last year through established phone lines and communications channels, according to people familiar with the matter. Nobody called back.

Trump next wrote a letter. The problem was getting it into Kim's hands. White House officials hatched a plan for a group to wait outside the Manhattan office of North Korea's permanent mission to the United Nations. When a North Korean diplomat walked past, the group swooped in and thrust the letter into his hands, the people familiar said. At a U.N. meeting, the diplomat later tossed the unopened letter in front of a U.S. representative.

The White House has said it is committed to North Korea's denuclearization. But the administration lacks a detailed policy plan for North Korea, according to people familiar with the matter, and Trump has left vacant the special envoy role for North Korea policy that existed during his first term. The White House's national-security strategy last year did not mention North Korea, the first such omission since the end of the Cold War.

The Pentagon's flagship policy document, the National Defense Strategy released in January, said North Korea posed a "clear and present danger of nuclear attack on the American Homeland." In a break from prior versions, it did not mention North Korean denuclearization.

Kim has moved the other way. In a February speech, he said the United States could win a peaceful coexistence by accepting his nation's nuclear program or face "eternal confrontation." He is now pushing for Washington to recognize North Korea as a nuclear power.

Since Kim last met Trump in 2019, the arsenal under Kim's control has grown from an estimated 20 to 30 nuclear warheads to an estimated 60, and North Korea now fields solid-fuel intercontinental ballistic missiles that can reach the United States. U.S. intelligence's own annual threat assessment last year acknowledged that "Kim has no intention of negotiating away his strategic weapons programs."

Pyongyang has built a defense against U.S. pressure through a growing alignment with Moscow. North Korea was among the few nations to back Russian President Vladimir Putin after his 2022 invasion of Ukraine, and U.S. officials later revealed that North Korea was shipping rockets and artillery shells to Russia. Kim traveled to Russia's Far East for a summit with Putin in September 2023 and received promises of help to build satellites; Putin's later trip to Pyongyang produced a mutual defense pact that paved the way for North Korea to send troops to Russia. Russian officials have called North Korea's nuclear weapons a reality that can't be denied.

The Kim regime has also financed itself through cryptocurrency theft. North Korea recently staged the largest crypto heist in history, stealing more than $6 billion from the cryptocurrency exchange Binance; the United States later said it was able to help freeze nearly half of the take. Pyongyang is allegedly responsible for 89% of crypto hacking losses worldwide this year, compared with 15% in 2021, according to analytics firm Chainalysis. With China and Russia's backing, the North Korean economy rose by 3.5% last year, the third year of growth above 3% for one of the world's most heavily sanctioned countries.

Beijing's posture has shifted as well. China had long opposed North Korea's push to obtain nuclear weapons but has more recently stopped making such calls, at least publicly, pushing instead for what it calls a "fair and impartial" approach toward peace on the Korean Peninsula — a stance suggesting it won't contest North Korea's de facto nuclear status.

U.S. officials discussed proposals to lay the groundwork for a Trump-Kim meeting, including a formal end to the Korean War, which terminated in an armistice more than seven decades ago, a person familiar with the situation said. Officials also considered releasing a statement that didn't formally recognize North Korea as a nuclear power but also didn't call for the dismantlement of its program, the person said. In return, the United States would seek nuclear and missile-testing caps, and Washington could pursue the opening of liaison offices, a steppingstone for more formal diplomatic ties.

Recognizing North Korea as a nuclear weapons state would be a significant shift in U.S. policy. Even engaging in talks with Kim without pushing for denuclearization would represent a major break with Washington's longstanding approach and raise fears among allies in South Korea, Japan and Europe, experts said.

En route to Asia last year, Trump hinted at the difficulties in contacting North Korea to explore talks. "Put out the word," he said of his desire to sit down with Kim. "They have a lot of nuclear weapons but not a lot of telephone service." There was no meeting. The only advance was Trump describing North Korea as "sort of a nuclear power." More recently, he has posted photos of past meetings, from a time when he would speak of their "very special relationship."

Over the past decades, U.S. administrations have tried to persuade North Korea to abandon its nuclear ambitions. They created special envoys and working-level groups, tried bilateral talks, and invited other nations to join. The United States looked at unilateral concessions, such as removing its tactical nuclear weapons from South Korea in 1991. A phased agreement struck in 1994 froze North Korea's plutonium-production capabilities in exchange for aid, sanctions relief and two light-water reactors; it collapsed in the early 2000s after the U.S. discovered North Korea had a clandestine uranium-enrichment program. Washington then pursued CVID — "Complete, Verifiable and Irreversible Dismantlement" — and former U.S. negotiators recalled their routine answer when North Koreans sought concessions: With denuclearization, all things are possible. Without denuclearization, nothing is possible.

Trump began his first term threatening tightened sanctions and "fire and fury." When Kim announced preparations on Jan. 1, 2017, for his country's first ICBM test, which put the United States within range for the first time, Trump said on social media, "It won't happen!" It did. Later that year, the Kim regime conducted its sixth nuclear test. Asked about possible negotiations, Trump said: "It would be very easy for me to make a simple deal and claim victory. I don't want to do that. I want them to get rid of their nukes."

After months of trading insults, Trump and Kim met in Singapore in June 2018 for the first face-to-face talks between a sitting U.S. president and a North Korean leader. The administration ditched CVID for FFVD, "final, fully verified denuclearization," and Kim reaffirmed a commitment in a joint statement to work toward the complete denuclearization of the Korean Peninsula. Kim, who had halted tests of intercontinental ballistic missiles before the talks, also paused short-range missile tests. In September that year, Trump said at a rally that they "fell in love."

At a second summit in 2019, Kim offered to close some facilities at the country's main Yongbyon nuclear complex in exchange for the removal of U.N. sanctions limiting trade; the administration pushed for a freeze of North Korea's nuclear and missile programs first. The talks failed and Trump departed abruptly. The breakdown embarrassed Kim, who brought a TV crew to document an agreement he was confident of winning, and North Korea instead began testing short-range ballistic missiles.

The two sides tried to revive talks. Trump sent Kim a letter the North Korean leader called "excellent," and Trump called Kim's return letter "beautiful." In a surprise meeting months after the failed summit, the two men shook hands and spoke in the Korean Demilitarized Zone — a reunion that generated historic photographs but little else.

In 2020, the pandemic slammed shut the door to diplomacy, and the Biden administration gave low priority to North Korea, outranked by Russia's invasion of Ukraine, war in the Middle East and the technological Cold War with China, according to people familiar with the matter. Biden officials also tried to write to the North Koreans through the U.N. mission in New York, sliding letters under the door, some of the people familiar said. Several times, the letters were later spotted abandoned in the hallway.

In Washington, attention to North Korea's rising nuclear threat has about disappeared, said Victor D. Cha, who worked for George W. Bush's National Security Council and was a negotiator in multilateral talks with North Korea during the 2000s. Cha has posited that arms control should now supplant denuclearization as a starting point for talks.

"For someone like me, it's kind of like screaming in a forest," Cha said. "Nobody is really paying attention."
