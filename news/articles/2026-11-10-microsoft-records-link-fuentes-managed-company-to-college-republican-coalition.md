## Microsoft records link Fuentes-managed company to college Republican coalition

**Subtype:** fact

- Microsoft’s public sign-in records showed CRPC’s domain in a Microsoft 365 account registered to NJF LLC, a company managed by Nick Fuentes.

- In Wednesday social media posts, CRPC blamed midterm defeats on the Trump administration and Republican Party.

## The coalition says it has 31 affiliated groups in 14 states

The College Republican Patriot Coalition says it has 31 affiliated student groups in 14 states. Its post-election statement listed college Republican groups including chapters at the University of Florida, Clemson and Iowa State University, plus state-level federations in Georgia, Maryland, New York, Tennessee and Virginia. The Guardian described CRPC as a coalition that criticized the Trump administration from the right. Its email infrastructure then became the focus of a Guardian review of Microsoft’s public sign-in records.

Microsoft’s public sign-in records put CRPC’s web domain in a Microsoft 365 account registered to NJF LLC, and the coalition routed its email through that same account. NJF LLC was managed by Fuentes and also ran his personal website and online store. Until Monday morning, the domain remained in the account. By Monday evening, hours after The Guardian sent questions to Fuentes and the coalition, the domain no longer appeared, a Microsoft verification code had been deleted from the account settings, and Fuentes’s own domains remained. The Guardian said the shared account provided the first confirmed operational link between Fuentes and CRPC; before that disclosure, the organization had only hinted at ties and no operational link had been definitively shown. CRPC has not publicly named its leaders and is not associated with any known registered legal or organizational entity. The Guardian contacted Fuentes and CRPC for comment.

## Shared infrastructure and overlapping organizations

The Guardian described Fuentes as a far-right podcaster and said CRPC had never admitted the apparent affiliation indicated by the shared account. Simon Dickerman, a former Fuentes associate, said in a 2022 live stream that the coalition “has to pretend like it has nothing to do with Nick Fuentes because Nick has made himself so radioactive to other people.” The Guardian said the shared account, rather than Dickerman’s remark, supplied the first confirmed operational link, and it contacted Simon Dickerman for comment. Many of the groups in CRPC’s statement also belonged to College Republicans of America, which was seeking recognition as the Republican Party’s official student wing and had publicly contended with the more established College Republican National Committee over who should represent college activists. CRA political director Kai Schwemmer appears in the coalition’s Instagram posts this year. The Guardian reported in March on Schwemmer’s appointment and his history of bigoted statements on live streams; Schwemmer claims he has abandoned his extremist views. The Guardian contacted CRA and Schwemmer for comment on CRPC’s shared email infrastructure with Fuentes’s company.

Ben Lorber, a senior research analyst at Political Research Associates, said: “[Fuentes] advises his young followers to join organizations that are closer to the conservative establishment, and he tells them to ‘hide their power level’ and maybe downplay their kind of most radical ideas.” Political Research Associates is a non-profit that researches extremist groups. The Guardian said the disclosure highlighted the inroads Fuentes and his white nationalist “Groyper” movement had made among younger Republican activists and student organizations. Lorber also said of Fuentes’s so-called “America first” movement: “From the very beginning, Fuentes positioned America First as an alternative to Turning Point USA, and so it makes sense that he has a toehold in alternative campus conservative groups that position themselves as more radical than the mainstream GOP campus groups”. One of CRPC’s main public outlets is its Instagram account, which features photographs of faceless members carrying out flyer drops around the country, memes that The Guardian described as racist depictions of Trump administration officials and retrospective albums of face-to-face meet-ups. Many photographed faces have been redacted, and the report said members presumably did not wish to be identified as adherents of Fuentes’s movement. Lorber said of the redactions: “It makes it clear that they [are] associated with a radical right politics that they don’t necessarily want traced to their names and faces right now.”

## Post-election messages echoed Fuentes’s criticism of Trump

In Wednesday social media posts, CRPC blamed Tuesday’s midterm defeats on the Trump administration and Republican Party, saying young voters had received “a war on behalf of Israel, stunted deportations, and a flailing economy.” The Guardian said CRPC’s post-election messaging echoed Fuentes’s pre-election criticism of Donald Trump. On 26 October, Fuentes posted that Trump had “started a war with Iran that we are not only losing but which also destroyed the economy.” The Guardian reported that Fuentes reportedly accused Trump of starting the war at Benjamin Netanyahu’s bidding. He criticized his failure to deport millions and told viewers of his stream: “Normies don’t like war with Iran. Normies don’t like 3.7% inflation!”

## Florida chapter was deactivated and sued

The Guardian reported that student groups closely affiliated to CRPC had often encountered local controversies over their promotion of white nationalist ideas, links to white nationalist activists and endorsements of political candidates who had themselves voiced views similar to Fuentes’s. The University of Florida’s College Republicans chapter co-authored eight Instagram posts with CRPC this year. The university deactivated the chapter in March after photographs reportedly appeared to show members flashing Nazi salutes and posing with Fuentes and Myron Gaines, whom The Guardian described as an antisemitic influencer and podcaster. The university said on social media that the Florida Federation of College Republicans had told it the state group had “disbanded the local chapter of College Republicans.” The Guardian reported in March that the chapter’s state leadership had cited “a pattern of conduct that violated its rules and values, including a recent antisemitic gesture.”

The chapter sued for reinstatement and was represented by Anthony Sabatini, a lawyer and former Republican state representative. A federal judge dismissed the Florida case in June after a bench trial, and CRPC reportedly co-hosted the chapter’s first meeting after the ruling. The Guardian contacted Sabatini, UF College Republicans and the Florida Federation of College Republicans for comment. A Guardian caption showed then-representative Sabatini after speaking during a legislative session in April 2021 at the state capitol in Tallahassee, Florida. Sabatini, a Lake County commissioner who served in the Florida House from 2018 to 2022, headlined a 2022 conference in Tampa that was reportedly organized by former members of Fuentes’s America First Foundation. He reportedly called for a moratorium on immigration and the abolition of the Juneteenth national holiday that celebrates the end of slavery, and told the crowd: “God bless the nationalist populist movement.”

The chapter’s complaint argued that the university had acted in part because the chapter had hosted Republican “gubernatorial candidate James Fishback, a critic of Israel.” In a post co-authored with CRPC, the chapter called the Fishback event the “Largest Candidate event at UF in almost 10 years.” Fishback, a 31-year-old investor, finished third in August’s Republican primary for Florida governor. The Guardian said his campaign was peppered with controversies arising from what it called “racist remarks.” He repeatedly referred to Byron Donalds, the Republican congressman who on Tuesday was elected Florida’s first Black governor, as a “slave.” In July, he wrote in a statement that a dispute had led Donalds “to chimp out on a 5’0 blonde woman in a Naples grocery store,” using a slur that likens Black people to apes. Fishback reportedly said he was “not aware of the history” of the comparison. A Guardian caption showed Fishback speaking at the Republican Assembly of Sumter County in the Warfield Auditorium in Wildwood, Florida, on 16 April. On 28 October, Fishback floated a run for president in 2028. College Republican chapters at Clemson and Georgia State, both on CRPC’s list, hosted him on 29 and 30 October. On Wednesday, Fishback quote-posted the coalition’s election statement with the words “Great statement.” The Guardian contacted Fishback for comment.

At the chapter’s first meeting after its return, the speakers included Marshall Rawson, the Republican nominee for a north Florida seat in the state House. The Guardian reported that Rawson had reportedly marched with the League of the South, described in the report as neo-Confederate, at the 2017 Unite the Right rally in Charlottesville, Virginia. In a statement, Rawson said he had been “very active defending historical monuments” throughout his adult life. “Who cares about the lying media when the largest and most active College Republican chapter in the United States is behind you!” Rawson posted on X that week alongside a photo of himself with chapter members. The chapter’s post about the meeting, co-authored with the coalition, also thanked Tim Marden, the chair of the Alachua County Republican Party and mayor of Newberry, who had publicly backed Rawson. The Guardian contacted Rawson for comment. In an email, Marden wrote: “I prefer to use my relationships as an opportunity to influence people in what I believe is a more positive direction. Life is not an all or nothing scenario.”

## Maryland event drew protest and heavy security

The Maryland Federation of College Republicans, which appeared on CRPC’s list and co-authored the Instagram version of Wednesday’s statement, invited Jared Taylor, whom The Guardian described as a white nationalist, to speak at Salisbury University that spring. Salisbury postponed the talk in March “based on public safety considerations,” saying anticipated attendance, “counter-demonstrations, and related security needs” had “increased significantly.” The university noted that the federation was “not an SU organization” but an outside group renting space on campus. The university’s College Democrats group had planned a protest for the day of the talk. Maryland Republican Party chair Nicole Beus Harris reportedly said the party had not been involved and condemned “the advertised event and the speaker’s despicable views.” The talk proceeded on 29 April under heavy security as dozens protested outside at a demonstration organized by the Salisbury College Democrats. Taylor reportedly called the protesters “a bunch of big babies.”

The federation’s then-chair, Colin McEvers, at that time a Salisbury student, reportedly introduced Taylor as someone who might have the key to “save our country, to keep it from becoming a non-English-speaking hellscape where white people are spit at, despised and persecuted.” College Republicans of America, which the Maryland federation joined in 2025, said it “did not endorse or sanction the Maryland event,” The Guardian reported in July. A Facebook account with McEvers’ name in its URL now posts under the name Maryland White Republican Council. On Wednesday, it said: “It is time for the pro-White flank of the right wing that has been censored, suppressed, and cast aside by the establishment to legitimately take the reins of the GOP.” The Guardian contacted the Maryland Federation of College Republicans for comment.

Asked about his call for “white advocacy” in the Republican Party, McEvers responded in an email that despite a failed effort from the Maryland GOP to censure him, he was able “to secure the Republican nomination for Delegate in Maryland Legislative District 32, become a member of the Anne Arundel County Republican Central Committee, and there have been many people within the party, of all age groups, who have come to accept that White advocacy is both acceptable and necessary for Republican success”. On the Maryland White Republican Council page, he wrote: “White people of all age groups failed to show up for the GOP this year, mostly among young voters. This proves that White people, namely young White men, are a legitimate voting bloc that the Republican Party must explicity advocate for; hence, the White Republican Council, which fundamentally promotes White Advocacy within the GOP in order to get Whites out to vote Republican.” He denied any direct relationship with CRPC.

## Fuentes’s movement and criticism of Trump

A Guardian caption showed Nick Fuentes with a crowd of supporters after he spoke at the America First Political Action conference in Detroit, Michigan, on 15 June 2024. The Guardian describes Fuentes as a white nationalist podcaster. The report said he dropped out of Boston University after he was seen at the 2017 Unite the Right rally in Charlottesville, Virginia, and built a following of young men known as “Groypers” through his livestreamed show, America First. It also said he was present at the riot at the US Capitol on 6 January 2021, though he did not enter the building, and that in 2022 he dined with Donald Trump and the rapper Kanye West at Mar-a-Lago. Trump later said he did not know who Fuentes was.

The Guardian reported that Fuentes was banned from YouTube and many other platforms for hate speech before Elon Musk allowed him back onto X in 2024. In October 2025, his podcast briefly became the highest-trending offering on Spotify. The Guardian described Tucker Carlson’s two-hour interview as friendly and reported in November that it had set off “a bitter and widening civil war within the American right.” Heritage Foundation president Kevin Roberts apologized after initially defending the interview. The Guardian also reported that Fuentes backed Trump in 2016 and 2020 but had increasingly criticized him from the right over Israel and what he saw as the Republican Party’s insufficient radicalism, especially on immigration.

The Guardian reported that growing disillusionment had led Fuentes to urge his followers in recent months to disengage from elections or even vote for Democrats. In February, he reportedly told his audience: “Do not vote in the midterms. The Republicans have to lose.” The Guardian also reported that, after Trump went to war with Iran, Fuentes condemned the war and mocked people who had voted for Trump believing he represented a break with US foreign policy. On election day, Fuentes posted: “Get out and vote Democrat today.” On election night, he reportedly said: “This is literally the worst case scenario for Republicans playing out tonight. And honestly, I could not be happier.”
