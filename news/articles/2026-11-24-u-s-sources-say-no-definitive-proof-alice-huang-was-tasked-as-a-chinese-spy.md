## Chinese intelligence expanded overseas, blurring the line between agents and unwitting assets

U.S. sources said data-industry executive Alice Huang was in contact with Chinese intelligence during her affair with Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang. People with knowledge of U.S. intelligence said there was no definitive evidence that she had been actively tasked with collecting information about Jensen Huang or passing along national-security secrets. Some officials described the links as circumstantial, while others said any information Huang collected and passed on posed a national-security risk regardless of its sensitivity because Jensen Huang is central to the U.S. artificial-intelligence industry.

The affair sparked an international scandal and precipitated Jensen Huang's short-lived exit from Nvidia. President Trump and his allies highlighted the lack of clarity in the intelligence as they successfully lobbied for his reinstatement. In closed-door briefings and television panel shows, the question of whether Alice Huang was a spy became one of the most fiercely debated issues surrounding the scandal.

Interviews with current and former U.S. intelligence officials, lawmakers and China analysts pointed to a broader concern. Under Xi Jinping, China's intelligence services have grown so expansive and opportunistic that the line between a recruited agent and an unwitting asset has blurred, making damage harder to assess and the threat easier to dismiss. The White House and Central Intelligence Agency declined to comment. The Office of the Director of National Intelligence did not respond to requests for comment. Beijing has repeatedly denied there was a Chinese plot to spy on Jensen Huang.

The Ministry of State Security has transformed during Xi's tenure from a shadowy, domestically focused agency into what some U.S. intelligence officials now describe as the world's largest and most active spy service, with an estimated 600,000 to 800,000 personnel. Xi's appointment of his protégé Chen Yixin as minister in 2022 accelerated the expansion. Chen pushed the ministry into the open by launching a WeChat account, producing videos warning that “foreign spies are everywhere,” and expanding national-security education into school curricula from primary grades through the university level. The ministry's outreach has framed ordinary Chinese citizens as expected to remain vigilant and participate in counterespionage.

The Ministry of State Security's reach now extends well beyond China's borders. It closely monitors Chinese nationals living overseas through surveillance of WeChat and other digital platforms and through on-the-ground operations. Recruitment abroad has intensified, with intelligence operatives using LinkedIn-style headhunter approaches, commercial pretexts and face-to-face meetings inside China to cultivate contacts with connections to politics, technology or business.

Intelligence analysts said Alice Huang would have been impossible for the Chinese security service to miss. She was a Chinese businesswoman with direct personal access to the CEO of the world's most strategically important chip-designer, and she posted intimate photos of herself with Jensen Huang on WeChat Moments, where anyone in her network could see them.

In March, Secretary of State Marco Rubio oversaw the drafting of a memo to warn Trump that Jensen Huang might have been compromised by Alice Huang's intelligence connections. The memo did not definitively say she was a Chinese spy directed to collect intelligence on the Nvidia chief.

Alice Huang's former company, Singapore-headquartered Megaspeed, has been under investigation by U.S. authorities since at least 2024 for allegedly smuggling restricted Nvidia chips to Chinese firms. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent told Fox News earlier this month that it “doesn’t matter to me whether she’s a spy or not,” adding that the larger issue was Nvidia's work with companies such as Megaspeed to potentially circumvent export restrictions.

Others who favored Jensen Huang's reinstatement, including Trump, suggested that the lack of clarity surrounding Alice Huang's intelligence connections showed the Nvidia board had acted too hastily in moving against him. Rep. Jim Himes (D., Conn.), the incoming chairman of the House Intelligence Committee, said the U.S. intelligence community did not have clear answers on critical questions about Alice Huang, including whether she was an active Chinese agent.

“We need to know what information he conveyed to her, what influence she might have applied to him, whether she was tasked by the Chinese, whether she was witting—we don’t know the answer to that question,” Himes said.

The Trump administration has shared little of the underlying intelligence about Alice Huang with Capitol Hill, according to lawmakers and congressional staff. A scheduled session to discuss the intelligence and other matters last Wednesday was postponed. Jensen Huang is scheduled to appear before the Senate Banking Committee on Dec. 3.

When Nvidia announced Jensen Huang's return, the board said that after receiving assurances from senior U.S. officials, it had concluded his “reinstatement poses no national security risk.” Nvidia did not respond to a request for comment.

Trump told CNBC on Monday that he had spoken directly with Xi about Jensen Huang. Trump said the Chinese leader's respect for the Nvidia CEO was “so great” and that Xi had called him “brilliant.” Trump also suggested that if Jensen Huang were treated badly, he could end up running a rival AI company in China.

Those gaps have already shaped decisions around Jensen Huang's leadership. Trump and his allies used the intelligence ambiguity to lobby for his reinstatement, while Nvidia's board relied on assurances from senior U.S. officials to conclude that his return posed no national-security risk. Himes said the intelligence community still lacks clear answers on whether Alice Huang was an active agent, what information Jensen Huang conveyed to her, and whether she acted knowingly.
