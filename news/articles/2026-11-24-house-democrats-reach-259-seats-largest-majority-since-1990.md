## A wider majority expands Democrats' room for oversight and policy votes

House Democrats reached 259 seats in the 2026 midterm elections on Tuesday, delivering their strongest general-election result in decades and an election sweep not seen since 1990. The milestone in the 435-member chamber leaves Republicans with at least 173 seats in the minority and establishes a Democratic majority ahead of the party's takeover of both chambers in January. The result sets up divided government, with Democratic majorities in the House and Senate facing Republican President Donald Trump in the White House.

The count reached 259 when Victoria Broderick defeated Republican Rep. Scott DesJarlais in Tennessee. Democrats have gained 45 House seats from where they started on Election Day, five more than the 40 they picked up during President Donald Trump's first midterm in 2018. The 259-seat majority exceeds the party's 258 seats in 1992, when Bill Clinton was first elected president, and its 257 seats in the election of Barack Obama in 2008. It remains below the Franklin D. Roosevelt-era high, when Democratic majorities grew past 300.

Three House contests remain unresolved: one in Minnesota and two in Louisiana that are headed to a December runoff. Republicans' 173-seat total is among their lowest House tallies in decades. The party won 247 seats in 2014 near the end of the Obama administration and 230 in 1994 with the rise of Newt Gingrich. Its high-water marks include the 1920 election, when Republicans won 301 seats.

Democrats will take control of both chambers in January. Senate Democrats won a 56-seat majority in the 100-member chamber, their biggest advantage in more than a decade. The Associated Press said the House result creates opportunities to govern but also intense pressure to deliver on campaign promises and priorities.

The party is preparing a 100-day agenda to address high costs of living while pursuing oversight of Trump for the remainder of his second term. Planned swift votes include reversing Republicans' Medicaid cuts and restoring Affordable Care Act tax credits that Republicans allowed to expire. House Democrats have also vowed to use subpoena powers and launch investigations to check presidential power, including probes into how Trump and his family have become wealthier since he returned to office.

The pressure is explicit inside the Democratic caucus. "Like, hell yeah, we should feel pressure," said Democratic Rep. Jason Crow of Colorado, a former Army Ranger, at the Capitol earlier this month. "There are people dying in this country and people losing their healthcare, losing their livelihoods, losing their farms, being terrorized from ICE," Crow said, referring to U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement. "If you don't feel a sense of urgency and pressure in this job, and a desperation to deliver for folks, then you don't belong under this Capitol dome."

The Associated Press reported that Democratic wins extended from the strongest Republican states to the Midwest and East Coast. Voters angry over high prices and the Iran war and concerned about corruption made clear their discontent with Trump, according to the outlet.

Democratic leader Hakeem Jeffries of New York, who is in line to become House speaker, has not ruled out trying to impeach Trump for a third time. At the Capitol, progressive leader Rep. Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez, D-N.Y., said, "This is a historic moment for our country. This is one of the largest classes we have seen," and called the large majority "a mandate for change."

Rep. Tom Suozzi, a centrist Democrat from New York and an accountant, said House Democrats should focus 75% of their time on affordability and 25% on accountability. "Everything should be tied back to the people — and how it's affecting the people," Suozzi said. As Democrats launch investigations into Trump's financial dealings, Suozzi said they should connect the economic questions to voters: "How does that affect people's pocketbooks?"

The White House warned against the planned Democratic probes into the administration, claiming the investigations may amount to "blatant criminality." The Associated Press described such investigations as a standard oversight function of Congress.

Republican Speaker Mike Johnson of Louisiana struggled to keep the House functioning for the past two years with a narrow single-digit majority, in which a few Republican lawmakers could derail the agenda. Democrats' wide majority gives their incoming leadership more room to maneuver. Jeffries, if elected by his colleagues on Jan. 3, 2027, would become the first Black House speaker. With 259 seats, he could lose dozens of Democrats on a vote and still have the 218 needed for passage.

Rep. Troy Carter, D-La., who is expected to lead the Congressional Black Caucus, said the caucus would remain diverse in its views. "We're not a monolith — people come to the caucus with different ideas — it's OK," Carter said.

Republican Rep. Jim Jordan of Ohio, elected by his colleagues to be the Republican leader in the minority, vowed to fight for a return to power. "Over the next two years, House Republicans will fight for American families," Jordan said.
