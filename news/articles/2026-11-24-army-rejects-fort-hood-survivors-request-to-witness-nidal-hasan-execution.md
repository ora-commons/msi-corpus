## Survivors cite broader victim definition; military cites space and security limits

The Army has denied three survivors of the 2009 Fort Hood shooting permission to witness Nidal Hasan’s execution, scheduled for Dec. 3. Hasan fatally shot 13 people at the Texas Army post and was found guilty of multiple counts of murder and attempted murder related to those killed and the more than 30 people wounded by gunfire. Military officials rejected the survivors’ request for reconsideration, citing security and space issues.

On Nov. 10, the Army said the three could not attend because none was a direct victim of the charges Hasan faced. Military regulations govern who may witness an execution. An email sent Tuesday and shared with the Associated Press by recipients said officials rejected the request for reconsideration. Hasan is being held at the military prison at Fort Leavenworth.

A letter signed by the Fort Leavenworth commandant said the three could not appeal again. “Please understand that this is not meant as a judgment on any emotional injury you or others suffered as a result of CP Hasan’s crimes,” the letter said. The Army said regulations specify who can witness a military execution and reiterated size and space restrictions.

The Army also said efforts had been made over the years, from the trial through notifications and memorials and now to the execution, to recognize the attack’s impacts and support the victims. The survivors disagreed with the Army’s reading of who qualifies as a victim.

The three asked the Army to apply a broader definition of “victim” from the Uniform Code of Military Justice. The definition says a victim can be someone who suffered “direct physical, emotional or pecuniary harm.” The survivors said the refusal treated them as outside that category because bullets had not struck them.

The military had planned to livestream the execution, the first since 1961 and the first by firing squad since World War II, but rescinded the plan after bipartisan backlash. An Army official, speaking on condition of anonymity to discuss sensitive plans, said the military was not planning to release details about the firing squad or protocols, including the number of servicemembers involved.

Retired Col. Kathy Platoni, who would have been Hasan’s supervisor during a planned deployment to Afghanistan, said the denial felt like a betrayal. “I would desperately like to attend,” Platoni said, describing a desire to look Hasan in the eye and tell him, “You didn’t get me.” Platoni’s unit deployed to Afghanistan 29 days after the attack because, “we still served our country, and if we didn’t, (Hasan) would have won.”

Platoni said the building where Hasan initially opened fire was crowded with returning soldiers and others preparing to deploy; she had been moved to another building when the attack began. She recalled colleagues carrying in the dying and wounded. “The looks on the faces of my friends and colleagues as they carried in the dying and wounded that day … it will haunt me for the rest of my life,” she said. A captain rushed Hasan with a chair in an attempt to stop the shooting.

“I was throwing computers to the ground trying to get the tablecloths because we needed blankets. We didn’t have medical supplies or even water,” Platoni said. She still had the blood-stained uniform from kneeling beside the young man and trying to keep him with them.

Julia Wilson, who was nine months pregnant when Hasan opened fire, said her request also was denied and the military’s handling of the case had caused her anguish. “We have faced institutional betrayal after institutional betrayal,” Wilson said. “We remain on that battlefield because of that … if they don’t fix it we’re never going to leave that battlefield. We’ll never heal.”

Platoni had been on the military’s list of victims and survivors since Hasan’s 2013 court-martial trial. Everyone on the list received notification in March that there had been movement in the execution’s scheduling. Platoni said she did not believe space was the actual obstacle. The military told victims and survivors that about 160 witnesses could attend, and Platoni believed about 90 people had decided to go, though she conceded those figures might have changed.

Retired Maj. C. Alan Hopewell testified at Hasan’s trial and confirmed Hasan’s identity after Hasan was shot by two service members attempting to halt his rampage. One of those officers asked Hopewell to accompany her to the execution.

Hopewell said he would accept the denial but with mixed feelings. “I’m disappointed that our status as victims is not recognized,” Hopewell said, adding that a bullet missed him and hit his car. He had recently undergone surgery unrelated to the shooting and was unsure whether his doctors would clear him to attend, but he had hoped the Army would grant him the right. “I guess it feels more like a duty, like a fulfillment of my responsibility to those soldiers who were killed and injured, who were threatened,” Hopewell said.

Platoni, Wilson and Hopewell said they would continue pushing for the Fort Hood shooting to be redefined as an act of domestic terrorism rather than workplace violence.
