## Coin-toss study links pattern-spotting to belief in conspiracies

The sequence began with outages at companies including Microsoft, United Airlines, JPMorgan Chase and U.S. Bank. The failures caused widespread banking issues, delayed plane departures and interrupted workdays across the world. Soon afterward, Marriott and Apple customers also reported digital problems, and 178 companies reported being affected by a Nov. 16 cyberattack.

Government systems did not escape the run. The Federal Aviation Administration, the National Security Agency and the National Weather Service all fell into disarray, according to the analysis, including a rare nationwide ground stop.

For many people, the pile-up of concerning news alerts carried a sinister feel. Nalin Haley, a conservative influencer and son of Republican politician Nikki Haley, wrote on Nov. 17: "We're under attack in a way we've never seen before." He added: "This is an AI cyberattack."

On Bluesky, Christopher Webb, a self-described "lifelong Dem" and party strategist, questioned whether the issues were connected. "Coincidence??? 4 major banks reporting outages, a massive Microsoft disruption, and a nationwide FAA ground stop, all within days of each other," he wrote Nov. 16.

The analysis, written by PolitiFact's Madison Czopek, concluded that the best available information points to mundane explanations — routine maintenance, one-off problems or isolated cyberattacks — rather than an imminent systemic collapse or a coordinated attack on digital systems. The rapid-fire succession of events did not help, the analysis found, and neither did the timing of a planned, routine Nov. 17 test of the country's Emergency Alert System.

Researchers who study why people latch onto conspiracy theories point to ordinary cognitive machinery. "Detecting patterns is important for perceiving the world and learning about cause and effect," said Javier Granados Samayoa, a psychology professor at Texas Christian University. That tendency "can sometimes lead people to see connections where none may exist," he said.

One paper on conspiracy thinking began with a coin toss. People who reported a tendency to notice patterns in a random string of coin tosses were also more likely to endorse conspiracy theories and supernatural beliefs, according to the analysis.

Other research shows that when people lack control, they are more likely to detect patterns that are not there. For people unable to reach their work platforms or stuck at airports, the analysis noted, powerlessness and confusion were familiar feelings — and those feelings contribute to embracing conspiracy theories.

"People are looking for ways to understand what is going on and they don't like the uncertainty that often surrounds unfolding events," said Karen Douglas, a social psychology professor at the University of Kent. "Also, a simple explanation is often not very appealing. People assume that there must somehow be a bigger explanation, or more going on than people know about."

Negativity bias compounds the effect, according to the analysis. Negative information has a stronger impact and people tend to think about it more, Granados Samayoa said. On social media, content is more likely to prompt engagement and go viral if it uses negative language or triggers a strong negative reaction.

Social media algorithms have continuously circulated some of the most alarming posts and conspiracy theories. Axios reported that an estimated 30% of them were pushed by suspected bots, including some with potential foreign influence.

Once such an idea takes hold, it can be hard to dislodge. "Once people believe in a conspiracy theory, it is often difficult to convince them otherwise," Douglas said.

The pile-up also fed on attention itself. Regular people and news reporters alike began paying more attention to outages that would otherwise have been routine — a phenomenon commonly called frequency bias. Kyle Potter, an editor at Thrifty Traveler, made the point on Nov. 16. "United's outage this afternoon is so pedestrian, it'd be a non-story if not for what's happened the last four days," he wrote.
