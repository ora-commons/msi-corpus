## European, Japanese craft will separate next month to map planet

A pair of spacecraft entered orbit around Mercury on Saturday, ending an eight-year journey through the inner solar system for a mission built to unravel some of the mysteries of the smallest planet. The two coupled robotic explorers — one European, the other Japanese — will separate next month to map and study the scorching planet from different altitudes.

The mission, known collectively as BepiColombo, costs $2 billion.

The European Space Agency announced the arrival on social media. A series of thruster burns reduced the spacecraft's speed enough for them to be captured by the planet's weak gravity, but several tense hours passed before flight controllers in Germany — 152 million kilometers (94 million miles) away — could confirm that BepiColombo had reached orbit. Teams pored over the data as it came in, and no further information was immediately available.

BepiColombo is only the third spacecraft to visit Mercury — the planet closest to the Sun and barely larger than the Moon — and the first in more than a decade.

The first was NASA's Mariner 10, which flew past Mercury three times in the mid-1970s. The late Italian mathematician Giuseppe "Bepi" Colombo, for whom the current mission is named, served on that team. NASA's Messenger spacecraft entered orbit around Mercury in 2011 and, after running out of fuel, crashed into the planet in 2015.

Launched in 2018, BepiColombo took a slower, more indirect route to Mercury: one pass near Earth, two near Venus and six near Mercury, using those flybys as gravity assists to steer its trajectory. All those loops added more than 10 billion kilometers (6 billion miles) to the mission's odometer. The duo of probes detached from their mothership in September for the final stretch of the trip.

Geraint Jones, the project's chief scientist, said he and others cannot wait for all the new data to begin arriving next year. The mission, he said, will allow scientists to compare measurements taken by two spacecraft at the planet at the same time, which he described as unprecedented.

Once the two craft orbit separately, the European Space Agency's satellite and the smaller probe of the Japan Aerospace Exploration Agency will study Mercury's dense interior and its outer magnetic field, along with everything in between: impact craters, volcanic plains, mountain ridges and peaks that rise as high as 4,8 kilometers (3 miles).

Temperatures on Mercury can exceed 427 degrees Celsius (800 degrees Fahrenheit), adding strain to electronic and engineering systems. Space agency officials want to keep the mission running for at least one Earth year, the equivalent of four Mercury years.

Jones said in an email that the spacecraft and its instruments must be kept as cold as possible, calling that requirement a huge technical challenge in itself.

Despite its relative proximity to Earth, Mercury is one of the least explored planets in the solar system. Only the outer planets have had fewer visitors: a single Voyager 2 in the 1980s.
