## Independent Australian MPs challenge Labor’s late election funding amendments

**Subtype:** fact

- Independent senator David Pocock said the amendments would allow an uncapped amount of money to flow from huge investment vehicles into the political accounts of major parties.

## Interest from political investment funds would not count toward donation ceilings

The Australian government introduced amendments to federal electoral funding laws on Tuesday afternoon, and independent MPs said the changes favour major parties. The government calls the amendments “technical” and intends to pass the bill in both houses during the current sitting fortnight, weeks before the incoming electoral laws take effect on 1 January, 2027.

From January, political parties and candidates will face campaign spending limits and donation caps during federal elections. Disclosure thresholds for political gifts and donations will be lowered to $5,000, and public funding per vote will increase from $3.35 to $5. The amendments would also allow political parties to invest and grow their funds without the interest earned contributing to donation ceilings.

The bill’s explanatory statement said, “The amendments would enable a federal account holder to continue to grow their capital in a competitive manner and when the federal account funds are not required for incurring electoral expenditure,” and added that federal account holders would gain “a greater degree of flexibility to administer their financial assets as they wish.” It also said, “This would allow federal account holders a greater degree of flexibility to administer their financial assets as they wish, protecting the right to take part in the conduct of public affairs directly.”

Major parties often set up investment arms to grow funds in time for election cycles. The Guardian reported that the Cormack Foundation in Victoria is a major financial backer for the Liberals, while Labor Services and Holding and Pilliwinks serve as the equivalents for Labor and the Nationals, respectively. The Guardian reported that in 2024-25, Cormack donated $4.7m to the Liberals, while Labor Services and Holding donated $598,00.

Independent parliamentarians have long criticized the overhaul as favouring major parties and incumbents. Their latest objections focus on whether interest earned by parties’ investment vehicles should count toward donation ceilings.

David Pocock, an independent ACT senator, said the bill continues the “major party stitch up” and enabled “an uncapped amount of money to flow from their huge investment vehicles into their political accounts.” He added, “They like to talk about taking money out of politics but they legislate to drive more in.”

Pocock also said the government was prioritising law changes that would “line their own coffers” while failing to bring forward overdue measures, including banning deepfakes in elections and legislating truth in political advertising.

Kate Chaney, the independent MP for Curtin, said the changes did not address the bill’s serious flaws and instead included tweaks to protect the existing investment vehicles and assets of major parties. She said, “Rather than being a new act of bastardry, this bill tweaks the act of bastardry performed on Australian democracy last year.” Chaney also said, “We don’t let Coles and Woolies make laws about supermarket competition, but this is what it looks like when major parties make laws about who can compete with them.”

The electoral overhaul is already being challenged in the High Court by former parliamentarians Zoe Daniel and Rex Patrick on three grounds. One ground concerns the $800,000 cap on spending in each electorate, which they argue impinges on the implied right of political communication. Although the cap applies to all candidates, the former independents say newcomers are disadvantaged because major parties can also tap the national campaign budget, which is capped at $90m.

Daniel and Patrick also argue that a $50,000 cap on individual donations, aimed at preventing Clive Palmer-sized campaigns, unfairly disadvantages candidates without political parties. They further challenge a rule stopping a single donor, such as Climate 200, from giving to more than five candidates in a state or territory.

The pair said the latest amendments were not about getting big money out of politics but about stopping competitive newcomers. They said the changes would entrench access to wealth for the ALP and the LNP through the enormous investment vehicles that only they have, and that no new entrant could amass such wealth under the new laws. “They are pulling up the drawbridge, and it is transparent to anyone who cares to look. It is classic cartel behaviour akin to Coles and Woolies land-banking to prevent competition from newcomers,” Daniel and Patrick said.
