## Order requires federal officers to assess whether a person is a flight risk

Chief U.S. District Court Judge Brian Morris on Wednesday ordered federal authorities to stop making warrantless immigration arrests in Montana unless officers first show that a person is a flight risk likely to flee before the government can obtain a warrant. His order applies to officers from the Department of Homeland Security, Immigration and Customs Enforcement, and Customs and Border Protection in the District of Montana. The Department of Homeland Security did not respond by press time on whether its officers would comply.

Morris said the government has an interest in enforcing immigration law but must follow the law. “Defendants repeatedly have shown they fail to abide by these requirements while making arrests in the District of Montana,” the order said. When an officer makes an arrest without a warrant, the government must show that the person is a flight risk likely to run away before officials can obtain a warrant. The order stops federal authorities from continuing the practice without a court order, at least for now.

The ruling followed a complaint filed by Upper Seven Law on behalf of Jesus Verde Hernandez, three other named plaintiffs and other people in similar circumstances. Upper Seven Law said it represented a similar class of people in *Hernandez v. Crosson*, which also contests illegal immigration enforcement. The law firm said it has secured the release of 71 individuals in wrongful immigration arrests since it started that work.

In a news release about the order, Upper Seven Law said federal immigration officials had arrested people in Montana without probable cause for 18 months. The firm said arrests occurred at gas stations, work sites and traffic stops, in some cases only because officers determined the individuals spoke Spanish.

Upper Seven Law said the plaintiffs have included an 18-year-old who was on a trip with friends, a 9-year-old arrested with her father and multiple families with minor children. Upper Seven Law said the arrests upended lives and prevented children from attending school. The law firm also said some people were held in cells for 24 hours a day, with no ability to go outside.

Morris said federal authorities started “pushing for mass immigration arrests” in January 2025, “resulting in a policy that violates” the Immigration and Nationality Act. His order said courts around the country had concluded that the policy of arresting people without first assessing their flight risk remained unlawful. Upper Seven Law said the court found the warrantless arrests illegal in August and that courts across the country reached the same conclusion in November, but federal authorities persisted.

The court found that federal authorities used the arrests to try to coerce people into voluntarily deporting and would continue doing so even when the people had meritorious claims for release. The court said the process offered no opportunity to speak with a lawyer and relied on misinformation about the immigration process.

The court cited the Fourth Amendment, which prohibits unreasonable searches and seizures. Morris said the protection applies to citizens and non-citizens. He said non-citizens receive constitutional protections after they have come within the territory of the United States and developed substantial connections to the country.

“The court has repeatedly told federal officials that their conduct is illegal, but the government continues to violate the law,” said Molly Danahy, Upper Seven Law's litigation director and counsel for the plaintiff class, in a statement. “This injunction is absolutely necessary to stop these unlawful policies and practices.”

Morris granted a preliminary injunction, described in the order as a temporary stop to federal government action. He said the injunction was appropriate at this stage of the litigation and that the plaintiffs had established the required elements to stop the government's action.
