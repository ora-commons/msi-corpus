## Mission of Love reports rising need as benefit requirements expand

Lisa Reese retired at 62 to care for her family in Capitol Heights, Maryland, then found that $63 in monthly federal food aid came with an 80-hour work, training or volunteer requirement. The obligation came from changes President Trump signed last year in the One Big Beautiful Bill, which expanded work rules for adults ages 55 to 64. Reese, who spent 25 years at Marriott, now volunteers at Mission of Love Charities to meet the requirement.

Reese’s retirement did not follow a period without work. She said she had spent nearly four decades working — at a liquor store, cleaning apartments and in hospitality — without receiving government assistance. Her 25 years at Marriott included work at the front desk, in room service and at events. “There was no sitting down … I was tired!” she said. Though tired, she chose retirement because she was needed at home.

Her 80-year-old mother is in senior living, while Reese shares a house with her uncle, who had surgery for liver cancer, and helps care for her daughter and 2-year-old granddaughter. Her daughter also receives SNAP but is exempt because her child is young, though she works in childcare. A waiver for people caring for someone incapacitated did not cover Reese’s uncle. “Daycare was expensive,” Reese said. “So I decided to retire to take care of everybody. I don’t know if that’s crazy.”

After leaving her job, Reese said, living mainly on Social Security was tough. Prices seemed to rise this year, especially the gas bill. “One of my bills was $500. Five hundred!” she said. She scrimped, followed a tight budget and visited a food bank, but she said it was not enough. She applied for SNAP and was approved this summer for a small benefit. The approval carried the requirement to work, train or volunteer for 80 hours each month.

Those conditions came through broader changes and cuts to SNAP and Medicaid. The legislation signed by President Trump last year reduced funding and expanded work requirements, including for people ages 55 to 64. The left-leaning Center for Budget and Policy Priorities estimated that some 5 million people nationwide have lost federal food benefits since the changes. Newly empowered Democrats want to roll back the provisions, while Trump administration officials have said repeatedly that the changes are meant to address waste, fraud and abuse.

Mission of Love Charities, which serves people who are homeless or low-income, has been swamped by increased need. The nonprofit reduced food pantry visits from once a week to every other week and hired a third case worker to help people sign up for SNAP and Medicaid and connect with housing and jobs. Chief executive Adenia Bradley said, “We see it every day, people get cut off” from food aid. She said red tape, small errors and the new work rules can each cause a loss of benefits.

Maryland has tried to make SNAP enrollment and renewal less complicated. The state created one application for multiple benefits that works on a smartphone and made eligibility verification and renewal easier and quicker. Enrollment has declined less sharply in Maryland than in some other states. When officials notify recipients that aid will end, they connect them with a job assistance center. Case worker Brooke Wright said, “They say that you have this appointment at this time. If you need to reschedule, call this number.”

The labor market remains difficult for some people seeking to satisfy the requirement. “One of our volunteers, she did three work applications already and she cannot find a job yet,” said Mission of Love case worker Jatmna Cabrera.

Bradley has allowed a few people to meet the work requirement by volunteering at the charity, including Reese. On a recent day, Reese filled bags with food for people who would arrive later to collect groceries. “Snacks, water, drinks and they get two sandwiches,” she said. Her four-hour shift is in the morning, allowing her to watch her granddaughter in the afternoons. Reese said she enjoys helping and feels better when people wave and say, “Hey, Miss Mission of Love!”

Bradley said the charity can accommodate only a limited number of volunteers. She said it is researching other places where people can volunteer so the organization can provide referrals, and she expects the need to continue growing.

Bradley said the expanded rules now apply to able-bodied older adults, parents of adolescents, veterans, homeless people and some noncitizens, including refugees. She favors case-by-case assessments and said, “I’m always for a case-by-case basis, but what they’re doing now is lumping them all in one pot.” She said a blanket work rule does not make sense for older people and that some recipients are being punished for receiving help the government said they qualified for. “They don’t deserve that,” she said.

The Department of Agriculture, which oversees SNAP, disputed that characterization. In a statement, the department said, “an expectation to work, train, or volunteer for 20 hours per week, as few as four hours per day, is not punishment. Industriousness and contribution to community should be applauded, not used as scare tactics.” The department also said, “Democrats and their pervasive advocacy arm have never met a welfare program they don’t want to bloat, overfund, or exploit.”

White House spokeswoman Allison Schuster said President Trump’s larger approach to addressing waste, fraud and abuse “has brought down costs significantly.” On SNAP, she said, “the program’s mission to be temporary help for those in tough times was a total failure, but President Trump’s historic legislation began the process to halt fraud and ensure that it only serves eligible Americans who need it most.”

Reese agreed that SNAP use should be temporary for some people. “The ones that’s been on it for 10, 15, 20 years, yeah, they do need to get off of it. It’s hurting folks like me,” she said. “I’m sorry if that sounds cruel. But go after them, not the retirees.”

Reese also faces new Medicaid work rules next year. She has been on Medicaid since retiring and doubts she can continue volunteering 80 hours a month because of her health. “I have arthritis in my hand real bad. My thighs hurt so bad, and sciatica, high blood pressure, you name it,” she said. She and her daughter are researching private health plans they could split the cost of if Reese finds part-time work. She expects to cover the additional cost only until she turns 65, when she plans to retire fully and spend more time caring for her family.
