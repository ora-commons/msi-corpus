## Ectopic pregnancy deaths rise sharply in states with abortion bans

## Federal and state officials had not publicly identified the trend

ProPublica's analysis of mortality data collected by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention from death certificates found that almost 200 women in the United States died after ectopic pregnancies between 2020 and 2025, nearly twice the number in the previous six-year period. In 2023-2025, the national ectopic-pregnancy death rate stood at 9.8 deaths per million live births, below 13.1 in states with strict abortion bans and above 7.1 in states without bans. The increase was particularly stark in states that had banned abortion.

The CDC publishes totals based on variables including location, demographics and cause of death, and the dataset is commonly used by journalists and academics studying mortality trends. ProPublica's analysis focused on pregnancy-related deaths at the national level.

ProPublica said that, as far as it could tell, the fact that the number of women dying from ectopic pregnancies had nearly doubled after abortion bans came into effect had not been reported before. More than a dozen leaders in maternal health were unaware of the spike when the newsroom confirmed the finding, and neither state governments nor the federal government had publicly identified the trend in the CDC data.

ProPublica's analysis put the ectopic pregnancy death rate in 2023-2025 at 13.1 deaths per million live births in states with a strict abortion ban. States without a ban recorded 7.1 deaths per million over the same period, while the national rate stood at 9.8.

Ectopic pregnancies occur when an embryo fails to implant properly in the uterus and develops elsewhere, most commonly inside a fallopian tube. The pregnancy grows until it causes the organ to rupture and triggers catastrophic bleeding. ProPublica described the condition as rare and estimated that it affects up to 2% of pregnancies.

Dr. Alice Abernathy, an OB-GYN in Philadelphia, said a death from an ectopic pregnancy should be a "never event." Kyleigh Thurman, whose right fallopian tube ruptured after she struggled to get ectopic care in Texas in 2023, said, "I genuinely thought I was going to die."

Leitaea Lowrimore of Oklahoma said in an ongoing lawsuit challenging an abortion ban that she was denied treatment for an ectopic pregnancy at multiple hospitals in February. "There were a few times I asked my husband if I was going to die," she said. "I kept thinking about our kids."

Even before ectopic pregnancy deaths began rising, the condition had been noted as the leading cause of death in the first trimester of pregnancy. It has received little research attention. The CDC does not track how many ectopic pregnancies occur each year or require healthcare providers to report them, ProPublica reported. The most recent nationwide study the newsroom could find examined data only through 2013.

The United States does not conduct national maternal mortality reviews the way the United Kingdom does. The United Kingdom responded to a similar spike in the years after the COVID-19 pandemic by making changes to emergency services and promoting awareness of ectopic pregnancy symptoms. In the United States, much of the work of investigating pregnancy-related deaths is left to state committees. The small number of ectopic pregnancy deaths in individual states and wide variation can obscure a trend.

After publishing its investigation, ProPublica contacted maternal mortality review committees in states with abortion bans. Many of the committees said they had not identified an increase in ectopic deaths and had no plans to examine the issue further despite the newsroom's findings. ProPublica said the near-doubling was visible only at the national level.

ProPublica asked the CDC what the agency had done to investigate or address the increase. The CDC has epidemiologists who review death and birth records and has in the past conducted its own investigations and analyses. A Department of Health and Human Services spokesperson did not provide a direct answer but said the agency continues to track maternal mortality trends, investigate their causes and provide funding for research. ProPublica said it remained unclear why the CDC and other federal agencies across two administrations had not publicly responded despite access to the same information.

Maternal health experts said the government's capacity to respond to the trend had been significantly reduced by staffing cuts initiated by the Trump administration since the beginning of last year. The CDC's Division of Reproductive Health, for example, lost most of its 100 employees, according to a lawsuit filed by more than a dozen states' attorneys general last year.

ProPublica's analysis cannot establish whether abortion bans caused the worse outcomes in states that have them. Experts offered no consensus on why the death rates are rising, and ProPublica said there was no evidence officials were stepping up to examine the question or offer solutions. The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists has not provided specific guidance to doctors on how to adequately treat women with suspected ectopic pregnancies in states with abortion bans, ProPublica reported.

ProPublica said the lack of awareness surrounding ectopic pregnancy deaths was imperiling women's lives. Maternal health experts called the trend unacceptable and said it required a response.

For years, ProPublica has examined maternal health and the ongoing impacts of abortion bans since the Supreme Court overturned Roe v. Wade. Data analysis has been central to that effort, offering a quantitative view of an area that has long been underresearched, especially as studying the impact of abortion bans has become politicized. Last year, the newsroom reported a dramatic rise in sepsis rates after Texas instituted its abortion ban, which carries the strictest penalties for healthcare providers. It also found a spike in emergency-room blood transfusions for first-trimester miscarriage in Texas.

Courtney Schreiber, a professor of obstetrics and gynecology, said ProPublica's work "raises concern that young women are dying from a preventable cause at an increasing rate. That is not what we should be seeing in the United States of America." ProPublica plans to continue reporting on maternal health and the impact of abortion bans for the foreseeable future. Andrea Suozzo, Sophie Chou and Kavitha Surana contributed reporting.
