## Chalabi says family wealth and class identity shape economic security

Chalabi said the book's title was deliberate. "It's called 10 Lives because I specifically didn't just want to focus on the 1%," she said. "Instead I wanted to talk about how the 99% of us aren't all in it together, and the kind of power differentials between us."

Her concern was not limited to people with low incomes. "Across the board, everyone just seemed to be expressing this feeling of panic that I think is really, really particular to U.S. capitalism, which is an incredibly precarious and cruel system where you are one crisis away from experiencing a real economic shock," she said.

Many of the people Chalabi profiled considered themselves middle class regardless of their actual income. She said the disconnect between actual wealth and a sense of class has consequences for how people see themselves and how they judge everybody else.

Chalabi linked that disconnect to the obstacles people face in poverty. "To be poor in this country is to encounter an enormous, enormous array of obstacles, including assumptions about your intelligence [and] your educational level," she said. "And so it's actually quite rational sometimes for people to try to present themselves as middle class, maybe even to try and signal wealth with the clothes that they buy or the cars that they drive. I don't think it's simply aspirational. I also think it is them trying to avoid the discrimination that they might face if their real income level were disclosed."

Understanding financial security, Chalabi said, requires knowing whom people can turn to when times become difficult. She cautioned that a household is an imperfect measure because people may live with spouses they cannot necessarily lean on. "But one of the things that came out time and time and time again from the book is how much money your parents have is so profound in shaping your economic security," she said.

She also said class identity extends beyond a paycheck or bank balance. "Class is about more than the number that is printed on your pay slip or the number that's in your bank balance," she said. Chalabi tied class identities to childhood households and parents' professions. "Most people who are born in poverty in this country do not make it out," she said.

Who people date and how much money they inherit are probably two of the largest factors affecting inequality in the country, Chalabi said. She described a TikTok trend in which people talked about looking for a partner who was 6 feet 5 inches tall, had blue eyes and worked in finance, while noting that none had been any partner of hers. Chalabi grew up with a model of feminism centered on supporting herself rather than relying on a man. She also described a generational understanding of romance as post-materialist, under which people were not supposed to discuss wanting a partner from particular economic circumstances.

Chalabi cited a study published by the National Bureau for Economic Research that compared 1960 with 2005. She said the study found that people were not necessarily seeking partners in the same income bracket, but they often sought partners with a similar educational background. Because education has a huge impact on income, Chalabi said, the trend toward educational similarity strengthened over that period and people are now more likely to date someone with a similar educational background.

Chalabi also confronted the financial asymmetry of interviewing people about sensitive subjects. She said she felt her subjects had a right to understand her economic privilege — including her large book advance — and her vantage point as she asked them deeply personal questions. Journalists typically do not pay people for their time, she said, but she described her interviews as a very different kind of equation, because she was asking people to share vulnerable parts of their lives and was profiting from their stories.

At the start of every interview, Chalabi told participants she would pay $40 per hour for their time and invited them to discuss the arrangement if it felt uncomfortable. She also told them they had the right to ask her the same questions she asked them, including how much money she made.

Chalabi said her own accent carries class signals, too. "The accent thing is huge," she said. People in the U.K. could often infer her class from her voice, she said, while Americans do not really understand the nuances of a British accent, so as an immigrant she is simply read as "posh" in the U.S. She said the accent conferred "huge class benefits" by counterbalancing some obstacles she might have faced because of race and gender. Others assumed she was educated and cultured, she said, while acknowledging that she was not sure how true those assumptions were. She nevertheless said the perceived status benefited her in a corporate environment.

Taken together, Chalabi's profiles return to the question that opened the project — how much money it takes to feel financially secure — and treat the answer, in her telling, as more than an income figure: she ties economic security to family resources, education, inheritance, relationships and class perception. Her central observation is that financial worry persists across the economic spectrum, even among those who earn plenty of money.
