## House and Senate control brought relief and concern worldwide

The Guardian's reader responses showed an outcome that was celebrated but not treated as an endpoint. The roundup gathered reactions from readers around the world to Tuesday night's election results and did not present them as a representative poll. Barbara Burkart, a 75-year-old psychotherapist in Massachusetts, said she was "equal parts ecstatic and relieved" and felt she could "breathe again." She said "The guardrails are back, to some extent" and described people around the country waking up to "the greed, cruelty and hubris that have marked Trump's presidency." She pointed to the cost of his "impulsive war on Iran," his cozying up to tech billionaires and their drive for AI data centers, the loss of health insurance and the rounding up of immigrants. Burkart hoped Democrats would "unify and move forward, taking this mandate seriously."

The roundup followed what The Guardian described as a historic Democratic victory, with readers saying Democrats had "taken back the House and Senate." A Guardian photograph showed supporters at a midterm election-night watch party for Texas Democratic Senate candidate James Talarico in Austin on November 3, credited to Brandon Bell/Getty Images.

One reader, who said they lived in Los Angeles for more than 10 years while attending the University of Southern California and that half their family currently lives in the United States, called the results "a seismic shift" and "a tidal wave of public sentiment" and "a powerful reminder that democracy ultimately belongs to the people." The reader said they were "genuinely joyous" watching the historic results unfold and that, against the backdrop of a "deeply polarizing, tough and self-centered presidency," voters sent a message that cannot be ignored: leadership must be accountable, power must have limits and arrogance must never replace public service.

Jayjay Epega, an entertainment publicist in London, said the results meant Michigan had made history with Abdul El-Sayed becoming "America's first Muslim senator," Texas had delivered James Talarico's defeat of Ken Paxton, and Georgia had elected Keisha Lance Bottoms as "America's first Black female governor." Epega said the wins were not simply electoral victories but represented changing political attitudes, greater representation and the determination of citizens to make their voices heard, and that the Democrats' victory in both chambers of Congress added another historic dimension — "This is democracy speaking loudly."

Pamela, who gave only her first name and identified herself as a reader from Virginia, said she voted Democratic across the board and was "thrilled, scared but hopeful" that solid changes would follow. She said she had moved past a centrist-to-left position and wanted younger, much more diverse people in national leadership, a diversity she said extended in many ways beyond race. She worried that "status quo liberals" would need to work more closely with progressives, said she did not expect conservatives to follow the rules or "go quietly," and was trying not to be cynical. Her greatest hope, she said, was that the country would start "digging in and sowing some seeds of love and change" and that newly elected leaders would find creative ways to govern, though she was not entirely convinced that could happen given what she called the country's "puritanical, corrupt and greedy roots."

S, a reader from Salt Lake City, Utah, described Utah as one of the reddest states and said they had been happy with its Republican governor and Senator John Curtis but did not like Senator Mike Lee. S said only one party appeared viable for national office in this election and hoped Democrats would undo every single thing the GOP had done over the previous two years, stabilize support for Ukraine, impeach a number of officials for the good of the nation and the world and bring economic policy back to sanity — though they doubted that was within Congress's capabilities — while still hoping some international relationships could begin to be repaired. Domestically, S wanted to see ICE off American streets, said they would not mind if the agency's immigration enforcement arm were entirely disbanded, wanted tariffs dropped and called for more skepticism toward AI, regulation of phone and social media use among young people, a revived Green New Deal and a shift from coal toward electric power. S also argued that electric was the technology of the future and that China was "eating our lunch" in the domain.

David Roy, executive director of the NGO Workshops for Biodiversity in Canada, said it would be good to have "a bit of our southern neighbor back" after two years in which it had often felt like Canada was in a political standoff with the United States. He said Canadian leadership had seemed willing in response to sacrifice environmental protections and climate commitments in pursuit of new economic partners and trade opportunities. Roy hoped the election would open the door to a more constructive relationship without losing sight of the importance of protecting nature and addressing climate change.

Crista, 61, a restaurant manager in Arkansas, said she was an independent who voted Democratic across the slate. She said she valued democracy and rule of law, while accusing the Trump administration of subverting the rule of law, starting an unnecessary war, worsening the economy and fraying international alliances that protect and defend the Western world. Elated by the results, she noted that Arkansas remained "ruby red" but was closer than in the past 20 years. She wanted oversight and accountability as well as investigations into corruption, but warned that Democrats could become too complacent and fail to do what voters had elected them to do.

Monica DeLaTorre, 64, an educator in New Mexico, said she had feared people would not turn out to vote, though positive messages from key candidates across the country had given her hope. She wanted Democrats to focus less on finger-pointing and investigations and more on stopping "military overreach" and securing food, gas and housing for average Americans. She said she hoped the country would return to kindness and quality of life as its measures of success. DeLaTorre said her biggest concern was that big dark money and the electoral college would be forgotten, and she wanted to "get rid of big dark money and also the electoral college in politics" because both make the public's voice harder to hear.

A reader who did not identify themselves said the election was not a vote of confidence for Democrats but "a vote of no confidence for Trump," meaning the party had to earn back the confidence Trump had lost. The reader hoped newly elected left-wing Democrats would keep moving the Overton window leftward, make left-wing economic, social and human-rights-centered foreign policy more viable and reduce the chances of another Trump administration. The reader said Democratic control of Congress would surely do much to rein in Trump's "dangerous politics," though to what degree remained to be seen, and hoped Democrats could force Trump's hand in bringing about peace in the Middle East, give Ukraine more support — support the reader said Trump had cut back on — and produce concrete results with Russia at the negotiating table.

Pietari Wishart, 18, a student in Finland, said they were "slightly afraid" European policymakers might return to "business as usual" if a "blue shock" allowed "Uncle Sam" to get his act back together and return to the usual level of international participation and soft power. Wishart framed the risk in terms of what renewed American engagement could mean for European policymaking.

Joep de Vries, a test engineer in the Netherlands, said he had followed US politics for years because of its enormous impact on the rest of the world. He noted that many commentators called the results a landslide and a strong rebuke of Trump and the Republican Party, but said almost half of voters were still happy to vote for the people in charge. De Vries said that left him with only "a sliver of hope" because America, once seen as a pillar of democracy, had shown how easily that could be undermined. He said he was nonetheless hopeful that change had proved possible in so many states. De Vries said Europe had to learn to manage with less reliance on the United States and that the Democrats needed a plan beyond saying they wanted to save democracy.

Emma Carroll, 24, an MEP engineer in Manchester, New Hampshire, said she was thrilled by the number of women elected, felt more represented and craved the change she felt women could properly execute. She hoped America would continue to wake up and recognize the choice before it, and wanted Americans to take better care of themselves, their loved ones and the planet — "Enough is enough." Carroll said every party has its downsides and hoped intelligent, empathetic people would gain more power and "right the ship." She called less polarization a must, said Americans needed to talk to each other rather than "hate everyone on the other side of the line," and urged Democrats and Republicans to understand each other's needs and compromise like a family.

Vusi, a self-employed reader in South Africa, called the Democratic wins "incredibly good news." Vusi said the incumbent administration had operated with "unfettered power and no accountability" and with disastrous implications for the world. Vusi hoped that sanity, normality, pragmatism and decorum could be restored to a once-esteemed office, now widely maligned and ridiculed, and noted the recurring commentary asking "where is Congress?" From outside the United States, Vusi said, only the courts had appeared able to curtail what looked increasingly like a slide from democracy into authoritarianism. Vusi said the results might bring back an era of accountability and democracy in the United States, with implications for the rest of the world, and mark the end of foreign policy decided on a whim or with personal interests at the forefront.

Victoria, an author in London who moved to the United Kingdom while Barack Obama was president and has watched the decade since from afar, recalled that her husband found Trump's first candidacy announcement amusing, but said her Texas upbringing taught her how easily politicians could use the demonization of others to gain power. Victoria stayed up for the results despite not intending to and described the outcome as "a good day" but only "a momentary reprieve." Victoria said years of attacks on democratic institutions, human rights and the rule of law had consequences around the world, particularly in the global south, and that the results did not undo the damage. Victoria worried the victories had been driven largely by concerns about the cost of living rather than a rejection of wider injustices, and said a lot of accountability remained needed. Victoria wanted a progressive agenda connecting healthcare and human rights to everyday economic concerns, called the result a step in the right direction but not "a silver bullet," and warned that the Democrats had squandered opportunities before.

Across the responses, the result was repeatedly paired with conditions. Readers called on Democratic control to deliver oversight, accountability and investigations into corruption, economic relief such as food, gas and housing for average Americans, climate and AI policy changes, an end to big dark money and the electoral college, and the removal of ICE from American streets. Other responses raised foreign-policy priorities involving Canada, Ukraine, Russia and the Middle East. Burkart treated the victory as a mandate, while Victoria called it a "momentary reprieve," Wishart warned about a return to "business as usual" and de Vries felt only "a sliver of hope." The submission-based roundup shows the hopes and anxieties surrounding the result without establishing how common each view was.
