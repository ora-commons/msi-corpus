## States lack guidance on Medicaid work-rule exemptions for Native Americans

- The Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services has not given states a standardized method for identifying Native Americans who are exempt from new Medicaid work requirements, two months before the Jan. 1 federal deadline.
- The One Big Beautiful Bill Act excludes Native Americans from the work requirements, but CMS guidance issued in June left proof standards to states, which can set thresholds higher than federal requirements.
- KFF Health News asked 14 states how they would apply the exemption and found that the states plan to verify tribal citizenship in different ways.
- An estimated 850,000 Native Americans lost coverage by May 2024 during the post-pandemic eligibility unwinding, often for paperwork reasons.
- About 87% of Native people live outside tribal statistical areas such as reservations, according to 2020 Census Bureau data.

## States set their own proof standards for tribal citizenship

Native Americans are exempt from the Medicaid work requirements that take effect under the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, but states are building their own systems to prove who qualifies — and federal regulators have not yet told them how.

Two months before the Jan. 1 deadline to implement the requirements, states had yet to receive detailed guidance from federal health officials on how to exclude Native Americans from the rules, leading to a patchwork of eligibility checks that pose challenges for qualified enrollees trying to keep their coverage. The law excluded Native Americans from having to meet the work requirements to keep Medicaid eligibility, but the rules issued by the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services didn't provide a standardized system to help states identify who should be excluded.

CMS published its guidance on work requirements in June, instructing states to use data they already have before potentially asking Native enrollees for additional records. But the rule didn't set a standard process for states to prove Native American status, allowing states to raise the threshold for proof higher than federal requirements. The rule also didn't require states to share data with tribes, coordinate with Native health providers, or advise on managed-care plans. Managed-care programs contract with the state to provide Medicaid benefits and connect patients to providers when they need care.

In July, a tribal advisory group for CMS recommended that the agency require states to allow Native Americans to self-attest to their eligibility as the standard proof. The group also wanted CMS to require states to share Medicaid eligibility data with tribal health programs to catch when tribal citizens are mistakenly kicked off Medicaid. The National Council of Urban Indian Health pushed for similar guidance.

Native leaders expect additional guidance to come, said A.C. Locklear, CEO of the National Indian Health Board, which advocates for federally recognized tribes. But Locklear said that if CMS waits until December to issue more specific guidance on the exemption, states will have already determined their own processes. CMS did not respond to KFF Health News' questions about whether it planned to provide more guidance.

"There's 30-plus states who are independently repurposing their administrative processes for this, and they really all happen in a vacuum," Locklear said. "That's why it's really important for CMS to provide guidance."

KFF Health News asked 14 states how they would apply the exemption and found that they plan to ask tribal citizens to prove their status in different ways. Arizona, Colorado, Montana, Nevada, New Mexico and Washington plan to let enrollees identify their tribal status on their Medicaid application forms. The Arizona Health Care Cost Containment System, the state's Medicaid agency, will continue to allow tribal members to self-identify, then confirm their status through state data on services received through the Indian Health Service or tribally run health centers.

South Dakota linked its claims system to identify people served by the Indian Health Service, according to the state Department of Social Services. Oklahoma relies on a data-sharing agreement with tribes dating to 2022, according to Cherokee Nation Health Services. North Carolina requires a tribal enrollment card, a tribal census record, or confirmation from the Eastern Band of Cherokee Indians' Tribal Option managed-care program.

"There's still going to be folks that fall through the cracks," said Locklear, a citizen of the Lumbee Tribe of North Carolina.

In North Carolina, 3,400 Native Medicaid enrollees are verified as eligible for the exemption from the work requirement, health department spokesperson Summer Tonizzo said, and those enrollees will be automatically exempted. But the state doesn't know how many more people might be eligible for an exemption through tribal citizenship. Most states responding to KFF Health News didn't say how many Native enrollees they had verified as exempt, and several said they don't track tribal affiliation, noting that ethnicity questions on applications are optional. In a comment on CMS' guidance, the National Council of Urban Indian Health warned that "not all states collect data on their American Indian and Alaska Native residents."

Native Americans who don't live in states where their tribes are based may face added difficulty. About 87% of Native people live outside of what the U.S. Census Bureau calls tribal statistical areas, such as reservations, according to 2020 data. A Navajo Nation citizen living in Connecticut, for example, may have a difficult time being identified by the state because it doesn't have a close working relationship with the tribe based in the southwestern U.S., said Liz Malerba, director of policy and legislative affairs for the United South and Eastern Tribes. Malerba is a citizen of the Mohegan Tribe in Connecticut.

The Eastern Band of Cherokee Indians, based in the mountains of western North Carolina, has spent five years building its managed-care program, which is the only tribal-run Medicaid managed-care program in the nation, said Gwynneth Wildcatt, a director with the Cherokee Indian Hospital Authority who sits on North Carolina's Medicaid Beneficiary Advisory Council. All Medicaid enrollees in the tribe who need exemptions have them, thanks to that program, Wildcatt said, because a close connection with the state has made verifying its citizens for work requirement exemptions simple — the program is already integrated into the state Medicaid system. Wildcatt is a citizen of the tribe. Work rules that begin in 2027 could risk health coverage for Native Medicaid enrollees in North Carolina, say Wildcatt and James Burns, leaders of the Eastern Band's health system.

James Burns, who oversees the Eastern Band's Medicaid managed-care program in the Qualla Boundary, the tribe's sovereign land in western North Carolina, said he fears a flawed rollout. "I'm nervous. I'm extremely nervous," Burns said. "When you look at any state that's rolled out Medicaid work requirements, the unintended consequences have been vast, and that's what we don't want to happen."

Montana, which has one of the highest percentages of American Indian and Alaska Native residents in the nation, began work requirement checks in July. It is one of four states, alongside Nebraska, Arkansas and Georgia, scanning ahead of the Jan. 1 federal deadline; Iowa begins its program on Dec. 1. Native people who self-declare their tribal status for Montana's work requirements won't be required to provide additional proof, said Jon Ebelt, a spokesperson for the state Department of Public Health and Human Services.

But Native leaders said Montana officials hadn't clearly communicated how Medicaid enrollees could prove their Native status when those checks began. Montana saw one of the nation's sharpest declines in enrollment during the Medicaid unwinding as people struggled to navigate a backlogged system, and most enrollees who lost coverage did so because of paperwork problems, which rippled through Native communities. Jason Smith, a citizen of the Confederated Salish and Kootenai Tribes who leads the Montana Consortium for Urban Indian Health, said clinics serving Indigenous patients are still working to reenroll members who lost Medicaid during that time.

Smith said the state has done a good job addressing tribal priorities while implementing work requirements, but he remains concerned about whether its system is ready for the workload of the new mandates. As of September, Montana still hadn't hired the number of workers officials estimated are needed, and federal data shows Montanans can face long waits on the state's helpline. "Have they improved their communication and outreach?" Smith said. "Will people be there answering phones? It will take time to see."

Native leaders fear the limited guidance will mean a repeat of the Medicaid "unwinding" in 2023, when states resumed checking qualifications for eligibility after a pandemic-era pause. An estimated 850,000 Native Americans lost coverage by May 2024, often for paperwork reasons. The latest carve-out for American Indians and Alaska Natives also left out some Indigenous people, such as Native Hawaiians.
