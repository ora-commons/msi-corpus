## New technology overloaded old equipment, officials say

FlightAware, a website that tracks flights, said more than 4,100 flights had been canceled, and Cirium, a similar site, put that number above 3,200 flights. Hundreds of thousands of passengers were stranded. Trump administration officials said new technology being added to the nation's air traffic control system overloaded antiquated equipment and caused "intermittent" communications and radar gaps, leading to the flight shutdown of more than four hours.

Airlines across the United States worked on Saturday to recover from the longest nationwide ground stop in 25 years, which canceled thousands of flights, spread ripple effects around the world and forced pro and college sports teams to scramble for alternate transportation.

The nationwide stop was the first since January 2023, when another key Federal Aviation Administration computer system that sends notices to pilots malfunctioned, and the second since the Sept. 11, 2001, attacks.

Andrew Charlton, an aviation consultant, said Friday's stoppage affected about a quarter of the 45,000 flights in the U.S. each day.

"The knock-on effect of that is incredible," said Charlton, managing director of Aviation Advocacy. "The aircraft doesn't just fly from A to B and back to B and then stop. It then moves onto C and then over to E and around and around and around. It is, of course, exacerbated if you're talking about long-haul flights, international flights over the Atlantic or over the Pacific."

The problem was due to updating a system that can never shut down, Charlton said. "What you're watching here is trying to change the tires of your car when the car's going at 80 miles an hour down the expressway, because nothing stops in aviation," Charlton said. "It's a 24/7 operation. And to make changes on the fly, if you'll pardon that pun, is incredibly complex and dangerous."

At London's Heathrow Airport, several flights to and from the U.S. were listed as delayed or canceled Saturday. "While U.S. flights have resumed, there may be some follow-on disruption as airlines work to rebook affected passengers and get all services back to normal," said Tim Johnson, consumer director at the U.K. Civil Aviation Authority.

Sports teams scheduled to play this weekend adjusted game start times, searched for hotel rooms, rescheduled flights and chartered buses. No. 19 Mississippi's game at Oklahoma was delayed a full day to Sunday. Start times for some other games were pushed back hours.

At airports, passengers stared at screens showing delays or rushed to find buses or trains to their destinations. Emily Brown and her 14-year-old daughter waited more than four hours Friday at the Dallas airport for their flight to Columbus, Ohio, before giving up. They were traveling to surprise her daughter's best friend. Instead, she had to settle for phoning the friend through tears in the airport, Brown said.

"She saved up her money to buy her own ticket, and she's planned this whole weekend," Brown said. "Now we're not sure when we're gonna be able to go."

Corbyn Wascher's flight was on the tarmac at the Charlotte, N.C., airport for four hours Friday on his way home to Moline, Illinois, from a conference in Miami. The 37-year-old high school teacher paid $100 to take an Uber to a hotel 25 minutes from the airport after he was unable to find a flight until Saturday afternoon to Chicago.

A Southwest Airlines gate agent greeted passengers at Dallas Love Field at dawn Saturday with, "Good morning, good morning," then repeatedly said all flights were full and asked passengers to have boarding passes ready to expedite the process and help keep flights moving.

Mullin told reporters at the White House that no one was in any danger as more than 3,000 flights in the air at the time of the grounding order landed safely.

Duffy said planes were taking flight and landing Saturday, but the effects could be felt for days. Officials say the system will likely return to normal in time to handle the Thanksgiving travel rush. Duffy blamed Congress for failing to approve more funding for new technology, and administration officials have said the effort to implement new fixes overtaxed the system. He suggested more disruptions might occur.

"We didn't think we would have this kind of outage. We did," he said. "We were able to review what went wrong, and then reboot the system and go. But, you will have more of these."

Duffy said the failure involved old equipment and was not linked to the FAA's new AI-powered system, which the agency is rolling out to help predict problems in the sky.

The FAA already replaced thousands of old copper wires with fiber optic lines and upgraded hundreds of radio systems as part of the overhaul that began last year after Congress approved $12.5 billion in funding. Duffy and the White House are pushing for $30 billion more to complete the work.

The problem appeared similar to a regional air traffic control communication outage in September that snarled hundreds of flights across the Northeast, although that disruption was linked to a construction crew cutting a fiber optic line. Aging equipment was also blamed for brief radar outages last year that disrupted Newark airport.

Aviation safety expert Steve Arroyo said the difference this time was the nationwide scale of the disruption, which left controllers unable to reliably talk to pilots. "If you don't have that ability, whether it's because the old wires that they have are just run down or for whatever reason, you've got to stop airplanes on the ground and not put them in the air, where you're going to risk not being able to communicate with them," said Arroyo, a former United Airlines pilot. It differed from the one after 9/11 because planes were not being told to land immediately and pilots likely were able to communicate with ground controllers at airports, Arroyo said.
