## Trump’s loyalty demands and economic messaging constrain Republicans’ political distance

The Associated Press analysis said Tuesday's elections were about President Donald Trump and that his fellow Republicans faced the risk that voters would agree with him. At virtually every juncture before Election Day, Trump's tariffs, the Iran war and lavish Washington renovations — made largely without congressional consultation — complicated the GOP's effort to protect House and Senate majorities. The analysis said there was a direct connection between his actions and higher gas prices, costlier groceries and persistently high inflation. It also said Republicans could not blame the fallout on past administrations because Trump had claimed ownership of those decisions. The analysis added that Republicans could not easily distance themselves from an unpopular president who demanded loyalty and routinely told voters the economy was just fine.

On household costs, the AP analysis said Trump had shown little sympathy for Americans coping with rising costs. Former Democratic President Barack Obama said in Ohio, “This administration’s actions are directly responsible for rising costs.” Trump dismissed affordability challenges as a “hoax” and told a Georgia rally that he did not “give a damn” about criticism of gas prices, even as he celebrated profits for U.S. oil and gas companies. The analysis reported that Trump had grown far more wealthy, taking in $1.2 billion from crypto businesses while most investors lost money.

Former Transportation Secretary Pete Buttigieg, campaigning with Democrats in competitive House races recently in Virginia, argued that “the corruption that we’ve seen is directly connected to the pain that people are feeling economically.” “You cannot separate these things,” said Buttigieg, a potential presidential candidate in 2028.

As of Nov. 1, 2026, the raw [FRED series](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/MICH) for the University of Michigan's median one-year inflation expectations showed 4.6%. The measure captures household inflation expectations one year ahead and adds dated context for the affordability dispute, though it does not establish how voters will vote.

About one-third of U.S. adults approve of how Trump is handling the presidency, according to The Associated Press-NORC Center for Public Affairs Research. That was lower than Biden's approval in AP-NORC polling just before the midterms and below Trump's first-term approval, which stood around 40% just before the midterms. Gallup reported around 37% approval for Bush in October 2006 and about 45% for Obama just before the 2010 elections. Gallup's presidential approval tracking question, discontinued by the firm earlier this year, used different wording and a different method than AP-NORC polls, so the findings were not directly comparable.

The AP analysis said previous presidents such as Bush and Obama, when their approval ratings were drooping, gave members of their party room to create some distance. Trump generally is not leaving that kind of space, so candidates have taken more subtle steps, including removing mentions of him from their websites.

Ashley Hinson, the Republican Senate nominee in Iowa, said in a debate that she would be Trump's “ally when it is important for Iowa, but I will push back on him.” Trump scheduled a rally in the state Sunday. Hinson toured an ethanol facility in Dyersville, Iowa, a few days later alongside Environmental Protection Agency Administrator Lee Zeldin, intending to underscore her advocacy for one of Iowa's most important industries. The visit prompted questions about Trump's controversial deal with Russia to lower diesel prices. “Lowering fuel prices across the board continues to be a priority for me,” Hinson said before adding that “it makes me a little bit nervous anytime we’re talking about relieving a sanction on Russia.”

The AP analysis said Trump's late moves to boost Republicans may not be enough. Republicans have had one distinct advantage: money. Trump's political groups spent nearly $277 million to boost Republicans since September, putting 2026 on pace to be the most expensive midterm ever. The analysis said the amount may not be enough to carry the GOP. Democrats have kept up the pressure, spending hundreds of millions on advertising that connects Republicans to Trump. A Democratic ad in Michigan called Republican Mike Rogers, a former congressman, “all in on the war, all in on Trump.”

The AP analysis said the war Trump had said would be over in weeks continued eight months later with no clear strategy for conclusion, a stark contrast with his pledge not to engage in Middle East conflicts. Rep. Derrick Van Orden of Wisconsin, one of the most vulnerable House Republicans, said in a debate that the war was “making it exponentially harder for Republicans to keep the House and Senate,” while noting his support for the effort.

The analysis said some voters were also upset about Trump's priorities, including the scale of his lavish Washington remake. A ballroom that Supreme Court Chief Justice John Roberts deemed “likely unlawful” is rising over the White House as the Republican administration signaled it could begin work on a triumphal arch near Arlington National Cemetery later this week. Trump said he would defy a federal judge and move ahead later this month with plans to close a park along the Potomac River, a place that has long served as the backdrop for family picnics, scenic bike rides and affordable golf, to make way for a “world class” golf course.

Midterms almost always serve as a referendum on the president's party, and losses typically follow, the AP analysis said. Democrats gained 40 House seats in 2018 and 31 in 2006 during George W. Bush's second term. Republicans picked up 63 seats in 2010 under Barack Obama but flipped fewer than 10 in 2022 under Democratic President Joe Biden. Redistricting has reduced the number of genuinely competitive House districts.

The analysis said Trump's import taxes disrupted the agricultural economy and triggered a trade war with Canada, putting Republicans on defense in Senate races from Iowa, Michigan and Kansas to Nebraska, Ohio and Maine. Trump's interventions in several Senate races could ultimately erase the Republican majority, the AP analysis said. Outgoing Republican Sen. John Cornyn warned that Trump's pick in this year's primary, state Attorney General Ken Paxton, was weak against Democrat James Talarico, a state representative. “Even in places like Texas, which are pretty red, we’ve got flawed candidates who I think are going to have a hard time,” Cornyn said in a brief interview.

Democrats also face risks as some in the party become bullish about their chances and warnings grow against overinterpreting the results. “Anger against Trump is going to be a waning emotion,” Maryland Gov. Wes Moore said in an interview. “We’ve got to deliver.”

Sen. Elissa Slotkin, D-Mich., recently traveled to Hungary to study how voters rejected Prime Minister Viktor Orbán and his authoritarian policies. In a speech, she spoke of a “tipping point” when “the public will refuse to accept this level of corruption.” Asked in an interview whether the U.S. was reaching a “tipping point,” she said “that’s the $100 million question,” cautioning that voters were not “falling in love with Democrats.” “A midterm is almost always a signal and a reaction to the party in power,” she said. “What we do, starting January 1, if we manage to flip the House, is going to be a proof point for those swing voters on whether they made a good call.”

For Republicans, the central test is whether nearly $277 million in spending can offset the political costs of Trump's tariffs, war and Washington projects. For Democrats, the test is whether they can convert opposition to Trump into durable support before anger subsides. Tuesday's results will show whether voters punish the president's party for his governing record or reward Democrats as the alternative.
