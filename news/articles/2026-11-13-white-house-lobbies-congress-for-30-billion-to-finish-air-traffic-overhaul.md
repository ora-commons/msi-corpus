## Arroyo says stop may be linked to aging controller technology

The government has been working to overhaul the outdated technology that air traffic controllers rely on since Congress approved $12.5 billion for the project last year. Miles of old copper wires have been replaced with fiber optic lines, and old radio systems are gradually being replaced at airports across the country. But much work remains: Duffy has said that another $30 billion will be needed to complete the work, and the White House has begun lobbying Congress to approve it.

The funding push sits alongside the nationwide stop the Federal Aviation Administration ordered on Friday afternoon. The problem appeared similar to a regional air traffic control communication outage in September that snarled hundreds of flights across the Northeast, aviation safety expert Steve Arroyo said, except this time on a nationwide scale. It may be linked to the outdated technology used by air traffic controllers.

“If you don’t have that ability, whether it’s because the old wires that they have are just run down or for whatever reason, you’ve got to stop airplanes on the ground,” said Arroyo, who is a former United Airlines pilot. “And not put them in the air where you’re going to risk not being able to communicate with them.”

Friday’s ground stop was different than the one after Sept. 11 because planes were not being told to land immediately and pilots likely were able to communicate with ground controllers at airports, Arroyo said.

The Federal Aviation Administration grounded all flights at airports across the United States on Friday afternoon because of communications problems at regional air traffic control centers. The FAA's notice said the ground stop was being imposed “due to extraordinary circumstances and for reasons of safety.”

Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy said there is a communications issue affecting the 22 regional air traffic control centers that direct planes en route between airports nationwide. He said FAA technicians are investigating.

It was the first nationwide ground stop since January 2023, when a key FAA computer system malfunctioned. Before that, the last nationwide halt came after the Sept. 11, 2001, attacks.
