## Lawmakers question accountability as projected costs near $14.4 billion

The Interstate Bridge Replacement Program received a single design bid in late September for replacing the Interstate 5 bridge across the Columbia River between Oregon and Washington. Columbia River Constructors, a joint venture of Guy F. Atkinson Construction, Traylor Bros. Inc. and Kiewit Engineering Group, submitted the bid; Kiewit would lead the project. News From The States described Kiewit, a subsidiary of Nebraska-based Kiewit Corp., as one of the nation's largest contractors with a recent history of bridge cost overruns in Oregon and across the country. Oregon state Rep. Thủy Trần, D-Portland, and state Sen. Bruce Starr, R-Dundee, asked program administrators how they planned to hold Kiewit and the other contractors accountable and manage costs.

Kiewit is building the Interstate 205 Abernathy Bridge in Oregon City. KGW reported that the project is more than two years behind schedule and has an estimated cost of $800 million, more than three times the original $250 million price tag in 2018.

Kiewit's pursuit of the Columbia River project came months after Maryland dropped it from the second phase of rebuilding the Francis Scott Key Bridge in Baltimore. The bridge collapsed in spring 2024 after a container ship crashed into it. Maryland Gov. Wes Moore said in an April statement that working with Kiewit was not in the best interest of the state and called the price tag and timeline “unreasonably high and therefore unacceptable.”

Kiewit's Oregon record has also drawn concern. The Portland Daily Journal of Commerce reported that the company scored poorly under the Department of Transportation's annual performance reviews for contractors, receiving negative marks on four consecutive ODOT annual reviews dating back to 2022. The Hood River-White Salmon Bridge Authority hired Kiewit in 2024 to design the new Hood River-White Salmon Bridge Replacement Project. That project's price has since doubled to more than $1.2 billion, partly because of inflation but also because Kiewit underestimated the cost of drilled shafts supporting the new bridge, according to Joe Cortright, who leads the Portland-based think tank City Observatory.

Kiewit spokesperson Bob Kula said in an email that the company does not comment on potential project pursuits. He added: “we stand behind our long track record delivering complex transportation projects across the U.S.”

The interstate bridge program itself has also seen its cost estimate rise, and Washington and Oregon previously scaled back the project's ambitions as the estimated cost rose to $14.4 billion. It was slated in 2022 to cost up to $7.5 billion, but by March, transportation officials announced a new price tag closer to $14.4 billion. Washington Gov. Bob Ferguson said the construction contract would be granted next year and that cars could cross the new span in 2035, roughly two years later than previously announced.

Washington state transportation official John White said the project will use progressive design-build, which brings the owner, designer and contractor together early in the design process. He said the method is known for greater cost certainty and transparent pricing, responding to questions about costs.

Washington state Rep. Ed Orcutt, R-Kalama, and co-chair of the Joint Oregon-Washington Legislative Action Committee, which oversees the bridge project, said accountability was a concern of his, too.

“Having multiple bidders is important to protect the taxpayers. If you only have one bidder, they can bid whatever they want,” he said in an interview. “Either we pay them or we don’t get the project.”

Oregon state Rep. Susan McLain, D-Forest Grove, said in an email that the three firms would bring a wider range of resources, expertise and accountability. She said, “There are very few firms in the country that have the capacity or experience needed to take on a project of this size.” McLain also said it was not uncommon for such infrastructure work to have a small number of submitters and that: “Moving ahead with a new, seismically resilient bridge is critical to the safety and economic wellbeing of our region.”

Washington state Rep. Jake Fey, D-Tacoma, who chairs the House Transportation Committee, said multiple bidders are preferred but can be difficult to secure for large projects.

“I am not concerned,” he said. “There were two firms that were interested and they decided to collaborate. What are you going to do? If you can’t make a deal with them, then we go back to the process of seeking qualified bidders.”

City Observatory economist Joe Cortright, who attended Monday's meeting of the Joint Oregon-Washington Legislative Action Committee, told lawmakers that having one bidder puts the two states in an untenable bargaining position.

“You are in an active dispute with Kiewit,” Cortright said to the committee members, referring to the Abernathy Bridge cost overruns. “You know that they go over budget, and you’ve been unable to negotiate with them.”

At the Joint Oregon-Washington Legislative Action Committee's Monday meeting, lawmakers expressed concern that the project was moving forward with only one option for the multibillion-dollar build, while some lawmakers appeared comfortable with Columbia River Constructors taking on bridge design in an uncompetitive process. With a construction contract planned for next year and the new span targeted for 2035, the program still faces questions over bidder competition, cost control and contractor accountability.
