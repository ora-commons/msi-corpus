## States add safeguards for private home listings amid real estate consolidation

**Subtype:** fact

- Washington generally prohibits private home listings except in cases intended to protect an individual's health or safety.

- Zillow research found homes sold without MLS advertising brought 1.5% less, or about $4,975 less per seller transaction.

## No Nevada lawmaker has agreed to sponsor proposed legislation

Private home listings are facing growing pressure as real estate ownership consolidates and states respond to concerns about affordability, fair housing and market transparency. News From The States reported that private, pocket or exclusive listings stay out of the Multiple Listing Service, the database that is the source for houses and condominiums listed for sale on platforms such as Zillow and Redfin. Select agents and their colleagues know about the unadvertised homes, giving them control over which buyers see them.

The growing use of pocket listings has accompanied consolidation in real estate company ownership. This year, Compass Inc. paid $1.6 billion to acquire Anywhere Real Estate, which owns Coldwell Banker, Century 21, Sotheby's, ERA and Better Homes and Gardens. Critics contend the practice lets agents double-end more transactions by keeping a sale inside the same brokerage and earning commissions from both the buyer and seller.

A Consumer Policy Center analysis found that 41% of Compass transactions in Washington, D.C., were double-ended. The center also identified a Compass program that offers any Compass agent a 10% commission if referring a seller or buyer to another Compass agent results in a sale. Critics said such incentives give large brokerages an advantage in keeping transactions in-house.

Compass contends that multiple listing services are the problem. The company filed 15 antitrust lawsuits against MLS in eight states the previous week, according to News From The States.

The industry's Clear Cooperation Policy has not ended the practice. Adopted in 2020, the National Association of REALTORS® policy requires a broker to enter a listing in the MLS within one business day of advertising it publicly. The policy states that “significant growth in the use of pocket listings and misuse of various limited exposure marketing tactics” are to the “disadvantage of homebuyers and sellers,” and “generally have been perceived as benefitting the listing firm more than the client.”

Wisconsin, Connecticut and New York have enacted laws requiring sellers to sign notarized disclosures acknowledging that their homes will not be advertised publicly. Washington's law prohibits private listings except in exceptional cases to protect an individual's health or safety. Real estate experts say association rules are no longer sufficient to protect consumers, and some are advocating legislation.

Nevada has no law regulating private listing networks, and Nevada REALTORS®, which has generally conducted policy campaigns for Nevada real estate associations, is wary of new restrictions. Julie Walburn, an association consultant of Red Rock Strategies, said by email that “MLS listing requirements vary based on the circumstances of the listing and the policies established by each individual MLS.”

Geoff Lavell of The Brokerage in Las Vegas argues that Nevada should require disclosures. “Nevada can do the same,” Lavell said. He added: “Privacy calls for withholding an address or photos. It does not require that only one company’s clients may buy the home.” Nevada REALTORS® CEO Tiffany Banks countered that the state's licensing laws already require disclosure, reasonable skill and care and accountability. “Those protections remain in place regardless of how a property is marketed or whether an MLS policy is challenged,” Banks said.

George Pickard, CEO of the Northern Nevada Regional Multiple Listing Service, said the organization, which is owned by Sierra Nevada REALTORS®, has hired its own lobbyist for the first time. He hopes the Nevada Legislature will take up private-listing legislation when it meets in February, although no lawmaker has agreed to sponsor it. Pickard said the model is not perfect but is more aligned with Washington's. “I think the most important part is that there’s a conversation happening about the issues posed by private listing networks to consumers, and that the MLS perspective is represented in those conversations,” he said. Las Vegas REALTORS® did not respond to a request for comment.

Lavell also argues that private sales deny small brokerages the level playing field the MLS provides. He said agents leave smaller companies for larger ones “because that’s where the action is,” and that large firms are less likely to offer the discounted commissions common among smaller brokers. Reduced competition, he said, raises costs for buyers. The structure also limits what buyers can see: “A buyer on a tight budget needs to see every home in her price range,” Lavell said, but private networks narrow choices to what the agent offers.

Researchers have also linked private listings to lower sale prices. Zillow research found that homes sold without MLS advertising brought 1.5% less than comparable listed homes, amounting to about $4,975 lost to the seller per transaction. Pickard said NNRMLS research indicates the difference can be as much as 17%. Critics said the commission structure benefits agents while leaving sellers to absorb the difference.

Critics say private networks can allow agents to engage in racial discrimination. A Zillow study found homes in predominantly white neighborhoods were more than two times as likely to be in a private listing network as homes in urban areas. Critics also say the practice tends to accelerate white flight—the migration of white people from ethnically diverse urban areas to suburbs—by steering buyers toward properties in predominantly white neighborhoods.

The visibility gap also affects valuations and oversight. Lavell said appraisers and lenders rely on public sales data to justify home prices, but private listings reduce the available history on days on the market and price changes. Lavell said the missing records make prices “get harder to trust.” He added: “Public accountability suffers. Researchers, fair housing testers and reporters use open listing data to spot discrimination. They cannot audit what they cannot see.”

Derrick Johnson, president of the NAACP, wrote in August that “The growing acceptance of private listing networks by powerful real estate interests signals a shift in how the market operates, with selective access becoming a central feature rather than an exception.” Both houses of Congress are now examining the practice, News From The States reported. Nevada REALTORS® said in a statement that the MLS promotes transparency and broad market exposure, while consumers need accurate information, an understanding of their options and informed decisions. The association said proposed legislation should be evaluated against existing protections, demonstrated consumer needs, unintended consequences and antitrust implications.
