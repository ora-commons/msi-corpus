## Internal review found searches unrelated to any police investigation

An Indiana police officer has been arrested on allegations that he misused Flock camera searches hundreds of times in a case involving his wife and a previous acquaintance of hers. Justin Craig, an officer with the Georgetown police department, was charged with official misconduct and two counts of fraud, according to documents reported by WLKY.

The documents show that Craig searched his wife's license plate more than 80 times and the plate of a “previous acquaintance” of hers nearly 250 times. The searches started in January and continued up to July.

A reporter from the Washington Post triggered the investigation after calling the Georgetown police chief with a tip, the documents show.

An internal review by the Georgetown police found that the searches Craig conducted on the Flock system were unrelated to any police investigations, and that he had intentionally mislabeled those searches to make them appear justified. The labels used on the searches included traffic infractions, narcotics, assault/battery, theft and stolen vehicles.

Craig's attorney, Matthew Lemme, released a statement to multiple news outlets. “Flock sells this same technology to cities, homeowners associations, and shopping malls,” the statement said, adding that when public criticism of the technology came, the response was to put it on the shoulders of individual police officers. “The conduct alleged does not violate any law or department policy.” Lemme said that if the public or police agencies believe the conduct should be prohibited, the answer is to write that law or policy, and that having that public conversation about privacy and practice before charging officers is appropriate. “Officer Craig will file a motion to dismiss the charges against him.”

Flock's cameras are automated license plate readers, which take pictures of vehicle license plates so police and security companies can log the movement of cars in a given area. There are about 120,000 Flock cameras across the US, which conduct roughly 2bn scans monthly, and the company provides cameras and software to more than 4,800 law enforcement agencies and nearly 1,000 businesses.

Flock says it intends for its technology to aid criminal investigations, but it has come under increasing bipartisan backlash over alleged privacy violations and data-sharing.

There have been multiple reports of police officers across several US states who have allegedly misused or abused the technology. That includes officers who have been accused of using the cameras to stalk former romantic partners and track down individuals seeking abortions.

The network the cameras feed into is also frequently used by US Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE), whose tactics under the second Trump administration have drawn nationwide criticism.

In August, Flock's CEO announced the company would take steps to curb police abuses of its technology by mandating that law enforcement agencies switch on features that could help audit the way individual officers use its tracking capabilities.
