## Affordability and divided government shape Hubbard's policy agenda

North Carolina Democrat Chuck Hubbard, who defeated longtime Republican incumbent Virginia Foxx in the state's Fifth Congressional District, said the party's election gains must now become governing action. In a Nov. 24 PBS News interview, Hubbard called divided government the central problem and said House Democrats would need Republican cooperation on affordability because they lack enough Senate votes to override President Donald Trump's threatened vetoes. PBS anchor Amna Nawaz reported that Democrats were on track for a 260-seat House majority with most races called and described North Carolina as the state where the blue wave was felt most strongly.

Hubbard, who had never been elected to office before, is a retired reporter and associate publisher of The Wilkes Journal-Patriot, the paper his grandfather founded. His path to Congress included a 19-point loss to Foxx two years earlier, followed by the narrow victory in the northwest corner of the state. Hubbard said that when he looked at how the statewide numbers fell, the outcome was "less of a shock than you think," and he called it "a great day."

Desjardins said affordability and corruption were issues everywhere before Hubbard attributed the shift to Republican overreach and the political terrain created by the October 2023 gerrymander. He said the map produced competitive districts across North Carolina and that such districts became vulnerabilities when Republicans did not have the issues on their side. He also said Republicans had issues statewide and cited affordability and what he called "this cavalcade of corruption out of the White House."

At the state level, Hubbard said Democrats had gone from a 10-6 Republican advantage to an 11-3 Democratic advantage in U.S. House seats. PBS reported that the party flipped seven North Carolina House seats and a U.S. Senate seat. Hubbard said Democrats also "kicked out some powerful Republicans." PBS identified the Fifth Congressional District in the state's northwest corner among the surprises.

Asked about the South's longer-term direction, Hubbard invoked the Civil Rights Act and President Johnson. He said that after Johnson pushed the Civil Rights Act, Johnson was told Democrats would lose the South for a generation — a prediction Hubbard said was wrong because the loss lasted "more than a generation, a couple of generations, maybe almost three." Hubbard said he believed the ice had been broken and "I think it's a new day in the South." He described a Republican "stranglehold" on the South for quite a while, particularly the Deep South, and said Democrats were back. The message, energy and candidates were now in hand, he said, and the next task was to govern.

Interviewer Lisa Desjardins described Hubbard's platform as a blend of progressive and pragmatic ideas. His immediate answer to the affordability question was that divided government stood at the center of the problem. Hubbard said the White House was controlled by Donald Trump, who was threatening to veto everything and, in Hubbard's account, changes his mind every 30 seconds. He said Democrats control the House with a large majority, but the party did not have enough Senate votes to override a veto.

Hubbard expected some interplay between the chambers but said Democrats would have to work with Republicans he described as people of good conscience who want the country to succeed and do not want people to suffer from a lack of funds and ability. He said he would not call that arrangement "dealmaking," preferring "work together" as the description, and said Republicans would need to come together with Democrats to help govern.

Desjardins said she had spoken with Jule, Hubbard's cousin and the current editor of The Wilkes Journal-Patriot. Jule described Hubbard as a particularly conscientious reporter, very good at courts and devoted to unbiased news coverage. Hubbard said political opponents can still find common ground: "People of good conscience can sit down and talk." He said one-on-one discussion, hearing opponents out and listening, can reveal shared central points and allow participants to set aside explosive material.

Desjardins noted that Hubbard's daughter, a lesbian, was one of his motivating factors and that he had discussed this on the campaign trail. After President Trump's election, the daughter was very concerned, Desjardins said, about how it would affect her life and her partners. Hubbard said she was excited that Democrats defeated Foxx and put a Democrat in her place. He also called Foxx "ostensibly the second most powerful person in the House," and said his daughter was proud of him and that she and Rachel were "over the moon" about the party's success.
