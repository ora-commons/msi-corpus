## Kentucky food pantries face rising demand as SNAP access falls

**Subtype:** fact

- Dare to Care President and CEO Vincent James said the food bank has had to turn people away as demand for food rises in Kentucky.
- James said around 80,000 Kentuckians have lost access to food benefits after changes to SNAP.

- James said Dare to Care’s service area has exceeded 200,000 people experiencing food insecurity.

- Dare to Care reported a shortage of U.S. Department of Agriculture food commodities that left the agency with 3 million fewer pounds of food.

## USDA commodity shortage leaves pantries with less to distribute

By late November 2026, Kentucky food banks were facing longer lines, shortages and a need to turn some visitors away. Local leaders attributed the strain to changes to the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program, or SNAP, that reduced access to federal benefits, along with higher grocery, gasoline and energy costs. Dare to Care President and CEO Vincent James said the Louisville-based food bank had to turn people away while demand rose.

The changes came through the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, a sweeping budget bill passed by Congress in 2025 that altered SNAP. Last November, veterans, people experiencing homelessness, recent foster care youth and adults ages 54 to 64 were no longer exempt from work requirements for themselves and their families. James said around 80,000 Kentuckians had lost access to food benefits. KFF Health News reported in October that around 5 million people, or 13%, fewer were enrolled in SNAP in June 2026 than in July 2025.

For Dare to Care, the rise in need has crossed a threshold the organization had not seen before. “For the first time in Dare to Care’s history, we’ve exceeded 200,000 individuals in our service area that are experiencing food insecurity,” James said. He attributed the pressure to gasoline prices, grocery prices and the economy, saying the government rose to meet the surge of hunger during COVID-19 but has not done so now. “For those who are on the margins,” James said, “they are now over.” Dare to Care recorded a 115% increase in food demand from January through September.

Supply has also contracted. James and Bozarth both pointed to a shortage in food commodities coming in from the U.S. Department of Agriculture. James said Dare to Care is down 3 million pounds of food from federal commodities. He described the shortage of food arriving from the U.S. Department of Agriculture as a pattern that normally ebbs and flows with the administration and has done so for years. This is the first time, he said, he has seen federal food commodities decrease while demand is increasing.

God’s Pantry, which serves 50 counties, said it served around 280,000 people in 2025 and had reached as many as 314,000 by Nov. 18, 2026. Danielle Bozarth, the organization’s interim president and CEO, said partner agencies were reporting longer lines, running out of food and people arriving anxious before opening. “What our … partner agencies are reporting is longer lines, running out of food. People are lining up earlier, before it opens, and just anxious,” she said. “Just making sure that they’re able to leave with food that day while they’re in line.”

Winter can exacerbate hunger, experts said: children who got lunches at school are leaving for break, and heat bills are higher. “I think families will struggle trying to put food on the table,” Bozarth said. “I think it will be a tough, tough December for families. And then with heat bills and buying Christmas presents, and … providing additional food for the kids when they’re home. I think January will probably be really hard for the families.” James does not expect demand to ease. “We’re consistently seeing more and more people entering into the roles of food insecurity,” he said.

Many people receiving food assistance are already working, experts say, contrary to the idea that people getting government assistance are not in the workforce. James called it a “classic myth.” He said two-income families are now unable to make ends meet. Gas inflation was costing some workers $100 a week compared with $70 previously, James said, calling the $30 difference huge for households already on the margin. Dare to Care has seen a 30% increase in two-income families turning to food banks for the first time this year. “Some of these are hardworking families, or these are seniors choosing between food and medicine,” Bozarth said. “There could be an unexpected expense or setback. It could happen to any of us.”

Monetary donations are often appreciated because bulk buying gives food banks more purchasing power than an average person has, James said. Food donations and volunteers are also welcome. “Food banks have such a large buying power that we’re able to buy more food at less cost than someone who would be purchasing a can of soup for $1,” James said. “We can provide two meals where for $1 purchasing some soup is only going to get you one can of soup.”

The power in Washington, D.C., shifted during the 2026 midterm elections. Democrats now control Congress and have signaled they want to restore funding for social-service programs cut in recent years. “We just simply need every elected official to be able to, at every level of government, understand that charitable food assistance again cannot make up for the reduction in the federal nutrition programs that we’ve seen already, and we need that continued support,” James said.

Melissa McDonald, executive director of Feeding Kentucky, made the scale of the gap concrete at a Nov. 18 meeting of the Race and Access to Opportunity Committee. “SNAP provides nine meals for every one meal that a food bank is able to provide,” she said. McDonald said she hopes Kentucky can reach a state where no family has to worry about where its next meal will come from, farmers have opportunities to succeed and food produced in the state helps people thrive. “I believe these goals go hand in hand,” she said.

For Daryl Caldwell, 63, the reduction in assistance is tangible. During the COVID-19 pandemic, he received around $300 a month in food assistance supported by higher pandemic-era reimbursement rates. He said the amount had left him without needing help from his local Louisville food bank. His current benefit is $24 a month, and he now receives a monthly box from Exodus Family Ministries containing items such as juice, cereal, vegetables and powdered milk. “Same things I like, some I don’t,” he said. “So what I don’t use, I give to the needy, so somebody could use it.”

Caldwell broke his ankle in 2013, developed an infection and filed for disability because he could not work. He had a heart attack and received a stent in 2019, then became homeless. He said he began drinking heavily after he could not afford housing, but a Louisville nonprofit later helped him settle into a reduced-rent apartment. “I get very little…SNAP, but other things help me,” he said. “I’m doing all right. I’m blessed. I’ve got a roof over my head.”


The Kentucky findings extend a holiday-season pattern documented in [Food pantries report surging demand as SNAP losses strain Thanksgiving budgets](/articles/2026-11-25-food-pantries-report-surging-demand-as-snap-losses-strain-thanksgiving-budgets/): pantry demand was rising as SNAP losses strained Thanksgiving food assistance, and Kentucky now reports that pressure through longer lines, lost benefits and reduced federal food shipments.
