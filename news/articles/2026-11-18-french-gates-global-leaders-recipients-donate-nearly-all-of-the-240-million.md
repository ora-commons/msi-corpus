## Recipient-led grants backed mental health, maternal care and gender equality programs

Nearly all of the $240 million Melinda French Gates distributed through her 2024 Global Leaders program has now been donated by the 12 people who received it. The recipients used their grants for mental-health services, maternal-health programs and gender-equality work, and Pivotal has called the trust-based initiative a “huge success.”

Pivotal launched the $240 million Global Leaders program in 2024. French Gates gave $20 million each to 12 luminaries without warning and required only that each person donate the money by the end of 2026. The program was part of her plan to donate $1 billion to support women and girls, at a time when nonprofits focused on those issues received about 2% of all U.S. philanthropic funding.

French Gates said the recipients’ different vantage points on the world’s challenges made Pivotal more responsive and helped the Global Leaders be more impactful. She explained that the initiative was intended to partner with experts whose firsthand knowledge could broaden Pivotal’s perspective and deepen its impact. “For me, this is as much about expanding who gets to make decisions about funding as it is about where funding goes,” she said.

According to Pivotal, the average grant made through Global Leaders was approximately $240,000, and 91% of donations went to organizations Pivotal had not funded before. Haven Ley, Pivotal’s chief strategy officer, said a final report will come next year but the organization is already looking for ways to build on the program. “We did this not just for the impact,” Ley said, “but because it was a model that was intriguing to us.”

Dr. Alfiee Breland-Noble was among the recipients. The psychologist and author founded The AAKOMA Project in 1999 as an academic research lab and turned it into a nonprofit in 2018 so its research findings could be put into action. The nonprofit supports the mental health of young people of color.

Breland-Noble was surprised when French Gates selected her to receive $20 million. “It really was shock and awe,” she said. She created The MATTIE Fund, short for Mindful Advocacy for Totally Transformative Inclusion and Equity, and named it for and drew inspiration from her mother, Mattie McLeod Breland. The grant let her honor her mother’s legacy by helping others, Breland-Noble said.

Breland-Noble donated to well-established groups such as Asian Mental Health Collective but deliberately expanded her giving to groups outside her network. She searched Google and LinkedIn for ideas, asked her Instagram community for recommendations and sought suggestions from audiences at her speaking engagements. Those searches led to support for BackSpinz Music Academy in North Carolina, which trains young people to use their creativity, and Daddy Stroller Social Club, which helps new fathers deal with postpartum depression.

Former New Zealand Prime Minister Jacinda Ardern said she took the decidedly tougher route by not making large gifts to established organizations. “I wanted to use this funding to achieve things that otherwise may not have existed were it not for this opportunity,” she said. “That took time.”

Ardern began working with the International Rescue Committee on a program to prevent postpartum hemorrhaging for women in areas affected by war and other humanitarian crises, where 58% of maternal mortality occurs. The resulting Safer Births in Crises Consortium focuses on preventing deaths of mothers in South Sudan, Burkina Faso and Papua New Guinea. Ardern’s donations flowed through The Matariki Fund for Women.

Her donations coincided with cuts to international aid, and Ardern said she had two reactions to seeing life-saving programs cut. “One is we’ve just got to get on with it. What do we do? How can we help?” Ardern said. “And then I think about how I can advocate for the restoration.” She also said she wanted to raise the profile of the collective approach among voters, who she said could press leaders to preserve the programs’ impact.

Ardern said she has no plans to become a full-time philanthropist. She announced last month that she would work as a professor at the University of Sydney, attached to the School of Public Health, while continuing to focus on global health issues and empathetic leadership. “My view is there is absolutely a role for political leadership to rebuild trust, reduce insularity, and reduce the grievance that people feel,” she said. “Actually, what’s interesting is that the public think that it’s their job too.”

Gary Barker, founder and CEO of the Equimundo Center for Masculinities and Social Justice, used his $20 million to launch the Caring Masculinity Fund. The center engages men and boys in promoting gender equality. Barker said the Minderoo Foundation in Australia looked at what French Gates had funded and asked him to do the same at the country level in Australia.

Barker said French Gates’ commitment that the funding would not take money or attention away from women and girls provided a political boost and added credibility for his organization. The experience, he said, led him to seek similar relationships with funders. “We don’t want to go back to piecemeal fundraising,” he said. “We’re not gonna wanna do it any differently from here on out.”
