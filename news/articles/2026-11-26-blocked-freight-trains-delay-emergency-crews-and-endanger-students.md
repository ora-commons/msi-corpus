## Federal Railroad Administration logged over 34,000 crossing blockage reports in 12 months

A Norfolk Southern freight train was stopped across several streets in Lockland, Ohio, last month as firefighters responded to a house fire on Patterson Avenue. The route from the fire station to the house should have taken no more than 45 seconds. East Wyoming Avenue and Davis Street were both blocked, forcing the fire engine to turn around and take a detour through nearby highway traffic. Firefighters reached the fire seven minutes after the call and found 89-year-old Marian Haefner dead in her home.

Lockland Mayor Mark Mason said another house was lost about five years earlier on Central Avenue, directly across the tracks, when firefighters also could not get through. “I’ve been mayor of Lockland for nine years. Frankly, [Norfolk Southern] don’t give a crap. If they did, they would do something about it,” Mason said. He said residents have faced dismissal threats for arriving late to work, parents have paid extra to daycares because they were late picking up their children, and college students have had to drop out of a class because they cannot get to campus on time. “I’ve had it. It’s 2026 – trains should not be affecting people’s lives like this, much less putting them in danger. It’s an easy fix – quit pulling out trains if they can’t move forward [but train companies] are a powerful entity,” Mason said.

Lockland lies several miles south of the Sharonville intermodal terminal, a Heartland Corridor link between the Atlantic seaport of Norfolk, Virginia, and Chicago. Local officials said trains are moved from the terminal onto tracks across Lockland streets, sometimes for up to 23 hours, to make room for other trains to be connected. Rail companies own most tracks and lines in the U.S., leaving local and state authorities with little power to regulate them. The Guardian reported that Norfolk Southern is a $73bn public company with a long line of environmental and other charges against it.

Similar rail crossing incidents have happened in Cleveland, Chicago, Houston and beyond. A 2023 ProPublica investigation found that children in Hammond, Indiana, regularly had to climb through cars to reach and return from school, and local administrators said Norfolk Southern backed out of building a pedestrian overpass. The Federal Railroad Administration counted more than 34,000 public reports of crossing blockages over the past 12 months, up from more than 25,000 in the previous 12 months. Texas accounted for almost 10,000 reports, and Ohio had the third-highest total.

A Kansas City Star/TNS photograph shows a Canadian Pacific freight train rolling through one of seven railroad crossings in Camanche, Iowa, on Wednesday, Sept. 28, 2022.

In almost a quarter of the reported incidents, pedestrians were reported crawling on, over or between rail cars; in nearly 22%, first responders were unable to cross the tracks. Numerous reports described students at the University of Alabama in Tuscaloosa climbing through stopped cars to reach campus. Houston logged 5,610 blocked-crossing reports in 2023, and its East End neighborhood generated the highest number of complaints in the country, according to local reports.

Texas Congresswoman Sylvia Garcia, whose district includes the East End, reintroduced a bill last December that would prohibit trains from blocking rail crossings for more than 10 minutes. “We tragically lost 15-year-old Sergio Rodriguez, who was killed by an oncoming train while trying to get to Milby high school. No child should have to risk their life getting to school, and no family should have to worry about a blocked train delaying emergency care. Our communities deserve better,” Garcia said. She said Republicans had allowed the bill to languish in committee while railroad companies put their bottom lines ahead of community safety, and she accused the companies of making promises while families continued paying the price.

In February 2023, a failed wheel bearing caused a Norfolk Southern train to derail and cause a chemical fire in East Palestine; the company settled with the justice department and the Environmental Protection Agency for more than $310m. Last July, 19 rail cars on a Norfolk Southern train, including one containing hydrochloric acid, derailed in Bucyrus, Ohio, forcing emergency services to establish a one-mile evacuation zone and drive residents from their homes. The Atlanta-headquartered company is pursuing a merger with Union Pacific, the owner of the train that killed Sergio Rodriguez in Houston.

Ohio native and Vice President JD Vance has pushed for passage of the Railway Safety Act, a bipartisan Senate bill calling for two-person crews to operate trains. Rail companies oppose the proposal, arguing that one person is sufficient. The Guardian described railroad lobbyists, who number in the hundreds, as among the most powerful and well-financed in Washington.

Norfolk Southern says scheduling changes in Hammond, Indiana, led to a 50% reduction in blocked-crossing calls from residents. A company spokesperson said, “Norfolk Southern (NS) continues working with local officials to reduce the impact of blocked crossings, particularly for emergency responders. Those efforts include discussions around crossing-detection technology that could provide real-time visibility when crossings are occupied.” The spokesperson also said, “NS is making infrastructure investments in the [Lockland] area intended to help reduce congestion and minimize crossing impacts while maintaining safe operations.” The spokesperson said the company is taking part in the Cincinnati Rail Congestion Mitigation Plan, “focused on improving rail fluidity and addressing blocked-crossing concerns across the corridor.”

There are no federal limits on maximum freight train lengths, and some recorded trains have been three miles long. Others have argued that deregulation by the Trump administration has made matters worse for communities. In August, Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy appeared in Cincinnati to announce funding for a range of national rail projects, including a proposed $40m grant to close two crossings and build a new bridge in nearby Fairfield, Ohio.

Federal Railroad Administration official Alex Murglin said, “The tragedy in Lockland is a reminder that the safest crossing is no crossing at all.” He added, “Events like [the death of Marian Haefner] are why Secretary Duffy recently announced $1.24bn in grants to eliminate key crossings in 37 states and why FRA continues to work closely with railroads and local municipalities to reduce the occurrence of blockages at crossings.”

Dozens of homes on Lockland’s east side are hemmed in by an interstate highway and creek to the east and the Norfolk Southern rail line to the west. Scott Fritsch’s Mueller Roofing Distributors, which has operated in Lockland since 1875 and greets customers with free hotdogs inside its main door, has some premises on either side of the railway. Fritsch said, “They can be stopped across the tracks for hours at times. You can’t get back and forth.” He added, “They say they’ll look into it but it’s not getting any better. It’s been a constant battle for customers and our trucks not being able to get where they need to go.”

Nearby Cincinnati is one of the busiest rail hubs in the Midwest, connecting industrial northern cities such as Chicago with freight destined for overseas markets through the Ohio and Mississippi rivers. CSX operates about 20,000 miles of track, mostly in the eastern U.S. and Canada, and a rail yard several miles south of Lockland handles about 3,200 train cars at a time.

Norfolk Southern representatives contacted Mason after Haefner’s death, but he said they could not make a November meeting work. “They couldn’t make [a meeting in] November work, so that tells me the sort of urgency that Norfolk Southern feels about this,” Mason said. “Lockland’s school is right here – it’s within a stone’s throw of the tracks, so you’ve got an unmanned train with all these chemical cars. Again, they don’t care.”
