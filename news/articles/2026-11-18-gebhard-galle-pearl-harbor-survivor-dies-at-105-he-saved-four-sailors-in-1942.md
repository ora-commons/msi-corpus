## Galle was 20 and served aboard the USS Nevada at Pearl Harbor

Gebhard “Geb” Galle, one of the few remaining Pearl Harbor survivors, died Saturday at his home in Moses Lake, Washington. He was 105, according to Pacific Historic Parks, an organization that helps preserve Pearl Harbor’s history. The Associated Press reported that Galle had saved four shipmates from a sinking vessel during a battle.

Galle died the same day as Freeman Johnson of Centerville, Massachusetts. Johnson, the oldest known Pearl Harbor survivor, was 106. Galle and Johnson were among the estimated 87,000 military personnel on Oahu on Dec. 7, 1941. The list of surviving veterans from the attack had stood at about a dozen names after the December 2025 death of Ira “Ike” Schab, who was 105.

Born in Germany, Galle came to North Dakota with his family in 1928 at age 7. He joined the U.S. Navy in 1939 at age 18.

On Dec. 7, 1941, Galle was 20 and served as a machinist’s mate aboard the USS Nevada. He was waiting below deck to be taken ashore for church services when he looked through a porthole and saw planes approaching. Galle went down to his battle station two decks below in the engineering section.

A torpedo struck the Nevada and allowed water to enter, but officers were able to get the ship moving. The ship was attacked again, causing more leaks in the hull and fires, before running aground. Galle could not see what was happening and described the attack to the Columbia Basin Herald, a newspaper based in Moses Lake, in 2020: “Of course, we felt a couple jolts, and that’s when we got hit with the torpedo, and we had a few bomb hits.”

After the Nevada was beached, Galle went up to the top side and saw the scene at Pearl Harbor, including the capsized USS Oklahoma and the partially submerged USS Arizona. “After we were beached and everything, and we got up to topside, then we saw all the mess that took place,” he said. “A pretty sad lookout.”

After the Pearl Harbor attack, Galle was reassigned to the USS Northampton. On Nov. 30, 1942, the Northampton came under fire from Japanese torpedoes during the Battle of Tassafaronga. Galle told the Columbia Basin Herald that an officer ordered him to close a hatch behind four sailors who would have been trapped as the ship sank. The officer left, but Galle defied the order and opened the hatch. He closed and secured it after the sailors escaped.

The Northampton sank, and Galle and other sailors spent more than a day adrift in life rafts before they were rescued. Then-Acting Chief of Naval Operations Adm. James Kilby wrote in a letter to Galle for his 104th birthday in 2025: “Your humility, quick thinking and selfless actions remain a shining example of Navy valor.”

In the letter, Kilby wrote: “From the decks of the USS Nevada to the battles of the Pacific aboard the USS Northampton and USS Mobile, your courage under fire and steadfast dedication were extraordinary.” He added: “You helped save shipmates from a sinking vessel, witnessed the launch of the Doolittle Raid, and fought in 19 of 20 Pacific naval engagements.”

In December 2016, Galle attended a 75th anniversary service at Pearl Harbor to honor and commemorate the USS Nevada and those who served on it. The Navy said a total of 76 sailors and Marines lost their lives as a result of the attack.

At the event, Galle said: “You can carry your hatred all of your life.” He added: “You have to remember, they had a leader who led them astray. We’re at peace now and I feel comfortable. I have no qualm. We’re all good people.”

After the war, Galle got married and opened a refrigeration and air-conditioning business in North Dakota. He later served as a civil engineer with the Air Force and the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers. He retired in the 1980s.
