## Poundland remains open as administrators seek bids before Sunday

**Subtype:** fact

- AlixPartners is weighing bids for the entire Poundland group, with about six main interested parties given until Sunday to put themselves forward as bidders.
- All 600 Poundland stores in the UK and Ireland, including the Dealz chain, remain open during the administration.

- About 2,000 Poundland suppliers face unpaid bills for stock they have already delivered.

## Suppliers face unpaid bills while stocks run low

Poundland's 600-store UK and Ireland group, which includes the Dealz chain, remained open on November 27 while administrators at AlixPartners considered bids for the whole business. AlixPartners, appointed Wednesday, has about six main interested parties that were given until Sunday to put themselves forward as bidders. Thousands of workers are at risk of redundancy, while more than 10,000 staff face an uncertain Christmas. About 2,000 suppliers face uncertainty over payment for goods they have already delivered.

Among the potential bidders were Martin Property Group, described as a Northern Irish company; Zachert Private Equity, a German fund founded by a former Bloomberg journalist; Xyra Group Holdings, a British fund; and Dubai's Premier Quality Foods.

The administration came after Gordon Brothers, the US-based owner, rejected at least two solvent bids and said it would not provide further funding to keep Poundland afloat. Gordon Brothers bought the retailer last year for £1. According to documents provided to MPs, the owner has already collected close to £20m in interest payments and fees, including those relating to store closures. As a secured creditor on the majority of a £30m shareholder loan, Gordon Brothers is near the front of the queue for administration proceeds, including money from the sale of stock and store leases.

Many industry watchers expect the chain to be broken up, with significant job losses and store closures. Stocks are running low because Poundland is short of funds, and many suppliers have stopped sending goods because they fear they will not be paid. Although trading improved in recent months, the retailer was understood not to be generating enough cash to cover its requirements and would need considerable funds to continue operating.

Kelvin Blake, who has worked on the shop floor of a Birmingham store for 25 years and is in his early 60s, said: “I feel Gordon Brothers punched us in the guts and took us down.” He also said the news had “put a downer on Christmas” but that he was “more worried about the future.” Blake reckoned it would be impossible to find another full-time contract with another retailer if his store closed. He said the administration followed a major staff effort to turn the business around. “The number of people coming through the doors was increasing day by day,” he said. “My store is a strong store.”

Several other employees said they felt the collapse was inevitable. One said, “We knew at some point this was probably going to happen,” and called the prospect of closure “kind of worrying.” The employee hoped a buyer such as Tesco or the owner of Poundstretcher might acquire stores, but said of Gordon Brothers: “They are doing everything legally right but not ethically right. Nobody can stop them.” Reports this week that Poundstretcher's US owners were lining up a sale suggested that it was unlikely to rescue Poundland.

The impact is being felt at Poundland stores across the country, as well as at its head office and distribution centres, including one in Andy Burnham's Makerfield constituency. Hundreds of people work at Poundland's two distribution centres in Bryn near Wigan and Harlow in Essex, as well as at the head office in Walsall near Birmingham. Nearly 300 people are understood to work at the eight-hectare (19-acre) Bryn site in the former coal-mining village of Bryn in Ashton-in-Makerfield, which the same report also placed within the prime minister's constituency. The site opened in 2018 and is one of the area's biggest retail employers.

Jo Welbourne, a regional organiser for the Usdaw union, said staff were “absolutely devastated,” adding that many had worked at the site for a long time and live locally. “It's devastating at any time of year but particularly around Christmas,” Welbourne said.

A Gordon Brothers spokesperson said: “Our thoughts are with Poundland colleagues. We recognise how difficult the continued uncertainty is for them. Efforts to preserve as many jobs and stores as possible will continue, led by the administrators.” The spokesperson said Poundland had “faced several years of instability” and that Gordon Brothers had provided £85m in new finance, “stepping in when no one else was willing to finance the business.”

About 2,000 suppliers will take a considerable hit because many have supplied stock they will not be paid for, with more money tied up in inventory or components they expected Poundland to buy. Davey Martens, at independent confectionery supplier Byxa, said the company was owed £68,515.20 for products already supplied. Byxa's future would be at risk if it could not recover “a meaningful amount” of the money owed. “This might not sound like an enormous amount,” Martens said. “But for a business like ours, it is. We can't simply absorb a loss of this size and carry on as though nothing has happened. The consequences could be devastating.”

Another supplier said he was owed more than $1.2m (£915,000) for clothing items already in stores or stuck in port in the UK. He said he, like many other suppliers, accepted a switch to longer payment terms of 120 days from 90 days, in the interest of helping Poundland, before Gordon Brothers said it wanted to sell the business. The supplier is paying for storage at the port because Poundland does not have the funds to release the goods, even though they are selling well in stores. “That is the part that is frustrating and upsetting – the small guys end up losing while big corporations make money,” he said.

In Woking, Surrey, the local mall already has a large boarded-up space still fronted by signs for a former Debenhams, while the former Wilko now houses a charity shop. Other units remain empty after the closure of Goldsmiths jewellers, Topshop and the exit of several other names such as Poundworld and Carphone Warehouse over recent years. Poundland's tills in Woking were busy, but shoppers' views ranged from “I love it” to “I never go there,” and several said they were “very sad” at the prospect of closure.

Christy Nucum, 52, said: “I feel sad, because we go there because it is affordable. People are struggling nowadays because everything is expensive.” She also shops at Home Bargains, B&M and Aldi and said she loved Woolworths and Wilko before they closed. “So many things are closing I don't know what is happening,” Nucum said. Emma Goodman said she visited before Christmas every year to pick up things she needed and would “have to go to a big supermarket” if Poundland closed. She added: “I do think you end up paying more in those kind of places.”

Annice Biggs, who was in Woking with her husband, Chris, said she popped into Poundland every week for items such as chocolate and toiletries. “It's part of the high street and I feel like we are losing Woolworths again,” Biggs said, referring to the budget chain that closed in 2009. “All the well-known names are disappearing from the high street and people still want their high street.”

Mark Sseba, 45, was in Woking with Simon Clifton, 47, whom he supports as a support worker. Sseba said it was not that surprising that Poundland was closing because “a lot of stuff you get here you can easily get online and have delivered.” He added: “Of course it would be missed.” He said buying in person is often easier and more enjoyable for his clients and “going to a shop within your budget is good for the community and people with challenges. It helps to step out and buy something and walk around.” Chloe Asher, reflecting the views of several shoppers who rarely visited, said: “Poundland is kind of old fashioned. I just go to the supermarket.”
