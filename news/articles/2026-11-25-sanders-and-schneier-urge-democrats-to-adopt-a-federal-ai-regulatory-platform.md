## Data center excise tax would fund payroll tax cuts and unemployment insurance

Nathan E. Sanders, a data scientist affiliated with the Berkman Klein Center at Harvard University, and Bruce Schneier, a security technologist who teaches at the Harvard Kennedy School, have set out a three-part federal policy platform for artificial intelligence regulation, aimed at a Democratic Party preparing to take office in Congress in January. In a Nov. 25 opinion piece in The Guardian's commentisfree section, the two authors write that the party must be ready to act: "The congressional caucus must walk into the Capitol in January prepared to take immediate legislative action."

The proposal follows the November elections, which Sanders and Schneier describe as demonstrating the American public's urgent demand for change. They say Democrats have not presented a compelling plan around AI and characterize AI as a primary force behind both the economic disruption and political corruption that drove so many voters to the polls. As AI companies and their boosters spent hundreds of millions of dollars in the 2026 elections, they write, the Trump administration lined up unequivocally with industry oligarchs and Democratic leaders had done more dodging than leading. Despite a massive overall shift toward the Democratic Party, they say voters emerged split over which party would best help control the technology. They set a target for the party: "We need to make AI work for the American public, not the tech oligarchs."

Their case rests in part on an October survey showing that 84% of voters say AI threatens American workers, that 57% say the Trump administration has not taken AI risks seriously enough or at all, and that 80% of Democrats and 61% of Republicans want stricter AI regulation. Sanders and Schneier describe AI as one of the rare, emotional issues that crosses party lines. They also point to Democratic candidates who ran and won against giveaways to tech billionaires amid a general undercurrent of backlash against AI: senators-elect Sherrod Brown in Ohio, James Talarico in Texas, Troy Jackson in Maine and Abdul El-Sayed in Michigan.

Sanders and Schneier say some politicians have channeled voter anger. They cite Vermont Sen. Bernie Sanders, who has advocated government acquisition of shares in AI companies; California Rep. Ro Khanna, who has called for giving workers a say in AI use in their workplaces; and New York Gov. Kathy Hochul, who has signed safeguards for consumers using AI companions into law. The authors say other critical aspects of AI have not yet made it onto the public agenda, where political leaders should put forward powerful ideas. Those areas, they say, include addressing the staggering inequality driven by growing big-tech wealth, ensuring fair and universal access as AI becomes increasingly embedded in life and work, and protecting consumers and workers from exploitative uses.

For success in the 2028 presidential campaigns, the authors say Democrats must align around an AI agenda and show willingness to fight for the change voters demand. They say the caucus should walk into the Capitol in January prepared for immediate legislative action, able to channel the wishes of the public and set aside the self-interested demands of AI billionaires. The authors say that means rejecting the AI billionaires' campaign funds and barring Congress members from stock trading, and they urge coordination across the state houses the party controls and those it does not. Sanders and Schneier also say their book, *Rewiring Democracy*, studied examples from around the world of how AI will affect democratic politics. Following the November election, they write, the path forward for US AI policy — and its intersections with broader economic and social policy — is clearer than ever.

The platform has three planks: curbing the power of big tech, building AI that works for everyone, and putting people in charge of where the technology is used. The authors frame the planks as responses to critical aspects of AI that have not yet reached the public agenda. Under the first, they say, the aim is to rein in the big tech companies that Trump has cozied up to. They argue that a few trillion-dollar corporations are rigging the tech ecosystem — and the country's entire economy — for their own benefit, generating wealth from models trained on shared knowledge, the grid, land, air and water while forcing the costs of their harmful practices onto the public. They call for making those companies pay for the problems they create for society.

Like many voters, the authors say they would start with data centers. They would match and extend Massachusetts' policy preventing companies from foisting pollution and price spikes on data-center host communities, require developers to match power consumption with new clean energy, and install energy storage systems to compensate for their strain on the energy grid. They cite the UK's experience, where a data-center developer secured more than £200m in public guarantees for a project before backtracking on promises to use renewable energy. The stated goal is local control, they write, while defending towns against developers with nation-state-like coffers. They want Congress to go beyond Massachusetts and insist that states receiving federal energy and economic grants require their cities to execute transparent, binding and enforceable agreements with data-center developers. Those agreements would meet a minimum standard of annual payments per megawatt-hour of energy consumption, accounting proportionately for local infrastructure impacts.

Borrowing from Sen. Elizabeth Warren, Sanders and Schneier would change incentives that give corporations tax benefits for replacing people with machines. They would levy an excise tax on the energy used by large AI data centers, a mechanism they say would force those profiting from the "hyperscaling" of AI to internalize its true costs. They would direct recovered funds to a payroll-tax reduction and support for unemployment insurance, so private investment in AI translates into public investment in people. The proposal lands in a labor market that [Federal Reserve Economic Data](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/U6RATE) lists at a broader U-6 underemployment rate of 7.9% as of Nov. 25, 2026, a measure that includes discouraged workers and involuntary part-time employment, while the [prime-age employment-population ratio](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/LNS12300060) stood at 80.7% on the same date. Sanders and Schneier say the party might also consider other structural changes, such as the sovereign wealth fund proposed by Sanders or the AI dividend proposed by Talarico. They urge inclusion of a mechanism like the excise tax because, in their view, it shifts incentives by forcing AI providers to pay all the costs of truly advantageous uses and discouraging frivolous ones.

Sanders and Schneier would also bar companies from consolidating control of the full AI stack across chips, compute infrastructure and model development, and strengthen SEC scrutiny of AI companies' business transactions and accounting. They say the steps would mitigate anticompetitive behavior and end the circular investment that, in their view, threatens to inflate a bubble that could lead to a catastrophic market crash.

The second plank, which Sanders and Schneier describe as building AI that benefits everyone, not just the industry barons, would make AI operate like a public utility: a regulated service built and operated for public benefit. They reject what they call an extractive product foisted on consumers by unaccountable corporations for the benefit of their owners. Their proposal starts with public AI models developed transparently under democratic oversight and control. The models would follow ethical guidelines, including compensating content creators for training data, and comply strictly with regulations.

Most importantly, the authors write, models developed by public agencies would be incentivized to create public good instead of private profit. They say public AI would not replace for-profit AI; instead, it would provide an alternative for consumers and enterprises wary of big tech and set a competitive baseline for corporate models. They note that Switzerland and Spain already pursue publicly developed models and call for a permanent federal AI research program, as proposed by a bipartisan coalition including New Mexico Sen. Martin Heinrich and New Jersey Sen. Cory Booker.

Public AI should include guaranteeing public access, the authors say. South Korea, they write, offers free universal AI access to its 52 million residents. For the US, they propose an approach modeled on New Deal programs such as the Rural Electrification Act, which provided federal low-interest loans to nonprofit, member-owned cooperatives to build local infrastructure. The data centers powering US AI could be small local facilities owned and operated cooperatively by the communities they serve, they write, rather than behemoths owned by big tech companies to maximize shareholder value.

The third plank would give voters and workers power over where and how AI is used. Sanders and Schneier say voters favor liability for companies that develop or deploy AI by nearly a seven-to-one ratio, although Congress has not established that accountability. They call the gap another corporate handout, asking why employers would hire human writers, lawyers or doctors when AIs are cheaper and absolve employers whenever they make a mistake. They want Congress to curtail corporate owners' ability to use AI to exploit and expand workers' control over AI used against them.

The plank would mandate disclosure of AI use where appropriate, impose strict guardrails in decision-making contexts involving human rights and civil liberties to prevent algorithmic discrimination, and establish clear rules for lethal military applications and surveillance, whether by marketers or police forces. The authors prefer Sen. Elizabeth Warren's Accountable Capitalism Act, which would give workers at least a 40% share of the largest corporations' board seats so they can influence corporate decisions, including political spending and AI use, over Abdul El-Sayed's proposal aimed solely at AI companies. They say most large corporations now use AI and that broad codetermination has longstanding practice in European countries such as Germany. Sanders and Schneier acknowledge that myriad details remain to be decided in redistributing power over how and where AI is used. They say they are likely to get only some right and that the technology's shifting capability and changing circumstances of use will cast doubt on those choices over time, which they say is to be expected.

The immediate imperative, they write, is a regulatory framework for governing AI's use that acts as a solid federal floor that can be tweaked and built upon over time. Because Americans do not trust the Trump administration or technology executives to carry out enforcement, they say Congress should legislate. The framework could be based on California's existing regulatory framework, reviewed and given greater specificity at least annually, with Congress clarifying the boundaries between federal agencies and strengthening their authorities and enforcement budgets.

They describe the administration's alternative as an administration "100% in favor" of "super intelligence," promoting "self-policing" by AI companies while blocking state efforts to regulate the technology and, in their description, giving corrupt handouts to corporations that benefit. They write that this path is justified through the lens of an arms race with China and accuse the administration of subverting sanctions to sell chips to Chinese firms and ignoring Chinese espionage targeting Trump's industry allies. On political feasibility, Sanders and Schneier argue it is more politically advantageous for Democrats to be caught trying than to give up without a fight. If Trump stays true to his promise to block Democrats' legislative action, they say Democrats should be prepared to work with Republican members to overcome vetoes, and they point to Republican AI defectors such as Gov. Ron DeSantis and Sen. Josh Hawley as evidence that this is possible. Where Democrats cannot work with Republicans, they write, the party should draw a contrast for voters and enter the 2028 presidential campaigns with a positive vision of AI policy that can lead to more enduring change.

Sanders is a data scientist affiliated with the Berkman Klein Center at Harvard University and co-author, with Schneier, of the book *Rewiring Democracy: How AI Will Transform Our Politics, Government, and Citizenship*. Schneier is a security technologist who teaches at the Harvard Kennedy School and the University of Toronto's Munk School of Global Affairs & Public Policy, and the chief of security architecture at Inrupt, Inc.
