## Roughly 75% opposed opening a binding secession referendum

The Guardian reported that Prime Minister Mark Carney had agreed with Alberta Premier Danielle Smith to back a new 1m-barrel-a-day oil pipeline. The outlet’s account said Carney also agreed to “tear up environmental rules.” In its October 20, 2026, editorial, The Guardian said Alberta voters had rejected a move to begin the process toward a binding secession referendum; an early count showed roughly 75% opposed and 25% in favor. The Guardian said the result strengthened Carney’s nation-building and energy strategy.

A photograph accompanying the editorial showed a pro-Canada rally in Edmonton, Alberta, on October 18. The image was credited to Timon Johnson/The Canadian Press/AP. The Guardian framed the result as strengthening Carney’s strategy amid Donald Trump’s trade war and threats.

The Guardian wrote that “Canadians are proving to be a voice of sanity” and said Alberta voters had “sensibly backed” Carney’s nation-building strategy. It said Carney had put Alberta at the centre of his plan to make Canada an energy superpower “much less dependent on the United States.”

The Guardian described Alberta as a landlocked western province rich in oil and gas and noted that Liberal prime ministers had historically been unpopular there. It connected Trump’s trade war and his threats the previous week to make Canada the 51st US state with Carney’s political opening in Alberta, calling the developments “a fillip” for Carney.

The Guardian said the stakes were high because a successful separatist campaign would have handed Trump leverage over Canada’s resource belt. Instead, the outlet said the result lets Carney present Alberta’s mineral wealth as a national asset rather than allow separatists to define it as a regional grievance.

The ballot did not offer a binding secession vote. Voters rejected the idea of beginning the process toward one, and the early result looked emphatic to The Guardian. Separatists had argued that voting for a future referendum would strengthen the province’s hand against Ottawa. The Guardian said Albertans did not buy that tactical argument. It separately described the province’s alienation as profound and cited polls showing that Albertans think most of Canada does not respect the region.

The Guardian described the political and environmental cost of addressing that alienation as “worrying.” Carney, who was raised in Alberta’s capital, Edmonton, made a series of pacts with Smith. The outlet said Carney had to deliver tangible gains from those commitments, or a sizable faction within Smith’s United Conservative Party that supports separation could entrench itself and look for another opportunity to act.

The Guardian said whatever replaced the “Let Alberta decide” movement would face an uphill struggle. Journalist Tyler Dawson wrote in *The Republic of Alberta* that Alberta’s separatist movement had “no charismatic leader like René Lévesque.” The Guardian described Lévesque as the Quebec separatist who took his province to its 1980 secession referendum.

Dawson also wrote that no culturally entrenched canon of nationalist literature existed to fill Alberta bookshelves. He said the province had no unique language or race or faith that bound its residents together.

The Guardian nevertheless noted a victory this month for the Lévesque-founded Parti Québécois in provincial elections. It used the victory to argue that separatists can win power even when their raison d’être is unpopular.

The Guardian compared the Alberta poll with the Brexit referendum, saying that for those who witnessed the earlier vote it was a “depressing rerun of nationalist gripes and political fantasy.” The outlet said the assurance that sovereignty could be recovered painlessly was “wearily familiar.” Keith Wilson, an architect of Alberta’s separatist movement, described founding a country as “super easy.”

Blackfoot leaders said separatists regarded treaty nations as a “speed bump.” The Guardian said the criticism showed the sovereigntist movement had not caught up with the fact that “First Nations’ rights are belatedly being recognised as a critical part of Canada’s constitutional makeup.”

The Guardian described Canada’s unity as a political achievement continually negotiated among regional interests, Québécois nationhood, Indigenous people’s rights, new arrivals and the continental pull of the United States. It argued, paradoxically, that the insecurity characterizing Canadians keeps them in check and holds the nation together. The editorial closed by attributing to one of Canada’s most famous novelists the description: “not a country you love, it is a country you worry about.”
