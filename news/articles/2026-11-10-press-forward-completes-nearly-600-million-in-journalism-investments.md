## A broad funding network supports local journalism

Press Forward announced Tuesday that it had completed nearly $600 million in investments in journalism organizations, about two years ahead of schedule. The nonprofit launched in 2023 to strengthen local news as outlets struggled with economic and technological changes. Executive Director Dale Anglin said the work was far from complete: Press Forward would continue raising and investing money and expand the range of initiatives supporting journalism. “The industry is going through an incredible transformation,” Anglin said. “It took 20 years to get to this point – if not longer. It’s going to take us a while to get out of it.”

Press Forward began in 2023 through donations from the John S. and James L. Knight Foundation, the John D. and Catherine T. MacArthur Foundation, the Democracy Fund and 19 other funders. Anglin said the organization had succeeded in convincing more funders to support Press Forward, which now has 144 funders. The nonprofit has explained that longer-term support for journalism is necessary, similar to arts or education funding in a community.

Its 50 local chapters have invested $87 million to strengthen journalism in their communities, including $53 million in the past year. “The challenges in local news were really kind of universal,” Anglin said. “But it turns out, as we have learned over the last three years, a lot of the solutions are local.”

## Impact loans and pooled funds broaden funding options

Press Forward has established a $21 million impact investing fund to support outlets with small loans they would likely not get elsewhere.

Press Forward Chicago accepts traditional donations and operates a pooled fund that uses contributions from multiple organizations to support specific initiatives. Executive Director Tracy Baim said her chapter believed it had developed part of the solution to journalism's funding problems. “It is a way to tiptoe into journalism funding,” Baim said.

Baim said many foundations, especially smaller ones, considered supporting individual news outlets too intimidating. The potential pitfalls of being associated with a controversial project or being accused of influencing coverage because of their donations kept some foundations from participating. “A pooled fund mitigates those risks,” Baim said. Chicago's pooled fund further minimizes risk for donors by distributing the donations among multiple outlets.

The first pooled fund, used last year, supported increased immigration reporting. Nearly $1 million was collected from 12 donors and distributed to 12 organizations, ranging from national outlets such as ProPublica and major local outlets such as Chicago Public Media and Block Club Chicago to smaller outlets serving Latino and Chinese communities. The donations allowed the outlets to add reporting, editing and presentation capacity right before the Department of Homeland Security launched the immigration operation “Midway Blitz” in Chicago.

A second Chicago pooled fund focused on education and enabled outlets to increase coverage of the Chicago Board of Education elections. “We are making these outlets stronger,” Baim said. “We’re not just giving an organization a grant to cover stuff. We are amplifying their work so that they can get new subscribers and that’s a pipeline of potential donors.”

## News deserts rose amid declines in advertising and audiences

Northwestern University's *The State of Local News 2026* counted 238 counties with no locally based professional sources of news as news deserts, an increase of 12% this year as advertising and audiences continued to decline. Dozens of newsrooms started this year, but more than two newspapers closed for each outlet launched.

Knight Foundation President and CEO Maribel Perez Wadsworth said the next phase of Press Forward's work — supporting sustainable growth — was more significant than cofounding the organization. “Announcing half a billion dollars in national funding toward journalism was important in terms of demonstrating the urgency and the importance of supporting local journalism,” Wadsworth said. “But the more important thing, ultimately, is to really catalyze and create deeper awareness at the local level about the essential nature of access to reliable news and information as a public good.”

There are signs that donors, both big and small, are making journalism a priority because it provides an information base that supports other initiatives. Last month, the Ford Foundation, one of the world's largest grantmakers, said supporting journalism would become one of its priorities as it overhauled its giving to focus on the building blocks of democracy. The much smaller nonprofit Public Media Co. has made providing trustworthy local information a cornerstone of its work to keep public radio stations operating following ongoing federal funding cuts.

Wadsworth said local and regional funders working together to keep local news outlets open in their communities offer a more sustainable solution to many outlets' budgetary problems. “Philanthropy writ large is not a business model,” she said. “And it’s not sustainable to believe that philanthropy is just going to always support whatever the thing is, no matter how important.”

Anglin said there is “no silver bullet” to solve journalism's economic problems. Philanthropy can help strengthen news outlets until they figure out a new business model that will make them sustainable.

## Press Forward's next phase targets policy and cooperation

Press Forward plans to promote solutions through public-policy changes, encourage cooperation and resource-sharing between outlets, and provide infrastructure and capacity-building grants. Its independently operated chapters have addressed varied needs, including helping defray the cost for some Minnesota outlets covering the surge of Immigration and Customs Enforcement agents in Minneapolis earlier this year and training local residents in Kentucky to become reporters. “You can’t nonprofit your way out of this problem,” Anglin said. “We understand that some things will still close. But we are building for the long term.”
