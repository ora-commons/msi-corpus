## Claim posted a day after Anthropic's Nasdaq listing

A mathematician at Anthropic is contending that the San Francisco-based artificial intelligence company's software has helped crack one of the most important unsolved problems in mathematics.

Levent Alpöge, a Turkish American mathematician, on Friday posted a note on his personal website saying that Claude, the AI software, had helped prove the Riemann hypothesis. He says Claude helped develop a solution to the problem during the course of about six weeks.

"Claude and I have had a great time together with such breadth in recent months, but this time Claude far outpaced me near the finish line," Alpöge wrote, anthropomorphizing the AI software. "No, Claude flew, and danced, and sparkled near that finish line."

Alpöge linked to computer code — written in a language called Lean — that he maintained could verify the findings. Mathematicians subsequently began working to see if the proof could be confirmed. If Alpöge's claim is ultimately confirmed, the proof could mark another breakthrough in the use of AI for complicated math problems.

Alpöge's note was published just one day after Anthropic went public on the Nasdaq stock market index. He acknowledged the timing in the note, writing: "The precise timing is horrible. If I could have, I'd have finished this all much earlier."

The Riemann hypothesis is a nearly 170-year-old math problem named after Bernhard Riemann, a German mathematician who proposed it in 1859. The hypothesis deals with prime numbers — whole numbers greater than 1 that can only be divided by themselves and 1. Riemann questioned how often prime numbers appear and how they are distributed along the number line, and he developed a formula for estimating how many prime numbers exist under a specific number.

The problem is one of the seven Millennium Prize problems selected by the Clay Mathematics Institute, which has offered $1m for each problem solved.

The claim arrived alongside a separate attempt on the same problem. The New York Times reported that on Friday, an independent mathematician in Italy released an incomplete proof of the Riemann hypothesis that he said he had been working on for almost 12 years. That mathematician, Alessandro Masullo, said he did not use AI because he did not fully trust the system.

The announcement follows a similar claim by an Anthropic competitor. In September, OpenAI claimed that a non-public version of its AI tools helped solve part of another Millennium Prize problem — but it was solved in a way that struck mathematicians and observers as strange: a near-trillion-dollar private company spent $15m to employ 10,000 AI systems to solve the problem. Alpöge was working on that separate problem for Anthropic when OpenAI made its announcement.

Some mathematicians have expressed discomfort with relying on AI to solve math problems. James Robinson, a University of Warwick mathematician, told the Guardian in September that "it's frustrating to see these big tech companies burning this fuel up just so that they can show off about how great their latest model is."

He added: "It seems like immature playground boasting writ large, underpinned by billions of dollars and the potential for significant environmental damage in an age when climate change is probably the biggest challenge we face."

The unease extends to the sums involved: mathematicians have expressed uneasiness around extremely lucrative private companies dishing out millions of dollars for the technology to solve problems. Masullo's decision to work without AI, he told the Times, rested on not fully trusting the system.
