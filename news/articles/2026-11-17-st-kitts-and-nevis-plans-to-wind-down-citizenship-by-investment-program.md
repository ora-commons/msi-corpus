## EU pressure puts Schengen access and government revenue at risk

Prime Minister Terrance Drew said Friday that the government of St Kitts and Nevis would wind down the citizenship-by-investment program after the European Union threatened to end the country's visa-free access to the Schengen area. The twin-island Caribbean nation pioneered the 42-year scheme.

The program, known as CBI, allows wealthy foreigners to obtain citizenship and ultimately a passport by investing in infrastructure and other development projects. It has brought billions of dollars into St Kitts and Nevis and accounted for the largest share of government revenue in some years.

Mark Brantley, premier of Nevis, which has autonomous self-governance in some aspects, said he understood that the “country was given very little choice,” but did not “support the winding down of the program.” Brantley warned that ending the lucrative program threatened the economy and the country's ability to support people in need.

In June, the European Commission wrote to the five Caribbean nations that currently operate CBI programs: St Kitts and Nevis, Dominica, Antigua and Barbuda, Grenada and St Lucia. The commission warned that the countries might lose visa-free access to its 29-country Schengen area if they did not phase out their programs. Brantley described the 1 June 2028 deadline given to St Kitts and Nevis as “frankly ludicrous.”

Drew said in Friday's statement that previous administrations had mismanaged the program, citing years of underselling to undercut other countries, a controversial prison project, weak escrow protections and investment sums that were not properly paid into the citizenship unit. He said the government had led major reforms to the Caribbean's programs with international partners, including the US treasury.

Drew argued that continuing CBI risked St Kitts and Nevis's visa-free travel to parts of Europe and access to international banking. He said that outcome could disrupt services allowing visitors to use overseas cards and families abroad to send money home.

Brantley said it was unfair for the EU to require St Kitts and Nevis to act without offering assistance for a transition to other industries. “I have not yet seen from the EU any corresponding suggestions of assistance,” he said. He said CBI programs in St Kitts and Nevis and smaller Caribbean countries had contributed significantly to gross domestic product and to ensuring that people had adequate access to water, education, healthcare and other services.

Drew said the CBI program “did a lot of good, but it also created a danger.” “When one source pays for half or more of everything, you are at its mercy. If it stops, everything stops. That is putting all of your eggs in one basket,” he added.

The government aims to diversify the economy by strengthening tourism, yachting, and digital and creative industries, among other sectors. Drew said the sale of clean geothermal energy is “at the heart of it,” with drilling and exploration already planned.

Drew also rejected raising taxes as part of the transition. “I will not pretend that the road is easy. Less CBI money means we must spend wisely and plan carefully. We will. Our plan is to make this change by growing our economy. Raising taxes is not part of that plan,” he said.

The other CBI governments have taken differing positions on the EU demand. Antigua and Barbuda has expressed willingness to end its program while trying to negotiate a longer transition period. According to local media, Grenada has indicated that it wants its program to continue until 2032. Dominica and St Lucia responded to Brussels without publicly committing to end their programs.
