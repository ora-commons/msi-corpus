## Democrat cites hometown roots and labor support in a 34-year Republican seat

Fred Wellman, a Democratic podcast host and Army veteran, defeated longtime Republican Rep. Ann Wagner in Missouri's second congressional district in Tuesday's midterm elections.

The result overturned the expectations that had defined the race. Wagner had been widely expected to hold the seat, and Wellman told supporters on election night that the win was such a surprise that NBC News data analyst Steve Kornacki had no graphic prepared for it. The Guardian reported that Wagner was swept aside as part of a broader wave of Democratic victories across the country.

Wellman said he saw an opening that others missed. "The Cook Political Report never moved us off as 'safe Republican'. Never," he said in an interview with The Guardian. He said forecasters looked at a district Republicans had held for 34 years, a 14-year incumbent who began the campaign with $4.5 million to his zero, and a race the Democratic Congressional Campaign Committee and other national groups never entered, and read their absence as a sign of weakness in his campaign.

"I believed that Representative Wagner was much, much weaker than people realized," Wellman said. "And I believed there was a chance, and I think my instincts proved out."

Wellman described his coalition as broader than a standard partisan one. "We've created a bipartisan, a nonpartisan movement," he said, adding that all of labor came in with him "short of police and fire," while even the rank-and-file of the police and fire backed his campaign.

Wagner's campaign worked to turn Wellman's media career against him, running an advertisement that played up his self-description, from a podcast several years earlier, as a "flaming liberal."

Wellman said he countered that line of attack by emphasizing his roots in Kirkwood, Missouri, where he grew up. "I didn't want to be a national figure or national anti-Trump guy," he said. "I wanted to be Fred from Kirkwood." He said he presented himself throughout the race as a local representative rather than a national personality. "I always said my job is to represent the people of Missouri," he said, calling that choice "a key strategy decision that I think paid off for us."

He said leaning on his online fame would have hurt him locally. "I think it's off-putting to people who are local," he said, adding that people dismissed him as "just looking for more clout." He said he made clear he "was running to be a congressman representing 759,000 people of eastern Missouri," and showed it by campaigning in person across the district, "meeting people where they are, not relying solely on being internet famous."

Wellman, 61, spent a lengthy career as an officer in the U.S. Army and later worked as a veterans advocate, according to The Guardian. He is better known nationally as an anti-Trump, pro-democracy online media personality with more than 358,000 followers on X, where he said he met his fiancee. He hosts the podcast On Democracy for the MeidasTouch network, which The Guardian described as progressive and anti-Trump.

"Fred has always been a fighter and a powerful voice for the people when it matters most," MeidasTouch co-founder Ben Meiselas said. "We're incredibly proud of his success and thrilled to see him win."

Wellman said his fiancee initially opposed the podcast, viewing it as "a little cliche being a middle-aged white guy with a podcast." He said four years of interviewing journalists, authors, politicians and thinkers about democracy and the threats facing it left him prepared for the job he now heads toward: "When it came time to be a congressman or a candidate, there's very few things that hadn't come across my radar at some point."

House orientation begins Sunday. "Got to figure out how to be a congressman. Congressman 101," Wellman said. He said the role is "a very different kind of service" from the public service he began with the "same oath I swore as a 17-year-old." "I've got 759,000 bosses now," he said. "I've got to answer to people who may not agree with me, who didn't vote for me." He said he owes those constituents his best effort and transparency, and "to be honorable."

The Guardian noted that the interview was condensed and edited for clarity.
