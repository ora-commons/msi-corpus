## HUD finalizes public housing eligibility rule amid tenant opposition

**Subtype:** fact

## 2025 analysis estimated nearly 80,000 people could be displaced nationwide

The US Department of Housing and Urban Development published the final rule Thursday and set it to take effect Dec. 14. The rule requires every member of a household receiving federally funded public housing to show proof of US citizenship or an immigration status that makes the person eligible to receive HUD housing assistance. The Massachusetts Union of Public Housing Tenants said it was reviewing the final rule with members and exploring all available avenues with allies to stop implementation. Researchers cited by News From The States warned that the change could disrupt 20,000 mixed-status families nationwide.

For three decades, mixed-status households could live together in federally funded housing as long as federal subsidies were prorated to cover only family members who could prove citizenship, a practice dating to 1995. Under the final rule, every household member must be a US citizen or a noncitizen whose immigration status makes the person eligible for HUD assistance. HUD described the rule as the agency’s final word on a policy change it had sought since President Donald Trump’s first term in office.

Tenant advocates have opposed the change. In an April comment letter, the Massachusetts Union of Public Housing Tenants and Massachusetts Law Reform Institute warned that the proposal “will result in eligible families being ousted from their homes, cause family division, and cost housing providers and HUD millions of dollars to implement.”

After the rule was published, the tenant group said, “We will not stand by while the Administration orchestrates a mass eviction of our immigrant neighbors.” The group said it was closely reviewing the final rule with members and exploring all available avenues with allies to stop implementation.

A coalition of 22 attorneys general, including Massachusetts’s top prosecutor Andrea Campbell, argued in an April opposition letter that the proposed change could displace tens of thousands of families and violated federal law. The attorneys general said nearly 1,000 mixed-status households in Massachusetts could be affected. In their letter, the coalition said the rule “would severely harm the States’ immigrant communities by forcing families to choose between evicting family members and losing their affordable housing, thus increasing the homelessness in our States.” They also said it would eliminate housing assistance for many additional state residents who are US citizens and eligible noncitizens “because the verification systems are prone to error.”

Federal rental assistance programs include Housing Choice vouchers, Section 8 vouchers and public housing subsidies. Most public housing nationwide is federally funded, but Massachusetts is one of just four states that also provides state-funded public housing. Bay State local housing authorities manage about 30,000 federally supported public housing units and 41,500 state-funded units.

A late 2025 analysis of the proposed rule by the Center for Budget and Policy Priorities, described as a nonpartisan research and policy institute, estimated that nearly 80,000 people nationwide could be displaced. The estimate included about 37,000 children who were mostly US citizens.

HUD proposed a similar change in late 2019 but withdrew it after a lengthy public-comment period when Joe Biden became president in 2021. The agency reintroduced it in February.

Massachusetts advocacy groups, individuals and elected officials criticized the change. A coalition of state senators said, “Massachusetts is in the middle of a severe housing shortage, shelter systems here are operating well beyond capacity, and waitlists for subsidized housing in most cities and towns run for years.” They added: “In this environment, a federal housing subsidy is not supplemental income for most families who hold one; it is what keeps them housed. This rule would take that away from thousands of households in our state without any sound policy justification.”

The Citizens’ Housing and Planning Association warned that overseeing the expanded verification process would create “significant regulatory burdens” for public housing authorities and housing providers. Advocacy groups and Democratic attorneys general have successfully sued the administration over previous HUD policies, while tenant advocates said they were exploring avenues to stop the new rule.

Separately from the citizenship rule, HUD Secretary Scott Turner in February announced a long-awaited proposed rule that encourages public housing authorities to limit assistance to no less than two years for nondisabled, nonelderly adults. It urges public housing authorities and landlords who rent to people using Section 8 vouchers to require full-time employment. The comment period has closed, but HUD has not issued a final rule. State officials estimate that, if finalized, the work requirement would put an additional 115,000 people in Massachusetts at risk of losing rental assistance, including 51,100 children and 79,500 people in working families.

HUD said it reviewed more than 11,000 comments on the mixed-status proposal. Supportive commenters generally said government assistance should be limited to people lawfully in the country and that the change would improve program integrity. Critical commenters said the rule contradicted the agency’s own analysis of its effects.

Critics said the change contradicted HUD’s regulatory impact analysis, which projected that the number of people eligible for assistance would fall by more than half, from roughly 55,100 to 24,100. They said the change would cost an additional $311 million to $385 million to serve the same number of households.

In a statement, Turner said, “President Trump warned that the days of illegal aliens and ineligible noncitizens exploiting government-funded welfare programs would be over.” He added, “American taxpayers will no longer foot the bill. Promises made, promises kept.” Turner also pointed to an audit of HUD-assisted households nationwide that identified nearly 200,000 tenants — just under 2 percent — whose eligibility verification was either incomplete or unknown. HUD-funded housing serves only a quarter of eligible households in need.

HUD said the assistance relinquished by families exiting mixed-status arrangements would be provided to fully eligible households on waiting lists. The department acknowledged the transfer and cost implications.
