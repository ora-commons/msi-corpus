## ProPublica contrasts Oregon law changes with Idaho faith-healing exemptions

ProPublica and The Oregonian published the photo essay “By Faith Alone” on Nov. 17, 2026. The essay first ran in ProPublica's weekly Dispatches newsletter. The project follows Evelyn through months of photographs of her baptism, home life and doctors' appointments, while returning the photographer to the courtroom where her birth parents were sentenced about eight years earlier. It is one of several recent ProPublica and The Oregonian projects about faith healing and the Followers of Christ.

Evelyn and her twin, Ginnifer, were born prematurely in 2017 in the Mitchells' home in Oregon City. Both infants gasped for breath during their first hours. Their parents, Travis and Sarah Mitchell, were members of the Followers of Christ, an insular faith-healing sect outside Portland, Oregon, whose doctrine forbids medical care. The couple turned to prayer rather than rushing the babies to an emergency room. Ginnifer lived for four hours. Evelyn was so small that the only items that fit her were doll clothes bought from a supermarket toy aisle.

Evelyn was not taken to the hospital immediately. A county deputy medical examiner arrived at the home and learned, though not right away, that she existed. The examiner sought help from police, who went to the Oregon City home and told reluctant family members to take the struggling infant to the hospital or officers would do it themselves. The family eventually sought medical care for Evelyn.

The Mitchells pleaded guilty to criminally negligent homicide in Ginnifer's death and to criminal mistreatment for failing to seek medical care for Evelyn. The photographer remembered a crowded courtroom gallery and the couple wearing black-and-white-striped prison clothing. The sentencing resulted in more than six years in prison. Their lawyer told the judge that the Mitchells grieved the loss of Ginnifer and loved Evelyn. “Travis and Sarah are utterly sincere, decent and caring human beings,” he told the judge. The Mitchells and their lawyer did not respond to ProPublica and The Oregonian's requests to be interviewed.

Within days of Evelyn's birth, she was placed in the custody of Marshall and Jennifer Phillips, another Followers of Christ couple who remained her permanent caregivers. About eight years after photographing the sentencing, the photographer returned to document Evelyn's life and the Phillips family's journey out of the sect. In a legal filing in an ongoing case concerning Evelyn's custody, the Mitchells criticized the Phillipses for allowing Evelyn to participate in the coverage.

According to the essay, changes in Oregon state law have led to a reduction in child deaths. Neighboring Idaho, where the Followers of Christ operate a handful of churches, has broad religious exemptions that keep authorities from intervening to protect siblings. The contrast places Evelyn's experience within ProPublica and The Oregonian's broader reporting on faith healing and government intervention.

A timeline entry dated Oct. 1, 2026, said one child after another died in a single Idaho faith-healing family and that the family was not alone in a series of losses. An Aug. 25 entry said ProPublica found 15 recent deaths among Followers of Christ children in Idaho that experts told the publication could have been prevented with medical care. Another entry, dated Aug. 26, described Idaho's legal protections for faith healers as relatively new rather than a deep-rooted tradition. It said that before Idaho changed the law in the 1970s, state authorities could intervene to save a child's life.

While the photographer documented Evelyn, colleagues worked on the wider project. Teresa Mahoney, a video editor at The Oregonian, produced an introduction that included footage recorded by police body cameras outside Evelyn's grandparents' house. The sequence showed officers first nudging and then ordering her family to take her to the hospital. The photographer said the footage showed officers politely letting time slip away and imagined Evelyn behind the door, struggling to breathe. The finished project combines reporter Noelle Crombie's writing, Mahoney's video and the photographer's photographs.

At 9, Evelyn was alive and thriving, according to the essay. The photographer said, “That Evelyn is alive at all is, depending on your worldview, a miracle.” The photographer also wrote: “My work is all about observing and capturing moments, about freezing time. Here, I was witnessing the unfolding of time. I got to see what happened next.”
