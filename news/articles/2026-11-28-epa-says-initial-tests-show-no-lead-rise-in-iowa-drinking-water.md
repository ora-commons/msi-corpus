## EPA awaits validation of results from more than 3,200 samples

The U.S. Environmental Protection Agency released initial laboratory results Saturday indicating that the controlled detonation of five tons of lead azide in southeastern Iowa had not caused a rise in lead in the bodies of water that supply drinking water to the area. The agency said additional samples still needed laboratory validation.

About 300 people spent Thursday in the field sampling schools, playgrounds, churches and homes across the region, the EPA said. More than 1,500 samples of air, water, soil and dust were taken before the detonation, and hundreds of samples afterward, including from homes, parks and drinking water.

The agency monitored for airborne lead for 30 consecutive hours after the controlled detonation. “The EPA monitored for the presence of airborne lead for 30 consecutive hours after the controlled detonation using the Xact air monitoring systems,” the EPA said in a post on X.

The agency said it would continue providing updates in the coming days as laboratory results from more than 3,200 air, soil, dust and water samples returned and were validated.

Lead is a heavy metal that is especially dangerous to children and pregnant women. But the detonation has spurred widespread concerns about potential environmental contamination as well as questions about how and when the federal government communicated its plans.

The U.S. Army said the safest option was to detonate the aging stockpile immediately because the lead azide was deteriorating and might explode uncontrollably at any time as the weather got colder.

Residents and some elected officials criticized the Army for not being more transparent and providing more notice when it first determined the stockpile was unstable.

In Middletown, where the Army stockpile was held, residents knew about the plans, but few others did. The warnings largely failed to register with elected officials and the broader public until days before the planned detonation.
