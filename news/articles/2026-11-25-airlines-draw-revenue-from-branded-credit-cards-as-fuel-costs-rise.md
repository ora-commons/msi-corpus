## Airlines draw revenue from branded credit cards as fuel costs rise

## Basic economy purchases earn no points on American Airlines

Flying during the holidays can be pricey, and Thanksgiving brings some of the busiest days of the year for airlines. Carriers are paying high fuel prices. Into that setting, airlines are pitching more and more specialty branded credit cards to passengers onboard, while the products help support carriers' bottom lines even as jet fuel costs far more than last year, according to NPR's Nov. 25 report. The products have evolved into broad rewards systems whose economics extend beyond the purchase of an airline ticket.

Delta said last month that its American Express card program will bring in more than $9 billion this year. To put that figure in perspective, the airline predicts a pretax profit of $4.5 billion this year. Delta CEO Ed Bastian described the partnership with American Express: "They are our No. 1 partner in the world, and we are theirs."

The approach reaches back 45 years to American Airlines' frequent-flier loyalty program. NPR included an advertisement from the 1980s in which an unidentified narrator said business miles could add up to free vacation trips and that the airline tracked the miles automatically. Within a few years, airlines began partnering with banks to issue branded credit cards.

John Breyault of the International Consumers League said the products are no longer simply a thank-you for choosing a particular airline. He said the broader system had made airlines resemble banks: "The rewards ecosystem has allowed airlines to become, in many ways, banks that just happen to fly airplanes."

NPR's Bill Chappell described airline points as a form of currency whose value the airlines control. Moshe Orenbuch, a research analyst at TD Cowen investment bank, called that comparison overstated. Orenbuch nevertheless agreed the cards help airlines focus on their most loyal customers, even when those customers are not using an airline credit card to buy a plane ticket.

Orenbuch said, "The vast majority of the spending is spending that's being done outside of that airline brand in order to accumulate points." Breyault said airlines often do not have to provide anything in return for points that customers never redeem. "A tremendous amount of airline rewards never actually get spent. It's what's called breakage, and that's basically free money."

NPR reported that credit-card companies pay airlines for each account. Orenbuch said airlines also receive payments when customers use perks, citing lounge access on high-end cards and miles earned by consumers as examples of revenue for the carriers.

NPR reported that credit-card revenue is a bright spot for airlines as jet fuel costs far more than last year. The programs continue to evolve, said Volodymyr Bilotkach, an associate professor of aviation management at Purdue University. "With American Airlines, if you buy the basic economy fare now, you don't get any points whatsoever." NPR reported that airlines get more revenue by unbundling services that used to come automatically, such as checking a bag or choosing a seat, then treating those services as perks for status. Bilotkach said the result is increasing differentiation between frequent and occasional fliers.

Consumer advocates say the system has problems, including that airlines could simply devalue points. Bilotkach said there were other risks, then advised consumers to use common sense: "As with any other credit card, you have to use your common sense." He said travelers should not pay high fees or overspend solely to chase airline status.
