## Middlebury's new promise covers tuition but not housing or meals

Middlebury College in Middlebury, Vermont, announced that incoming students whose families make $175,000 or less will receive free tuition starting next fall. The promise excludes housing and meals, and the college's website lists a price of more than $97,000.

National polls cited by NPR show confidence in higher education at near-record lows. NPR reported that many Americans think colleges are overpriced and unaffordable, while parents and students wonder whether college is worth it. A growing number of colleges are trying to reduce that confusion by disclosing more clearly what attendance will actually cost.

Third-year student Anna Gardner led a campus tour past ornate granite buildings with views of mountains. The tour stopped at a newer dorm. Gardner said the college has its own golf course and ski mountain. Her biggest class was 36 students and her smallest was three. For Liz Mark, who came from Wisconsin with her daughter to tour the school, the amenities were setting off an alarm bell. Mark said, "This place is expensive."

Mark said she was pretty sure her daughter would not pay Middlebury's full published price but could not determine the actual amount. "It's a little bit like going to the doctor, and you don't know exactly how much the procedure is going to cost until you get all of that information," she said.

Many students in the $175,000-and-below income bracket already receive free or nearly free tuition. Middlebury President Ian Baucom said college officials did not always make that clear. He described the new approach this way: "Some of it is just saying clearly, let's make it as noncomplicated as possible and then let the students decide."

Middlebury is not alone in trying to reduce price uncertainty. Princeton, the University of Chicago and MIT have similar programs, while the University of Pennsylvania recently launched a financial aid calculator. Mariana Valdes-Fauli, the university's associate vice president for student registration and financial services, said the calculator is meant to show students their actual cost. "The sticker price should not keep them away from applying to Penn," she said.

An analysis of federal data by The Hechinger Report found that around 80% of students at private four-year colleges get some sort of discount. NPR said the story was produced in collaboration with The Hechinger Report.

Phillip Levine, an economics professor at Wellesley College, said charging different amounts requires schools to provide students with an indication of their actual price: "If you're going to charge people different amounts of money, you have to provide them with some indication of what that's going to be." Levine described schools such as Middlebury as a step in the right direction, but said colleges still need to communicate what their pricing systems are for all students.

Baucom said he hopes the initiative will allow more students to look at Middlebury and that he does not want confusion over price to cost any students a chance at their dream college. "I just want to be as simple and clear as possible - we want every family and families of all sorts," he said.

The changes make different forms of price information more visible, but higher education remains expensive. Middlebury's promise addresses tuition for eligible families while leaving housing and meals payable; the University of Pennsylvania's calculator seeks to show actual cost; and Levine says colleges still need to explain their pricing systems to all students.
