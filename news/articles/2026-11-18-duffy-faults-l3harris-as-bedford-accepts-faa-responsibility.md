## Root cause remains unknown as FAA pauses new router installations

Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy continued to blame L3Harris on Wednesday for the nationwide flight ground stop last week, although investigators had not determined the root cause. Aviation was disrupted for more than four hours on Friday.

L3Harris owns and maintains the FAA's telecommunications network. Before the incident, a section of old copper wire in Arkansas was being replaced with fiber-optic lines. FAA officials said a Juniper router installed as part of that upgrade in El Dorado, Arkansas, suddenly began sending thousands of inquiries every few seconds. The traffic overwhelmed older parts of the network, leading to inconsistent radar and communications signals at 17 of the 22 key air traffic control centers across the country. The FAA grounded planes. It was only the second nationwide ground stop since the Sept. 11, 2001 attacks.

Duffy said on "The Morning Meeting" show Wednesday that a contractor had failed to identify and isolate the router issue quickly enough. "The monitoring of the network matters," he said, adding that officials were working on remedying the problem. The FAA has paused new installations of the same routers, and technicians have disabled the function linked to Friday's outage. The agency offered no updates Wednesday on its investigation.

At a separate event hosted by The Hill, FAA Administrator Bryan Bedford said the agency was ultimately responsible even if L3Harris was doing the work. "I am the chief accountability officer. I take full responsibility for it," Bedford said. "I wish people would lay off the secretary. He's got a lot of work to do, but I'm the guy they hired to get this done. So, blame me."

L3Harris had not commented on the ground stop since issuing a statement Saturday that pledged support for the FAA's investigation, and the company declined to say more Wednesday.

It remained a mystery why the device installed Friday seemed to behave differently from the other routers installed during 68 previous copper-to-fiber upgrade projects completed across the FAA network. Officials suggested that the router may have been configured incorrectly. Bedford said the technician who performed the work was a highly trained Air Force veteran who had completed the same work dozens of times before.

After the ground stop, the FAA nearly doubled its funding request to Congress to $50 billion to pay for the rest of the overhaul of the nation's air traffic control system. The agency received $12.5 billion last year to begin the project. Bedford said he understood why members of Congress might be having "some indigestion associated with that."

Key lawmakers said Tuesday that Congress was unlikely to approve the full $50 billion right now, particularly because the FAA had not spent all the money approved last year. Only about $10 billion of the request would go to air traffic control technology. The rest would fund other projects, including upgrades to runways, airport towers and aging air traffic control centers built in the 1960s.

Chris Sununu, president of the Airlines for America trade group, praised Bedford for keeping airlines informed during the ground stop. Sununu said Bedford asked to set up a conference call with airline chief executives and operations leaders within about half an hour of the disruption starting and briefed them on what was known. Several additional calls were held throughout the ground stop.

"To be honest, he stayed on the phone effectively with all the CEOs all night long," Sununu said. He thought there were three or four calls over the next few hours, with operational personnel joining as officials learned more. Sununu said the FAA faced a difficult job: overhauling "what is really the most robust telecom system on the planet" while using it 24 hours a day and ensuring safety.

Sununu compared the work to getting an iPhone to talk to a flip phone and warned that it might not be the last technical problem during the project. "Can we say, 'oh, you know, this is really the last time we're going to have a bump like this?' No, no. Because the technologies we're replacing and that we're asking to talk to each other are literally multigenerational," he said.

As of Wednesday, the FAA had not released an update on the cause. Duffy was continuing to direct criticism toward L3Harris, while Bedford maintained that the agency bore responsibility for the more-than-four-hour disruption.
