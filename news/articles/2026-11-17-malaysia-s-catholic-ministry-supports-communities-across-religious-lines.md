## AOHD’s 10 ministries assist women, prisoners, migrants and children with special needs

The Archdiocesan Office for Human Development, known as AOHD, oversees 10 ministries in Kuala Lumpur. Its programs assist women, prisoners, migrants and children with special needs. The Rev. Frederick Joseph, a member of AOHD’s board, said Catholic priests, nuns and religious brothers reach people in need regardless of whether those people are Catholic. He said the ministry’s work is for all Malaysians, including Muslims, Buddhists, Hindus and nonbelievers.

Noorzainny Luthfi’s family was already familiar with hardship when the COVID-19 pandemic hit Malaysia. Her husband lost his job as a truck driver, and the couple struggled to provide for their three children in one of Kuala Lumpur’s low-income neighborhoods. “We didn’t know what to do,” Noorzainny said.

She now makes a living from Upcycled, the family venture. Her husband began by making coconut-shell piggy banks; friends and neighbors placed a few orders, turning the hobby into a small business. A year later, in 2022, a British friend suggested creating a recycling program that would turn waste into products the family could sell. Following that advice, Noorzainny’s family founded Upcycled.

The family’s connection with the Catholic Church began after they set up a shop in their neighborhood. Catholic, Muslim and Hindu religious leaders visited from time to time to make donations to vulnerable people. The bishop saw their small factory and asked what they were doing. Noorzainny said she told him they were collecting plastic, trying to educate children and making products to support people because many in the community had lost jobs. Upcycled is now supported by the Catholic Church.

AOHD was founded in 1992 and follows in the footsteps of the Society of St. Vincent de Paul, a Catholic charitable organization that has helped people across Malaysia for almost a century. Joseph said the Archdiocese of Kuala Lumpur has about 45 churches and serves an area with a population of roughly 12 million.

Joseph said the Catholic Church’s social work took on a new direction following the Second Vatican Council, held from 1962 to 1965. He said bishops and priests gathered in Penang in 1976 to discuss putting the council’s teachings into practice. Those discussions included a greater focus on helping marginalized communities.

In Malaysia’s religiously diverse society, the ministry’s efforts also include building relationships across faiths. The nation’s latest census in 2020 counted 63.5% of the population as Muslim, 18.7% Buddhist, about 9% Christian and 6% Hindu. Joseph said, “The Catholic Church takes the initiative in ecumenical and interfaith dialogue.” He said the church helps poor people, migrants and refugees because of its commitment to “the preferential option for the poor, not in terms of evangelization.”

One of AOHD’s ministries is a learning center for children with special needs. The center opened in 2001 after three parents seeking educational opportunities for their children with autism could not find a suitable place for them. Gunavathi Thurchinamurthy, the school’s coordinator, said the children’s needs differed from those at other centers, prompting the founder to approach the church about opening a center.

The school serves children from Malaysia’s lowest-income households and offers three programs for different age groups, from early intervention to vocational training for young adults. Classes are taught in English to provide a common language. Although AOHD supports the center, it welcomes students from all religious backgrounds. “We don’t offer spiritual classes because we have people from different faiths,” Thurchinamurthy said. “We are open to anybody.”

At a community dining hall near Upcycled, Malaysians from different religious backgrounds often gather for a meal provided by the church. Nor Aqidah, a Muslim woman whose family has benefited from AOHD donations, said, “We can live together as long as we know each other and respect each other’s beliefs.” She added, “We celebrate different festivals here. We have Eid al-Fitr, Chinese New Year and Diwali.”

In a corner near the entrance of Upcycled, Noorzainny keeps a sewing machine that her community gave her after a landslide destroyed the family’s previous workshop. She uses it to teach other women how to sew. “They can make clothes for their children instead of buying them,” she said. The women turn old clothes into baby clothes, extending the family’s recycling work.

Some of the plastic used at Upcycled comes from corporations, while neighbors collect other waste. Some of those neighbors are Catholic or Hindu, while others are Muslim like Noorzainny. A few call her “Mak Aini,” an affectionate term for a motherly figure. “Everybody has their own God and the same goes for me,” she said. “If you respect other religions and other people, you can become like brothers, like a family.”
