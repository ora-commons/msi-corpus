## Backers say the measure could reduce electricity-system costs and emissions

U.S. Sen. Maria Cantwell (D-WA) and tribal leaders are sounding alarms over the Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act, a comprehensive permitting reform measure they say would weaken protections on public land. The bill fast-tracked to the Senate floor after the midterm and is slated for a passage vote as early as this week. If the Senate passes it, the House of Representatives could take it up by the end of the year.

Introduced in September, the bill has not gained nationwide traction, according to News From The States. A recent Data for Progress poll found that sixty-five percent of likely voters had not heard about it. Sen. Martin Heinrich (D-N.M.) is a key Democratic sponsor, but the measure has faced criticism from tribal opponents and others.

Tribal opponents say the bill would weaken protections on public lands for clean water and endangered species, undermine tribal sovereignty by reducing current tribal-consultation standards, limit public input and block courts' ability to halt development. Cantwell made the case at a Tuesday press conference: “Generations of Americans have fought for these rights and their voices deserve to be heard.”

News From The States reported that over 95% of all federal land is concentrated in 11 contiguous states and Alaska. The report said Western states would feel the brunt of the legislation.

Tribal leaders also are denouncing a provision that would limit the Historic Preservation Act. The law requires tribes to be involved in federal discussions about developments affecting sacred places, burial sites and other culturally significant lands. Joseph “Zeke” Rupnick, chairman of the Prairie Band Potawatomi Nation, said, “These are not simply historic landmarks, they are sacred places that connect our people to our history, identity, and ancestors.”

In south-central Washington, the Yakama Nation's reservation encompasses part of the Columbia River, and the nation is concerned about the bill's implications for salmon and fishing communities. Tribal councilman John Washines said, “We know the dangers of treating environmental harms as something to fix later.”

Washines added, “The Columbia Snake River dams helped power America’s growth, but promised mitigation has never restored the salmon abundance we lost.” Of the Yakama Nation's 16 historic salmon stocks, four are extinct and seven are listed under the Endangered Species Act, Washines said.

The bill would cut back development requirements under the Endangered Species Act again. It would limit the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service's ability to consult with developers and ensure projects minimize harm to endangered species. It would give states the option to step in as a consultant. If the bill passes, it would define “best available science” under Section 3 of the act. The term would include “all relevant and objective scientific and commercial language,” according to the bill's text.

A Third Way poll found that 66% of likely voters supported the measure after hearing arguments on both sides. Last week, the New York Times editorial board endorsed it, telling Democrats to ‘take the deal.’

Supporters point to the speed of the permitting process. The bill would allow a rapid buildout of renewable energy and quicken the pace of building transmission lines, which can take ten years or more to complete, a problem Washington is familiar with. The Climate Leadership Council said the measure could reduce greenhouse-gas emissions and electricity-system costs.

Communities across the country, including in Washington state, are demanding moratoria on data centers. Last week, Veterans for Public Lands called on the U.S. Interior Secretary to immediately pause data-center development until guardrails were established. As Washington wrangles with data-center regulations, the permitting proposal could sway public support.

Although the measure would cut back permitting regulations for federal developments, including data centers, the Bipartisan Policy Center said it would also charge data centers for the grid power they use. Those payments could theoretically shield electric ratepayers from rising costs. Under the bill, new data centers consuming 20 megawatts or more would pay the full cost of the infrastructure powering them. Rulemaking by the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission would take years, so consumers would not see an immediate rate change. If those steps are taken, backers say the measure could decrease rates and improve grid reliability for decades.
