## Budget control and Iran investigations could test the new Democratic majority

Democrats won majorities in both chambers of Congress in the 2026 midterm elections, giving the party control of appropriations and oversight. The result was described as a “generational wipeout” for Republicans, while The Guardian said foreign policy may have played an unusual role in the election. The Iran war and international tariffs were seen as playing a key role in the inflationary pressures that drove many Americans to vote against Trump's party. Smoke rose over Tehran after US missile strikes on 1 March 2026, in an image credited to AFP photographer Atta Kenare.

The incoming Democratic majority could threaten investigations of some of Trump's top envoys and Pentagon contractors, set up fights over appropriations linked to the Iran war and increase scrutiny of U.S. relationships with allies such as Israel. Those are prospective levers rather than completed policy changes, and Democrats still cannot override a presidential veto.

Ryan Crocker, a former career diplomat and diplomatic fellow at the Middle East Institute, a thinktank, said: “The Democrats taking control of both houses gives them unprecedented leverage – especially using the budget.” He said the Pentagon's request to dramatically increase funding to support the war with Iran has already come in for substantial criticism from Democrats, and said he thought it would be the first battleground.

Democrats will have an early opportunity to pressure the Trump administration during the lame-duck session of Congress in December, when funding for the government through a continuing resolution is set to expire. They could also hold up passage of the National Defense Authorization Act, demanding concessions on Iran or limits on Trump's authority in order to pass the Senate version of the bill, which would authorize about $1.15tn in national defense spending for 2027. One progressive foreign-policy think tank proposed inserting language into the continuing resolution that would prevent Trump from using congressionally authorized money to fund the war in Iran, as well as in Yemen, Lebanon and Gaza.

Crocker warned that a shutdown would be a “two-edged sword.” He said voters were angry at Trump and his actions and policies as much as they were voting for Democrats, meaning the new majority would have to use its budget leverage carefully rather than shut down the government.

The limits are not new. Democrats had little success in 2019 when they tried to restrain Trump's abrupt withdrawal from Syria, which abandoned Kurdish allies, and his support for the Saudi war in Yemen. Congress passed a bipartisan resolution invoking the War Powers Act to end U.S. support for Saudi Arabia, and Trump vetoed it.

The Guardian said that even with majorities in both chambers, Democrats would still find it difficult to constrain Trump's power to dictate foreign policy over tariffs, the Iran war or aid to Ukraine. Those choices include intelligence sharing, deliveries of desperately needed Patriot interceptors and access to the Starlink satellite internet service.

Jennifer Hendrixson White, a visiting scholar in the American Statecraft Program at the Carnegie Endowment for International Peace, said it was worth watching how and where the new Congress would convert its investigative energy into real constraints on the executive branch. Investigations into the Iran war or defense contracts with Trump-linked firms could pressure or embarrass Trump, but White said it remained less clear whether a Democratic-controlled House or Senate could turn them into meaningful action.

The election also exposed a split within the Republican Party. JD Vance announced in a postmortem on X that the Republicans' shellacking was directly linked to Trump's decision to strike Iran. “The reason we lost is the war,” he said, adding that Trump had made the same admission. Donald Trump Jr endorsed the assessment, writing: “Every word of this is 💯💯💯.” The Guardian said the result upended the foreign-policy status quo in both parties, leaving Trump under pressure from his own allies as well as Democrats.

Trump partly confirmed Vance's account, admitting that the war had pushed gasoline prices up while maintaining that it was worth continuing. He said, “we had to do it, we continue to do it,” and added, “Long term, it’s the only answer,” arguing, “You can’t let Iran have a nuclear weapon.”

The Guardian assessed that Trump may resume strikes now that the midterms have passed, with fewer concerns about voter backlash, while seeking to secure his legacy. Vance separately prescribed what he said the party should do: “1) End the war 2) Focus on cost of living/inflation 3) Put America First 4) Change our relationship to Israel.”

Amit Segal, a political analyst close to the Netanyahu administration, wrote that the pressure on Trump to end the Iran war also marks a dangerous moment for Israel, which now faces its biggest challenge in Washington since at least the 1975 “reassessment,” when Ford and Kissinger froze arms deals to pressure Jerusalem.

Abdul El-Sayed, the Michigan Democrat expected to become the first sitting Muslim senator next year, praised Trump's decision to end military aid to Israel at the end of the year, which The Guardian cited as an illustration of how far U.S. policy has shifted in the election. He said, “I think the president is actually right on this policy,” and also said, “I’d also like to see us stop selling weaponry that gets used to do the worst things, including genocide and apartheid, to Israel.”

The new Congress therefore enters the December lame-duck session with openings to investigate the Iran war, condition spending and examine contracts. Its leverage is meaningful but not decisive: Democrats cannot override a veto, and Trump retains discretion over tariffs, military action and aid to allies and Ukraine. The unresolved issue is whether investigations and budget conditions can become enforceable constraints rather than only political pressure.
