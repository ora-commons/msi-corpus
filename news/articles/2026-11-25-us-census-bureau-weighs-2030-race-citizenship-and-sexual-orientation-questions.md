## US Census Bureau weighs 2030 race, citizenship and sexual orientation questions

**Subtype:** fact

- The US Census Bureau is considering rules for the 2030 census that could alter who is counted and how the population is counted.
- The Trump administration proposed removing the census's race and ethnicity question.
- The Trump administration proposed adding a citizenship question to the 2030 census.
- The Trump administration proposed formally barring questions about sexual orientation from the census.

## Civil rights groups called for public opposition to the census proposals

The US Census Bureau is considering changes to the regulations governing the 2030 census that would remove the race and ethnicity question, add a citizenship question and formally bar questions about sexual orientation. The Trump administration proposed the changes before the midterm elections.

Census data are used for legislative redistricting and congressional apportionment. Race and ethnicity data also help researchers examine health disparities and environmental factors, while census counts guide decisions about schools, roads and hospitals. Civil rights and public-health advocates said changes to the questions could produce undercounts or reduced participation.

The Census Bureau said the proposals are “intended to ensure fidelity to the Constitution, improve and protect the core decennial census data, and promote efficiency.” Some civil rights advocates warned that the changes would lead to a miscount and misrepresentation of the population. Meeta Anand, senior director of census and data equity for the Leadership Conference on Civil and Human Rights, said the proposals would change “who we count, where they are counted, and how we count them.”

A race and ethnicity question has appeared on the census since the first one in 1790. Knowing the race and ethnicity of different areas helps researchers understand health disparities and environmental factors. Anand said, “Civil rights laws that are on the books need race and ethnicity data for implementation and enforcement.” She called decennial census data the gold standard, saying public and private sample data rely on census results to extrapolate figures across populations.

Amid mid-decade redistricting, voting rights activists called on legislators to wait until after the 2030 census before redrawing electoral district boundaries. States have historically waited for census data before redistricting so they can respond to population changes. The 2030 count offers an opportunity to produce accurate, representative data reflecting where the population lives today.

The South remains the region in which the majority of Black people live, and Black Americans turned out in large numbers during the midterms, contributing to a blue wave. Mitchell D Brown, senior voting rights counsel at the Southern Coalition for Social Justice, said: “Now that Black voters don’t give power to the south in the way that the south wants power, they’re trying to take away metrics to count Black and brown voters.” Brown also noted that the late-1700s three-fifths compromise counted Black Americans held in bondage as “three-fifths of a person,” giving the South more political power.

Concerns about accuracy also follow the 2020 count. The Census Bureau’s Demographic Analysis estimated that children under 18 were undercounted by 2.1%. Children under five had an undercount rate of 5.4%, the highest of any age group.

The 2020 Post-Enumeration Survey (PES) recorded undercounts in Arkansas, Florida, Illinois, Mississippi, Tennessee and Texas. It recorded overcounts in Delaware, Hawaii, Massachusetts, Minnesota, New York, Ohio, Rhode Island and Utah. Despite the undercounts, Florida, Texas and Tennessee redistricted in the middle of the decade, while Mississippi’s governor indicated that he expected the state to do the same.

Adding a citizenship question could also discourage participation, advocates said. Brown said undocumented people and their families may be scared by the question, warning that communities with high populations of undocumented residents could be severely underrepresented if people did not respond.

John C Yang, president and executive director of Asian Americans Advancing Justice (AAJC), called the proposed changes “clearly unconstitutional.” Yang said, “The census drives so much of our decision-making.” Census data are the basis for decisions about where to open a new school, widen a road or add hospitals, he said. Government and business planning require an accurate understanding of who lives in a place, Yang said.

Hillary Samples, an assistant professor of health systems and policy at the Rutgers School of Public Health and a population health researcher, said the changes have “significant implications for population health research and public health.” She called eliminating race and ethnicity questions of “particular relevance to the research community” and said researchers use census data to understand how the communities where people live influence their health and healthcare.

Although the public comment period ended on 2 November, researchers and civil rights groups continued calling on the public to contact their representatives and speak out against the proposed changes.
