## Dorset finance sector employs 24,000 across 2,500 firms

A few minutes' drive from Bournemouth's sandy beachfront, on the site of a former farm, lies an unlikely nerve centre of international finance. Here, miles from the glass towers of London's Canary Wharf, more than 5,300 staff including compliance officers, analysts and software engineers help process more than $1tn (£750bn) worth of global transactions each day for the largest US bank, JP Morgan. The sprawling five-hectare (13-acre) campus, which hosted just 650 workers when it opened in 1986, has solidified Dorset's status as one of Britain's largest financial hubs outside London.

Financial services now accounts for 10% of the county's turnover — the total revenue generated by all businesses in the area — with 2,500 companies, including Nationwide Building Society, the insurer LV= and the asset manager Canaccord Wealth, collectively employing 24,000 staff, according to the Dorset Chamber of Commerce. Alongside other regional finance centres outside London, from Leeds to Birmingham, and Swindon to Cardiff, Bournemouth now faces what The Guardian reported on November 13, 2026 as an existential threat from AI, as bosses consider how the technology can automate costly human-led jobs. That automation push puts at risk a swathe of back-office roles such as data processors, compliance checkers and entry-level analysts.

The Barclays chief executive, CS Venkatakrishnan, last month announced 5,000 job cuts linked to advances in AI, while another London-headquartered bank, Standard Chartered, said in May it was planning to slash 7,000 roles as it doubled down on artificial intelligence.

Caren Evans, a national officer for the Unite union, said AI now poses the biggest threat to the UK's financial workforce since the 2008 financial crisis, an era when banks needed multibillion-pound taxpayer bailouts and subsequently embarked on swingeing cost cuts. Unite members, who include staff at NatWest, Lloyds and Barclays, are "very very worried about AI", Evans said. "They're worried about losing their job, they're worried about having future skills, they're worried about having sustainable careers."

That is hitting staff morale. One Barclays employee, speaking to the Guardian on condition of anonymity, said they felt "very undervalued and insignificant". "It's kind of hard to be enthusiastic about your work when you know, at any moment, senior executives are thinking about how to get rid of you. You're not seen as an asset, you're seen as a problem to be removed."

The JP Morgan chief executive, Jamie Dimon, has warned of the fallout from AI, saying widespread job losses could spark "civil unrest" unless governments and business supported displaced workers. He said retraining programmes, and even state-sponsored wage support, relocation schemes and early retirement could be needed to ease the blow.

In Dorset, the banking tech push has already left its mark. Bosses closed Barclays House in Poole in 2022 after 46 years of operation. "It was the original site that all the cheques in the country used to go through, to be checked," Gareth Sherwood, the president of the Dorset Chamber of Commerce, said. "And of course, that went with digitisation." That office block still stands empty today.

Sherwood's organisation has been coaching local business to embrace AI and increase productivity. "There's winners and losers in all change," he said. But the chamber would "still encourage people to use human beings", both to support local jobs and to mitigate cybersecurity risks and unplanned outages. "You can't switch a human off in the same way that you can switch an AI off," he added.

If cost-cutting bank bosses start to axe back-office roles at pace, The Guardian reported, there would be ripple effects throughout regional economies such as Dorset: demand for professional support from law firms and consultancies could start to dry up, and the wider ecosystem of services, such as gyms, hairdressers, restaurants and estate agents, could start to suffer. "The collateral would be to some of the more service aspects of [the economy]," Sherwood said. "And it's hard enough to keep young people in this area."

Lawrence Williams, the Conservative councillor for Littledown and Iford — the ward hosting JP Morgan's campus — has served there since 2011, and originally moved to the area in the early 1980s, shortly before the bank, then known as Chase Manhattan, took up its lodgings on former farmland. The 76-year-old regularly meets JP Morgan bosses but says he has heard nothing of AI-related job cuts. The bank says the Bournemouth campus is a "centre for financial innovation", adding: "AI will enable us to do more, and we train our people to make the most of it." Williams said that if companies start to follow in the footsteps of Barclays and Standard Chartered, that "could hit quite hard", particularly for an economy otherwise reliant on seasonal tourism.

Bournemouth originally developed as a health and wellness destination for Victorian-era medical patients, which makes it a bit of an outlier amid other regional finance hubs across the UK, whose roots are more closely tied to the Industrial Revolution. In the late 1800s, banks and insurers congregated around big industry, providing loans and services to Birmingham's engineering firms, Manchester's cotton sector, Leeds' wool operations and Newcastle's shipbuilding industry, and by 1873 approximately four-fifths of the banks in England and Wales were headquartered outside London. Edinburgh was in its own right a big financial centre, but was unique in that companies kept decision-making centralised in the Scottish city.

Those hubs continued to grow until the latter half of the 20th century, when postwar deindustrialisation took hold, reducing local demand for financing. But by the mid-1980s, the Thatcher government's "big bang" changes ushered in an era of deregulation aimed at transforming London into a global financial powerhouse that would rival New York. With fewer regional industries to tie banks to their local areas, lenders started to merge, triggering a wave of consolidation across the UK's financial sector. Many regional offices were maintained, but power shifted back to London. Jobs in the regions were also filled by experienced jobseekers moving out from the capital and south-east England, rather than by local laid-off workers still suffering the fallout of factory closures.

That influx was a double-edged sword for the regions: in many cases the arrival of wealthier bank staff drove up house prices, but also sparked demand for a wider ecosystem of barber shops, trades people and entertainment. Overall, the influx of capital and skilled staff from London had been "very positive, in the sense that these centres – Leedsand Manchester, in particular – have had significant economic benefits", Peter Howard-Jones, a senior lecturer in economics at Bournemouth University, said.

Two-thirds of the UK's 2.5 million financial and related professional services workers are based outside London, according to the industry body TheCityUK, and the sector accounts for more than 11% of the UK's total economic output. Sherwood said central government should be drawing up contingency plans for AI-related job cuts across the financial sector. "Heck yes," he said. "Because this thing is a revolution alongside the lines of the Industrial Revolution, and represents a socioeconomic threat, not just economic threat."

Howard-Jones was less sanguine about AI: "[There is] no question that AI could be significantly disruptive," he said, especially while there is a "reluctance by governments to control the technology." "If human beings are currently driving $1tn of daily transactions [at JP Morgan], and if that can be done by AI, then of the 5,000 jobs currently at JP Morgan in Bournemouth, how many of those are going to be at risk?" Howard-Jones asked.

Unions are mobilising. Unite is now drafting an "AI agreement" aimed at protecting workers across the financial sector, with commitments to up-skilling staff rather than cutting their jobs — a push that would build on the Financial Services Skills commission's AI skills compact, rolled out earlier this year, Evans said. AI itself is not the problem, she added, noting that the technology is genuinely improving some members' working lives. "But it's also a massive threat, because it's being used as an excuse for cost saving and job cuts."

Some staff are sceptical that unions can stem the losses. "This is the first time where it feels like, no matter how skilled you become, how certified you become, it's not going to matter," the anonymous Barclays employee said. "Because the most important thing for these blue-chip companies, is overhead [cost reduction] rather than world-class innovation." Barclays says it is investing £150m in staff skills by 2030 and is providing tailored career support and coaching services. "We recognise this is an uncertain time for colleagues and are focused on supporting them through the process," the bank adds.

Back in Bournemouth — less than four miles from JP Morgan's campus — groups of university students in caps and gowns huddled on the sandy boardwalk, posing for proud parents, but what should be a day of celebration is overshadowed by job jitters. The Guardian photographed Bournemouth University graduates Timothy and Amine on the beach. Amine, a 25-year-old cybersecurity graduate, considered staying in Bournemouth after his studies and took work at a large insurance company in the town, hoping it would later turn into a full-time job. "It's been quite difficult," he said. "Because of AI, they've cut all the entry-level jobs." "I'm not really sure where to go from here so I just hope for the best really," Amine added. "I will go wherever the jobs are."
