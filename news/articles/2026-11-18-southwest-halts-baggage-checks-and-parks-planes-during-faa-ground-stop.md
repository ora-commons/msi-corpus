## Crews and aircraft recovered as Southwest added about 300 extra flights

For hours, Southwest Airlines halted baggage checking and moved aircraft away from gates after the Federal Aviation Administration's ground stop took effect across the country on Friday, trying to avoid gridlock at airports and prevent the disruption from spiraling into an operational meltdown. Airports were warning that they were running out of concrete, and the mounting number of planes on tarmacs made it urgent to unload them and move them away from gates as quickly as possible before backups left passengers sitting for hours on tarmacs.

Shortly after the stop, Southwest sent emails and other messages to gate agents ordering baggage checking to halt. The airline intended to keep luggage from becoming stranded as flights were held and reshuffled. It also unloaded and relocated aircraft so gates would remain available.

Chief Operating Officer Andrew Watterson, a former Army signals-intelligence analyst who had worked at Southwest for 13 years, led the response from a situation room at the carrier's Dallas headquarters. On his iPad, Watterson watched video calls that FAA Administrator Bryan Bedford held to update industry leaders, sometimes turning up the volume so deputies beside him could hear.

Periodically, Watterson and other airline leaders went to the control center next door, where Southwest oversees thousands of daily flights and tens of thousands of pilots and flight attendants. There, the executives conferred with crew schedulers and dispatchers dealing with the ground stop's effects on aircraft and crews.

Southwest officials modeled different outcomes based on when the FAA would lift the stop. Watterson described stopping all departing aircraft around the country as “extraordinarily, extraordinarily rare,” a frequency he said he expected to encounter only a couple of times in his career, at most.

At one point, it seemed Southwest might cancel the rest of the evening, said Capt. Jody Reven, who heads the union representing Southwest pilots. Some other airlines did. Reven then received a text from a Southwest executive in the situation room: “Hey, man we may be able to move some metal in like 20 minutes.” He shared the news with union representatives in crew bases handling calls from pilots. Planes were allowed to resume takeoffs about 8:15 p.m. ET.

Southwest's rapid response reflected what the airline had learned through painful experiences. Southwest concluded that its own sluggish decision-making was a key factor in the meltdown that followed winter storm Elliott over Christmas in 2022. The storm forced the cancellation of close to 17,000 flights and stranded passengers, baggage and crews across the country.

The airline developed plans for different scenarios and regularly held mock exercises. Just a week before the ground stop, Southwest's on-call support team had drilled the scramble for hotel rooms during disruptions. After the stop, Watterson and his team put the first step of that well-rehearsed playbook into effect: act quickly. Once flights resumed, the playbook called for avoiding mass cancellations and getting planes, pilots and flight attendants moving again.

Recovery carried additional stakes because Southwest needed to get as many crews as possible to their scheduled destinations, even though they had already missed flights. The crews had to reach the places where they were scheduled to begin work the next day; otherwise, the weekend schedule could unravel. Southwest's planes generally hopscotch from one city to the next rather than orbit a major hub, so a disruption early in the chain can have knock-on effects.

Pilots and flight attendants needed hotel rooms to get the rest required to fly again legally. Every airline and stranded passengers were competing for the same rooms, creating a severe crunch. The airline and the union told pilots they could book their own hotels and receive reimbursement, Reven said.

Reven said Southwest had learned from the Elliott meltdown not to cut corners on reservations and recovery efforts. Reven said the airline offered as much as double pay to encourage pilots and flight attendants to pick up extra flights.

Southwest flew 150 crew members from Denver to Phoenix because no hotel rooms were available there. Even Phoenix lacked enough rooms: some crew members had to stay in Sedona, Ariz., and make the two-hour drive to Phoenix the next morning. Some flight attendants obtained rooms at the Four Seasons Hotel Las Vegas.

Southwest booked rooms even when it was unsure crews would need them. Watterson said, “I was, like, I’m buying insurance. So we grabbed as many as we could.”

Analysts estimated that the ground stop cost Southwest as much as $135 million, partly because of labor and other expenses.

By 11 p.m. Friday in Dallas, Watterson said he was relieved the airline would be able to operate Saturday, though substantial disruption remained. About 400 planes had been slated for inspections overnight Friday so they could fly the next day. Each aircraft needed to reach a city with a maintenance facility or have a mechanic or contractor available to meet it.

Dispatchers and crew schedulers worked until 3 a.m. Saturday going through the sequences of flights for each plane and creating new trips for pilots and flight attendants to pick up. Some 2,400 flights did not have the crews they needed. By the end of Saturday, 500 uncrewed flights remained; by Sunday morning, operations were almost back to normal.

Over the weekend, Southwest added about 300 extra flights to put its schedule back in place. Some carried passengers. In other cases, aircraft flew with only pilots and flight attendants because the airline could not assemble a full complement of crew. Southwest canceled more than 2,000 flights, or about 18%, from Friday through Sunday, a smaller share than almost all of its rivals.
