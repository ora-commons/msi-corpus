## Survey of more than 800 colleges blames visa process and travel restrictions

International enrollment at U.S. universities fell 9% this fall compared with last year, according to a survey from the Institute of International Education released Monday. The number of new students entering the United States for the first time fell 15%.

The survey covers more than 800 U.S. colleges and universities and offers an early look at trends before full data is released next year. The biggest drops came at the graduate level, where international enrollment fell 15%, compared with a 3% decrease at the undergraduate level. The report also found declines among students from India, which sends more students to the U.S. to study than any other country.

Among the schools surveyed, 88% blamed the visa application process and 70% blamed travel restrictions for the enrollment drops. If options for post-graduation work are limited, 95% of schools predicted that international students would likely look to another country.

Although the U.S. remains the top destination for international students globally, college leaders told the IIE the changes are having an effect.

The policy backdrop includes a proposed Department of Homeland Security rule requiring schools to pay a $70,000 fee for every student who participates in the optional practice training program, known as OPT. The rule has not been finalized and is expected to face legal challenges, but participation in OPT dropped 6% this fall, according to the survey.

DHS efforts also include terminating the legal status of thousands of international students, along with detaining and deporting many of them. This summer, the administration finalized a rule preventing international students from staying in the U.S. for more than four years without federal approval, although a judge has temporarily blocked it. DHS Secretary Markwayne Mullin has said the changes were intended to crack down on loopholes that international students were exploiting by extending their studies.

Last year, enrollment decreases were partially offset by large numbers of students who stayed in the U.S. for temporary work after graduating.

"No one aspires to send their kids to get educated in the U.S.," said Veena Dubal, general counsel for the American Association of University Professors, whose father traveled from India to attend Tuskegee University, then the Tuskegee Institute, in the late 1960s on a student visa. "It feels like an unsafe place. That's the message that the Trump administration has been sending to families all over the world and they're listening."

Politics are not the only reason students are looking elsewhere, said Mirka Martel, head of research, evaluation and learning for the institute. Its survey found more global competition for international students and the rising cost of a U.S. degree also contributed.

Fanta Aw, the executive director and CEO of NAFSA, an association that represents international education, said students also are worried about research funding. Last year, the Trump administration began making major changes to the federal grant-giving process, including how it decides what projects get funded.

"They're saying to themselves, 'Too much uncertainty in the United States. It's too difficult, and you know, more importantly, we have many more choices today than we did 10 years ago. Why subject ourselves to this?'" Aw said, noting that students are going to places like China.

NAFSA, in partnership with the international education technology company JB International, found that amid the enrollment decline, international students' contributions to the U.S. economy dropped by $2.4 billion this fall.

The financial pressure is landing on individual campuses. American colleges and universities heavily recruit students from overseas as a source of tuition revenue, and the drops have cut into tuition revenue at some schools. At Cleveland State University, international enrollment has dropped 46% in two years, from 1,542 in fall 2025 to 828 this fall. Fewer than 50 new international students started this fall, said Provost Nigamanth Sridhar, who estimated the budgetary impact at $2 million to $4 million.

Sridhar arrived in the U.S. on an F1 student visa and participated in OPT before becoming a naturalized citizen. "I am here in this position, in this job, because I had the opportunity to come to the United States as an international graduate student and then decided to stay here and build a career," said Sridhar, who is from India. "So international education is personal to me."

Sarah Spreitzer, vice president at the American Council on Education, which represents college and university presidents, said substantial immigration bills are unlikely while Trump remains president. But she said congressional oversight might halt new policies targeted at international students.

"Those types of things could provide more certainty for our international students that there's not just going to be these sudden or unexplainable changes," Spreitzer said.
