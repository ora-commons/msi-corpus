## North Carolina Republicans lose Senate seat and five House seats

**Subtype:** fact

- Democrats flipped a North Carolina U.S. Senate seat for the first time since 2008 and five House seats, one of which Cook Political Report had rated "solid" Republican and four others considered "likely" Republican.
- Representative Richard Hudson, who led House Republicans' re-election efforts as chair of the National Republican Congressional Committee, lost his seat — the first time an NRCC chair has lost a general election.
- North Carolina's statehouse flipped to Democrats for the first time in more than a decade, while Republicans hold the state Senate by a one-seat margin in a race headed to a recount.
- North Carolina Democratic Party chair Anderson Clayton said Republicans "took voters for granted" and expected gerrymandering to substitute for campaigning.

## Party figures blame turnout lapses and taken-for-granted voters

Democrats flipped a U.S. Senate seat in North Carolina for the first time since 2008, took five House seats and captured the statehouse for the first time in more than a decade, according to The Guardian's account of the midterm results. Republicans retained control of the state Senate by a single seat, a margin currently headed to a recount.

The Associated Press had not called four of the House races as of Monday, with two leaning in favor of Democrats. Democrats also held the state's first congressional district, which Republicans had targeted in a redistricting effort; the incumbent Democrat, Don Davis, kept his seat.

One of the flipped seats belonged to Richard Hudson, who led House Republicans' re-election efforts as head of the National Republican Congressional Committee. The Guardian reported it was the first time an NRCC chair had ever lost a general election.

The losses came in a state that had voted for only one Democratic presidential candidate since 1980 and had not elected a Democrat to the Senate in almost 20 years. "They took voters for granted," said Anderson Clayton, the 28-year-old chair of the North Carolina Democratic Party, who has worked to flip the state blue since she was elected in 2023.

"They thought they could gerrymander themselves into having a seat and not actually have to go out and fight for it, and I think voters looked at them and said: 'That's not how we roll. That's not how it works in North Carolina,'" Clayton said.

Clayton pointed to several factors: Trump's unpopularity fueling frustration with the status quo, and a slate of younger candidates running for office. But she emphasized Republicans' failure to address issues affecting voters, especially in the state's rural districts.

As an example, she cited Donny Lambeth, a seven-term Republican state representative who lost by eight percentage points. Lambeth told the New York Times that he mistakenly listened to members of his caucus who told him he was in a "safe district" and did not need to spend "any money." In 2024, Lambeth had won by approximately 14 percentage points.

Clayton said she responded to that admission by asking whether Lambeth had gone out to talk to voters at all — "did you actually speak to somebody on the ground in your district?"

Felice Pete, an RNC committee member from North Carolina and a longtime Republican activist, acknowledged the party's shortcomings. "You have places where we didn't think we're going to lose, like Virginia Foxx, and looking back, people took it for granted. These were safe, red counties. And it's just getting out the vote effort," Pete said. The AP has not called Foxx's race in the state's fifth congressional district because of an ongoing recount, though her Democratic opponent, Chuck Hubbard, leads by a thin but likely insurmountable margin.

Pete said the coalition that won in 2024 was not the party's traditional base. "The 2024 win for Republicans was not done with traditional Republicans," she said. "They were first-time voters, they were independents, there were a lot of young minority men, a lot of young college-aged men."

"I just think, with redistricting, we just did not have the effort focused with getting out the votes," Pete said.

Republicans had invested time, money and energy into holding their majority through redistricting, under pressure from the US president. They redrew district lines to try to flip the first congressional district; it did not work. Clayton accused Republicans of getting "elitist" in the midterms and leaning too heavily on consultants — a criticism often aimed at Democrats. "Instead, you had real grassroots energy from Democrats that won seats that honestly, they shouldn't have, in some ways, on Tuesday night," she said.

In comfortably red territory the swing was stark. In Davidson County in central North Carolina, the electorate moved 16 percentage points in favor of Democrats compared with 2024, according to NBC's decision desk. Christopher Jessup, chair of the Davidson County Republican Party and a longtime party advocate, said he was not sure why that happened but attributed it to "probably some voter turnout." He noted the county still overwhelmingly voted for Republican candidates and praised the work of his colleagues and teams; even so, the sixth congressional district, where Davidson County sits, flipped to Democrats. Asked for the top issue among county voters, Jessup said it was "a very negative view of Roy Cooper."

Cooper, the former governor, defeated Michael Whatley — the former Republican National Committee chair chosen directly by Trump — by nearly 20 percentage points. Republicans had anticipated the result: by October, the top Super PAC for Senate Republicans had cancelled the remaining ads it had booked in the state.

Pete is still working through why messaging that won North Carolina in 2024 did not land in 2026. She said the word she hears most is betrayal. "The word that I hear over and over is betrayal," Pete said. "Many of those people felt like voting did not matter because they felt like 2024 was their last hope to have this correction, and then they go, and here we are."

Pete pointed to Trump's campaign promise not to start more wars, which she said he failed to deliver on when he started a conflict with Iran in February. That conflict, which Congress never formally approved, has pushed gas and diesel prices up, setting off a chain reaction that affected other everyday costs such as groceries. She added that messaging that won voters over in 2024 "started to shift" or was not being communicated well from "some higher-ups, or even our administration" — including on mass deportations, where Trump promised during the campaign to target every undocumented immigrant while administration officials now say they target undocumented immigrants with violent criminal histories.

Citing a personal example, Pete said the young men she gathered to go door-knocking in 2024 did not want to work the 2026 midterms, an experience she called "a microcosm of what happened." "We have to have an honest assessment of that and just the detriment of not fulfilling, or seemingly fulfilling, the promises you make," she said.

Three weeks later, the dimensions of the collapse beneath that result — five House seats, the statehouse, and a Senate margin of nearly 20 percentage points — are the subject of the state party's own post-election accounting.
