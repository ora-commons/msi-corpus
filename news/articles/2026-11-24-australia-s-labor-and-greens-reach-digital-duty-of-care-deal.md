## Maximum fines could reach $109m or 5% of global revenue

Australia's Labor government and the Greens reached a compromise on digital duty of care legislation on Tuesday, clearing the way for the government to put new online safety rules before parliament. The government's legislation was expected to pass parliament this week, potentially as soon as Tuesday afternoon, after the Greens agreed to support it in return for amendments covering individual lawsuits, addictive design features and expanded powers for the eSafety Commissioner.

The agreement came after intense negotiations. The Coalition had opposed the laws from the outset on free speech and censorship grounds, leaving the Greens to negotiate with Labor. The planned framework would set online safety standards for social media, games, apps and chatbots. Labor said the law would place "an overarching obligation" on tech platforms "to provide a safe online environment for all Australians," replacing a range of existing online codes and standards with a broader duty of care.

Wells said the amendments would give Australians a "private right of action" to sue social media companies for harm suffered online, a local equivalent to successful US laws allowing citizens to sue large companies. Individuals would gain a direct route to the courts. Companies would also face heavier penalties. Wells said social media and technology companies could be fined up to 5% of their global revenue for extreme breaches. The maximum fine for breaches of the rules could reach $109m.

The Greens had long pressed for stronger fines, an ombudsman scheme and stronger action on addictive design features. "The Greens have long campaigned for big tech to be held accountable for the harm they profit off online," Greens senator Sarah Hanson-Young said. She said the provision on addictive design features would have the effect of "dumping the doomscroll with rules that force tech companies to prevent harm from their addictive design feature."

The amendments would add "addictive" features to the list of risks tech platforms must address. Those changes would sit alongside the original requirement for social media platforms to offer an "opt-out" feature for their algorithm and suggested content. Wells said the new elements would come into force 12 months after the laws passed, with Australians able to opt out of addictive online algorithms from late 2027. She nominated design features such as "the toxic popularity meters, the endless scroll" as those that could be banned, but said final decisions would be made by eSafety.

The government will consider setting up an independent ombudsman or watchdog for big tech. The Telecommunications Industry Ombudsman, which has volunteered to act as a big tech watchdog, welcomed the move to set up an independent complaints mechanism. "Online services are a core part of our lives. It's great that Australians will soon have access to a free, fair and independent dispute resolution scheme when big tech gets things wrong."

Other amendments would clarify the definitions of "hostile attitudes to women and gender equality." Wells said the change was "not about disagreeing with feminism or expressing an opinion," but would force platforms to avoid exposing children to content promoting hatred of women, including violence, death threats, coercion or harassment. She had previously stated that "manosphere podcasts" would not be affected.

The amendments would also expand eSafety Commissioner powers to remove "nudify" apps and content and add stronger privacy protections.

Wells had suggested the laws would capture content promoting misogyny, terrorism, suicide, self-harm and eating disorders. Labor said the broader duty-of-care requirement would protect users from certain "harmful" content.

Wells said she had met US ambassador David Brat about the legislation as the government committed to "constructive dialogue" with the United States. "I think we would say there are points of differences, and we're ironing them out," she said. The moves could draw the ire of US-based tech giants and the Trump administration, and Wells had previously said Donald Trump's Truth Social platform would be subject to the laws.

Wells said she had stared down potential legal challenges from some of the world's most powerful firms. On the prospect of a legal challenge, she said: "I was at the high court a couple of weeks ago as the second respondent in the case against the social media minimum age. If people want to have another go, bring it on." Meta, Google, TikTok and Snapchat were contacted for comment.
