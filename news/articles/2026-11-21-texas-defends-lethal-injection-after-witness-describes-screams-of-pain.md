## Texas defends lethal injection after witness describes screams of pain

**Subtype:** fact

- Texas executed Reinaldo Dennes, 70, by lethal injection in Huntsville on Thursday after the state failed to place IV lines in his hands and arms and found veins instead in his neck and left foot.
- Texas Department of Criminal Justice spokesperson Amanda Hernandez said all of the state's "processes and procedures were followed and that it was carried out as planned."
- The Rev. Jeff Hood, Dennes' spiritual adviser in the death chamber, said Dennes was "clearly in tremendous distress" and repeatedly shouted that his right leg was killing him.
- Hernandez said Texas plans no additional inquiry into Dennes' claims of pain and said there were reasons to suspect he was exaggerating.
- It was the ninth execution this year in Texas, which has a long history of using the death penalty as punishment for murder.

## Prison agency says IV attempts in hands and arms failed

Texas prison officials on Friday defended how the state carried out the execution of Reinaldo Dennes, a 70-year-old man who loudly insisted he was in pain and pleaded, "Don't let me suffer like this," during a lethal injection that followed a struggle to find a suitable vein.

Hernandez said initial attempts to place two IV lines into Dennes' hands and arms were unsuccessful. She said she did not know how many attempts were made.

"We feel that all of our processes and procedures were followed and that it was carried out as planned," Hernandez told the Associated Press.

According to Hernandez, Dennes was strapped to the gurney at 6:04 p.m. An IV was successfully placed in the left side of his neck 16 minutes later at 6:20 p.m., followed by another line in his left foot at 6:38 p.m. Witnesses, including Hood and an Associated Press reporter, were escorted into the death chamber before Dennes began his final statement at 6:52 p.m., and lethal drugs began flowing less than a minute later.

"It's not a race — it's not how quickly can we get this done," Hernandez said. "It's something you want to do correctly. There is no normal time limit. It's what it takes for it to be successfully done."

Almost immediately after the drugs began, Dennes exclaimed: "The pain! Go! Go!" He uttered an expletive and implored "the father" to "take this pain." He subsequently shouted, "My right leg is killing me!" and described the sensation as a cramp. After another profanity, he grunted and said, "I don't think this is normal. Take me, father. Don't let me suffer like this. C'mon."

The drugs were stopped at 7:07 p.m., and Dennes was declared dead a minute later — slightly more than an hour after he was restrained in the death chamber, Hernandez said.

Hood, a death penalty opponent who kept his hand on Dennes' shoulder inside the chamber, said he felt like he was holding a bucking bronco. "Twice he said, 'It's not working. It's not working. It's not going how it's supposed to go. This isn't right.' Those types of words kept coming," Hood said. He said Dennes was "clearly in tremendous distress" and called it torture.

Hernandez said the state plans no additional inquiry into Dennes' claims of extreme pain. She said there were reasons to suspect he was exaggerating, adding that he complained even before the lethal drugs were given and grumbled about pain in a leg that did not have an IV line.

Asked whether the high-profile case of Christa Pike, who survived two doses of pentobarbital in Tennessee in September when the drug did not enter her bloodstream correctly, had influenced death row inmates, Hernandez said it "makes you wonder." She said of Dennes: "They watch the news. He was aware of the other executions."

Dr. Paul Wax, executive director of the American College of Medical Toxicology, said he could not think of any reason why an injection of pentobarbital into the left foot or the left side of the neck would cause pain in the right leg.

Associated Press reporter Michael Graczyk, who attended the execution, said witnesses cannot see when the drug starts to be administered. Dennes' foot was covered by a sheet, and the IV in the left side of his neck was not visible from their position. It is not possible to definitively know what caused him to scream in pain. Hernandez said the lethal drugs had not passed their expiration date, though she did not know the date.

Hood called assertions that Dennes was faking pain "disgusting." "We have heard them say repeatedly since last night that this was not botched. That is absolutely, unbelievably absurd," he said.

Dale Baich, an Arizona lawyer who has represented death row inmates and witnessed 17 U.S. executions, said the latest Texas lethal injection raises concerns. "Calling a prisoner's screams an act is not a medical finding. It is an accusation, and the state should be required to produce evidence to support it," Baich said.

The execution was the ninth this year in Texas, which has a long history of using the death penalty as punishment for murder. Executions across the United States have drawn more attention since Pike's case. In May, Tennessee called off the lethal injection of Tony Carruthers after failing for over an hour to establish an IV line. Idaho in 2024 tried eight times to establish a line to execute Thomas Creech before stopping, and Republican Gov. Brad Little subsequently signed a law making a firing squad the primary method of execution.

Associated Press reporter Kate Brumback in Atlanta contributed to this story.
