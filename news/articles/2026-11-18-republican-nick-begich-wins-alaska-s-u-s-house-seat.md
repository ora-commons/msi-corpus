## Hafner voters chose neither Begich nor Hill

Nick Begich III won reelection to Alaska’s U.S. House seat after the Alaska Division of Elections completed a ranked-choice tabulation, defeating independent challenger Bill Hill. Begich received 51.4% of the vote to Hill’s 48.6%, a final margin of 7,385 votes, according to News From The States. The Republican’s victory came as Democrats won close races across the country, a trend the report called a “blue tsunami.”

Begich also drew more votes than any other Republican on Alaska’s general-election ballot. In the state’s U.S. Senate race, incumbent Republican Dan Sullivan had more than 4,000 fewer first-choice votes than Begich.

More than 22,000 Democratic voters chose Eric Hafner rather than Hill for their first-choice House vote, a factor the report said helped Begich. Hill had criticized Begich for supporting President Donald Trump’s policies.

Alaska uses ranked-choice voting in general elections. If no candidate receives more than 50% of first-choice votes, the lowest finisher is eliminated and that candidate’s ballots go to voters’ second choices. Libertarian Jim McDermott received 2.6% of the first-choice vote, and his voters split their second choices somewhat evenly among Begich, Hill and no one; fewer chose Hafner. Hafner was eliminated next with 7.9% of the first-choice vote.

Almost half of Hafner’s voters — 11,496 in total — picked neither Hill nor Begich as an additional option, and the report said that margin was decisive. Begich’s final 7,385-vote advantage followed those transfers.

News From The States described Eric Hafner as a perennial candidate who has run for office from a federal jail cell in New York. Convicted in 2022 of threatening to kill officials in New Jersey, Hafner is serving a 20-year term. He has previously registered to run for federal office in Hawaii and Florida, among other states.

Hafner ran for Alaska’s U.S. House seat as a Democrat in 2024. After the Alaska Division of Elections certified his candidacy, the Alaska Democratic Party sued, arguing that he was ineligible. A lower-court judge ruled against the party, concluding that the U.S. Constitution requires state residency when a person is elected but that Hafner’s ability to run for office was protected by the First Amendment even if he could not serve. The party appealed with only procedural arguments, and the Alaska Supreme Court ruled for Hafner in summer 2024.

Hafner’s mother, Carol Hafner, was a Democratic candidate for U.S. Senate this year and finished fifth in the primary. She has argued on social media that her son’s candidacy is legitimate and that he could serve if President Donald Trump pardoned him. Hill had the Alaska Democratic Party’s endorsement, while Hafner was the lone listed Democrat on the House ballot.

Democratic candidate Matt Schultz, who had been favored by Alaska progressives, suspended his campaign and withdrew after the primary. Hill, despite the party’s backing, declined during the campaign to say whether he would caucus with Democrats or Republicans if elected. Fifteen candidates ran in the August House primary, where Begich finished first, followed by Hill, Schultz and Hafner. Schultz withdrew to support Hill. Three other primary candidates — Democrat John Williams of Fairbanks, Republican Clay Strickland and Democrat Yaquelin Reynoso of Massachusetts — also withdrew, placing eighth-place finisher McDermott in the general election.

In a victory statement, Begich said, “To the hundreds of thousands of Alaskans who made your voice heard this month, thank you for engaging in this important process.” He described serving as Alaska’s sole representative in the U.S. House as an honor and said he looked forward to serving all Alaskans — young and old, rural and urban, Republican, Independent, Libertarian and Democrat alike — during the 120th Congress. He thanked his competitors and said, “Alaska’s best days remain in front of us with opportunities for new responsible resource development, new infrastructure, and new industries and the jobs that come with them.” He said Alaskans must continue working together to fulfill Alaska’s promise: “North to the Future.”

Hill called Begich to concede and said in a written statement: “I wished him well as he continues representing Alaska in Congress, because all Alaskans have a stake in his success.” He also said, “This campaign has always been about taking power back from the DC elites and putting it in the hands of the hardworking people of Alaska. While we did not prevail in this election, we will continue that fight together.”

Begich’s campaign raised significantly more money than Hill’s, but Hill benefited from millions more in spending by third-party groups. Wheel Dog PAC spent almost $9 million. Hill and Wheel Dog PAC ran a two-pronged advertising effort to boost Hill’s name and raise awareness of Hafner’s status. Polling averages during the campaign showed Hill narrowing the margin, but he never eliminated it.

More Alaskans voted in the midterm election than in any other midterm election in state history, and turnout in the primary was high.

First elected in 2022, Begich sponsored seven bills and three repeal resolutions during his first term that passed the Republican-controlled Congress and reached President Donald Trump’s desk. News From The States reported that Begich had become “the most successful freshman lawmaker in the history of the U.S. House of Representatives.” Begich served his first two years as a member of a Republican majority in the House and will return as a member of a shrunken Republican minority.

He will be the lone member of Alaska’s three-person congressional delegation with friendly ties to Trump. The report said Sen. Lisa Murkowski, R-Alaska, was among a few Republicans in the Senate who repeatedly opposed Trump and voted to impeach him after he encouraged the Jan. 6, 2021 insurrection at the U.S. Capitol; Trump has criticized Sen.-elect Mary Peltola, D-Alaska.
