## Investors remain bullish on AI while showing greater caution

Mark Zandi, chief economist for Moody's Analytics, told NPR on November 23, 2026, that Anthropic's IPO was one of several forces behind volatility in AI-related stocks. Anthropic's Wall Street debut started strongly, Michel Martin said, but other technology stocks began sliding on the first day. Zandi, who directs economic research and consulting for clients including businesses and institutional investors, called the market moves the product of "a long list, a confluence of factors" rather than one isolated event.

High valuations topped Zandi's list. He said investors had expected an awful lot from AI companies and marked down those expectations slightly, a change reflected in lower prices.

Referring to Austan Goolsbee of the Chicago Fed, Zandi said the Fed was focused on inflation and would raise rates. He said higher interest rates would make conditions difficult for highly valued stocks.

The midterm elections supplied another concern. Zandi said many candidates ran on anti-data-center development, which made AI investors nervous.

Zandi also pointed to Anthropic's IPO. He called it the largest IPO in history and said it involved "over a hundred billion dollars." He said the scale probably created "some indigestion" and that investors had to sell some AI stocks to raise cash to buy Anthropic.

Zandi said investors remained "enamored with AI" and believed future AI profits would continue to drive the market. He noted that futures were up again that morning, citing Scott, while also observing that markets move both down and up. Investors, he said, anticipated boom times but were showing some caution.

He estimated that the decline was maybe 10% from the peak and called it a "garden-variety correction," saying pullbacks occur every 18 or 24 months. Zandi stressed that his assessment applied "at least so far" and said there was "a lot of script to be written."

Martin said Anthropic had so much hype around it and that she thought the IPO was oversubscribed, with many more people wanting to buy than could get in. Zandi agreed with that assessment.

For small investors, Zandi recommended investing on autopilot rather than reacting to short-term swings. People should put money away every couple of weeks in a stock index fund or 401(k), he said, with a horizon of three or four decades rather than the next month, next year or even 10 years. He said the money could later support retirement or a child's education.

Martin noted that roughly half of Americans do not invest in stocks and asked how volatility could affect them. Zandi said a little over half of Americans own stock, but ownership of any consequence is concentrated among the "top 10, 20, 30% of the income distribution."

Zandi said AI-stock gains had made those owners wealthier over the previous few years and that their spending was driving up inflation and interest rates. He said the effect reaches people who do not own the shares: credit card borrowers pay more, while prospective homebuyers and car buyers can face higher mortgage and auto-loan costs.

For households in that category, Zandi repeated the autopilot recommendation and warned against becoming caught up in alternating periods of depression and euphoria surrounding market swings. His advice was to ignore short-term moves, look through the volatility and follow "Life as usual."
