## The diary records a spiritual crisis that began on Jan. 24, 2021

The Vatican published excerpts from Pope Francis' private diary on Tuesday, revealing that the late pontiff saw the “gates of hell” after recognizing the depths of the clergy sexual abuse scandal and endured a profound spiritual crisis tied to his own temptation to exercise power. Curated by Cardinal Víctor Manuel Fernández, the Argentine theologian who heads the Vatican's doctrine office and one of Francis' closest aides, “The Private Diary of Pope Francis” was released globally in several languages.

The book draws on a leather-bound calendar Francis used as a notebook. It begins in 1992, when Jorge Mario Bergoglio was auxiliary bishop of Buenos Aires, and continues through his time as cardinal archbishop of the Argentine capital and finally as pope. The last entry is dated Aug. 18, 2024. Fernández's commentary contextualizes entries that present Francis as hard on himself, lamenting his own sense of worthlessness and shame and recording the fears, insecurities, dreams and temptations he kept hidden from the world.

The passages about the abuse scandal center on Francis' 2018 visit to Chile, where he created an uproar by discrediting victims of Chile's most notorious abuser. Journalists, including from The Associated Press, challenged him during the in-flight news conference on the return flight. Francis commissioned an investigation and concluded that the victims were right, that he was wrong and that the Chilean hierarchy had misled him.

His diary entries register that change in judgment. Francis later wrote, “Devastated because of the situation in Chile and the dialogue with the journalists on the airplane,” and added: “I became aware of how serious the problem was.” He also wrote, “It was hard for me to admit what happened in Chile; I didn’t think it was so serious.” On June 7, 2018, a few months after returning from the trip, Francis said he “saw the entrance to hell. Pagan seduction in full force.”

In entries from July and August 2018, Francis wrote: “What shame and evil this has brought on the church!” and, “Once more the gates of hell. … May holiness and conversion abound in the midst of so many scandals.” The passages show Francis returning to the language of hell as he reckoned with the scandal's scale and its effects on the church.

The diary also records what Francis called spiritual torments and encounters with the devil. Two of the described encounters occurred in Buenos Aires, and the third followed the 2018 trip to Chile. In a Jan. 24, 2021 entry, Francis described one such meeting: “A few days after returning to Rome, I was in a meeting. I saw him and heard his voice, triumphant possessing everything.” He quoted the devil as saying, “Now I’ve gotten everything I wanted,” and wrote: “Just as I can’t forget his eyes from the previous two, in this case, I can’t forget the powerful raspiness of his voice.”

Fernández dates the onset of Francis' spiritual crisis to that same Jan. 24, 2021. The day before, Francis canceled several public appearances, and the Vatican attributed the decision to a resurgence of his sciatica. Fernández, however, wrote that Francis was suffering from “insupportable” pain caused by an intestinal problem. In July 2021, Francis spent 10 days in the hospital for surgery to remove 33 centimeters (13 inches) of his colon.

The crisis unfolded against a Vatican overwhelmed by a financial investigation that Francis had authorized. In September 2020, he had dismissed a one-time close collaborator, Cardinal Angelo Becciu, convinced that Becciu had embezzled money from the Holy See. The scandal and Francis' belief that a web of corruption had engulfed the Vatican's secretariat of state led to an unprecedented Vatican investigation and years of legal proceedings.

The trial produced convictions, including against Becciu, although much of prosecutors' theory of a vast conspiracy was thrown out. The appeals process is ongoing, with Francis' own role in the investigation a key question for the defense. Fernández's book makes no mention of the trial, though he indicates that the 2021-2022 crisis was “mixed with governance issues, pressure regarding decisions to make, and aridity and desolation.”

Fernández writes that the crisis was primarily connected to Francis' exercise of power, his temptation to omnipotence and his belief that he was above the world. Francis had acknowledged the same tendency publicly in 2013, in his first interview as pope, when he said he had a propensity to wield power in an “authoritarian” way. His critics had often faulted him for a go-it-alone governing style, and he held the authority of an absolute monarch and supreme leader of the world's 1.4 billion Catholics.

Francis interpreted the temptation as the devil's lure toward “reductive curiosity” — the belief that he alone could solve everyone's problems and that he was, in effect, God. By Dec. 30, 2022, Fernández writes, Francis had apparently overcome the crisis by entrusting himself to God. Francis came to see what began on Jan. 24, 2021, “was a powerful invitation from the Lord to become aware of an accumulation of interior darkness that he had to overcome so he could be free.”

Fernández says the crisis also shaped Francis' final years of teaching. He identifies the 2023 text on confidence in God's merciful love and the 2024 encyclical on Christ's human and divine love as deeply spiritual documents borne out of that period. They differ, he writes, in tone and content from the texts often associated with Francis' pontificate.

The published selections also disclose their limits. Fernández says Francis wrote his most confidential thoughts in an indecipherable code, which the book does not reproduce. He says the diary contains hardly any information about church matters, describing it instead as a spiritual repository, and that he omitted entries affecting third parties. The volume does include personal assessments of then-Cardinal Robert Prevost, now Pope Leo XIV, whom Francis described as “A great pastor, a generous missionary and an irreproachable man.”

Francis had shown Fernández the diary during one of their meetings and told him, “From this, you will all see,” essentially entrusting the writings to him after Francis' death on April 21, 2025. Fernández raised the materials with Leo a few weeks after Leo's election, writing: “I added that I wished to study these materials — to make them known and interpret the exact meaning of their contents.” He added, “The Holy Father showed intense interest.”

Posthumous publication of a pope's private spiritual writing is not unusual. Pope John XXIII's autobiography, “Journal of a Soul,” was published by his secretary in 1960, and St. John Paul II's personal notes were published in 2014 after his secretary disregarded his wish that they be burned following his death.
