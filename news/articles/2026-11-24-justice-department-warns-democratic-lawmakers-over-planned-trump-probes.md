## Justice Department warns Democratic lawmakers over planned Trump probes

**Subtype:** fact

- The Justice Department sent letters to leading Democratic lawmakers warning that aggressive oversight of President Donald Trump and his aides could bring criminal prosecution.
- U.S. Attorney Aaron Reitz wrote on Nov. 19 to Rep. Jamie Raskin that planned investigations must have a “legitimate legislative purpose,” and included similar language in letters to other Democratic lawmakers.
- President Donald Trump signed a Nov. 20 executive order requiring federal contractors to notify the White House of congressional information requests and follow instructions to withhold communications the Trump administration says are covered by executive privilege.
- Constitutional law experts interviewed by States Newsroom said congressional oversight authority is sweeping but not unlimited.
- Constitutional scholars were virtually unanimous in saying the Constitution's Speech or Debate Clause would protect Democratic lawmakers from criminal cases for launching investigations into the Trump administration.

## Executive order requires contractors to notify White House of congressional requests

The Justice Department has warned Democratic lawmakers who are preparing investigations of President Donald Trump and his aides that the investigations could expose them to criminal prosecution. The letters sent in recent days turn on the phrase “legitimate legislative purpose.” They come as Democrats prepare to take control of Congress with majorities in both chambers in early January, and as legal experts have widely dismissed the threats as empty because the Constitution affords lawmakers wide protections from prosecution for official actions taken in Congress.

In a Nov. 19 letter to Rep. Jamie Raskin of Maryland, U.S. Attorney Aaron Reitz wrote, “As you should know, whatever investigations you and your fellow Democrats have planned must have a legitimate legislative purpose,” according to States Newsroom. Reitz, the top Justice Department official in the Southern District of Texas and a former Republican candidate for Texas attorney general, included similar language in letters to Rep. Robert Garcia and Sen. Adam Schiff of California, Sen. Elissa Slotkin of Michigan and Sen. Elizabeth Warren of Massachusetts. The lawmakers who received the letters are Democrats likely to play prominent roles in investigating the Trump administration after the party takes control of Congress in January.

States Newsroom said the letters and the phrase within them offered a blueprint for how the White House would try to undermine the investigations and discredit the investigators. The Justice Department was signaling that it would challenge congressional subpoenas seeking documents and testimony from federal officials as driven by retribution and outside the bounds of traditional oversight. The government appeared ready to argue that such requests crossed a line into illegality. The letters allege that Democrats intend to violate federal laws prohibiting people acting in an official capacity from violating others' constitutional rights; those supposedly facing violations would be Trump's associates.

Trump was bracing for the final two years of his term as House and Senate committees prepared aggressive oversight of alleged corruption and mismanagement. Many Democrats credited their majorities in both chambers to voters' frustration with self-enrichment by Trump and other government officials.

White House counsel Will Scharf reinforced that posture in a Nov. 9 letter to Garcia, writing that “abusive and capricious threats” were spreading among Democratic legislators. Scharf warned that the Trump administration “will not be extorted” and said he expected lawmakers to investigate within settled constitutional limits. White House spokesperson Lauren Bis told States Newsroom that Trump was willing to work with anyone “to continue delivering victories for hardworking Americans.” She warned, “If Democrats choose to pursue sham investigations and radical left policies, they are choosing to work against the American people.” The Justice Department did not respond to a request for comment.

President Donald Trump signed an executive order on Nov. 20 requiring federal contractors to notify the White House of any congressional requests for information. Contractors also must abide by any White House instructions to withhold communications that the Trump administration says are covered by executive privilege. The confrontation could end in court, according to States Newsroom, as the parties dispute the scope of congressional subpoenas.

Raskin pressed ahead with his oversight preparations despite Reitz's letters. On Nov. 20, the ranking Democrat on the House Judiciary Committee sent letters to the Justice Department, FBI, Department of Homeland Security, U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement and U.S. Bureau of Prisons, demanding that they preserve all records. The requests included Signal chats, which can be set to auto-delete.

Slotkin responded to Reitz last week, calling congressional oversight an important constitutional responsibility. She called the Justice Department official's threats frivolous and said they violated his ethical duties as an attorney. The nonpartisan watchdog Campaign for Accountability filed a complaint against Reitz with the State Bar of Texas. Slotkin said in a video response, “I’m not going to participate in some sham investigation.” She added, “Every American has freedom of speech. In particular, senators have special privileges and special responsibilities given by the Constitution for oversight.”

Constitutional law experts interviewed by States Newsroom said lawmakers do not have unlimited investigative power, but their oversight authority is sweeping and rooted in the need for information before legislating. John Vile, a professor of political science at Middle Tennessee State University who has written books on constitutional law, said, “Although they are not stated in the Constitution, there is a clear line of cases that say as long as investigations are related to possible legislation, then they can proceed.”

In 1957, the Supreme Court found that the U.S. House Un-American Activities Committee had exceeded the bounds of congressional power after labor organizer John Watkins was prosecuted for refusing to answer lawmakers' questions about whether certain individuals were members of the Communist Party. The court threw out Watkins' conviction, finding that his due process rights had been violated. Reitz cites the case, known as Watkins vs. United States, in his letters to Democrats as he warns lawmakers that their planned investigations could be unlawful. In the court's 6-1 decision, Chief Justice Earl Warren wrote that investigations undertaken solely to punish or for the “personal aggrandizement” of investigators were not permissible. Earlier in the same paragraph, Warren wrote that Congress's power to investigate is “broad” and encompasses looking into both how current law is being applied and possible future law.

Warren wrote: “It includes surveys of defects in our social, economic or political system for the purpose of enabling the Congress to remedy them,” and, “It comprehends probes into departments of the Federal Government to expose corruption, inefficiency or waste.” The Supreme Court reaffirmed the sweeping scope of congressional investigatory powers in 2020 in the 7-2 decision Trump vs. Mazars USA. The justices did not immediately allow a congressional subpoena for Trump's personal financial records to proceed, but the majority repeated Watkins' description of lawmakers' authority. Chief Justice John Roberts wrote, “The congressional power to obtain information is ‘broad’ and ‘indispensable,’” in the majority opinion.

Constitutional scholars in recent weeks were virtually unanimous in arguing that the Constitution's Speech or Debate Clause will protect Democratic lawmakers from criminal cases for launching congressional investigations into the Trump administration. The clause provides that lawmakers, “for any Speech or Debate in either House, they shall not be questioned in any other Place.” Its roots are in England, where members of Parliament feared prosecution for offending the crown.

The protection appeared in a recent dispute involving Minnesota Rep. Ilhan Omar, a Democrat. Federal agents searched Omar's D.C. residence in October, and recently unsealed court documents show that a federal judge asked prosecutors for additional information about the “potential Speech or Debate Clause implications” of the search before signing off on a warrant.

Steve Vladeck, a professor of federal courts at Georgetown University Law Center and a leading Supreme Court expert, wrote Nov. 23 in his newsletter One First that an indictment alleging lawmakers had conspired to deprive individuals of their rights through a “weaponized” oversight process would require proving the illegitimacy of the investigation's subpoenas, hearings or committee votes. He wrote, “Every one of those is a legislative act, for which the members’ motives are off the table,” and added, “When the oversight itself is the alleged crime, the Speech or Debate Clause is arguably at its zenith.”

Lawmakers also cannot be sued for their speech in Congress, a protection they sometimes use for remarks that might otherwise expose them to allegations of defamation. In a dispute last year over remarks on the House floor, South Carolina Republican Rep. Nancy Mace accused her ex-fiance and three other men of being sexual “predators.” A federal judge later dismissed a defamation lawsuit brought against Mace by one of the men.

States Newsroom also reported that the Trump administration had so far failed in other efforts to target Democratic lawmakers over their communications. Just over a year ago, several lawmakers with military or national security backgrounds, including Slotkin, posted a video telling members of the military and intelligence community they should refuse illegal orders. Trump called the video evidence of seditious behavior and, as recently as this month, asked whether the lawmakers would ever face prosecution. The Department of Defense attempted to demote Arizona Sen. Mark Kelly, a retired U.S. Navy captain; a federal judge temporarily blocked the demotion, and the Trump administration appealed.

Even without successful prosecutions, Molly Nixon, a senior fellow in executive power at the libertarian Cato Institute, said the White House can still work to slow investigations. She said the letters and the recent executive order serve as “markers” that could deter cooperation with Congress. Individuals pressed for information might weigh the tradeoffs between quick and full compliance, Nixon indicated. “The fact that if I were a member of Congress I would not be particularly worried about a successful criminal prosecution doesn’t mean the administration doesn’t have a lot of tools at its disposal,” Nixon said.
