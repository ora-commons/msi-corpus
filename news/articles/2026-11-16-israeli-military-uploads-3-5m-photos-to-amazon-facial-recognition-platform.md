## AWS says it lacks visibility into customer workloads in its cloud

The Israeli military uploaded 3.5 million photographs to Amazon's cloud platform in order to use the US company's facial recognition technology to analyse images of Palestinians, The Guardian reported on 16 November, citing leaked documents. The catalogue of images was moved into Amazon's cloud in the early months of Israel's aerial bombardment and ground offensive in Gaza, and was transferred into an Amazon datacentre near Tel Aviv by a department commonly known as Rolling Stone, which sits within a military software unit and holds a vast collection of photos covering Palestinian populations in Gaza and the West Bank.

According to the files and interviews with military sources, Israel has in recent years migrated huge volumes of data, including sensitive intelligence material and information about Palestinians living under occupation, into Amazon's datacentres.

The department informed Amazon of the decision to upload the images in early 2024 so it could use Rekognition, the company's AI-powered image analysis platform, for a facial recognition project. At the time the department was exploring the use of facial recognition technologies to analyse its catalogues of images of Palestinians, several sources with knowledge of its work told The Guardian. It is not clear whether the 3.5 million images were captured at Gaza checkpoints, which after Israel's ground invasion of the enclave in October 2023 served as pop-up surveillance posts where the military routinely collected images of Palestinians as they passed through, often after being forcibly displaced by the bombing campaign.

A military source familiar with facial recognition efforts at the checkpoints said Amazon's cloud infrastructure was used because of the "very large scale" of images collected at the sites. Documents suggest AWS's cloud technology was used at times during 2024 to support the work of the checkpoints, which soldiers referred to as Nakaz, a Hebrew word for drainage. In Gaza, cameras at temporary checkpoints established after the ground invasion captured images of Palestinian people as they approached, and facial recognition was then used to match people to images held in Rolling Stone's systems, sources said.

Amazon Web Services declined to discuss specific customer workloads. A spokesperson for AWS said it had no "visibility" into workloads held by its customers in its cloud, and added: "We respect the privacy of our customers, and we do not discuss our relationship without their consent." A spokesperson for the Israel Defence Forces said it "does not intend to comment on engagements or services related to operational needs".

In early 2024, a small group of Israeli soldiers worked from AWS's offices in Tel Aviv, located on the 28th floor of one of Israel's tallest skyscrapers, exploring ways to use the company's cloud infrastructure to more effectively operate a facial recognition programme underway in Gaza, several military sources said. It is not clear whether their work was made operational or whether it related to the 3.5 million images uploaded to Rekognition. The presence of the group at AWS's offices underscores the close relationship between the Israeli military and the cloud provider. An AWS spokesperson said its customers "regularly visit our offices for briefings and meetings", and added: "Any suggestion that these visits are anything other than routine customer engagement is wrong and unsubstantiated."

The foundation of Israel's relationship with Amazon is Project Nimbus, a 2021 multibillion dollar contract under which AWS and Google provide cloud services to a wide range of government and military clients. The Guardian reported last year that the Nimbus contract contains unusual terms that prohibit the US companies from restricting or suspending Israeli government and military use of their cloud services, even if Israel breaches their terms of service — preventing the kind of unilateral action taken by Microsoft when it disabled the Israeli military's access to technology used to operate a mass surveillance system uncovered by The Guardian and +972 Magazine.

As part of the Nimbus deal, AWS opened new datacentres in Israel in early 2023, allowing Israeli government and military clients to migrate data from their own servers, according to documents, with a large proportion of data also moved from AWS datacentres in Ireland. Files show a central component of the contract was an obligation on AWS to provide the Israeli military with access to a segregated area within its Tel Aviv datacentres, fortified with enhanced digital and physical security measures, used to store sensitive material relating to military operations and intelligence collection. The company and its client described that isolated area as a "walled garden".

The Rolling Stone department migrated significant volumes of the data it holds into that "walled garden" throughout 2023, files show. Documents suggest the department is among AWS's most significant Israeli military clients, and its use of Rekognition in early 2024 to analyse 3.5 million images came after it had expanded its use of AWS products and services to store and analyse its data. A military source involved in the effort, who spoke on the condition of anonymity, said: "The plan [at Rolling Stone] was to migrate pictures, videos, all the confidential intelligence information – everything." The source said the department had planned to move all of its databases from military servers into AWS's cloud.

The Rolling Stone department oversees several expansive databases that collate data on Palestinians, including the system from which the branch takes its name. Used by the military for decades, that system is constantly updated and includes personal and family details, property holdings, and information on checkpoint crossings, work permits and criminal activity. As a result of Israel's control of the population registries covering Gaza and the West Bank, the department has amassed millions of Palestinians' photographs taken for the identity cards issued to every resident when they turn 16 years old. Sources familiar with its work said the images were routinely used for facial recognition programmes, including Blue Wolf, a smartphone app Israeli soldiers use in the West Bank to scan Palestinians' faces and access information about them, including whether they have been flagged as suspects for arrest.

A soldier who provided testimony to Breaking the Silence, an Israeli NGO, in 2016 described Rolling Stone as "a computerised system where all the information relevant to the control of the Palestinian civilian population is centralised".

The new documents reveal the Israeli military's use of Amazon technology, both for experiments with facial recognition and for a more far-reaching project to move its data on to the servers of the computing arm of the conglomerate founded by billionaire Jeff Bezos.

Israel has long used facial recognition technology, particularly in its occupation of the West Bank, where face scanning is routine at checkpoints and soldiers use custom-built smartphone apps to collect images of people living in the Palestinian territory. In the early stages of its campaign in Gaza, Israel used Rekognition to identify hostages captured by militants in the Hamas-led attack on southern Israel on 7 October 2023, and the military also deployed facial recognition technology to identify people it suspected were militants. The New York Times has previously reported that the military experimented with commercially available technology in Gaza to detect faces in crowds and in drone footage, at times wrongly flagging civilians as militants.

The documents also shed light on how the Israeli military uses Amazon Web Services infrastructure to support the collection and processing of Palestinians' personal data, typically gathered indiscriminately, through intrusive means and without their consent, The Guardian reported. The files suggest ties between AWS and the Israeli military deepened during a period in which Israel has been accused of committing grave crimes in Gaza, including genocide — a charge denied by Israel, but supported by a UN commission of inquiry, Israeli human rights groups and genocide experts. The reporting also reflects the Israeli military's embrace in recent years of leading US tech companies to meet the technological demands of war with rapidly scalable storage, computing power and AI-driven services.

The checkpoints became known in Gaza as places where people were pulled aside and detained. Many were taken into Israeli custody and held incommunicado, amounting to enforced disappearances, according to several UN bodies and rights groups. The role of AI in Israel's offensive in Gaza has come under fresh scrutiny after the release of NAZA, a Guardian-produced documentary about the remote surveillance and killing of Palestinian civilians, co-directed by Yuval Abraham. Harry Davies is one of the Guardian producers on NAZA, which is now in cinemas worldwide and will screen for free on the Guardian's website from early December.
