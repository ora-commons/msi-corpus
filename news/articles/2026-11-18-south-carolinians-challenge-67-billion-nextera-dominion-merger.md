## Federal, Virginia and North Carolina regulators must also give their approvals

At a Tuesday public hearing in northeast Columbia on Nov. 17, 2026, about 30 South Carolinians spoke against the proposed $67 billion merger of Florida-based NextEra Energy and Virginia-headquartered Dominion Energy. The hearing was one of three held by state utility regulators across the Lowcountry and Midlands as they weigh whether to approve the takeover that would create the world’s largest regulated utility. About 30 people also protested outside the hearing.

The Public Service Commission scheduled another set of meetings for Dec. 8, from 9 a.m. to noon and 5-9 p.m., both virtual and in person at the Public Service Commission in Columbia. The commission expects a final decision by Jan. 29 under a schedule it accepted in August. If approved, the combined company would serve 10 million customers across four of the nation’s fastest-growing states: Florida, North Carolina, Virginia and South Carolina, where Dominion has 820,000 customers.

The transaction still requires approval from federal regulators and state regulators in Virginia and North Carolina. NextEra, which has a market value of about $194 billion, has proposed a stock swap of just over four-fifths of a NextEra share, or 0.814, for each outstanding Dominion share. Under that proposal, NextEra shareholders would own about 75% of the combined company.

Opponents said NextEra’s promise of monthly bill credits valued at $2.25 billion—$10 for every 1,000 kilowatt-hours used, lasting two years—was “a joke.” The companies’ proposal offered Virginia four years of credits and $100 million in bill assistance and energy-efficiency programs for low-income, disabled and elderly customers. Separately, Virginia’s governor was pushing for $29 monthly credits and a six-year rate freeze.

Anjani Moro, a member of the South Carolina Tenant Union and co-founder of the grassroots Stop NextEra SC Coalition, was among the protesters and spoke against the merger. “No amount of credits or benefits that they promise us makes this a good deal,” Moro said. “A mega monopoly, the world’s largest utility company, will not have our interests at heart.”

Moro said she has also struggled under Dominion. The company expanded into South Carolina in 2019 after SCANA, the parent company of South Carolina Electric & Gas, abandoned a joint effort with state-owned utility Santee Cooper to expand the V.C. Summer nuclear plant. SCANA spiraled toward bankruptcy before Dominion bought what was the only Fortune 500 company based in South Carolina. “Ever since then the points of contact for people have diminished,” Moro said. “I know people that used to be able to pay in person can’t pay in person. And the rates have continued to go up.”

In May, South Carolina regulators approved a nearly 8% rate increase for Dominion Energy South Carolina, the company’s second increase in two years; the higher rates took effect in July. Moro was among those who testified against the increase. “My electric bill was almost $200 when I testified,” Moro told the commissioners Tuesday. “Now it’s almost $400.”

She said she had to withhold paying health insurance to keep up with rent and electric bills while living in a 1,000-square-foot house with her small child. Days after her May testimony, Dominion and NextEra announced their merger intentions. “Their shareholders get $360 million,” Moro said. “We get $10 a month for two years.”

Other people who spoke described monthly Dominion bills of more than $300 in a high-energy-use month, $450 when temperatures drop and $387 for natural gas near Florence. Patty Reid of Winnsboro was among the roughly 30 people outside the hearing. Other opponents cited figures from the California-based Energy and Policy Institute showing that NextEra’s Florida Power & Light had some of the highest profit margins in the country.

The institute’s analysis of Federal Energy Regulatory Commission filings put Florida Power & Light’s 2025 net income at $5 million. The institute estimated that 27% of customers’ monthly bills went toward paying for that, the highest share among the 79 utility companies it analyzed nationwide. Dominion Energy South Carolina ranked 41st in the analysis, with 15% of customers’ bills going to company profits.

“They (NextEra) are a company that comes and just tries to acquire other utilities, and their goal is to make as much money as possible,” Moro said. NextEra has sought to expand through utility acquisitions for years, though with little success. Proposed purchases of Hawaiian Electric, Texas-based Oncor and Duke Energy fell through, as did its bid for Santee Cooper after the failed V.C. Summer expansion.

Beyond profits, 2024 data from the U.S. Energy Information Agency showed Florida Power & Light ranked second in the country for shutting off customers’ power when they could not or did not pay their bills, according to the Energy and Policy Institute. “The next year they asked for the largest rate increase in the history of the United States,” Moro said. “They got it.”

Opponents told the South Carolina Public Service Commission that they did not trust the commissioners to keep NextEra’s rates in check. Annie Mahaffey, a member of the Party for Socialism and Liberation in Columbia, said, “Why would we believe that it would be any different in South Carolina?” She added, “Oversight by a Public Service Commission that continues to favor greedy corporations provides no peace of mind.”

Mahaffey, a single mother who works three jobs, said she had difficulty paying Dominion’s current rates. “I didn’t turn my heat on this fall until the freeze over this past weekend,” she said. She said her thermostat remained set at 62 degrees and that anxiety about the accumulating bill often woke her at night and kept her from returning to sleep. Commissioner Swain Whitfield asked Mahaffey whether she had sought help under Dominion Energy’s efficiency programs to reduce her energy use. She said she had, but Dominion told her she did not qualify because she is a renter; her landlord would have to apply.

In an October meeting with state legislators on the Public Utilities Review Committee, NextEra executives batted around the idea of reinstating the South Carolina Electric & Gas name. The move would likely stir bad memories among customers who continue to pay SCANA’s share of $9 billion in debt from the failed nuclear expansion. Dominion said it will maintain its Virginia headquarters and its South Carolina offices.
