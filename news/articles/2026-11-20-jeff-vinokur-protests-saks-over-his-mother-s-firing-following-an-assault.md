## New York City protections do not specifically cover non-sexual assault, Jump says

Jeff Vinokur is paying people to hold signs outside Saks Fifth Avenue in New York, pressing the retailer to answer for his mother's termination. He hired the sign holders after his mother, Laza Susuni, was fired from her job when she reported an assault and is using wealth from the sale of his edtech company to fund the campaign and push for broader worker protections. Beginning on 12 November, Vinokur said he was paying anywhere from more than one to several dozen people to stand outside Saks each day from 1pm to 7pm throughout the holiday shopping season, potentially indefinitely. The signs have included the message: "MY MOM IS THE 7th VICTIM. BEATEN AT SAKS. THEN FIRED. I WANT ANSWERS." Vinokur is also seeking broader New York law to prevent employers from firing workers who have been victims of violent crime both on and off the job.

Late in the afternoon of 28 June, Vinokur received a call from his mother that sent him running. "I was assaulted," he recalled Susuni saying. Vinokur, 36, is a popular science educator known as the "Dancing Scientist." He was visiting Susuni in New York City when the call came; he bounded out of bed, threw on clothes and rushed to the department store without taking a moment to brush his teeth.

Susuni, 62, was working in the women's ready-to-wear section at Manhattan's Saks Fifth Avenue when a client attacked her in the dressing room. Susuni said she was kicked in the stomach, evaluated at a hospital and discharged later that day without permanent physical injuries. She said she reeled from panic. A court document reviewed by The Guardian corroborates that a woman was arrested at that location on assault and harassment charges.

Susuni, who came to the United States from Russia as a teen, said, "I could not sleep at all." She feared the client would return to hurt or punish her or send someone to stab her because the client had been handcuffed in front of the client's own children. Susuni said she asked Saks for five days off to recover from her anxiety, and Saks terminated her instead.

Vinokur contacted lawyers because he believed such a firing would surely be illegal. When an attorney said there was nothing he could do, Vinokur thought: "This guy is an idiot, this cannot be, let’s go talk to other lawyers." Vinokur and Susuni spoke with about 10 attorneys, who he said all told him the same thing: no law protected against what had happened.

With legal action unavailable, Vinokur first considered a protest, maybe signs that said "My mom was beaten here and fired." He then began thinking through the plan "as a scientist," wondering whether anyone would care about a beating. "It's shocking, but that's not crazy," he said. Vinokur said he had not originally set out to change the system; he wanted Saks held accountable. "It was the realization that they got away with this, that they did this to my mom, and they got away with this because there's no law against it," he said.

Vinokur holds a PhD in biochemistry from UCLA. His media savvy propelled him from science educator to edtech founder, and the vast resources he had after selling the company funded the ongoing in-person protest against Saks. Vinokur said, "I have this $100m startup exit. I was majority shareholder." He added, "I don't have a boat. I don't fly private. It's not my style – but I can hold them accountable in a way that I think maybe nobody else can." Describing his ability to act, he said, "I have the money. I have the time. I have the temperament. I can take risks. I can't be canceled at this point."

Vinokur started researching whether anything similar had happened at another Saks store. An internet search yielded another example, so he deployed artificial intelligence to further research crime-related termination allegations and found more. "Now my jaw starts dropping," he said, adding that the issue was "bigger than just a woman beaten." His Saksfiles.com website outlines Susuni's situation and several other alleged instances at Saks stores where former employees have claimed they were fired in relation to being purportedly victimized. He hired three freelance researchers who spent "hundreds of hours" investigating the allegations and producing legal information about past cases for the website. He thought the issue might be "the tip of the iceberg" and said, "I've got to do something about this."

Vinokur described the campaign as a response to a power imbalance. "There's a power element of this – a billion-dollar corporation, the disposable minimum wage worker who is my mom," he said. He added: "To me, it's like: wait a second, I have power now." He called the demonstration an unusual, deliberate form of protest. "It's a paid protest. We're owning it," he said. He paid for the daily action because, in his view, holding signs for one day would not draw attention: "if we just go hold signs for a day, nobody cares."

The Guardian's legal analysis distinguished an employer's stated reason from the practical effects of retaliation. An employer almost certainly would not tell an employee they were fired for being a crime victim, but a workplace might effectively do so by taking issue with time off or medical requests, or by making spurious claims about lapsed job performance after the crime. Alice Jump, a partner at the Reavis Page Jump law firm, said such employer behavior "doesn't seem rational." She imagined employers might use "taking a lot of time off, or being distressed or emotionally or physically compromised" as an excuse to terminate a crime victim.

New York state human rights law bars employers from discriminating against, or firing, people because they are domestic-violence victims. State law also prohibits employers from penalizing or firing crime victims for taking time off to appear in court or meet with prosecutors, provided the employees give notice of the absence. New York City law separately makes it illegal for an employee to be fired because the employee is a victim of domestic violence, sexual assault or stalking, Jump said. "So if it's a non-sexual assault, then you're not specifically covered," Jump said. "The law doesn't extend to all crime victims." Jump also said no federal law provides termination protections for crime victims.

Jump said amending legal protections to shield employees who suffer other assaults or violent physical attacks would not be difficult. "Any kind of assault or violent physical act – you shouldn't be fired because you are a subject of that."

Vinokur is demanding that Saks apologize, investigate and fire those responsible for his mother's firing, and he wants the retailer to help create a law that would "protect every worker at every company." Asked for comment, Saks' parent company, Exemplar Luxury Group, said: "We take matters of employee and customer safety and security extremely seriously." Exemplar also said: "It is the company's firmly held policy that we do not retaliate against any employee who reports violations of our policies or the law. All reports are investigated promptly and thoroughly." The statement did not address Susuni's account, the reason for her termination or the allegations compiled on Saksfiles.com.
