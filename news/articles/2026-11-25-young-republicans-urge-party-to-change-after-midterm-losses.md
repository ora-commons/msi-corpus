## Members cite affordability, messaging and Trump's role in party strategy

Several dozen younger members of the Republican Party gathered at a brewery in Fort Worth, Texas, three weeks after the midterm elections to assess the party's losses and consider its future. The meeting was organized by the Tarrant County Young Republicans.

The chapter's previous leader resigned shortly before the midterms because she could not bring herself to vote for some of the party's candidates. Tarrant County, once a conservative stronghold, flipped blue.

Democrats won the U.S. Senate race and the Texas governor's office, their first statewide victories in over three decades. There was no consensus about the Republican Party's path forward, but several attendees said they were bored by "owning the libs" and put off by meme-driven political messaging they viewed as flippant in the face of real challenges. Many said President Donald Trump had not followed through on his promises, while others advised party leaders to "grow up," "take themselves seriously" and "be humble."

Texas Young Republican Federation chair Derrick Wilson, whose coalition includes the Tarrant County chapter, said, "Everyone has someone to blame, and that finger is never pointed at ourself," and added, "So we're going to have to get past that and figure out, what did we do wrong?"

The meeting opened with someone introduced by Wilson as a "survivor" of the midterms. State Appeals Court Judge Brian Walker, a Republican who won reelection, said the party had stumbled down the wrong path with Trump-style belligerent politics led by the "loudest" and "craziest" voices.

Robert Coe, a 29-year-old in a black cowboy hat, murmured agreement as Walker spoke. Coe had voted for Trump three times and still voted for Republicans in the midterms, but he gave the party an approval rating of 4 out of 10 after seeing little progress on affordability. Coe said, "Me and millions of other Americans are just tired of that, and we're not seeing any viable solution to it," and added, "It's almost as if the people in power don't even want to try and solve it."

Coe, the attendee who wanted party leaders to "grow up," said the party had put him off with "the high school level name-calling, the infighting on an almost childish level." Citing Trump's attacks on talk show hosts, Coe said, "there is no decency."

Andrew Gochis, a 29-year-old coach and teacher who has worked in politics, voted for a mix of candidates rather than only Republicans. Gochis said Republicans had conflated being respectful and being weak. The party has to "do better," he said, at standing on their principles of deportations or opposition to abortion while also treating people such as immigrants or women who get abortions as "a human being that has value."

Stephen Nesbitt, a 26-year-old former U.S. Marine, advocated a more aggressive approach. "The Republican Party needs to get meaner," Nesbitt said. "We need to get stricter, we need to triple down." He listed non-Christians, immigrants and communists as the "enemy." His view aligned with some who believed the Make America Great Again movement had not gone far enough, but he was an outlier among those interviewed at Monday's meeting.

Wilson wants the party to "return to its roots" of "fiscal and social conservatism." He and the state organization are currently in "listening mode" to be better prepared ahead of 2028, a strategy he felt was missed before the midterms. On housing, Wilson said, "Young people wanted to buy homes and start families," followed by, "then they look on the news and they see Trump say, 'I don't want housing prices to go down, I want them to go up.'"

Trevor Carr, 25, attended the meeting for the first time because he viewed the midterm elections as a repudiation of the party's old guard and wanted to play a role in its future. Carr did not vote in the midterms because he doubted Trump would fulfill his promises. He wants a party less focused on loyalty to one man and more closely tied to an ideologically sound platform.

Carr said Trump's approach was once helpful for shaking up the system 10 years ago, but "it's served its purpose, and it's time to move on to be a serious political movement."

The gathering did not settle the party's platform. Several attendees gave similar advice — "grow up," "take themselves seriously" and "be humble" — even as their prescriptions for the party diverged. Affordability, party messaging, Trump's influence and the treatment of immigrants and women who get abortions remained central to the debate.
