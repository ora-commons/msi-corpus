## Jeffries in line to become first Black House speaker

WASHINGTON — House Democrats have secured at least 258 seats in the 435-member chamber, their largest general election result in decades, after Democrat Chuck Hubbard defeated Republican Rep. Virginia Foxx in North Carolina. The tally was reached Sunday, leaving four House races outstanding: one each in Minnesota and Tennessee and two in Louisiana that are heading to a December runoff. Republicans will have at least 173 seats in the minority.

Democrats have gained 44 House seats so far, four more than the 40 they picked up in Trump's first midterm in 2018. Their wins spanned from the strongest Republican states to the Midwest and East Coast, as voters angry over high prices and the Iran war and concerned over corruption made clear their discontent with Trump.

The majority will be bigger than any the party has seen in elections since Bill Clinton was first elected president in 1992, when Democrats won 258 seats, and larger than the 257 won in the election of Barack Obama in 2008 — though not quite the high-water mark of the Franklin D. Roosevelt era, which saw House Democratic majorities swell past 300.

With the Senate also in Democratic hands — a 56-seat majority in the 100-member chamber, their biggest advantage in over a decade — and a Republican president in the White House, the result sets up divided government. Emboldened Democrats are preparing a 100-day agenda to address the high costs of living while confronting Trump with a vast oversight agenda for the remainder of his second term.

Rep. Jason Crow of Colorado, a former Army Ranger, said the pressure that comes with the majority is warranted.

"Like, hell yeah, we should feel pressure," Crow said at the Capitol. "There are people dying in this country and people losing their healthcare, losing their livelihoods, losing their farms, being terrorized from ICE," he said, referring to U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement. "If you don't feel a sense of urgency and pressure in this job, and a desperation to deliver for folks, then you don't belong under this Capitol dome."

Rep. Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez, D-N.Y., the progressive leader, called the numbers "a mandate for change." "This is a historic moment for our country. This is one of the largest classes we have seen," she said at the Capitol.

Rep. Tom Suozzi, a centrist Democrat from New York and an accountant by profession, said House Democrats should focus 75% of their time on affordability issues and 25% on accountability. "Everything should be tied back to the people — and how it's affecting the people," he said, adding that investigations into Trump's financial dealings should connect the dots: "How does that affect people's pocketbooks?"

As part of the 100-day agenda, Democrats are planning swift votes to reverse Republicans' Medicaid cuts and restore the Affordable Care Act tax credits that Republicans allowed to expire. They have also vowed to marshal their subpoena powers and launch a flurry of investigations to exert Congress's role as a check on presidential power, including plans to probe how Trump and his family have become wealthier since he returned to office. Democratic leader Hakeem Jeffries of New York, who is in line to become House speaker, has not ruled out trying to impeach Trump for a third time.

The White House has issued a warning to Democrats over their planned probes into the administration, going as far as claiming that such investigations — a standard oversight function of Congress — may amount to "blatant criminality."

The wide margin contrasts with the past two years, when House Speaker Mike Johnson, R-La., struggled to keep the House functioning with a narrow 218-214 Republican majority, meaning any few GOP lawmakers could derail the agenda. Jeffries is poised to make history as the first Black speaker of the House if his colleagues elect him to the job on Jan. 3, 2027, and he will be able to lose dozens of Democrats on any given vote and still have the 218 needed for passage in the face of GOP opposition.

"We're not a monolith — people come to the caucus with different ideas — it's OK," said Rep. Troy Carter, D-La., who is expected to lead the Congressional Black Caucus.

Republicans saw their number of House seats fall to one of the lowest tallies in decades. They are far from the end of the Obama administration, when House Republicans won 247 seats in the 2014 election, and from the rise of Newt Gingrich, when they won 230 seats in 1994. The GOP's high-water marks include the 1920 election, with 301 seats for House Republicans.

Rep. Jim Jordan of Ohio, who was elected by his colleagues to be the Republican leader in the minority, vowed to fight their way back to power. "Over the next two years, House Republicans will fight for American families," Jordan said.
