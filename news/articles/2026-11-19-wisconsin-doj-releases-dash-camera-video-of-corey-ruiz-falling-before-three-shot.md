## Officer says Ruiz’s expression and body did not change

The Wisconsin Department of Justice released investigative files Thursday on the July 22 shooting of 38-year-old Black man Corey Ruiz by Kiel Baitinger-Peterson, a white Madison police officer. The files included photographs and video. One police dash-camera recording captured three officers trying to wrestle Ruiz into submission as they repeatedly yelled, “He’s got a knife! He’s got a knife!” The video shows a knife in Ruiz’s right hand with the blade pointed downward. A fourth officer arrived and deployed a Taser; Ruiz stumbled and fell onto his right side before Baitinger-Peterson fired three shots at close range.

Records released Thursday also include an interview with Baitinger-Peterson conducted days after the shooting. He said he got on his knees and grabbed Ruiz’s right arm before realizing Ruiz was holding a knife. He said he struggled to control the arm and feared Ruiz would stab the officers. Baitinger-Peterson said he fired a third time because he did not think the first two shots had struck Ruiz because Ruiz’s facial expression and body did not change. He did not respond to an emailed request for comment Thursday.

At a Thursday news conference, civil rights attorney Ben Crump, who represents the Ruiz family and was surrounded by family members, said the videos Ismael Ozanne showed the family did not justify the shooting. “In the video you see they tackle him, they tase him, they kick him and then the police officer executes him,” Crump said. “The video is the objective unbiased witness here.”

Crump said the medical examiner told the family that Corey Ruiz would have survived had the third shot not been fired and struck his head. He described the case as one of the worst cases of excessive force he had worked on and said the video showed Ruiz being hit in the chest and the final shot hitting his head. Asked whether the family would file a lawsuit, Crump said his team would “explore every possible avenue.”

Baitinger-Peterson, an officer with more than a decade of law-enforcement experience, was one of four officers who responded on July 22 to calls about someone checking out parked cars and an alleged burglary attempt. Police said the officers surrounded Ruiz, who was on a bicycle. The Wisconsin Department of Justice said immediately after the shooting that two officers attempted to use a Taser on Ruiz but were not successful. It said Ruiz “brandished a knife and injured” Baitinger-Peterson.

The file release came the same day that Dane County District Attorney Ismael Ozanne announced that Baitinger-Peterson would not face criminal charges. The decision drew calls for justice from family members and community advocates. Adam Ruiz, Corey Ruiz’s brother, said the prosecutor had “mistreated” his family. “We’re destroyed,” Adam Ruiz said. “We won’t accept this.”

Ozanne said he reached his decision after reviewing evidence that included police reports, diagrams, surveillance videos, police squad-car videos and citizen videos. He said: “I conclude it was reasonable for the officer to use deadly force when this person resisted officers, produced a knife, and was not affected by multiple attempts to use Tasers while officers attempted to take him into custody.” Ozanne also said his decision was a correct interpretation of the law. “A person does not have to like and/or agree with the law, however I am sworn to apply the law to the facts at hand,” he said. “The law applies equally to members of law enforcement and to those who are not.”

Madison Police Chief John Patterson said he had trust in the city’s police officers but realized the decision not to bring charges would bring various emotions. He urged protesters to remain peaceful. Patterson said Madison police would conduct an internal review of the shooting and that all officers involved would remain on administrative leave. Baitinger-Peterson and the other three officers who interacted with Ruiz had been on paid administrative leave since the shooting.

Jim Palmer, executive director of the Wisconsin Professional Police Association, said in a statement that the conclusion that the officer’s use of deadly force was legally justified “is supported by the evidence and consistent with the legal standards governing the use of force by law enforcement officers.”

The July 22 killing was the third time since 2012 that Madison police had shot and killed someone in the same neighborhood. It sparked days of protests and calls for police reform to address racial justice issues, and city leaders were bracing for more protests after the no-charge decision.

Madison police officers do not wear body cameras, so no body-camera footage exists from the shooting, although bystanders recorded the confrontation. City officials said all Madison police officers could be equipped with body cameras by the end of 2027 thanks to a $350,000 donation from a local nonprofit announced in September. The donation will help cover operational expenses, while other costs are being funded through grants and other means.

Associated Press journalists Claudia Lauer in Philadelphia and Sudhin Thanawala in Atlanta contributed.
