## Halloween candy prices jump 15.3% as shoppers trim treat spending

**Subtype:** fact

- Halloween shoppers in the United States are paying sharply more for seasonal treats, with the Groundwork Collaborative and the Century Foundation finding popular chocolates and confectionery cost 15.3% more this year than in 2025.

## NielsenIQ data show Halloween treat sales down 11.7% by mid-September

Demetrio Beltres, a 33-year-old financial adviser, was browsing the rows of colour options at a Target in Manhattan, searching for Halloween bargains for trick or treaters. He said he was spending more than his usual $15 to $20 this year because prices were much higher.

"I'm trying to spend at least $20 to $30 just to make sure that I get the kids something," he said.

Beltres is not alone in finding the holiday costlier than expected. Analysis from the left-leaning non-profit Groundwork Collaborative and the Century Foundation found the cost of popular Halloween chocolates and confectionery was 15.3% higher this year than in 2025.

Separate research from personal finance firm Finance Buzz found prices for Halloween treats have soared by 71% over the past five years — a much bigger increase than the general pace of price rises over the same period.

The price of confectionery generally has been pushed up by higher cocoa prices. The cost of a tonne of cocoa skyrocketed to a high of more than $10,700 in January 2025, according to figures from the International Monetary Fund via the Federal Reserve Bank of St Louis, as climate change contributed to severe weather which affected supplies.

"The cocoa that is now being consumed was bought when the prices were high," said Stephen Zagor, an adjunct associate professor at Columbia Business School who specialises in food businesses.

Cost of living pressures have also increased, with inflation running at 3.7%, and fuel prices remain high. Diesel, at $6.32 a gallon, is only 20 cents below the record reached in September.

People shopping for Halloween treats in New York were noticing the price pressures. "Everything is expensive, so it does not surprise me that Halloween candy is expensive too," said Mallory, who declined to give her last name.

Zagor said people have only so much to spend, and when everyday essentials are costing more, they may feel less inclined to make this the most extravagant Halloween. "I think the holiday's gotten scary, more from an economic [perspective], than it might be for scaring people at the door," he said.

The combined cost of living pressures have affected how much people are buying in the run-up to the holiday, according to consumer and market research company NielsenIQ. It found Halloween treat sales were down 11.7% by the middle of September, and by the middle of October sales were down 6.7%.

Chris Costagli, NielsenIQ's vice-president of US food thought leadership, said shoppers appeared more willing to spend as Halloween neared, but remained cautious.

"Sales have improved considerably from where we started in September, with smaller packs gaining ground while larger sizes continue to struggle," he said. "This feels like a consumer who still wants the occasion, but is paying much closer attention to what goes into the basket."

Zagor, who is a fan of Halloween as well as traditional Halloween treats, said cutting back on costs could present a good opportunity to offer trick or treaters a different kind of experience when they come to the door.

"It could be how you present the candy when kids come to your house as much as the one piece of candy that you're going to give," he said.

Options he suggested include using a spooky fake skeleton hand to share out treats, rather than letting children reach into a bowl, or dressing up to meet trick or treaters. Zagor said making treats at home and inviting people over to make them together is another route.

"You could certainly do something slightly magical, slightly interesting," he suggested. "The trick literally could be as important as the treat."
