## Green Units patrol Afghanistan’s last forests amid mass replanting drive

## Planting reached about 126 million seedlings and cuttings, but survival remains uncertain

Deep in the mountains of eastern Afghanistan, a Green Unit police team climbs steep slopes and treks through deep ravines for days. The patrol members wear military fatigues, with automatic rifles slung over their shoulders. The Green Units are a special task force established by the Taliban government to safeguard Afghanistan’s last remaining forests.

From the air, vast swaths of land appear devoid of vegetation. Ridge after barren ridge, Afghanistan’s mountains stretch into the distance, hues of brown unbroken by any hint of green.

Four decades of war, climate change, uncontrolled logging and timber smuggling have decimated Afghanistan’s forests, sometimes resulting in deadly flooding and landslides.

The most severe deforestation occurred between the Soviet invasion in 1979 and the fall of the first Taliban regime in 2001. Afghanistan lost an estimated 50% of its forests during that period, said Mohammad Nasir Shalizi, a research assistant professor at North Carolina State University’s Department of Forestry and Wildlife Resources.

Much of that loss was due to illegal logging and timber smuggling, particularly high-value oak, cedar and other conifers in the eastern provinces. The U.N. Food and Agriculture Organization said war, fires and heavy reliance on wood for heating, cooking and construction also contributed, as did climate change, agricultural growth, construction and mining.

The FAO said centuries ago forests covered about 5% of what is now Afghanistan. Woodland dominated by pistachios, almonds and junipers covered around 33%, but forests and shrubland now account for just 2.8%.

The United Nations says Afghanistan is among the nations most vulnerable to climate change, which is caused mainly by the burning of gas and oil, and among the least prepared to adapt. Sparse tree cover is already raising temperatures, said Abdul Hadi Achakzai, head of the Environmental Protection Training and Development Organization, a nongovernmental organization focused on climate change adaptation and ecology in Afghanistan.

Tree roots help prevent soil erosion, so deforestation can have deadly consequences in Afghanistan, where avalanches and flood-triggered landslides often claim hundreds of lives. With climate change driving frequent extreme weather, forest regeneration is critical. The Aga Khan Foundation has several forest-planting projects in Afghanistan’s mountains and prioritizes areas where communities live below treeless slopes. The projects are essentially “disaster-proofing the communities,” said Apoorva Oza, the foundation’s global program lead for agriculture and climate change. It is “not only a livelihood or an environmental thing,” Oza said. “It is actually a life-saving intervention.”

Successive Afghan governments have backed efforts to safeguard remaining trees and regenerate lost woodland. The current government and the previous U.S.-backed government both enforced laws to protect forests and supported tree planting. After the Taliban seized power in 2021, it created the forest patrol units. In the five years since, authorities have planted around 126 million seedlings and cuttings, said Sher Mohammad Hatami, spokesperson for the Ministry of Agriculture, Irrigation and Livestock. Afghan officials were invited to the U.N. climate summit, COP31 in Turkey, as observers for the second time since the Taliban came to power.

Mohammad Halim Rafi, spokesperson for Afghanistan’s National Statistics and Information Authority, said forest cover increased by 427,000 hectares (1.06 million acres) over the past 15 years. Shalizi said the rate of deforestation slowed considerably over the past two decades and there are indications that forests are slowly returning in some areas.

Shalizi, who was also on the faculty of Kabul University’s Forestry and Natural Resources department from 2009 to 2015, cautioned that the planting won’t necessarily result in healthy forest regeneration. “They plant trees, they have those numbers, but they do not come back and check how many of those trees that they planted survive,” he said. “Usually … the mortality rate is very high.” The main threat forests now face, he said, is gradual degradation from mismanagement, such as allowing livestock to graze on saplings. A forest patrol “helps with the deforestation, like a direct deforestation or material cutting of trees. But it’s not going to help with degradation.”

The eastern province of Kunar, which borders Pakistan, is one of the few parts of Afghanistan where sizable forests remain. A bone-jarring three-hour drive along a rough dirt road through the mountains leads to one area where Green Units patrol to prevent illegal logging of pine, oak and cedar. Each 10-man unit patrols for around 10 to 12 days at a time, often alongside locals who know the mountains well. Countrywide, about 1,000 men serve in forest patrols, Hatami said.

“I can now provide full assurance regarding our forests: no one in Kunar is currently able to cause them any harm,” said Sgt. Khair Ul Rahman, 22, who has served in a Green Unit for five years.

For Master Sgt. Mohammad Naeem, Kunar is far removed from his home province of Helmand, a largely desert region in southern Afghanistan. “Forests possess great beauty, which is why I came here,” said Naeem, who has served in Kunar for two years. “I am very happy in my role here, surrounded by all this beauty.”

Cutting down live trees is strictly prohibited, and even felling dead ones requires a permit, said Kunar police command spokesperson Maj. Faridullah Dehqan. Humans “have a vital need for oxygen and clean air, a need that can be met solely and exclusively by forests,” Dehqan said. “Therefore, the protection and preservation of forests are of great importance, and it is a duty incumbent upon all of us to protect them.”

While the previous U.S.-backed Afghan government also strictly controlled logging, a lucrative wood-smuggling trade flourished across the Pakistan border. Afghan authorities say their crackdown has curbed that smuggling.

Green Units also conduct community outreach and education. Their work includes explaining a hunting ban to encourage wildlife to return, Rahman said. “We visit forested areas, gather people together and speak to them about the importance of forests and the harmful consequences of cutting down trees,” he explained. Rahman said the public-awareness campaign is not only a directive from Kabul but also in line with the teachings of the Prophet Muhammad. “In an Islamic society,” he said, “obeying such directives is a mandatory duty.”
