## Conservative outlets split over distancing from Trump after midterms

## Some weigh a post-Trump pivot; Newsmax and OAN say nothing changes

On the night of 3 November, conservative hosts and commentators on Fox News displayed an almost virtuosic ability to avoid directly blaming Donald Trump for the Republican party's defeat, instead pinning the blame on impertinent voters who didn't grasp the strength of the economy.

"In some sense, Mr Trump was too successful in the first year and didn't sell it enough in the second year," said Larry Kudlow, a Fox Business Network host who served as the president's director of the National Economic Council during his first administration.

Two weeks on, the wipeout is facilitating difficult conversations about the future of the conservative media ecosystem — and whether outlets that target a Republican-voting audience need to create some distance from the president. Some other outlets, like the Maga-aligned Real America's Voice News network, are planning to go the other direction and double down on Trump.

"The whole landscape can now be reordered, and I think it will be," said Steve Bannon, a former chief strategist to Trump during his first administration who now hosts a show for Real America's Voice.

Several conservative media personalities pinned the blame on Fox News for leading the party — and Trump — astray, with former host Tucker Carlson saying that the results signaled the end of "the Fox News era." Carlson didn't respond to a message seeking comment.

Bannon, who contends that Rupert Murdoch has long "hated Trump," predicted that the network will use the party's recent "rejection" at the polls to move away from Trump and embrace more traditional, George W Bush-era Republican priorities. "They're going to try to kick Trump into the dustbin," he said. "They clearly have moved beyond Trump and they want to move beyond Trumpism … What they're going to do is, with a thousand cuts, convince their ageing audience that Maga and what Maga stands for is too extreme."

In contrast, Bannon said he expects Real America's Voice and other outlets to "triple down" on the Maga movement and "populist nationalism."

Bannon is not alone in his criticism of Murdoch's media empire. Conservative commentator Ann Coulter said she views the channel and its sister outlet, the New York Post, as "90% responsible for the GOP wipeout in the midterm elections," blaming voices in the Murdoch media empire for encouraging Trump to launch — and sustain — an unpopular war against Iran.

Atlanta-based conservative radio host Erick Erickson said a realignment away from Trump will probably start in his medium before it carries over to conservative television — though he said it will take time, and will depend on how important access is for certain personalities. "The more often you spend time at Mar-a-Lago, the less likely you are to distance yourself from Trump, because you still want in," said Erickson, who vocally opposed Trump in 2016 before coming around ahead of his 2020 re-election bid.

Charlie Sykes, a former conservative radio host who also opposed Trump from the beginning, said the election has shown "cracks in that alternative reality silo" on Trump-aligned channels and platforms. "In general, I think the scope of the blowout was just so great that even deep in the rightwing media ecosystem, they had to feel the tremors of it," said Sykes, a former MS Now commentator who now writes on Substack. "And so that has been kind of interesting, this sense of like, 'OK, we need to really come to grips with this and maybe figure out a way to move on.'"

Trump's decision to attack Iran in February fomented division within the conservative media ecosystem, with many commentators viewing it as an abdication of his promise to abstain from foreign wars and focus on the US. "I think those divisions are just going to widen," Sykes said.

The Daily Caller, which was co-founded by Carlson in 2010 as something of a conservative alternative to the New York Times, made waves ahead of the midterms for calling out Trump's war against Iran — and the high prices that have come out of it. "The Daily Caller is clearly positioning itself for a landscape not just post-midterms but post-Trump, and for a bitter and intensifying intraparty media battle," the digital publication Semafor wrote on 1 November.

In an interview, Daily Caller editor-in-chief Amber Duke said the publication is more focused on reflecting the mood of the American people — particularly younger people affected by a challenging economy — than boosting Trump and the Maga movement. "I think what we are doing is so unique that we are really setting ourselves apart," she said. "We view ourselves as a principled, America-first outlet. I think there's a difference between Maga and America-first."

Duke also acknowledged the economic opportunities that drive support for the president. "Trump is a content goldmine. He drives a lot of traffic, a lot of clicks," she said. "That content mine is eventually going to dry up. I would definitely be concerned if I had my entire business model tied around this uniquely celebrity-oriented newsmaker. How am I going to continue to stay in business after that? For us, it's: Let's just tell the truth to the American people, and that truth is not going to be dependent on any one person or political party."

Some conservative outlets that are closely tied to Trump, however, don't see any reason to do anything differently. "Nothing changes with us," Newsmax chief executive Christopher Ruddy said. "We're going to continue reporting on the administration and we will continue to cover the Congress. We make a very concerted effort to always get both sides on our reports and in our TV opinion segments."

Charles Herring, president of the far-right One America News Network, echoed that sentiment: "Our reporting doesn't change based on the political pendulum or who resides in the White House. We are working hard every day to further enhance our coverage for our viewers."

Still, there are limits to how far some conservative media organizations can stray from the president, considering the economic imperative to retain a pro-Trump audience. "The thing to understand about conservative media is that it's not necessarily always top down," Sykes said. "It's often: What will the audience tolerate? … Nobody wants to get too crosswise with their audience."

The vast majority of conservative news sites have already seen declines in online traffic in recent months, according to industry tracker Howard Polskin, an early warning sign for the party's midterm chances. "Audiences are not buying what Maga media is selling anymore," he said.

"My prediction would be that in the next two years, rightwing media will become fractured with more infighting, breaking into two camps: pro-Maga/Trump and anti-Maga/Trump," Polskin added. "Traffic to rightwing websites will continue to nose dive, much more so than mainstream media."

Several conservative media veterans who spoke with the Guardian said the selection of a 2028 Republican presidential nominee will probably be a defining moment for the sector, and one that could split conservative media into different camps. On the notion that vice-president JD Vance will be Trump's heir apparent, Erickson said: "I actually am kind of encouraged by the level of pushback after the election that maybe this isn't a good idea."

Brian Kilmeade, the co-host of the influential Fox News morning show Fox & Friends, has been particularly critical of Vance for seeming to distance himself from Trump's decision-making on Iran. On his radio show this past week, Kilmeade said that Vance "never should have been vice-president" and that he's "amazed that President Trump puts up with it."

Over the next two years, conservative media personalities will have to put their chips down on a candidate. The challenge, Sykes said, is "figuring out where the audience is going, and getting there first." He also expects conservative media outlets to prioritize attacking Democrats and undermining their control of Congress. "It's no longer about defense of Trump," he added. "It's like: What's next? And everybody's got to make that pivot."
