## Republican voters cited Epstein files and candidate accessibility

A small but potentially significant group of voters who backed Donald Trump in 2024 chose Scott Colom in Mississippi’s 2026 U.S. Senate race against Republican incumbent Cindy Hyde-Smith, according to interviews with Mississippi Today. The crossover voters cited Trump’s war in Iran, his failure to release the Epstein files and what they called an uninspired Hyde-Smith campaign, while also crediting Colom with a more accessible and attentive style of campaigning.

In early September, Colom sat down at a table with a group of Trump supporters at AC’s Steakhouse in Hernando. His campaign organized the small, in-person roundtable with Trump-supporting Republicans as part of his effort to ramp up outreach to the president’s voters. The race was tight, and Trump had won Mississippi by 23 points less than two years earlier. Over Cajun chicken tenders and fries, attendees questioned Colom about capitalism, transgender participation in women’s sports and government spending. Mississippi Today reported that Colom had won the group over by the end of the meal.

The meeting took place in DeSoto County, a Republican stronghold that Trump won by about 24 points in 2024. In November, Colom won the county by about seven points. U.S. House results across Mississippi suggested that tens of thousands of voters backed Republican House candidates but not Hyde-Smith; the Republican House candidates collectively won more than 20,000 more votes than Hyde-Smith.

Political observers noted a massive Democratic turnout in Colom’s historic victory in the heart of Trump country. The Mississippi Today interviews pointed to another factor: a small but potentially significant number of skeptical Republicans who crossed over to support Colom. Those voters cited Trump’s war in Iran, his failure to release the Epstein files and what they called an uninspired Hyde-Smith campaign as reasons for their choices.

Mark Vines, 50, a Southaven businessman who said he had voted Republican for 20 years and supported Trump in 2024, was among the Trump backers who crossed over to support Colom. A few weeks before the Hernando roundtable, Colom’s campaign filmed an advertisement with him titled “Lost Her Way.” Vines said in the ad that he would not vote for Hyde-Smith because she had voted to block the release of the Epstein files. He had criticized Colom on X shortly after the Democrat entered the race for “quoting platitudes.”

Vines said the push by Trump and his allies, Hyde-Smith among them, to conceal the Epstein files left him increasingly dismayed. He called Hyde-Smith’s office and wrote letters urging her to support release of the files. Vines said he never received a response, not even a robo-email.

“The first thing they did was make a farce of the whole Epstein thing, which was probably the biggest piece of corruption. So that kind of set the tone that OK, this is not going to be the change that we voted for,” Vines said. “So we felt betrayed.”

Some elected Republicans in Mississippi have said the sentiment might be widespread. Rep. Jansen Owen, a Republican state lawmaker from Poplarville, wrote on X that Republicans should be clear-eyed about the political cost that came with congressional votes to block the release of the Epstein files. “I’ve heard from a lot of constituents in my very Republican district who cite the Epstein file vote as their reasoning for going left, voting third party, or skipping the Senate race altogether,” Owen wrote.

Vines said he felt alienated from the party he had supported for decades and the president he had backed less than two years earlier. He attended one of Colom’s town halls in Horn Lake and said he was immediately struck by Colom’s work ethic and the way he made himself accessible, even to voters who disagreed with him. Vines believed that approach stood in stark contrast to Hyde-Smith’s cloistered approach.

In explaining his shift, Vines said Hyde-Smith had been appointed and linked that fact to what he saw as a broader, non-merit-based history in Mississippi. “She’s a nepotism hire, she was appointed,” Vines said. “And then I looked at the history, and that’s kind of been our history in the state. It’s not really merit based, and that kind of flies against the whole fundamental principle of being a conservative.”

For some Republicans, Colom’s personal work ethic as a candidate and willingness to offer ideas beyond increasing spending on government programs seemed to embody an embrace of meritocracy they felt Democrats had abandoned in recent years. “You make your own way with meritocracy,” Cook said. “I’m more in line with that, which I know is less traditional, or at least in the past 5-10 years or so, less associated with the Democratic Party. But that’s where I started to see a little bit of a moderate in him, and not just that extreme left sort of perspective.”

Chris Cook, 60, who works for a financial advisory firm in Hernando, was invited to the roundtable by Vines. After filming the advertisement with Vines, Colom’s campaign asked Cook to help assemble Republicans who could meet with the candidate and identify the issues that mattered to them. Cook came to the lunch. He said the attendees did not hold back on questioning Colom and that he was struck by Colom’s overtures and willingness to incorporate their concerns into the campaign rather than dismissing them.

Cook said he voted for Trump because he disliked career politicians, grew frustrated with political stagnation and saw no substantive change despite promises from establishment politicians. Trump’s pledge to “drain the swamp” of entrenched government bureaucrats appealed to him. Cook also said he believed the Democratic Party had swung too far left on some cultural issues under former President Joe Biden. As a youth soccer coach, he was concerned about the prospect of trans girls competing against biological girls.

On the campaign trail, Hyde-Smith called Colom a “transgender defender” and attempted to cast the Democrat as an out-of-touch radical. Cook said those attacks fell flat after he met Colom. Cook said Hyde-Smith refused to acknowledge the issues he raised and seemed to him to be part of the same political machine he had initially supported Trump to dismantle. He was open to pivoting away from Trump and Republicans because he believed the president had shown himself to be unrestrained in his thirst for raw power, focusing on pet priorities such as renaming buildings while treating economic strain on Americans caused by his policies as a “bargaining chip” in political battles.

“I really chose Scott more so because it went back to the career politician thing with the senator that had been in place for so long, and stuff just wasn’t really changing. And I didn’t see anybody really doing anything to try to get my vote,” Cook said. “And I did see him.”

Cook said he is far from alone among voters on both sides of the aisle who will refuse to prioritize party loyalty over everything else. “Myself personally, and I feel like a large part of the country are no longer just going to support a party because I’m a Republican and my father was a Republican, or I was a Democrat and my father was a Democrat,” Cook said. “I think people are now paying a lot closer attention to what’s really going on.”

Younger voters were described as the demographic perhaps most likely to swing back and forth.

Mississippi Today also spoke with a 23-year-old woman from Brandon who voted for Trump in 2024 and Colom in 2026, works in marketing and was not at the Hernando lunch. She asked that her name not be used so she could discuss her political beliefs without professional consequences. Raised by Republican parents, she was a loyal Trump supporter and attended his inauguration in 2024. One of Trump’s campaign promises that appealed to her most was his promise to limit America’s interventions in foreign conflicts.

“These are topics that Trump ran on and then Cindy Hyde-Smith ran on, no more forever wars,” the Brandon woman said. “Not only did he not get out of the wars but he continues to perpetrate the wars, and it’s American citizens’ tax dollars that are going to the Middle East, to Israel.”

Colom, by contrast, repeatedly criticized the war in Iran and vowed not to take money from the American Israel Public Affairs Committee. Some researchers said support for Israel had become a generational chasm within the Republican Party. A 2026 Brookings Institution analysis citing Pew data noted that unfavorable views of Israel among Republicans aged 18 to 49 shifted from 35% in 2022 to 50%, compared with a shift from 19% to 23% among Republicans aged 50 and older.

As the war in Iran dragged on and Hyde-Smith seemed to the Brandon woman to be a rubber stamp for Trump’s agenda, she decided she could not vote for Hyde-Smith. But she did not immediately decide to vote for Colom. Her decision came days before the election, when she saw Colom strolling through the line outside Trump’s rally for Hyde-Smith in Corinth on Monday, Oct. 26, 2026, talking to the president’s supporters. To her, that showed respect for voters and the office that Hyde-Smith had refused to muster.

“She wouldn’t have a debate with Scott Colom,” the Brandon woman said. “She basically just assumed that because she was the Republican candidate and had an R next to her name that the Mississippi Senate race was hers.”

After his historic win drew national attention, Colom continued meeting voters who might disagree with him. On Thursday, he announced a community conversation for the following week in Itawamba County, where he received his lowest percentage of the vote on election day. “No matter where you live or how you voted, your voice matters, and I’ll always show up for you,” Colom said. “That’s what Mississippi deserves because Mississippi Matters.”
