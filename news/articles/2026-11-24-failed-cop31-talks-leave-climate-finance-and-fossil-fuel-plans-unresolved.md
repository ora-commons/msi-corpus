## Regional, Indigenous and local climate action continued without a summit pact

The United Nations' COP31 climate summit in Turkey closed without consensus on how the world should confront climate change, and the text it produced left fossil-fuel policy and climate financing unresolved. Dozens of nations objected on Sunday because the final text did not mention fossil fuels — primarily coal, oil and gas — or increased financial commitments for nations facing the worst effects of a warmer world. Scientists cited by the Associated Press warn the world is on track to exceed the warming goals set in the 2015 Paris Agreement.

The final text was meant to guide climate action, and two omissions defined the dispute. It did not include language on fossil fuels, and it did not increase financial commitments to help hard-hit nations adapt. Vulnerable countries pressed wealthier nations they identified as most responsible for the climate crisis for more support, and the summit closed without resolving either point.

Action continued outside the summit's formal process. Nations, states, provinces, Indigenous groups and local officials have continued to act on their own, and a new voluntary initiative on transitioning away from fossil fuels operates outside the United Nations' purview. Almost 60 countries participated in the first International Conference on Transitioning Away from Fossil Fuels in Colombia this year, and the second is scheduled for next year in the island nation of Tuvalu.

Katharine Hayhoe, a climate scientist with The Nature Conservancy, said countries "are building new networks to work together." She said most climate action takes place below the national level, including in the United States, which did not participate directly in the United Nations' climate talks for the second year in a row. President Donald Trump pulled the United States out of the Paris Agreement, but a United States delegation that included California Gov. Gavin Newsom attended the summit to pledge climate action.

The summit followed a year of devastating natural disasters that climate change had made worse and far more likely. The Associated Press reported that the disasters hit hardest in vulnerable countries that did not cause the climate problem. A summer heat wave in Europe killed more than 50,000 people, with nearly two-thirds of those deaths blamed on human-caused climate change. In Nepal, a partially collapsed Himalayan glacier triggered flooding that washed away entire villages and left thousands dead or missing, and more melting glaciers face the same risk.

Wildfires raged across the globe, including in Canada, and the smoke made air quality in several U.S. cities among the worst in the world, prompting warnings that everyone's health was at risk. The Associated Press reported that those disasters and other climate-related events did not move developed countries to confront fossil fuels more forcefully at COP31, even as heat records fell daily, monthly and annually.

A report released during the summit by the independent nonprofit Climate Action Tracker found that progress in slowing global warming has essentially stalled for the past five years. The tracker said the world is almost certain to overshoot the 2015 Paris Agreement goal of limiting warming to 1.5 degrees Celsius (2.7 degrees Fahrenheit) above pre-industrial temperatures. Even if every country fulfills its promises — an outcome the report called increasingly unlikely — the globe will end the century 2.6 degrees Celsius, or 4.7 degrees Fahrenheit, warmer than pre-industrial times. Experts cited by AP warned that the world must urgently and drastically reduce emissions to avoid the worst effects of climate change.

Countries have established plans to reduce fossil-fuel emissions, but transitioning away from fossil fuels takes money. Vulnerable nations pressed the wealthier nations they identified as most responsible for the climate crisis to do more to help them. At past summits, rich nations had promised to help poorer countries finance the transition and harden their infrastructure against natural disasters. This year's meeting failed to produce an agreement on increasing support, on determining who pays what, or on getting rich countries to pay what they already promised. Experts said the amount currently pledged falls vastly short of future needs.

That gap has direct consequences for countries already dealing with disasters. Hayhoe said early warning systems in Nepal, which was devastated by this year's flooding, will be more difficult to build. Island nations battered by rising seas might also be unable to afford projects without easier access to money.

International climate adaptation and disaster-preparedness aid programs have been slashed. The Associated Press reported that the Trump administration's abrupt cuts to U.S. foreign aid in 2025 eliminated or sharply curtailed programs serving millions of people worldwide, including for disaster preparedness.

Climate change is also affecting wealthy countries. The United States and countries in Europe have experienced larger and more intense heat waves, droughts and wildfires, record flooding and other costly disasters. Experts cited by AP said those impacts underscore the scale of the crisis in poorer and even more vulnerable countries.

Hayhoe described the consequences of failure in terms of inequality: "If we fail on climate, the inequities we see today will grow almost beyond imagining: widespread crop failures, people losing access to water, rising conflict over basic resources, mass migration and growing tensions between countries."

Tasneem Essop, executive director of Climate Action Network International, blamed developed countries for the failed talks. "We are witnessing human suffering on an unimaginable scale, and we had hoped this COP would take steps to protect people from the worsening climate crisis," she said. "Instead, rich nations gutted the very measures that could have made a difference."
