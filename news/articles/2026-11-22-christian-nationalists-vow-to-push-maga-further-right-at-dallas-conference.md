## About a thousand attend pastor Joel Webbon's three-day Christ is King event

The event ran three days in Dallas, and Webbon, whose reach on the Christian right has been growing, used it to tell the crowd not to read the midterm results as an ending.

"I'm not demoralized. I'm not defeated, and neither should you be," he said. "There will be an America after Trump."

The midterm election results have some people writing obituaries for the MAGA movement. This gathering was framed around what comes after Trump, and several national news outlets were there to cover it. About a thousand people attended, the vast majority of them white men, many in their 30s and 40s, some with meticulously coiffed beards and mustaches. The quotations that follow include antisemitic, racist and misogynistic language.

From the stage, Webbon mixed jokes with explicit statements about race and about women.

"Most of you are right-wing. God bless you. Right? You're going to disagree with me because it's like — Joel Webbon's kind of embarrassing. I mean, he's to the left of George Washington. What's the guy even doing?" he said — a joke he likes to use because Washington owned slaves. Webbon claims he believes that there are at least some Black men in America who should retain the right to vote.

"It's not just OK to be white. It's good to be white. Praise God," he said. Another of his favorite talking points is repealing women's right to vote, as well as eliminating their place in public life.

"Every time you see a woman in leadership, I guarantee there were at least a thousand men who were more qualified for that position than her," Webbon said.

He also addressed antisemitism directly. "I'm not trying to be a jerk about this or overly edgy, but the leading cause of the rise of antisemitism remains kind of Jewish behavior," he said.

Webbon is not the pastor of a megachurch. His actual congregation near Austin has about 200 people. But his profile online has spiked as his messages about race, women and Jews have gotten more explicit, and it got a boost earlier this year when he began hosting Fuentes, whom NPR described as "a 28-year-old far-right streamer and self-described racist and antisemite who's advocated for raping women." A majority of Webbon's most popular podcast and video episodes have been the ones that feature Fuentes.

Webbon belongs to the branch of Christianity whose adherents call themselves Reformed — an estimated 2% of Christians in America. Du Mez, the Calvin University historian, said she worries his influence has begun to break containment from that small community.

"Reformed is a Protestant denomination going back to both Martin Luther and especially John Calvin," Du Mez said. "And the idea that to be a faithful Christian is to shape the rest of society around God's norms — when you accomplish that, then you're going to flourish."

Du Mez, who is Reformed herself, said some in the denomination interpret their religious purpose as the pursuit of social justice, while she said the Webbon branch tends toward what she called a more coercive approach.

"As a mandate to seize control over all sectors of life and to really freeze out anybody of a different religious faith or of a Christian faith that doesn't align with their understanding, to freeze them out of all positions of power and influence — this is what they mean when they say Christ is king," she said.

At one point the crowd took up the phrase as a chant: "Christ is king. Christ is king. Christ is king."

Du Mez said evangelicals, like everyone else, exist across a political spectrum, but they often share biblical language and phrases, and she said figures like Webbon use that common vocabulary to promote very far-right interpretations. She said she has been watching the more extreme versions of Reformed theology begin to spread, usually through men, across the community and beyond.

"Things that seemed unacceptable before become, at first, controversial — you know, part of the conversation — and then increasingly become kind of acceptable and even the norm," she said.

Fuentes was the surprise special guest Webbon invited to close out the conference. He rarely speaks to live audiences, but he had this one on its feet.

"You'll find that throughout history, it is a small group of highly motivated people that change the world," Fuentes said. "It's a small group of fanatics, zealots, the martyrs, the people that are willing to die for what they believe in. That's us. That's the people in this room."

He finished with "Thank you. God bless you. Goodnight," and the room cheered.

He and Webbon do not agree on everything. Webbon thinks it may take longer than the next two years to gain enough traction to take power, something he has talked about on his podcast.

"Our plan does not require a majority vote," Webbon said. "So when I say we will win, I'm not saying that we will win through democracy."

One of the young men at the conference, in his early 20s, said he got into some of these ideas after a friend introduced him to music by one of the event's speakers. It became really special to him, and he sang a few lines: "Don't give any heed to cowardly tongues. There's glory out there, out there to be won." He asked that NPR refer to him by his last initial, C, because he doesn't want to be associated with a specific conference speaker.

C has gone to the same church all his life, one with women pastors. But as he has listened more to Webbon and podcasters like him, he has started worrying that is wrong.

"And so I'm kind of in the phase right now of asking questions — like, asking the men who are the foundation of the church, like, what does Scripture say? And how should we handle these things?" he said.

He said some of the elders have told him privately that they agree women shouldn't lead churches, but that getting rid of them might drive people away from the message of Jesus.

Both Webbon and Fuentes are young. So are a lot of their followers, and they are all counting on being the future of the right.
