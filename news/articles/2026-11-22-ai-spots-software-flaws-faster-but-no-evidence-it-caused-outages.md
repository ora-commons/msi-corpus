## AI spots software flaws faster, but no evidence it caused outages

**Subtype:** fact

- Computer science and cybersecurity experts say AI tools can find flaws in software 10 to 100 times faster than human specialists, pushing organizations into far heavier rounds of patching.
- No indication has emerged that AI-related cyberattacks or rogue AI agents caused the recent outages at Microsoft, airlines, banks and the National Security Agency.
- The cybersecurity firm Qualys reported that Microsoft addressed 1,235 vulnerabilities in November, compared with 68 in November 2025.
- Microsoft said its outage was unrelated to AI or AI agents and stemmed from a "network capacity upgrade."
- Senate Intelligence Committee Vice Chairman Mark Warner said some of the recent outages were "AI-related," citing a rush by companies to patch vulnerabilities that AI tools had exposed.

## Microsoft patched 1,235 vulnerabilities in November, Qualys reports, up from 68

Microsoft services went down, flights were grounded nationwide, some banks' services briefly stopped working and an internal platform for the National Security Agency went dark — all within the span of one week. The run of failures left people asking whether artificial intelligence was the culprit, and officials have offered explanations that point in different directions.

Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin, in a Fox News interview, said he asked someone in the tech industry what could be causing the outages, and that the person answered it was "super intelligence," the Trump administration's preferred term for AI. The two are not the same thing.

"That kinda makes sense to me, because I can tell you with 98% certainty, we're not seeing the cyberattack, no one's out there taking claims for it," Mullin said Nov. 17.

In a separate Fox Business interview the same day, National Intelligence Director Jay Clayton pointed to flaws that AI detected in a programming language used in systems across the world, and he said fixing them caused unexpected problems.

"The patching that has gone on following that has caused some of these systems to not come back up when you patch them as expected. That is what has happened at the NSA today," Clayton said.

Senate Intelligence Committee Vice Chairman Sen. Mark Warner, D-Va., said in a Nov. 18 CNBC interview that some of the recent outages were "AI-related."

"What's happened is that these AI tools, what they've been able to do is spot vulnerabilities in all of our existing systems," Warner said. "Then all of these companies are in this mad rush to create patches because nobody wants to have their system vulnerable."

High-profile AI hacks have happened this year, but there is no indication that the recent outages were the result of AI-related cyberattacks or rogue AI agents, according to the analysis published by PBS News. Experts quoted in it say AI is boosting companies' ability to detect weaknesses in their systems, and therefore requiring more fixes, which could lead to outages — but no evidence has shown that caused the recent string of tech disruptions.

"Vulnerabilities are being discovered and exploited faster than ever before, especially with increasingly capable AI models," said Bryan Ward, a Vanderbilt University computer science assistant professor.

In the past, organizations would task specialists with finding flaws in their software systems, said Stuart Madnick, an MIT Sloan School of Management information technology professor. AI is often able to do the work 10 to 100 times faster than humans, he said, so an organization that previously found four or five flaws in a year would now be aware of 40 to 400.

"The question is: Is (it) the fixing that's causing the problem, or the fact you got 400 open windows and doors in your system that are now known that's causing a problem?" Madnick said.

Leading AI companies such as OpenAI and Anthropic have released sophisticated models that have uncovered serious vulnerabilities in software. On Nov. 17, a cybersecurity start-up using an OpenAI model found a critical software flaw in a programming language used by government agencies, banks, hospitals and the majority of Fortune 500 companies.

The volume of fixes at one major company illustrates the shift. Qualys, a cybersecurity firm, reported that Microsoft addressed 1,235 vulnerabilities in November, compared with 68 in November 2025.

More secure software systems must be built in the future, Ward said, but there is an immediate need to fix problems now.

Based on what the government and affected companies have reported so far, the outages did not happen because of a common weakness. Microsoft said it had a technical issue unrelated to AI or AI agents and that the problem stemmed from a "network capacity upgrade." It has not given more details.

The nationwide ground stop happened after a router upgrade disrupted the Federal Aviation Administration's telecommunications infrastructure, government officials said. Investigators have not determined whether there was an installation error or a faulty router, but they have ruled out an AI system the FAA recently rolled out.

JPMorganChase attributed its outage to a "temporary issue related to a software bug in on-premise hardware systems."

Unintended consequences, such as services becoming unavailable, can be a trade-off when companies update their systems, said Nicholas Leiserson, president of the Center for Advancing Cybersecurity at the Institute for Security and Technology.

"That's like a perennial challenge that has existed in information technology operations for a long time, and AI can potentially accelerate it," Leiserson said.

"I think recent events should be a wake-up call and the public should take the opportunity to improve their cybersecurity posture," Ward said. "Recent outages demonstrate how critical and intertwined software systems have become in our society today, and how important cybersecurity truly is."

MSI reported on Nov. 19 that the [week of tech outages](/articles/2026-11-19-a-week-of-tech-outages-disrupts-flights-banking-and-email/) reached email, workplace software, air travel, banking and the government network used for classified communications, and that the Trump administration and the affected companies each said they had no evidence linking the disruptions to a cyberattack.
