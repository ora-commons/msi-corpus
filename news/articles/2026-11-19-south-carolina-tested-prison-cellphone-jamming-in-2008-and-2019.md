## Officials said illegal phones helped orchestrate a 2018 riot that killed seven

South Carolina served as the testing ground for state prison cellphone jamming. In 2008, the FCC allowed the state to conduct a one-time test at a maximum-security prison. A briefcase-sized device emitted a frequency that immediately shut down cell phones around an auditorium, while cell service outside the area continued uninterrupted. Another test took place at a different state prison in 2019, the first time federal officials collaborated with a state prison on such a test.

The 2019 test came a year after seven South Carolina inmates died in the worst U.S. prison riot in 25 years, a gang fight officials said was orchestrated in part using illegal cell phones. Corrections officials said inmates had also used contraband phones to run drug rings, coordinate prison riots and order assassination attempts against prison guards. Officials described methods used to move phones into prisons, including hollowed-out footballs, corrupt employees and drones.

FCC Chairman Brendan Carr stood with state law enforcement and prison officials in Columbia before a display of hundreds of confiscated phones and large drones used to ferry contraband over prison fences. He said the agency was ready to take action against what he described as a scourge many people did not understand.

“People really don’t understand the threat that these pose,” Carr said, noting that 25,000 cell phones were confiscated in state and local prisons each year. “We’re going to put a new tool in the hands of law enforcement, and it’s going to make a dramatic difference.”

Corrections authorities have called contraband phones the No. 1 security threat in the nation’s correctional facilities. For decades, state and local officials had unsuccessfully pushed for FCC permission to use jamming technology already allowed in federal facilities. Under a nearly century-old communications law, state prisons require FCC approval before using the technology.

U.S. Attorney Bryan Stirling, who led the South Carolina Corrections Department for more than a decade, led counterparts across the country in seeking use of the technology. South Carolina Attorney General Alan Wilson, elected this month as the state’s next governor, marshaled fellow prosecutors in urging Congress unsuccessfully to change the law.

Robert Johnson, a South Carolina Corrections officer, was nearly killed at his home in a 2010 rain of bullets in an attack officials said had been orchestrated by a prisoner using a contraband cell phone. After nearly three dozen surgeries and now in “pain 24/7,” Johnson said Wednesday that he refers to the phones as “weapons of mass destruction” because of the dangers they can be used to cause. Johnson had testified in previous FCC hearings and often spoken publicly on the issue.

“No one should have to go through what I went through,” Johnson said. “But I also believe that the Lord chose my wife and I to go through this because someone had to carry the banner, and he chose us to do it.”

The FCC has also shown willingness to work on eradicating prison cell phones, holding a 2016 field hearing in South Carolina and hosting meetings with members of Congress, prison officials and stakeholders from the wireless industry. Since becoming chairman, Carr has signaled that the agency was ready to move on jamming. He announced the upcoming vote last month during an appearance with U.S. Sen. Tom Cotton, R-Ark., who has led multiple pushes to change federal legislation to allow state use of the technology.

The FCC is expected to take action Thursday on a proposal that would let state prisons across the United States jam the signals of phones smuggled to inmates. Carr said the decision would extend a capability already allowed in federal facilities, and that the agency was not considering jamming outside the prison context.

Groups that advocate for inmates’ rights opposed the move. They argued that the high rates inmates must pay to use official prison phones have pushed them toward contraband devices simply to stay in touch with family. The telecommunications industry had also long opposed jamming, saying the technology risks interfering with legitimate communications.

On Wednesday, Carr said he believed the industry was softening on jamming, in part because of recognition of the problem’s size and agencies’ experiments with targeted technologies.

“We aren’t considering jamming outside this unique context,” Carr said. “Maybe people aren’t cheering it on from industry, but the opposition has receded substantially.”
