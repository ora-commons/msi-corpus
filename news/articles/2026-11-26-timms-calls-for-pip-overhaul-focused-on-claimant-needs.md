## Government accepts fast-track claims and fewer reviews for lifelong or unlikely-to-improve conditions

Social security minister Stephen Timms told the House of Commons on Tuesday that personal independence payments required a "fundamental overhaul," according to The Guardian. His intervention followed the Milburn review, which pitched a set of changes last week and proposed £3.6bn in cuts to benefits for under-25s. Timms's recommendations put the emphasis on making the system work better for claimants rather than shrinking the bill, and were not explicitly cost-saving.

The Guardian reported that the government has already accepted two changes: a fast-track system that would spare people with the most severe and easily evidenced conditions an interview, and fewer award reviews for people whose conditions are lifelong or unlikely to improve. Disability advocates have campaigned against what they describe as "pointless" reassessments since the coalition government phased out lifetime awards as part of Pip's creation in 2013.

The review also targets assessment errors, particularly for claimants with fluctuating conditions, and focuses on "rehumanising" a process long accused of harming disabled people's mental and physical health. Several of the larger proposals remain thinly specified, including changes to the assessment and payment process that ministers must now decide whether to develop and implement.

One of those proposals is a new impact assessment designed to measure the actual costs that come with someone's health conditions, with the aim of tailoring payments to need rather than paying a standard weekly amount to every claimant.

According to the Resolution Foundation, one in six working-age PIP claimants say they have no additional costs relating to their disability. Scope research last year found the shortfall between the average Pip award and average costs was £630 a month.

Timms's report states that Pip is "not appropriate" for people who do not incur additional costs, and disability campaigners are highly concerned that years of faulty benefit testing will make it harder to establish who needs what — or that the change will simply narrow eligibility.

Timms also proposes a "service exchange scheme" under which a claimant could voluntarily swap part of their financial award for a service, such as access to education or referrals into health or social care. The idea draws on the long-running Motability scheme, in which some people swap the mobility component of Pip for a leased car, though its effect on choice within a service — including whether people could hire their own care staff — is unknown. Any swap would be voluntary, a point that puts Timms at odds with Milburn, who said most young people should lose the right to monthly cash.

Timms's review followed a consultation with more than 38,000 people and organisations. The government will now consider the recommendations alongside Milburn's and set out a response in the coming weeks, ahead of a white paper in the new year. Downing Street is expected to stress that the two reviews are not a competition and that the ministers agree on many points, though the government faces two sets of recommendations for a single benefit.

Stephen Timms has spent the last year reviewing Pip in light of Keir Starmer's abandoned measures last summer. The Guardian assessed that the Labour MPs who scuppered Starmer's Pip bill last summer are unlikely to mount the same rebellion under a popular Andy Burnham premiership, while concern remains on the party's left over Milburn's "two-tier" plan to cut benefits for young people.

Smaller recommendations — improved assessor training and a digital "Tell Us Once" approach that would stop claimants being asked for the same information again — risk being lost in the wider debate, The Guardian said. Ministers also face a question of whether any adopted policies would apply only to new claimants or to the several million existing ones.

Warnings about the outcome already diverge: some say adopting Timms's ideas could increase the benefits bill, while others say the changes will mean hundreds of thousands losing their benefits. Burnham entered office promising he would not bring in "crude cuts," and the review leaves the government to decide which recommendations to adopt. The Guardian said Burnham may now be asked to put his money where his mouth is.
