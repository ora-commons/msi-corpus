## Philanthropists have committed over $6 million in cash toward the flight

**The offer and its conditions.** Australia's federal government has sent an offer to the European Space Agency to send Katherine Bennell-Pegg, the Australian of the Year and an aerospace engineer, to the International Space Station on a mission slated for 2028. If she embarks on it, the flight would mark the first time a qualified astronaut has flown to space under the Australian flag. The offer drew support from philanthropists and the Australian space industry, and the office of Science Minister Tim Ayres had previously described the project as “a very expensive undertaking” that no government alone could fund.

Announcing the decision on Friday, Ayres said the government had been engaging extensively with the private sector, philanthropists and other levels of government to raise funding to support a space flight, “which is a significant cost.” He said: “We are grateful to the philanthropists who have committed over $6m in cash, plus in-kind support, and some indicative support from some state governments.”

Bennell-Pegg's flight depends on a suitable mission being found. Macquarie University professor of physics and astronomy Richard de Grijs said: “She is qualified, she has undergone significant training, so she is set to go up if a suitable mission can be found.”

**The funding commitments.** Philanthropists have committed over $6 million in cash toward the flight, along with in-kind support, and some state governments have provided indicative support. Entrepreneurs Dick Smith and Jim Whalley each pledged $1 million for the mission. Former prime minister Kevin Rudd supported the campaign on social media, saying the “project makes long-term economic sense for Australia beyond the usual sensibilities of the Treasury bean counters.”

In August, Ayres and assistant science minister Andrew Charlton wrote to state and territory leaders asking them to “partner” with the federal government to fund the mission. The letter, according to The Australian, described it as a “complete Australian mission that includes locally developed experiments and technology.” In May, the Australian Space Agency announced it had entered into “treaty-level discussions” with the ESA, which “would establish a formal framework for enhanced cooperation between Australia and Europe.”

**Bennell-Pegg's qualifications and the Australian spaceflight record.** Bennell-Pegg is director of space technology at the Australian Space Agency and completed astronaut training with the ESA in 2024. The agency paid $466,000 for her training at the European Astronaut Centre in Cologne, Germany. De Grijs said: “The government has actually contributed quite a lot of money to that education already.”

Bennell-Pegg is not the first Australian to have gone to space. Andy Thomas and Paul Scully-Power, both Australian-born, went to space as US citizens on NASA missions. Elaine Hyde, a dual Australian and US citizen, took a suborbital flight aboard Jeff Bezos's Blue Origin NS-30 last year, in 2025. Bennell-Pegg would be the first Australian to qualify for spaceflight under the Australian flag.

De Grijs also pointed to Australia's spaceflight involvement “by another means”: an Australian-built lunar rover named Roo-ver will be sent to the moon on a NASA mission in 2030, he said.

**Institutional support and the cost debate.** South Australia, where the Australian Space Agency is headquartered, has publicly committed to supporting Bennell-Pegg's space bid. Premier Peter Malinauskas said: “South Australia stands ready to partner with the commonwealth in what should be a national endeavour.” Malinauskas said Bennell-Pegg had the potential to inspire a new generation to dream big and to address what he called the single biggest challenge to Australia's economy, productivity. He said Australia had “a massive shortage of young people taking up the fields of science, technology, engineering and maths,” and added: “Nothing would motivate young people more to take up these fields of endeavour than seeing Australia's first ever flagged astronaut in space.”

In a speech earlier this month, Bennell-Pegg described space as “a gateway – a lure – into the full breadth of STEM fields. Especially for those that dream of going up there.”

De Grijs said there was “an inspirational value in sending a citizen from your country to space who is also the Australian of the Year … but the question is, is that worth $100m?” He added: “It's a lot of money, but on the other hand, it might actually lead to returns that are way more than the estimated number.”

Darin Lovett, chair of the Space Industry Association of Australia, said the industry had “a strong track record of return on investment.” He put the cost of the flight at around $100 million and said: “For around $100m, less than the price of a single coffee for every Australian adult, this is not a ticket to space. It is a down payment on Australia's future in one of the defining industries of this century.”
