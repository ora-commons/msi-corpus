## Legal experts dispute White House warning that investigations could constitute a crime

On November 18, Robert Garcia, the top Democrat on oversight and the expected next chairman of the committee, sent a dozen letters to President Trump's allies and some family members, NPR congressional correspondent Barbara Sprunt said. Garcia's letters told recipients to hold on to their documents for upcoming investigations. Sprunt said the targets fall into four broad buckets: alleged corruption within the Trump family, corporate corruption, misconduct in the Department of Homeland Security and what Democrats call the White House Epstein cover-up.

Sprunt identified recipients including President Trump's two sons, his son-in-law, White House Chief of Staff Stephen Miller, former Department of Homeland Security Secretary Kristi Noem, Noem's adviser Corey Lewandowski and Vice President JD Vance. NPR host Sacha Pfeiffer described the list as a target list of sorts, and Sprunt agreed.

Democrats have said since the election that they will aggressively conduct oversight of the Trump administration when they take over in January. Garcia's letters name the people from whom the party says it wants documents.

The White House has previously rejected the accusations of misconduct and has warned Garcia that investigations could constitute a crime. Legal experts have pushed back on that warning, Sprunt said.

House Democrats also gave Hakeem Jeffries the official nod to be the next speaker. House Minority Leader Jeffries ran unopposed in a closed-door 251-11 vote, Sprunt said. The vote was not unanimous: some newly elected progressive members had said they wanted to see new leadership, and Sprunt described grumbling from those members. She called the vote an overwhelming show of support, and the full House will vote for speaker in January.

In an archived recording, Jeffries said, "The people of this country are demanding bold, dramatic and transformational change. The status quo is unacceptable."

Outside the oversight agenda, the Senate's Democratic leadership remains unsettled. Senate Minority Leader Chuck Schumer is facing a challenge from Maryland Sen. Chris Van Hollen. Some of Schumer's allies and Van Hollen are making competing arguments about whether the party should keep its current leader or change direction after the election.

Some Schumer allies have used a sports metaphor to make their case, asking, "if the manager brings a team to the World Series, why would they then fire him?" Sprunt said many in the party view Schumer as instrumental in flipping nine Senate seats for Democrats, far beyond what many had expected going into the election cycle.

Van Hollen argues that voters made clear they want change, and that the demand extends to Democratic leadership. Progressives are excited about his candidacy, but Sprunt said moderates and centrist Democrats really opened up the map for the party this election cycle, particularly in the South. That leaves the party weighing the argument against switching horses midstream against the view that the moment calls for shake-ups.

Whether Van Hollen represents change depends on whom one asks, Sprunt said. He calls himself an underdog and has positioned himself around populist economic policies that resonated with many voters this cycle. He joined calls to restrict arms sales to the Israeli government fairly early on and has been increasingly critical of the Democratic Party and the way congressional leaders have historically approached the Trump administration.

Others point out that Van Hollen is not a generational-change candidate. He is 67, roughly eight years younger than Schumer, represents a blue state, has been in politics for decades and has already chaired both the House and Senate campaign committees, Sprunt said.

Both leadership contests remain in flux. Jeffries's closed-door vote sets him up for the full House's January decision, while the Senate choice between Schumer and Van Hollen is unresolved. Pfeiffer said the investigations ahead amount to "months and maybe years of coverage," and Sprunt laughed.
