## Greenhouse highlights stakes for workers, retirees and communities

Anthropic, the developer of Claude, saw its shares fall 9% on Friday, two days after the company raised $81 billion in the largest initial public offering in history at a $1.5 trillion valuation. Technology stocks continued to slide during the same week. Guardian columnist Steven Greenhouse wrote that many economists debated whether there was an AI stock market bubble, while more and more people were convinced that an AI bubble had already begun to burst. Greenhouse, who focuses on labor and the workplace as well as economic and legal issues, examined the offering through its consequences for workers, retirees, investors and communities.

The market exposure Greenhouse described reached beyond people who had bought Anthropic stock directly. He said many workers and retirees had only recently discovered that their 401(k) plans and index funds held investments in AI companies they did not know about or choose. He wrote that there were growing fears among workers and retirees that an AI bust or burst bubble could take a hugely painful bite out of their retirement funds. According to a recent JP Morgan analysis, companies with AI ties make up 49% of the S&P 500's market value, and a 20% or 30% fall in those shares would hit the finances of millions of Americans.

Greenhouse reported that Anthropic's seven co-founders each came away with stakes worth about $20 billion. By one estimate, 600 of the company's more than 5,000 employees will have holdings topping $100 million, and 2,800 others will have shares worth more than $10 million. He wrote that the IPO and the huge media attention it received had also spotlighted the country's immense wealth and income inequality.

Greenhouse also wrote that he was sure many Americans resented the fact that Anthropic's employees and early investors had become immensely rich, even as millions of Americans distrusted and dreaded AI and feared that it could have disastrous results for workers, the overall economy and humankind. He wrote that people felt "left out and left behind" in the IPO's financial frenzy, and that many Americans were deeply worried that AI was a "high-tech grim reaper" that could someday take their and their children's jobs.

In a separate passage on data centers, Greenhouse wrote that "millions of Americans are furious" about the many data centers that have been "foisted on their communities." He said many of those centers had pushed up electricity prices, disrupted communities, received big tax breaks and created "annoying, round-the-clock noise."

The regulatory question surfaced in the same week. Speaking on Thursday at the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation meeting in Shenzhen, China, Donald Trump again ruled out any regulation, saying: "We oppose any form of globalist regulation or anything to control or stifle SI [super intelligence], and we won't allow that to happen." Greenhouse wrote: "If anything, the Anthropic IPO seems to have encouraged Donald Trump to continue giving a thumbs down to imposing any regulations on AI." He also wrote that Trump "seems to fear" that regulating AI may slow the industry's breakneck growth and hasten a decline in AI stocks. Greenhouse said that outcome could deflate the stock market and dismay the billionaire tech entourage that constantly flattered Trump.

At the Nasdaq's opening bell on Thursday, the day after the IPO, Anthropic Chief Executive Dario Amodei said: "This technology has very real risks." He continued: "These range from misuse of the technology by individuals or by nation-states to economic disruption to losing control of AI systems." He added: "We think [these risks] are avoidable and we can avoid them."

Greenhouse said Amodei deserved praise for taking the industry's lead last September when he wrote: "We must slow the pace at which we improve the capabilities of AI models," a call intended to avoid wrong paths that could harm humanity. He noted that Anthropic's seven co-founders had pledged to donate 80% of their equity stakes to philanthropic causes. The company was founded as a public benefit corporation with a mission to develop safe AI systems "for the long-term benefit of humanity." Greenhouse wrote that he did not mean to slam Anthropic, which apparently was one of the more public-minded technology companies, but that the gap between the IPO's success and the concerns about AI was huge.

Amodei has led the push within the industry to slow the pace of AI development and agree on regulations and guardrails, Greenhouse wrote. The columnist argued that the IPO's success could undermine those efforts: many technology executives might conclude that all was well with AI, that there was no need for change and that the industry should double down in opposing regulation and government interference.

Greenhouse cited what he called a growing number of examples of AI agents bypassing guardrails, taking unauthorized actions and engaging in illegal actions. He also said many technology executives might ignore warnings like those from Geoffrey Hinton, considered the godfather of AI, who said there is a 10% to 20% chance that AI could lead to the extinction of humanity within the next 30 years.

Some technology experts and investors said Anthropic was still moving too fast in developing AI. Dustin Moskovitz, a Facebook co-founder and an early investor in Anthropic, told The New York Times: "A.I. is moving too fast. Anthropic and everyone else on the frontier should slow down and stop racing."

Greenhouse's economic argument was conditional: if a bubble burst on inflated AI stocks, the decline could drag down the overall stock market and pull the US economy into recession, throwing millions of Americans out of work. If that happened after AI had generated billions for founders, employees and early investors, he said, the federal government and the states might have to spend tens of billions of dollars on unemployment benefits and other measures. Greenhouse argued for a federal tax on IPOs or a federal wealth tax so that the general public would share in some of the IPOs' benefits.

Greenhouse said he hoped Trump's takeaway from the successful IPO would be to "listen far more to Amodei about the dangers of AI" and to take the work of minimizing those dangers seriously. His appeal followed a week in which Anthropic's shares fell 9% two days after the record offering, while Amodei said the technology's risks were real and avoidable and Greenhouse argued that the listing's success could weaken support for slower development, regulations and guardrails.
