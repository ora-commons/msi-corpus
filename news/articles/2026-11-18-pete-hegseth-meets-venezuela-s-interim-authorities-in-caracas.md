## Pete Hegseth meets Venezuela’s interim authorities in Caracas

**Subtype:** fact

Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth met Venezuela’s interim authorities in Caracas on Wednesday, the latest member of President Donald Trump’s Cabinet to visit the country after U.S. forces ousted then-President Nicolás Maduro in January. The trip came as the administration sought to deepen its influence in Venezuela, and Venezuelan state television showed Hegseth at the presidential palace alongside acting President Delcy Rodríguez.

Hegseth followed visits this year by the Interior and Energy secretaries, who focused on efforts to tap Venezuelan oil reserves and critical minerals. Trump said in August that Hegseth had helped broker a sweeping deal for the United States to take control of some Venezuelan oil reserves. Trump also said Venezuela had sent him 1 million barrels of oil as a “gift.”

The Pentagon had not publicly announced Hegseth’s visit, but Venezuelan state television broadcast footage of the defense secretary at the presidential palace alongside Rodríguez. The visit came months after American forces carried out a surprise nighttime raid in the Venezuelan capital, captured Maduro and took him to New York to face drug-trafficking charges. Maduro denied the accusations in U.S. court.

Gen. Francis Donovan of U.S. Southern Command, the head of U.S. military operations in Latin America, also met interim authorities at least twice in Venezuela this year. Since Trump returned to the White House last year, the U.S. military has expanded its attention on the region as the Republican president promised a greater focus on the Western Hemisphere and an effort to battle Latin American drug cartels.

American forces have attacked what the Trump administration says are boats smuggling drugs in the Caribbean Sea and eastern Pacific Ocean, aiming to reduce the flow of narcotics into the United States and the scourge of fatal overdoses. At least 240 people have been killed since the administration began targeting those it calls “narcoterrorists” in September 2025, although U.S. officials have released little evidence publicly to support claims that the vessels carried drugs.

The visit came at a time of uncertainty over who would lead Venezuela in the coming months and years. Rodríguez, a Maduro ally, took over after Maduro’s capture. Her court-allowed term expired in July, but she continues to function as Venezuela’s head of state.

Opposition leader María Corina Machado recently returned to Venezuela from exile after seven failed attempts that opposition activists said were thwarted by the government. U.S. Secretary of State Marco Rubio said the “safe” return was the result of “reconciliation talks” supported by his country. He called it “the best opportunity” Venezuela had had in three decades to achieve free and fair elections and a lasting democracy.

The absence of a date for the next presidential election has left many Venezuelans conflicted about Trump. The Associated Press reported that many were relieved that Maduro was no longer president, while many believed the White House’s decision to prioritize reviving Venezuela’s oil industry over pro-democracy steps was the wrong approach for meaningful changes in their country.

The Trump administration said it had returned more than $12 billion, or almost 99% of the revenue obtained from Venezuelan oil sales, to the country under the terms of the energy deal reached with Rodríguez’s government, according to a report released this month.
