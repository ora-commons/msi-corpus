## Spacecraft aims for midair grab by tower arms after 22-hour flight

SpaceX's attempt to recover its entire Starship back at the launch pad was thwarted on Friday when the Super Heavy booster malfunctioned and an automatic abort sent the first stage into the Gulf of Mexico instead of back to its Texas pad following liftoff. Despite the setback, the bullet-shaped spacecraft continued toward orbit in the mission's boldest test yet, and Musk's company had to lower its expectations when the first part of the double catch flopped. SpaceX's plan called for the spacecraft to steer toward the pad on Saturday for a similar grab, barring further trouble.

A Reuters photograph of the return, taken by Joe Skipper, carried a caption describing the Super Heavy v3 Booster 22 exploding upon being diverted to the water while returning from the launch of a Starship 42 during the 15th test flight from the SpaceX launch complex in Starbase, Texas, on Nov. 13, 2026.

The spacecraft's own return is the second half of the double catch: it will try to steer back to the pad from orbit and be plucked in midair by mechanical arms secured to the launch tower, the first time the company attempts such a grab of the upper stage. If all goes well, the flight will last 22 hours. The original goal was to retrieve every single piece of the rocket for the purpose of future recycling — something yet to be achieved anywhere in the world — and Musk sees that achievement leading to the kind of fast and consistent launch rates necessary for building moon bases and sending people to Mars.

Shortly after reaching orbit, Starship prepared to deliver 26 of the company's most advanced Starlink satellites. The plan is for Starship to complete 15 laps around the world at an altitude of around 170 miles (275 kilometers) before heading back to the launch site for Saturday's finale.

SpaceX had already made three booster grabs at the company's sprawling Starbase near the U.S.-Mexico border. Friday's flight was the first attempt to capture the upgraded booster version, which stretches 236 feet (72 meters) and is the production model for moon and Mars expeditions, and it will also be the first time the company attempts to catch the spacecraft, which spans 171 feet (52 meters). Stacked together, the two stages form the world's biggest and most powerful rocket, 407 feet (124 meters) in height.

None of the 14 previous Starships brought its spacecraft stage home: each one ended up in the ocean, either by design at flight's end or by accident in a stream of flaming wreckage. At the first full-scale test flight in 2023, multiple engines failed to ignite and the tumbling rocket's self-destruct system was activated. The most recent test, in September, was the first to reach orbit, but SpaceX did not try to recover any of the rocket stages.

SpaceX plans to start launching Starships from Florida within a few months and from Louisiana in a few years. Scaled-up infrastructure, combined with full rocket reusability, is "how you go from zero launches to 10 to 100 to 1,000 to 10,000 to 100,000," SpaceX's Kiko Dontchev, vice president of launch, said at a NASA tech summit last week at Florida's Kennedy Space Center.

The smaller Falcon rockets will keep flying from Cape Canaveral, carrying astronauts to the International Space Station for NASA, and from California carrying Starlinks. Satellites also will keep hitching Falcon rides for now, and Musk wants Starships to eventually replace Falcons. Falcon rockets "are still not fully reusable. We still throw that second stage away every single flight. Starship, we're going to catch both the first stage and second stage," Dontchev said.

NASA is paying SpaceX billions of dollars to land Artemis astronauts on the moon with Starships, and Jeff Bezos' Blue Origin is the other major player. NASA expects to decide a year before the Artemis IV lunar landing — the first by astronauts in more than half a century — whether to get the moonwalkers to the surface with Musk's Starship or Bezos' Blue Moon.

During next summer's Artemis III mission, astronauts will practice docking their capsule with a Blue Moon and then a Starship in orbit around Earth. That will set the stage for an Artemis moon touchdown as early as 2028.
