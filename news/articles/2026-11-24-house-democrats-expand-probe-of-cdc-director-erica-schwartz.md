## House Democrats expand probe of CDC Director Erica Schwartz

**Subtype:** fact

- Reps. Frank Pallone Jr., Diana DeGette and Yvette Clarke are expanding an investigation into CDC Director Erica Schwartz's measles-death reporting and whether changes to the agency's statutory authority are required.
- Some Democratic legislators have accused CDC Director Erica Schwartz and U.S. Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. of downplaying measles.
- Schwartz said her death-certificate review policy was her decision and intended to create a uniform definition of a measles death; the policy resulted in the CDC counting only about half the measles-associated deaths that states are reporting.
- U.S. Sen. Michael Bennet said he had no confidence Schwartz would course-correct and called for her immediate resignation.
- Eight current CDC workers, speaking anonymously because they feared retribution, described morale as poor and said some had lost confidence in Schwartz.
- U.S. Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. said Schwartz had his full support.

## CDC workers report low morale and burnout

Schwartz, 54, took the helm of the Atlanta-based Centers for Disease Control and Prevention in mid-August as the agency confronted the worst U.S. measles epidemic in more than three decades, a large dengue fever outbreak in Florida and the fastest-growing overseas Ebola outbreak in history. She reports to U.S. Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. In an Associated Press interview Tuesday from her home in Naples, Florida, she said she was trying to restore trust in the CDC, including by changing how measles-associated deaths were reported. She said some criticism reflected misperceptions and that she was making progress. “I do understand why people have concerns,” Schwartz told the AP. “Have I made mistakes? Certainly. But I’m here to work on the behalf of the American people.”

Public health professionals outside the agency have expressed alarm, while employees say morale is as bad as ever. Noel Brewer, a University of North Carolina public health professor, said, “She has a tough job, at a tough time, and a tough boss.” Some experts say Schwartz has made the situation worse. Critics are also citing the Pan American Health Organization’s declaration last week that measles is continuously spreading in the United States, marking the re-establishment of a public health threat eliminated in the last century. U.S. Sen. Michael Bennet, a Colorado Democrat, said, “I have no confidence that she will do what it takes to course-correct at the CDC, and she should resign immediately.”

## House Democrats question CDC's measles-death policy

Some Democratic legislators have accused Schwartz and Kennedy of downplaying measles. Kennedy has challenged health officials’ accounts of measles deaths, and the CDC has stopped quickly relaying state reports of measles deaths. The CDC adopted a new policy requiring review of death certificates. That policy has resulted in the CDC counting only about half the measles-associated deaths that states are reporting. Schwartz said the policy was her decision and reflected her desire for a uniform definition of what counts as a measles death.

Democrats on the House Energy & Commerce Committee questioned why the CDC changed the policy. “As the crisis worsens, we are concerned that you continue to direct an effort at CDC to downplay the outbreak and artificially reduce the number of deaths that CDC reports,” according to a statement from Reps. Frank Pallone Jr. of New Jersey, Diana DeGette of Colorado and Yvette Clarke of New York. They added, “We are expanding our investigation to examine the threats your actions pose to Americans’ health and whether changes to your statutory authority are required.”

The statement followed a briefing at which Schwartz was supposed to answer congressional staffers’ questions about the measles data. Committee leaders described Schwartz as unresponsive and, at times, misleading. Schwartz disputed that assessment, arguing that she was “fully transparent. And I am really concerned that they think there is more to the story. There isn’t.”

## Schwartz's early tenure and public health challenges

President Donald Trump nominated Schwartz to lead the CDC in April and called her “a STAR!” on social media. She holds both medical and law degrees. As chief medical officer for the U.S. Coast Guard, she oversaw 41 clinics and 150 sick bays. She also served as deputy surgeon general in the U.S. Public Health Service, helping lead uniformed medical and health professionals posted at the CDC and government health agencies.

Many public health leaders were cautiously optimistic because the CDC needed steady leadership after more than a year of turmoil and months under interim directors. Schwartz was Trump’s third nominee for CDC director in less than 18 months. The first, a former Florida congressman, had his nomination pulled an hour before a Senate confirmation hearing. The second, Susan Monarez, won Senate approval but was ousted less than a month into the job when Trump administration officials said she was not aligned with the president’s agenda.

The Associated Press reported that Kennedy, a longtime critic of vaccines and federal public health policy, played a role in decimating CDC staffing last year. Under Kennedy, around 30% of the agency’s staff have left, mostly through layoffs, resignations and retirements. At her nomination hearing, Schwartz pledged “radical transparency” to rebuild public trust in the agency, which she noted had declined during the COVID-19 pandemic and eroded further under Kennedy. “I will always stand on science and speak the truth, even in difficult moments,” she testified.

A week after starting, Schwartz held her first “all hands” staff meeting. She promised to advocate for the CDC and said she would not hesitate to disagree with Kennedy, but only privately. Some employees said their optimism began to fade after that meeting. Public misperceptions about vaccines have persisted: A recent Annenberg Public Policy Center survey found that about 1 in 6 Americans believed that “vaccines given to children for diseases like measles, mumps and rubella cause autism,” even though dozens of studies have determined there is no causal link. Roughly another 1 in 5 said they did not know whether the claim was true.

Schwartz has also been less visible than some predecessors. She has not held a news conference or used her public platform to encourage vaccination against measles, the flu or COVID-19. She told the AP that, concerning measles, she did not believe a federal health official on television would sway most unvaccinated people. Many public health and medical leaders say she should be strongly urging Americans to get vaccinated. Dr. Wendy Armstrong, president of the Infectious Diseases Society of America, said, “I hope to see her display independence from the vaccine rhetoric and challenges that have been part of this administration. I don’t think we have seen that yet.”

Kennedy continues to support Schwartz. In a statement responding to Associated Press queries, the Health and Human Services Department quoted Kennedy as saying she had his full support. “Dr. Schwartz brings the judgment to make hard decisions and the courage to see them through. She is reforming CDC and sharpening its focus on its core mission: protecting Americans from disease,” the statement said.

## Staff morale, security and research publication concerns

Despite Schwartz’s transparency pledge, CDC and Health and Human Services officials for months would not release her salary, even though it was public information. CDC officials finally told the AP this week that she is paid about $375,000 a year, above the typical range for previous CDC directors. A CDC health scientist told the AP, “I don’t have a clear sense of what is important to her, what are her red lines.” The scientist added, “If we’re talking about radical transparency,” it is appropriate to know on what issues and what messaging she will not compromise. The employee spoke on condition of anonymity because of fear of retribution. Schwartz responded, “I would never do anything unethical or illegal. ... That is a red line.”

The AP interviewed eight current CDC workers about Schwartz’s tenure. They all spoke on condition of anonymity because they feared retribution. Some said they never had high expectations. Others said they were initially hopeful but lost confidence, with some blaming her handling of the measles death count. One said it was still too early to judge her. Several said they had expected more improvements and stability. Employees said announcements about leadership appointees arriving, departing and changing titles continued in a blur. None of the agency veterans interviewed by the AP said they had spoken with Schwartz.

Some employees also mentioned Caroline DeBerry, a former vice president at a conservative think tank and the author of independently published novels, including “Esther: The Woman Who Didn’t Know Her Place.” DeBerry started as acting director of communications in August. Employees said she gained notoriety within the agency when CDC website updates ground to a halt while awaiting her approval. Schwartz said, “There was a bit of clunkiness that happened one day.” She also said DeBerry was on “terminal leave,” that her last day would be in December and that she did not immediately offer additional detail.

Security has been a sensitive issue at the CDC, especially since an August 2025 shooting outside the entrance of its main campus. A Georgia man who had blamed the COVID-19 vaccine for making him depressed and suicidal opened fire, killed police officer David Rose and sprayed bullets into 150 windows. Rose is widely held up as a hero by CDC employees, and some expressed irritation that Schwartz referred to him as “Officer Stone” during a recent health summit appearance.

Tom Frieden, who served as CDC director in the Obama administration, said he occasionally had an escort outside the campus after receiving death threats. Schwartz moves through the CDC with a large security team, and staffers said they did not recall seeing that before. “It feels like they’re afraid of us,” one health scientist said. Schwartz said security leadership initially recommended that she walk the halls with about a half dozen guards, but that the detail had been reduced to one plainclothes guard. Some of the agency veterans interviewed by the AP said they had seen her around the CDC campus accompanied by a group of security guards.

The Associated Press described a directive about research publications as showing how far apart CDC staff felt from political leadership. Two CDC scientists told the AP that they were directed during a recent meeting to stop submitting research to journals run by organizations that have sued Kennedy. The prohibition would cover Pediatrics, one of the most-cited journals for doctors who specialize in children’s medical care, and Clinical Infectious Diseases, a highly regarded publication for doctors focused on germs. The scientists said the directive was clearly stated but was never written in a memo or email. Health and Human Services and CDC officials disputed the account, saying neither Kennedy nor any other Health and Human Services or CDC official ever issued such a directive.

At the health summit, Schwartz acknowledged that CDC employees had lost trust in the political appointees placed in charge of the agency, “and I’m trying to earn that trust every day.” She described work underway to stabilize the agency’s leadership and develop a “strategic hiring plan” for 2027. Employees described a workforce marked by exhaustion and uncertainty. “I think everyone is just exhausted,” one health scientist said. “There’s just a sense that we can’t go on like this anymore.”


Schwartz’s tenure has moved from Senate confirmation and a hearing at which she promised transparency and scientific independence to an early congressional test of her leadership.
