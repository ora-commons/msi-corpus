## More than half of hyperscaler electricity came from fossil fuels

The rapid buildout of "hyperscaler" datacenters across the United States has produced planet-heating emissions of roughly the scale Denmark emits in a year, according to new research. The study, published in Nature Sustainability and led by Gianluca Guidi, examined federal government data covering a year of operations ending in April 2025.

The buildout is visible on the ground: an aerial photograph taken on 9 November by Jeff Roberson of AP shows an Amazon datacenter under construction in New Florence, Missouri.

In that period, 403 hyperscalers — the largest datacenters, used primarily to handle the growth of artificial intelligence — emitted about 25.7 million metric tons of carbon dioxide. That equals the entire annual carbon pollution of Denmark, a country of 6 million people, and is about two to three times more than a previous estimate of hyperscale emissions in 2018. The facilities were also responsible for about 1.8% of total US electricity consumption.

The hyperscale group consumed an estimated 68 to 99 terawatt-hours of electricity over the year, the study found. Demand was concentrated in states such as Virginia, Oregon, Ohio and Iowa, and researchers said the figure is almost certainly a conservative estimate, since the rapid expansion of datacenters has only accelerated further since the study period closed.

"There has been an explosion in these datacenters and the CO2 emissions are pretty substantial," said Francesca Dominici, a professor of biostatistics at Harvard University and co-author of the study. "Since 2025 there have been many, many more datacenters so we need to think of this level of emissions as a lower bound. This is something that needs study and for policymakers to think about how to do this in a responsible way."

More than half of the generation used by hyperscalers came from fossil fuels, the research found. The growth of AI is being underpinned by a rise in new gas-fired power, sometimes built at the site of the facilities themselves, which is being favored over installed renewables such as solar and wind.

Gina McCarthy, a former climate adviser to Joe Biden's administration, said the study is an "important step" toward assessing the impact of datacenters. "Sadly, it doesn't surprise me that the majority of hyperscale datacenters rely on fossil fuels for their power generation despite the profound and lasting climate and health impacts that could be, and should be, avoided," McCarthy said.

The rise of datacenters has caused alarm among climate groups amid worsening global heating that may surpass previously agreed "safe" levels and cause an acceleration of the sort of deadly events seen this year, such as roasting heatwaves in Europe, wildfire smoke in North America and the disastrous landslide on the Nepal-Tibet border.

In the US, developers have defended the use of energy and water by the facilities as marginal when compared with other industries, such as agriculture, and have touted associated jobs and raised local taxes as beneficial for communities. Donald Trump, a vocal supporter of the AI industry, has said people who oppose the growth of datacenters will end up "backwards and poor."

But a bipartisan public backlash to datacenters shows little sign of abating in the US. Local opposition has focused on several big developments, such as a cluster of datacenters in the greater Memphis, Tennessee, region that are central to Elon Musk's AI ambitions but have faced protests and lawsuits over their noise and air pollution. Musk's AI company has responded to this by buying the homes of those who live nearby.

The vast water use of one of the world's largest planned datacenters in drought-hit Utah has also mobilized grassroots opposition. The site, called Stratos, will also emit a large amount of air pollution, a separate study recently found.

A separate study commissioned by the Natural Resources Defense Council concluded that Stratos could cause health-related economic costs of up to $690 million each year if it uses the dirtiest of the fossil fuel options to power itself, releasing up to 3,140 tons of soot-like particulate matter linked to cardiovascular and respiratory problems. Those plumes would travel across Utah and into Idaho, Wyoming, Colorado and Illinois, the research found.

"Stratos is a modern-day Trojan horse that would unleash deadly air pollution linked to serious, life-threatening health damages upon Utah communities and millions of people living downwind of this massive datacenter," said John Walke, director of federal clean air at the Natural Resources Defense Council, which commissioned the report. "Pollution from Stratos will contaminate the air and compromise the ability to live a healthy life both around the facility and in states hundreds of miles from the datacenter."

The developers behind Stratos have been planning to use combined-cycle natural gas but are also reportedly considering renewables and other options, and have been under political pressure to reduce the project's scale. Last month the project filed a new water application that reportedly claimed its water demands would be lower than historical agricultural uses at the property, a claim some environmental critics have questioned.
