## Section 301 proposal targets subsidies after Supreme Court ruling

President Donald Trump’s administration released a wide-ranging tariff proposal early Wednesday, the day before Thanksgiving, that would add 20% to duties on Chinese goods and raise import taxes on products from the European Union, Japan, South Korea, Taiwan and nine other major trading partners. A senior administration official disclosed the details on a late-night Tuesday call with reporters, ahead of a series of trade-practice investigations to be posted by the U.S. Trade Representative’s office. The official insisted on anonymity as a condition for discussing the reports before they were posted by the U.S. Trade Representative’s office. The official stressed that the tariffs remained at the proposal stage and provided no strict implementation timelines. The Associated Press described the tariff rollout as “another controversial twist in Trump’s economic policy.”

The administration said the duties are intended to discourage excessive manufacturing output in targeted economies, attributing that output to subsidies and other trade barriers that it says put American manufacturers at a disadvantage. Trump has promised that import taxes will bring back manufacturing jobs and reduce the federal deficit. The proposal also seeks to replace tariff revenue lost after the Supreme Court ruled in February that Trump could not declare an economic emergency to bypass Congress and impose across-the-board tariffs.

The administration plans to rely on Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974, which enables the United States to investigate and retaliate against foreign trade practices that violate existing deals, are not justified by market forces or actively discriminate against American goods. The senior official said the proposal would be consistent with trade deals Trump reached last year with dozens of countries after threatening severe tariff increases. The U.S. Trade Representative’s office was scheduled to release a series of investigations into the trade practices of 12 countries, the 27-member European Union and the self-governing island of Taiwan.

For some Chinese goods, the new 20% tariff would be stacked on other import taxes for a total rate of 45%. The USTR said on Wednesday that, like China, Vietnam also faces a 20% rate pending the finalization of trade talks, while tariffs for other trading partners range from 10% to 18%.

Under the proposal, Brazil and China would face some of the largest tariff increases. The Trump administration argues that these countries have helped spur an influx of goods at lower prices that undercut American companies, creating a trade deficit in goods that has recently exceeded $1 trillion annually.

A senior administration official said Brazil was singled out for additional duties after talks on trade issues stalled. The United States was proposing higher import taxes on raw and refined sugar and a new 10% tariff on aircraft, a move that would affect Embraer, Brazil’s largest aircraft maker.

The other nations named for the new tariffs were Bangladesh, Cambodia, India, Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Switzerland, Thailand and Vietnam. The senior official said Mexico and Norway were the two other countries with pending investigations.

The senior administration official said the new, product-specific duties would not stack with separate tariffs on automobiles, steel, aluminum and other products imposed under Section 232 of the Trade Expansion Act of 1962. The official also said they would not stack with levies of 10% to 12.5% announced in July for 60 trading partners that the White House said had inadequately enforced bans on goods produced by forced labor.

The announcement came after midterm voting gave Democrats majorities in the House and Senate. About two-thirds of voters said Trump’s policies had made the economy worse, according to The AP Voter Poll, a sweeping survey of the American electorate conducted in all 50 states. The Associated Press reported that last year’s tariffs contributed to higher inflation, which angered voters and led to calls by Democratic lawmakers to require congressional approval of import taxes going forward.

China has pushed back consistently on U.S. tariffs. On Wednesday, China’s Ministry of Foreign Affairs again stated its opposition while calling for the United States to resolve the issue through dialogue. “A tariff war or a trade war serves no party’s interests,” spokesperson Guo Jiakun said. “The so-called overcapacity is a false narrative, and China opposes using it as a pretext for political manipulation.”

The senior administration official said the new tariffs would not surprise Beijing and were unlikely to undermine the U.S.-China trade truce reached last year, which is set to expire on Jan. 10. Trump met Xi Jinping in Shenzhen, China, last week and plans to host the Chinese leader next month at the Group of 20 summit in Florida. The summit could address the truce, access to rare earth minerals and China’s purchases of American agricultural products.

The Associated Press confirmed the planned tariff announcement before the call, according to people who insisted on anonymity because they were not authorized to comment publicly. Bloomberg and The Wall Street Journal had reported the plans earlier Tuesday.

AP writer Huizhong Wu in Bangkok contributed to this report.
