## Myanmar calls the nine-to-six decision unfair

The United States said Thursday that Washington was examining the International Court of Justice's judgment finding Myanmar committed genocide against the Rohingya minority, while stressing the need to avoid the “politicisation” of the court. The European Union called the decision a “landmark” and said Myanmar must implement it “without delay.” China highlighted “issues such as jurisdiction overreach and expanded interpretation,” while Myanmar's presidential spokesperson called the nine-to-six decision “unfair.”

The State Department said it was examining the judgment and reiterated US support for the people of Myanmar. The department said it shared “grave concerns over the continued abuses of human rights against Rohingya Muslims and members of other minority groups” and noted that the US was the largest single humanitarian donor to the Rohingya humanitarian crisis response.

Donald Trump's administration had vowed to dismantle the International Criminal Court but had yet to question the legitimacy of the ICJ. The ICC prosecutes individuals, while the ICJ decides whether states are responsible for genocide.

In Brussels, the EU said Myanmar must fully implement the ruling “without delay” and declared that “compliance with ICJ’s decisions is not optional, it is an obligation under international law”. An EU spokesperson said, “The European Union holds the ICJ in the highest regard and remains steadfast in its support for its critical role in the peaceful resolution of disputes and the upholding of the rule of law at international level”.

The United Kingdom reaffirmed “full support” for the court and the international legal order. It expelled Myanmar's defence attache and called for measures to “ensure the court’s ruling sends a clear message that such crimes cannot be committed with impunity”. Australia called on Myanmar to comply fully with the judgment and its obligations under international law.

China, which has offered rare international support for Myanmar's military rulers since they seized power five years ago, highlighted “issues such as jurisdiction overreach and expanded interpretation”. Foreign Ministry spokesperson Guo Jiakun told a news briefing that China believed “such practices are not conducive to the settlement of disputes”. He said, “The international community should make joint efforts in this direction to avoid complicating this issue.” Western nations cut ties with Myanmar after the 2021 coup that ousted a civilian-led government.

Myanmar's presidential spokesperson said Friday that the ruling was “unfair”, noting the judges' nine-to-six vote. Khaing Khaing Soe said at a press conference in the capital, Naypyidaw: “When we look at the result, not all 15 judges agreed with the decision.” He added: “So this was not a unanimous decision … I would say it is not fair.” Myanmar's military rulers have denied carrying out genocide, saying their actions targeted a Rohingya militant group that conducted insurgent attacks.

UN Secretary-General António Guterres called the ruling “a demonstration of international justice and a reaffirmation that impunity for such horrific acts must not stand”. At a Friday rally in Cox's Bazar, UN General Assembly president Khalilur Rahman said: “The Rohingya people have endured immense suffering, including displacement and violence, and their plight must not be forgotten. This landmark ruling underscores the imperative of justice for victims and the necessity of upholding international law.”

The ruling, delivered Thursday by the court based in The Hague, was the first time the world's highest court had found a state responsible for genocide. The decision could set a precedent for future genocide claims, notably the case against Israel.

The ICJ cannot enforce its own rulings, but its decisions are considered binding, carry diplomatic weight and can shape international action, including sanctions. Under the 1948 convention, the court had to find an intent to destroy the Rohingya “in whole or in part” to establish genocide.

The ruling capped a lengthy legal campaign dating to August 2017, when Myanmar's military launched what it called a “clearance campaign” in Rakhine state in response to an attack by a Rohingya insurgent group. Reports rapidly emerged of mass rapes and killings by security forces, as well as the burning of thousands of homes. Judges rejected Myanmar's claim that its actions were primarily intended as counter-terrorism operations, or even that they were an attempt to punish or forcibly displace the population but without committing genocide.

Rohingya refugees watched the ruling on a screen at a camp in Bangladesh's Cox's Bazar on 19 November. About 1.2 million Rohingya refugees now live in Bangladesh, primarily in crowded camps across Cox's Bazar district, where they face fire hazards, infectious diseases, monsoon landslides, inadequate sanitation and strict work restrictions.

Min Aung Hlaing, the country's leader and the former general who headed the military during the 2016-17 attacks, has a pending ICC arrest warrant on allegations of crimes against humanity. The former general overthrew the elected government of Aung San Suu Kyi in the 2021 coup. Tens of thousands died in the conflict that followed. He was installed as civilian president in April after elections that were widely discredited.

Judges at the court based in The Hague have also been asked to rule on other genocide allegations. South Africa has accused Israel of breaching the genocide convention during its offensive in Gaza, a claim Israel vehemently denies; that case is years from completion. A 2024 interim ICJ judgment ordered Israel to ensure its forces do not commit acts of genocide against Palestinians in Gaza.

Ukraine also has a case pending against Russia. Shortly after Russia's full-scale invasion in February 2022, Kyiv accused Russia of fabricating genocide claims against Ukraine to justify the attack.
