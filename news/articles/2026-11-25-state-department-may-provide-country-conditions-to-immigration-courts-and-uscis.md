## Justice Department did not explain the reports' role in asylum decisions

The U.S. State Department said that, in line with U.S. laws and regulations, it “may provide information about conditions in foreign countries” to immigration courts and U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services. The Justice Department, which oversees immigration courts, did not respond to a request for comment about what role the reports play in influencing asylum decisions. The Associated Press review examined six-page reports prepared for use in immigration courts on Guatemala, Honduras, Mexico and El Salvador without resolving how immigration judges weigh those documents.

The Associated Press found that the reports use cherry-picked data, careful phrasing and, in some cases, facts that do not stand up to scrutiny, creating misleading depictions of countries often plagued by violence, government corruption and police inaction. The AP said the reports appear designed to make it easier for judges to deny asylum to people fleeing domestic violence and criminal gangs, painting pictures of life in their home countries that often clash with reality.

The clearest contrast concerns Honduras' Gracias a Dios region. The State Department travel advisory warns Americans not to visit the area, citing violent crime, armed robbery, murder and active terrorist groups. A State Department background report obtained by the Associated Press for use in immigration courts states, “Overall, Gracias a Dios is a low-crime, functioning department.”

Elliot Young, a professor at Lewis & Clark College who analyzed the documents for the college's Migration and Asylum Lab, said the reports were “reverse-engineered.” Young, a scholar of Latin America and asylum law who has testified as an expert witness in more than 700 asylum cases, said: “They're trying to hit the most frequent kinds of (asylum) cases that come up and provide counterevidence to the overwhelming evidence that these are dangerous places.”

Immigration judges must determine whether people seeking asylum based on domestic violence or criminal threats have a reasonable fear of persecution because of race, religion, nationality, political views or the often-debated catch-all category, “membership in a particular social group.” Applicants must also show that their government cannot or will not protect them and that they cannot move elsewhere in their home country to escape persecution. The AP said the reports appear aimed most directly at the regulation requiring applicants to show that persecution cannot be escaped by simply moving within the home country, a rule that is key in domestic- and criminal-violence cases.

The reports mention good schools, low crime, quality hospitals and constitutions that protect individual rights, with brief notes that “the availability of services” can vary across each country. The AP said the descriptions can sometimes seem like travel brochures. The documents highlight an off-the-beaten-track tourist destination in Guatemala and a province in El Salvador with low crime and 24-hour emergency medical care.

For El Salvador, where the government of President Nayib Bukele has long faced accusations of widespread human rights abuses, including torture, the State Department report says the government's approach to rights abuses “is no different than that of developed countries in Europe and North America, including the United States itself.” It highlights a handful of prosecutions tied to the civil war that ended in 1992 and says, “In any country, a rogue official can mistreat those under their power.” For Honduras' Gracias a Dios province, the report says violent crime rates “compare favorably with rates of violent crime in many parts of the United States.”

The AP said the specific place descriptions tell only part of the story. Neela Chakravartula, an asylum attorney with the Center for Gender & Refugee Studies at the University of California College of the Law, San Francisco, said: “The fact that there may be pockets of a country that are relatively safe for some people doesn't mean that everybody can just move to that part of the country.” She added: “We'd all be living in Malibu.”

Former officials and advocates said the six-page reports pale in comparison with traditional State Department human-rights reports, lengthy dissections of more than 100 nations that have been truncated and delayed under the second Trump administration. Scott Busby, a former deputy assistant secretary in the State Department's Bureau of Democracy, Human Rights and Labor who is now a senior adviser at Human Rights First, said: “These shorter reports clearly don't address the full range of human rights issues that the country reports have traditionally addressed.” Busby called the issue especially problematic because the country reports for the previous year had not been issued.

Even during President Joe Biden's years, most asylum claims were rejected, the AP said. The denial rate rose from roughly 72% at the end of that administration to 94% in June under President Donald Trump, according to Transactional Records Access Clearinghouse, a data research nonprofit.

The AP wrote that Trump “had a point about poverty,” noting that legal and illegal migration, particularly from Latin America, had expanded over the past two decades as people sought more economic opportunity. The AP also said increasing asylum claims based on safety fears from domestic partners and criminal gangs accompanied that expansion.

The AP said Trump was elected to a second term in part by focusing on frustrated voters who had seen illegal immigration soar under Biden. Border arrests from Mexico reached a record 250,000 in December 2023, while the asylum system faced a backlog of millions of cases.

Cutting back on asylum has been a centerpiece of Trump's immigration policy dating to his first term, when he dismissed migrants waiting in Mexico to get into the U.S. as “not legitimate asylum-seekers.” Trump said most asylum seekers were fleeing poverty. At a 2018 White House appearance, he said, “Asylum is a very special protection intended only for those fleeing government persecution based on race, religion and other protected status.” The AP said Trump's description was years out of date: asylum was largely seen after World War II as protection for people fleeing political persecution, but its definition had been expanded long ago to encompass some people fleeing domestic and criminal violence.

Meanwhile, the Trump administration has reshaped immigration courts as part of its push to increase deportations, organizing mass hearings, authorizing military lawyers as immigration judges and giving judges more leeway to summarily dismiss claims. Associated Press reporter Joshua Goodman in Miami contributed to the report.
