## A 2023 review included 39 of 2,349 mushroom-cancer studies

Registered dietitian Abby Langer, based in Toronto, offered a practical starting point for consumers considering functional mushroom supplements for gut health or cognition. "Take a closer look at what you're eating, how you're eating, when you're eating," she advised, "and see if there's any tweaks that you need to make." Langer framed possible adjustments rather than a prescribed diet: "Maybe it's possible to eat more fiber or protein, reduce consumption of alcohol or sugar-sweetened beverages, skip fewer meals, or eat out less often," she said. "There's so many things that people can potentially improve on," she said, "before adding something else that's promised to be this panacea for everything that ails you."

The clinical evidence summarized by Langer and Narayanan was limited and uneven. In their 2023 analysis, Narayanan and her colleagues surveyed 2,349 studies examining the relationship between medicinal mushrooms and cancer, but only 39 were robust enough to include. Of those, 31 showed beneficial effects, six did not reveal any advantage and two highlighted worse outcomes. The report said the beneficial results included improved quality of life, reduced side-effect profiles of different chemotherapeutic drugs, improved anti-inflammatory and anti-metastatic effects, and improved disease-free survival as well as overall survival in a few trials. Overall, the analysis could not make clear conclusions about "the complex biological effects of mushrooms on the immune system in cancer."

In Narayanan's estimation, there is not yet enough evidence to support routine clinical use of mushrooms in cancer treatment.

One specific clinical result involved PSK, a purified turkey tail extract. Japanese work looked at how PSK improved immune response in cancer patients. A meta-analysis considered 8,009 patients in eight randomized controlled trials who had undergone surgical removal of a gastric tumor and, in almost all cases, chemotherapy. Those who were also given PSK showed a 12% reduction in risk of death.

"This is not something that we brush off," said Santhosshi Narayanan, an integrative oncologist based at the University of Texas MD Anderson Cancer Center who recognizes the potential of PSK. She said the result "does not mean that PSK or any mushrooms alone cures cancer. It just shows to us that we need to pay attention to these natural products."

Narayanan said much remained to be learned about the extract, particularly for populations outside Japan with different genetics or treatment regimes who might experience unwanted side effects. "We need to do more research and understand how we can use it," she said. "Like which people, which type of patients benefit from which type of mushroom — because that itself is a whole science."

Langer said robust clinical trials proving connections between mushrooms and advertised benefits, as well as their safety, were largely lacking. "In my opinion, I don't think that there's much evidence for functional mushrooms as of now," she said. Langer said mushrooms accumulate and concentrate materials from the ground in which they are grown, both good and bad, and advised caution.

Langer noted that mushrooms had been incorporated into healing practices for generations in certain communities, including parts of Asia. But, she said, "just because something has been used for a long time doesn't mean that it's safe, that it's effective, that the dosing has been established." "There are a lot of unknowns," she said.

Still, Langer said empirical support for mushrooms' potential positive impacts was growing. "The clinical research on specific functional mushroom products is young," she said, "but it's accumulating fast and already includes some impressive results." Mushrooms are a good source of vitamin D2 and antioxidants, and their cell walls contain beta-glucan, which has been linked to gut health, including a lower risk of Crohn's disease, and to immune response. Research into mushrooms' effects on cognition has been mixed.

Julie Daoust's route into mushrooms began in the French Alps, where she grew up foraging. "So as a kid, we'd go out to forage," she recalled. "We'd come across species like lion's mane or reishi. And I became more and more interested in those species." She later earned a Ph.D. in chemistry while searching nature for novel pharmaceuticals, and her work led her back to mushrooms. Humans are more closely related to mushrooms than plants, even though many modern medicines and therapeutics come from plants.

Daoust is vice president of science and innovation at Monterey Nutra, a company specializing in the genetics and cultivation of mushrooms that sells fungal extracts and powders. Her team does not work with poisonous varieties or psychedelic mushrooms containing psilocybin. It focuses on functional or medicinal mushrooms, which she likens to tiny cauldrons of potentially useful molecules. Daoust said the closer biochemical relationship gives researchers "you have more chances of finding something that's going to have bioactivity in a human than you would with other source organisms."

She also said, "We love to look for soft things that can't run away very fast," because "they typically produce really interesting chemistry to defend themselves or to communicate with others in their environment to survive." Daoust said lion's mane, described as "a soft chandelier of a fungus," may aid cognition, while reishi and turkey tail, which grow as hard brown scales on decomposing trees, could bolster immune health. On social media, influencers and advertisers promise that medicinal mushrooms taken as supplements can slow aging and improve energy levels, focus and immune health.

PSK recipients showed a 12% reduction in risk of death in the analyzed trials, while the broader 2023 analysis could not make clear conclusions about mushrooms' immune effects in cancer. Narayanan said there is not yet enough evidence to support routine clinical use of mushrooms in cancer treatment. For consumers weighing a supplement, Langer recommended examining nutrition basics first. For researchers, Narayanan said, the next task was to determine which people and patient types benefit from which mushrooms.
