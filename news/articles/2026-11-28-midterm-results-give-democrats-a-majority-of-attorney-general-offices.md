## New AGs could pursue cases on economy, environment, abortion and civil rights

Following the midterm elections, voters elected Democrats to serve as attorney general in Georgia, Iowa, Kansas, Ohio and Texas. The wins give Democrats a majority of the state offices, and Alaska's incoming Democratic governor announced his pick for attorney general after Republicans had run that state office. NPR reported that roughly seven in ten Americans are set to live in a state with a Democratic attorney general.

State attorneys general typically have sweeping discretion over what alleged crimes to investigate, what lawsuits to bring or drop for state residents and which court cases to pursue. The Democratic victories could therefore produce more cases across the country advancing their positions on the economy, environment, abortion, civil rights and crime rather than the Republican perspective.

Sean Rankin, president of the Democratic Attorneys General Association, said in a statement to NPR, "Voters picked AGs who will stand up and fight for them, whether that's taking on rising costs, pushing back against a lawless administration, or holding bad actors accountable. It's a time to celebrate, and also to get to work."

Republican leaders were focused on rebuilding after the losses. Adam Piper, executive director of the Republican Attorneys General Association, said, "There's no sugarcoating it, this was the worst election for Republicans since 1974." He contended that Republican attorneys general policies remained broadly supported by voters but were overshadowed by the unpopularity of the rest of the GOP. He noted that Republican attorney general candidates ran ahead of GOP candidates for governor and U.S. Senate in some states.

Republican attorneys general convened for their fall meeting in Dallas, Texas, where outgoing Attorney General Ken Paxton had lost his bid for the Senate. Paxton will soon be replaced by Democrat Nathan Johnson. Piper said attendees grimly reflected on their challenges while also strategizing how to position Republican attorneys general to support the GOP as 2028 approaches.

Piper said the association was watching for potential "lawfare" by Democrats, including efforts to change the number of Supreme Court justices or welcome new states into the Union. He also set priorities for the party: "We have to have better quality of candidates at the top of the ticket," Piper said. "We have to have better message discipline, and we need other committees in Washington to focus on what RAGA has done, and that is making sure we're good stewards of resources."

Democratic attorneys general held their own conference in Chicago, which some attendees described as both a celebration of the victories and an orientation for incoming officeholders. Illinois Attorney General Kwame Raoul said at a Nov. 19 press briefing with other Democratic attorneys general, "While a lot of the focus turns to what's next in Washington, we understand and we're not naive to the fact that we will continue to have a critical role in protecting our republic."

Nathan Johnson, the incoming Texas attorney general, said the Democratic wins in the state house and governor's mansion could prompt Republicans to cooperate on a "cleansing" anti-corruption agenda. He also signaled support for legislation and legal challenges to President Donald Trump's efforts to build a wall in Big Bend National Park on Texas' border with Mexico.

The Texas attorney general's office has traditionally brought cases for conservative legal causes with effects across the country and has served as a training ground for Republican legal talent. U.S. Sen. John Cornyn and Texas Gov. Greg Abbott both served as attorney general before seeking higher office, while Sen. Ted Cruz served as solicitor general in the office before running for Congress. Other staffers went on to serve as judges, law clerks and prosecutors.

James Tierney, a Harvard Law School professor who has advised more than thirty attorneys general from both parties on staffing and legal philosophy, said, "Texas was a major intellectual force for the Republican attorneys general, and that's not going to be easily replaced." Tierney added, "There are a lot of very smart, good lawyers in various AG offices who are very conservative." He continued, "But they don't have the heft and the size and the number of attorneys that Texas brings, and so for them, it's a very significant loss."

Anthony Michael Kreis, a constitutional law professor at Georgia State University, said, "These new attorneys general are not going to be particularly favorable towards the Trump administration, and will take an oppositional position in a number of different cases." Kreis said the shift could produce direct lawsuits against the administration in coalition with other attorneys general.

Many incoming Democratic attorneys general campaigned on bringing cases against alleged price gouging and digital surveillance by companies, as well as taking a tougher approach to antitrust concerns. They also face pressure to listen to local business communities, including major multinational corporations.

Swain Wood, a partner at WilmerHale who now co-chairs the firm's practice advising clients on state attorneys general, said, "I think it's actually likely to increase bipartisan coalitions, particularly on economic issues." Wood, who served as assistant attorney general to North Carolina Democratic Attorney General Josh Stein, cited landmark settlements against tobacco and opioid distributors as cases where bipartisan groups of attorneys general found common ground.

Attorneys general from both parties have also worked together in recent years on cases regulating social media platforms. Democrats elected in newly-minted swing states may already face pressure to choose popular or bipartisan legal actions, one way to maintain voters' support as they prepare for stiff reelection challenges.
