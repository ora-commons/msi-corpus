## Affordability and turnout test Blair's midterm model

James Blair, the 37-year-old White House deputy chief of staff who became political director of Donald Trump's 2024 campaign, has stepped away from his administration role to run Maga Inc and take the lead in the Republican effort to hold the House and Senate in the 3 November midterms. Trump has said Blair is expected to return to the White House after the election and described the absence as a “short” leave. The Guardian reported that Maga Inc, the political action committee Blair now runs, had $415m in the bank by the end of August.

The Guardian credited Blair's data-driven approach with delivering Trump's 2024 victory but assessed that Trump's unpopularity and Trump's idiosyncratic style did not bode well this time. Blair acknowledged that the midterm mechanics are harder: “Yes, it’s harder. Being a challenger is easier in some ways than being the incumbent party. That’s borne out by the entire history of the midterms. And of course with President Trump on the ballot, we historically do much better.” Asked whether he was adopting the same strategy as in 2024, Blair said: “There are elements that are similar. Low-propensity voters are the bread and butter of our coalition and they’re even harder to turn out in a midterm year.”

Blair described the coalition behind that effort in more qualified terms: “President Trump built a historic coalition and movement that is uniquely powerful and at the same time hasn’t been entirely transferable to other Republicans. We are all trying to change that, and nobody more than him, as evidenced by him pouring his energy, sweat and treasure into the midterms.”

Chris Whipple, author of *The Gatekeepers: How the White House Chiefs of Staff Define Every Presidency*, recalled a candid December conversation with Blair about the midterm outlook. “Blair said essentially that it doesn’t matter what we do about [Jeffrey] Epstein or any of the other problems we have. If we don’t do something about affordability, we are fucked. And so here we are,” Whipple recalled. He added: “He’s a smart, savvy political operative who understands that Trump should have been talking about affordability and gas prices ... but he also understands the nature of the beast, that Trump is going to talk about whatever he wants to. He’s a smart political operative but he’s also a realist.” Separately, [Federal Reserve series FEDFUNDS](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/FEDFUNDS) puts the effective federal funds rate at 3.75% as of 25 October 2026, the raw monthly average of the Fed's policy rate and one channel through which borrowing costs reach households.

Larry Jacobs, director of the Center for the Study of Politics and Governance at the University of Minnesota, said national Republican groups under Blair's influence refused to put major funding behind Minnesota Senate candidate Michele Tafoya despite close polls. “Blair’s fingerprints were all over it,” Jacobs said. “The data doesn’t support it. Yes, the polls show her close, but close is not winning. They’re now in the business of trying to cut their losses rather than pick up wins.” More broadly, The Guardian reported that the congressional redistricting push personally championed by Trump and Blair had appeared to fail in the short term in Texas and elsewhere. The strategy relied on replicating 2024 gains among Latino voters, gains that looked set to erode amid anger over inflation and what The Guardian called “hardline immigration crackdowns.”

Earlier in October, it was revealed that Blair and Chris LaCivita had traveled to Republika Srpska, a self-governing region of Bosnia and Herzegovina. The New York Times reported that the pair received substantial payments to advise the regional government before local elections. Trump distanced himself from both men on 7 October, describing them as “outside consultants” in a Truth Social post and warning that if he found a conflict of interest, he would “terminate them, immediately!” Reuters reported that senior White House officials questioned why Blair was taking on lucrative overseas work while he was supposed to oversee the final stretch of the Republican campaign. For Trump, The Guardian said, the larger concern was that Blair's outside work distracted him from his political responsibilities.

Blair responded with a rare public statement. “All of this mudslinging is ridiculous, and counterproductive,” he insisted. “President Trump and me are focused on helping Republicans win in November. Every other Republican should be doing the same.” On Thursday, Blair tweeted that he and LaCivita were donating their full fee to the future Trump presidential library. He wrote: “I love the President and am eternally grateful for the opportunities he has provided me. Contributing to the preservation of his legacy of greatness is my honor!”

The Guardian described Blair and Stephen Miller as both young, bald and fathers of four and as figures behind Trump's political operation. It characterized Miller as the president's “ideological enforcer” and relayed Time magazine's assessment that Blair could “be the most important person in Donald Trump’s Washington you’ve never heard of.” The Guardian said a tight-knit circle of Florida loyalists, strategists and political fixers had become central to Trump's political operation, with the Mar-a-Lago club serving as a center of political power. Blair was born in Tampa. His father was a small businessman who worked in printing; his mother was a homemaker. He has three older brothers and a younger sister.

Blair graduated from Florida State University with a bachelor's degree in finance and in 2013 founded the political consulting firm Rapid Loop Consulting. At 22, he interviewed for a policy and fundraising position under Richard Corcoran, then a Florida powerbroker and future speaker of the state house of representatives. The Guardian said Corcoran was instantly struck by the young applicant's intellect and unusual composure. “I was blown away,” Corcoran said by phone, and hired Blair as his go-to staffer throughout the legislature. The Guardian reported that Blair developed a reputation for working around the clock, combining ruthless efficiency with an easygoing, personal charm among colleagues.

Corcoran, now president of the New College of Florida in Sarasota, said: “James Blair is one of the finest young men I’ve ever met. He’s an A-plus in almost every category. He was tremendously discreet. He worked around the clock. He was extremely bright.” He added: “James is a great hang. He’s very personable. Every single legislator that worked with him – he worked with all of them because I was my go-to guy – will tell you that James Blair is one heck of a great guy.”

By 2018, Blair was senior adviser to Republican Ron DeSantis's gubernatorial campaign before joining his administration as deputy chief of staff, where he formed an alliance with political strategist Susie Wiles, now Trump's chief of staff. Chris Whipple said: “He is clearly one of Susie Wiles’s favourite people, one of her so-called junkyard dogs. She thinks very highly of him. That’s a pretty strong bond.” Blair resigned from DeSantis's office in 2019, weeks before DeSantis turned against Wiles, ousting her from his political committee and helping engineer her expulsion from Trump's political operation. Wiles returned to Trump's camp the following year, running his Florida re-election effort and bringing Blair with her. Trump lost that election to Joe Biden.

Wiles hired Blair again for Trump's 2024 bid. In March 2024, Blair became political director of the presidential campaign, helping shape its voter-targeting and get-out-the-vote strategy. Larry Jacobs observed that Blair quickly established himself as Trump's “political brain,” using data, polling and voter modelling to dictate strategy. The Guardian described Blair's defining contribution as a willingness to discard Washington's conventional wisdom. Traditional operations relied on costly brick-and-mortar field offices and heavy television advertising, while Blair argued that broadcast television was losing influence to digital-first strategies. Blair closed field offices and outsourced get-out-the-vote operations to independent super political action committees.

Jacobs said: “Washington is filled with folks who make their living from consulting, and Blair came in with a whole new approach and, frankly, hurt the bottom line for a lot of consultants. He took a lot of abuse, but the president’s comfortable win was read as a confirmation of Blair’s data-driven approach.” A Republican strategist who did not wish to be named added: “The thing about James is that, when he believes something is right, he’s willing to be attacked for it. He will pursue a strategy he thinks is right even if it’s unpopular in the Beltway.”

Roger Stone, a longtime Florida political operative, argued that Trump's core strategy team — Wiles, Blair, consultant Chris LaCivita and pollster Tony Fabrizio — deserved credit for orchestrating what he called the greatest political comeback since Richard Nixon in 1968. “People don’t understand that political strategy is a data-based science,” Stone said. “It takes a certain skill not only to interpret sophisticated polling data, but also to use the numbers and put them into action. This is something I think James excels at. Blair is among a handful of those who really understand how to interpret and implement the data.” Sean Spicer, a former White House press secretary, said Blair “made a lot of big good decisions that resulted in a win,” while cautioning: “He’s fairly data driven but, like a lot of things in politics, you’ve got to have a good sense of judgment where the data guides you 80% of the way and then 20% is judgment on timing and on tone.”

After Trump's second inauguration, Blair entered the White House as a deputy chief of staff, bringing political affairs, intergovernmental affairs and legislative affairs under his control. The Guardian characterized the resulting portfolio as unusually broad. He worked with Congress on the president's “One Big Beautiful Bill Act.” Stone described Blair's working style as “intense, blunt, extraordinarily organised” and fueled by “incredible” energy levels. Spicer said: “He’s a guy’s guy. You can joke around with him. He doesn’t take himself too seriously, but he knows his stuff. He puts his head down and gets the job done.”

The Guardian described Blair as maintaining a much lower public profile than Stephen Miller and said it took Trump a while to fully appreciate his talents. The president told a holiday reception last year: “I’d hear from other people he’s a total political genius, but I didn’t see it. And you know what? He’s not quiet. He’s incredible – the job you’ve done, thank you, James.” In a phone interview, Blair said Trump “is the most patriotic person of anyone you know. He loves America and he wants it to do well. He’s willing to make hard decisions.” The Guardian wrote that Blair had sufficiently impressed Trump to earn the nickname “Brilliant James.” A well-positioned source told the Guardian: “I would say that James is probably twice, five times, 10 times more effective than Stephen Miller.”

The Guardian said the setback came as little surprise to those who believed Blair had been riding for a fall. One person who worked closely with Blair for many years told The Guardian that Blair “is an arrogant asshole who always thinks he’s the smartest guy in the room,” adding that “his arrogance will result in the worst Republican election result for 20 years.” The same person called him “a mercenary” who “will go to the person with the most money,” and said, “If success is measured by how much money there is in your bank account, James is a winner.” Blair rejected that account: “A quick look at my professional history proves I’ve worked for both well-funded candidates and those with few resources and helped them succeed,” he said. “I work for candidates whose cause I believe in. I work intensely hard, and I don’t apologise for that.”

Spicer pushed back on suggestions that Blair was in politics for personal enrichment. “I find it kind of bullshit, to be honest with you,” he said. “The guy works his tail off. That’s an undeniable fact. He was a government salaried employee making $170,000 a year. Just after winning the 2024 election, the guy could have hit the speaker circuit and made a heck of a lot more money.”

Trump had said Blair was expected to return to the White House after the midterms, describing his departure as a “short” leave of absence. The source who worked with Blair for years disagreed: “There’s no way he’s returning to the White House. James won’t go to work for JD Vance in 2028, because he thinks he will lose. He will present himself as Marco Rubio’s campaign manager in six years.”

On 3 November, voters will determine whether Blair can defy the odds again. The Guardian warned that if the results were as catastrophic for Republicans as opinion polls suggested, he could face the wrath of a capricious president looking for a scapegoat, and “Brilliant James” could find himself with a less desirable nickname. For Rick Wilson, a Florida-based political strategist and co-founder of the anti-Trump Lincoln Project, no amount of data modelling could overcome what he called the “fundamental flaw at the heart of Trump’s political machine.” “It’s hard for the Republicans, including smart kids like Blair, to convert on that stuff right now because they don’t have a steady hand at the tiller,” Wilson said. “Trump is crazy, and he’s gonna act crazy, and he’s gonna be crazy – all day long, every day, and no one’s going to turn that around.” He added: “James Blair can be a raging political genius, and it’s not going to change that Donald Trump is an insane person.”
