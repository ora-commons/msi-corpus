## Backers include energy companies, big tech, some Democrats, labor and environmental groups

Sarah Gonzalez-Rothi served as the senior director for water at the White House Council on Environmental Quality under President Biden. She opposes the Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act now before the House, as do hundreds of climate, tribal, public health, historic preservation and civil rights groups. Gonzalez-Rothi said of the bill's supporters: "People see a very slim chance to address a very urgent issue, and they think they've found the way to do it." "I think the way they've come up with doing it is looking at rose-colored glasses at text that, if they are wrong, has such severe consequences that it could actually make things worse," she said.

Wesley Furlong is a senior staff attorney with the Native American Rights Fund. He says tribes were not consulted about the bill even though it threatens their lands and sovereignty. "From our perspective, there are no pros. There are only cons," Furlong said. "It will fundamentally change the federal government's relationship with Indian tribes in a really negative way."

Xan Fishman leads the energy program at the Bipartisan Policy Center. "This isn't just, like, a mushy, middle, moderate, do-nothing bill. Like, it's actually a big-deal bill," he said. Fishman said the bill tries to address a permitting system that has grown out of proportion, while protecting the environment and reaching a yes-or-no decision within a reasonable amount of time.

The measure has drawn an unusual alliance of support from energy companies, big tech, some Democrats and a mix of labor and environmental groups.

That measure has already cleared one chamber. The Senate first voted to bypass the committee markup process, skipping hearings and rewrites, and the Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act, or BAAJA, passed last week with only one amendment by a vote of 62 to 33. The nearly 500-page bill was introduced in the Senate at the end of September and now heads to the House. President Trump has said he will sign it. Supporters say it would speed the approval of federal infrastructure and energy projects ranging from wind turbines to data centers, improve America's aging grid, create jobs and lower electricity bills.

Critics say BAAJA would weaken bedrock environmental and historic preservation laws, including by limiting input from tribes and the public. Critics also say lawmakers did not give the bill the time or debate it deserved in the lead-up to the midterms. "And then they come back, and the first thing that they do the Monday after the election is vote on this bill," Furlong said.

One provision would require data centers to cover their own energy costs. Critics say that could make it easier to build data centers on public lands. Furlong said other provisions would make it harder to challenge or change permits once they have been issued. "Courts can't really stop these projects, and future administrations can't stop them, and so as soon as they are permitted, we are stuck with them forever," he said.

The House could vote in the days after Thanksgiving. Critics want the House to either fix or reject the bill before the year ends. Some key Democrats have already said they oppose the bill, but it is not clear whether they have the support or the time to revise it before Congress adjourns on January 3.
