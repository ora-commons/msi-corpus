## Ohio households report medicine, food and utility tradeoffs

The U.S. Department of Agriculture released a proposed rule on Nov. 10 that would eliminate broad-based categorical eligibility for the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program, also known as food stamps. SNAP is the biggest federal assistance program. The policy currently lets states align SNAP income and asset limits with those for Temporary Assistance for Needy Families. Beyond the Cliff Coalition estimated that 3.7 million households receiving SNAP would no longer qualify, while the Children's Defense Fund said the proposal could put the food security of more than six million children at risk. The rule is open for comment through Dec. 10 and has an expected implementation date of Oct. 1, 2027 if approved.

The proposal would add new eligibility limits in the same federal policy environment created by the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, which President Donald Trump championed and signed in 2025. Since the law was signed, SNAP cuts have remained a leading concern for anti-hunger advocates. The spending law made the biggest cuts to SNAP in the program's history, and experts estimated millions would be left without food assistance, including hundreds of thousands of Ohioans. More than 1 million U.S. children have already lost SNAP access, and the cuts were projected to spill over into free breakfasts and lunches during the school year. Medicaid cuts and new work requirements are also expected to make some people ineligible for help.

Most Ohioans currently must have monthly household income at or below 130% of the federal poverty level to qualify for SNAP, according to state guidance. Households with elderly or disabled members, or those meeting other eligibility factors, can receive assistance up to 200% of the poverty level. That threshold equates to $5,500 a month for a family of four.

A 2026 Ohio Association of Foodbanks study found that more than half of surveyed Ohio households had to choose between paying for medicine or food. Six in 10 also had to choose between buying food or utilities while dealing with health problems and other financial struggles. “Ohio families are visiting food pantries as they work hard to keep up with rising food costs, inflation, and other external pressures,” Ohio Association of Foodbanks executive director Joree Novotny said when the survey was released.

Feeding America reported that more than 1.8 million Ohioans experienced food insecurity in 2024, the most recent data released. That amounted to 15.3% of Ohioans, with an estimated 60% of those affected living above the poverty-level threshold for SNAP qualification.

A Feeding America report released in July put the national average cost of a meal at $3.70 in 2024 and the national food budget shortfall at more than $33 billion that year. Ohio's average meal cost was slightly lower at $3.53, while the state's annual food budget shortfall was $1.22 billion in 2024. The report said: “Food insecurity is often linked to one or multiple factors, creating a cycle that can be hard to break.” It listed limited or unstable income, high food and essential costs, housing, healthcare, utilities and child or dependent care among the factors. The report also cited health emergencies, limited access to community resources and “historical and ongoing discrimination.”

The data came from the USDA's Current Population Survey Food Security Supplement, an annual survey the agency has now canceled. Because of the cancellation, national food-insecurity data may be less readily available in coming years. “Given the broader social and economic impact of food insecurity, it is vital that local governments, public health agencies, school districts, and workplaces understand the level and distribution of food insecurity in their communities,” the report said.

The proposal comes alongside SNAP cuts in the federal spending bill, Medicaid cuts and new work requirements that will also make some people ineligible for help. The Ohio Workforce Coalition said the USDA proposal would “impose significant benefits cliff penalties on working families with low income, and eliminate states' ability to align income and asset limits that match local economic conditions.” Families and individuals who are eligible for SNAP can also be eligible for programs under the Temporary Assistance for Needy Families program, another federal assistance program. Broad-based categorical eligibility lets SNAP use TANF's income and asset thresholds. The national Food Research & Action Center said the policy “streamlines the administration of (SNAP).”

In a statement, the Food Research & Action Center's Gina Plata-Nino and Dory Thrasher wrote: “(Broad-based categorical eligibility) lets SNAP align eligibility thresholds for income and assets with TANF's eligibility thresholds.” They said ending it would increase administrative costs and processing delays, shift greater cost burdens to states, intensify food insecurity and undermine work incentives.

Keith Barnes of the Beyond the Cliff Coalition, a national anti-poverty group that includes the Ohio Workforce Coalition, estimated the proposal would reduce SNAP benefits by about $14 billion in the first full year. Citing USDA estimates, he said affected households would lose about $340 per month on average. Barnes said 3.7 million more households currently receiving SNAP would become ineligible, with a disproportionate impact on working families.

For SNAP households with children, Barnes said nearly 18% of those currently receiving benefits would no longer qualify. The Children's Defense Fund analysis found the proposal “would risk the food security of more than six million children in America.”

The rule would add another layer to an assistance system Barnes described as “not particularly cohesive or sometimes even coherent for families navigating it.” “Instead, it's a patchwork of programs, and families with low incomes are left to piece together multiple programs often administered by different agencies with different access points and applications to make ends meet,” he said. People who lose SNAP eligibility could also be barred from employment and job-training programs provided through SNAP under the new rules, Barnes said. “So individuals who lose SNAP eligibility could find themselves, you know, potentially midstream in a program that's designed to help them get ahead suddenly having to disenroll from that program,” he said.

If the rule is approved, the Ohio Workforce Coalition projects Ohio would lose 10% to 15% of its SNAP caseload. The coalition said the decline could be even larger. Barnes framed the underlying issue broadly: “To be fully transparent, this is a cost of living problem that impacts every state and community in the nation.”

The USDA proposal remains pending. Public comments are due Dec. 10, and the expected implementation date is Oct. 1, 2027 if the rule is approved.

The proposal now under public comment would add another eligibility restriction, extending the federal changes that have reduced access to SNAP.
