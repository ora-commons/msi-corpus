Congress has two chambers, the House of Representatives and the Senate. The speaker of the House is the chamber's presiding officer, elected by the representatives. Exclusive House powers include initiating revenue bills, impeaching federal officials and electing the president in the case of an Electoral College tie.

The Senate has 100 members who sit for six-year terms. One-third of the seats come up for election in each two-year cycle. Each state has two senators regardless of population, so Wyoming, with a population of fewer than 600,000, carries the same weight as California, with almost 40 million. Most legislation needs to pass both chambers. The Senate also approves senior presidential appointments, including Supreme Court appointments; has the sole power to provide advice to the president; consents to ratify treaties; and tries impeachment cases for federal officials referred to it by the House.

In most states, the Senate candidate with the most votes on election day wins the seat. Georgia and Louisiana require the winning candidate to garner 50% of votes cast; if no one does, they hold a runoff election between the top two candidates.

Maine and Alaska use ranked-choice voting instead of first-past-the-post voting in congressional elections. Voters rank candidates in order of preference. If no candidate receives a majority of first-choice votes, the least-supported candidate is eliminated and the ballots are transferred to each voter's next-ranked candidate; the process continues until a candidate wins a majority. In some states, top-two or jungle primary systems bring candidates from all parties into the same primary, with the two receiving the most votes advancing regardless of party. The finalists could both be Democrats or both Republicans.
