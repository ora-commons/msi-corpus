## Natsal documents lower sexual activity without establishing causes

The National Survey of Sexual Attitudes and Lifestyles, known as Natsal, found that one in three people under 25 in Britain reported never having sexual intercourse. The study, which has captured trends in British intimacy every decade since 1990, also found that sexual activity had declined among adults of all genders and sexualities. The Guardian described the pattern among younger people as a “sex recession,” while saying the standout finding obscured the reasons behind it.

The average age at first sex rose to 18 in the latest survey, up from 16 in 2010. Natsal also found that low sexual well-being was very common among younger people. Luke Muschialli, a 24-year-old research assistant in epidemiology at University College London who worked on the survey, said Natsal can identify the trends but cannot explain why they are happening.

Muschialli spoke after an initial wave of headlines portraying British young people as sexually inactive, and he was wary of choosing an instant culprit for the decline, including blame directed at people’s phones. The newsletter writer asked why some older adults react strongly to research about younger people’s sexual activity, considering prurience, anxiety about intimacy in a digital-first world and memories of how their own generation behaved at the same age.

Natsal cautioned the writer against drawing a causal link between high levels of online sexual behavior, including sexting and porn use, and reduced sexual activity. Muschialli said: “But our study is not able to show whether there’s a direct substitution for partnered sexual activity with online sexual behaviour, which should be the focus of future research.”

Muschialli said much of the reporting had glossed over the prevalence of low sexual well-being among younger people. He warned against using the findings to argue for reduced investment in sexual and relationship health services: “My biggest worry is that people will look at the findings and think it’s a reason to disinvest in sexual and relationship health services. Young people are still really in need of STI and HIV support that can adapt to their changing behaviour,” he said.

Aneesa Ahmed, a 26-year-old Guardian writer who reports regularly on Gen Z attitudes to sex and relationships, described a generational shift. The newsletter writer recalled that, when the writer began dating in the 1990s, people had a “Number,” a tally of sexual partners while searching for “The One.” It was a balancing act between appearing desirable, experienced and up-for-it while avoiding sounding like “a slag.” Ahmed said of that practice: “That’s not really a thing any more.”

Ahmed described her generation as born into a “sex positive, ‘woke’ era,” where “it’s bad to slut-shame women, though sadly it still happens.” She said “decentering romantic partnership and monogamy is big among people my age.” The newsletter writer said attitudes toward sex and identity had transformed between the generations, generally for the better in terms of nuance around gender and consent and sometimes for the worse, most obviously with the rise of the manosphere. The writer noted that Natsal was not the first study to identify declining physical intimacy among Ahmed’s peers.

Ahmed said: “In the heterosexual context, women are much more independent, not looking for a husband to provide for them, and not willing to put up with some men who have horrible, misogynist opinions.” The writer cited earlier reporting on an ideological gender gap in which younger British men were almost twice as likely to vote for Reform UK as their female peers. The writer also said young men found prevailing templates of masculinity overwhelming or unappealing: Some believed the only way to approach women was to be an aggressive alpha, while others feared making a move because they might come across as creepy.

The Guardian said raw data could not fully capture changes in the social dynamic. Ahmed said her peers were more interested in “situationships,” and many had never had a committed partner. They were less bound to the traditional linear progression toward a nuclear family, although the writer noted that many also could not afford to start one. Nurturing friendships and platonic love had become more important than the idea of a “fix-all romance.”

Ahmed linked later first experiences to greater education about consent and boundaries, saying “people don’t put up with disrespect.” She also said growing numbers of young people identifying as LGBTQ+ had reduced the urge to rush into intimacy “with people that might not care or understand.”

The newsletter offered one potentially positive interpretation: The data could reflect young people feeling better able to negotiate safe and wanted sexual encounters. Muschialli said Natsal could identify trends but not explain them, and he stressed that he spoke primarily as a researcher rather than as a representative of his entire generation. His own hypothesis was that young people were also experiencing “a constrained opportunity for sex.”

Muschialli wondered how his friends could meet partners through the tradition of a night out when their entry-level salaries could not cover an “£8 pint or a staggeringly high nightclub entry fee.” Dating apps could make introductions, he said, but did not reduce the cost of a date. He also asked whether opportunities for sex decrease as more young people live with their parents well into their 20s and whether a generation increasingly grappling with its own mental health has space for the human connection and vulnerability that comes with sex and romance.

## Childbearing preferences

The newsletter separately examined women’s preferences about children. Its description of the measure was inconsistent: It called the change a “substantial drop” in the proportion of women wanting children while also stating that the share had “more than doubling from 8% to 17% over the past decade,” with the change driven primarily by women under 35. The writer asked whether younger women were giving up on a heteronormative progression, recognizing that child-rearing is expensive and exhausting, or seeing more role models of single childless women than Bridget Jones. Ahmed said: “This is very much a product of the society that we live in,” citing a lack of support with childcare, particularly for single mothers. She added: “Seeing the women who came before us having to work full-time, and still taking on all the domestic labour … it’s off-putting.”

The newsletter returned to the finding that two in three under-25s were sexually active. It said Gen Z are typically branded as prudes when such surveys appear, but their pursuit of pleasure takes place in an evolving context. Ahmed rejected that characterization: “The idea that we’re an uptight generation is totally untrue.” She added: “Yes, we want these experiences, but in a safe, consensual way, with partners we can trust. And for us, that’s what is important now.”
