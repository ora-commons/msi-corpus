## Huawei acquitted on racketeering and conspiracy to obstruct justice

A federal jury in Brooklyn convicted Huawei Technologies and several subsidiaries on Tuesday of criminal fraud, bank fraud, wire fraud and conspiring to violate U.S. sanctions. The companies were guilty on 10 of 12 counts but were acquitted of racketeering conspiracy and conspiracy to obstruct justice.

Prosecutors described the acquitted racketeering charge as a “sprawling two-decade conspiracy to control the global telecom market.” During the two-month trial in federal court in Brooklyn, prosecutors accused Huawei and several subsidiaries of operating as a racketeering conspiracy to expand the company’s business empire across the globe. Huawei’s lawyers argued that prosecutors were criminalizing regular business activity. The jury began deliberating last Wednesday afternoon.

Huawei said the acquittal on the racketeering charge “confirms that these allegations lack factual basis and vindicates our consistent position over the past eight years.” It said the remaining charges lacked legal or factual merit. The Justice Department did not immediately respond to a request for comment.

At sentencing, Huawei faces financial penalties and potential court-ordered oversight, and the company already faces sanctions. The Wall Street Journal assessed that any new penalties would likely pale in comparison with the reputational blow of a conviction and could prompt an angry response from Beijing, possibly including threats of future retaliation. The jury’s rejection of the government’s central charge gives Huawei and Beijing room to argue that the United States was overzealous in its long-running campaign against the Chinese technology company.

Prosecutors told the jury that Huawei employees stole trade secrets from other companies and bragged about the thefts on an informal company forum. “Committing crimes to get ahead was ingrained in the very culture of Huawei,” Assistant U.S. Attorney Meredith Arfa said during closing arguments.

In one incident detailed at trial, prosecutors said Huawei gained access to a T-Mobile laboratory and stole technical data about T-Mobile’s robot. They said Huawei engineers took photos and stole a piece of the robot, named Tappy, by slipping it into a backpack. Defense attorneys said the employee involved was fired.

Prosecutors also said other Huawei employees took steps to evade U.S. sanctions and lied to banks about the work the company was doing in Iran.

Defense attorney Brian Heberlig told the jury, “It’s about competition, not conspiracy; innovation, not theft; ordinary business dealings, not criminal misconduct.” He accused the Justice Department of cherry-picking individual incidents. He said Huawei was investing more than $100 billion in research and investment and had no reason to lie to banks because so many of them wanted to work with the successful company.

Huawei, one of China’s premier international brands, has been at the center of U.S.-China competition for decades. American technology giants have long accused the company of stealing their business secrets. Starting with the Obama administration, U.S. authorities publicly warned that Huawei’s expansion strategies posed risks to the United States. The Wall Street Journal reported that a widespread view in Washington was that Huawei had furthered China’s military ambitions, powered its surveillance systems and posed a national security threat.

The Justice Department’s case, brought during the first Trump administration, has deepened distrust between Washington and Beijing. At the United States’ request, Canada detained a senior Huawei executive and the founder’s daughter in 2018, prompting Beijing to jail two Canadians on spying charges. The standoff was defused after more than 1,000 days with a three-nation prisoner swap, and the episode supplied prosecution ammunition in the trial.

The verdict came after President Trump visited Shenzhen, where Huawei is based, for a summit with Xi Jinping. The official agenda suggested that U.S.-China tension over technology received little attention, even as semiconductor maker Nvidia confronted allegations that a woman suspected of being in contact with Chinese intelligence had a relationship with CEO Jensen Huang. Huang stepped down and then returned to Nvidia less than two weeks later.
