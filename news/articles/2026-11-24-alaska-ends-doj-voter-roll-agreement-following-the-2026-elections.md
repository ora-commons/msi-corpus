## Federal court said DOJ lacked authority over state voter lists

On Nov. 24, Alaska Lt. Gov. Nancy Dahlstrom said the state was ending its agreement with the U.S. Department of Justice to share uncensored voter information, effective immediately. The announcement came hours after the Alaska Division of Elections, which Dahlstrom heads, certified the results of the 2026 elections.

The agreement had drawn public criticism, an investigatory hearing by the Alaska State Legislature and a lawsuit alleging that the state's sharing of voter rolls violated Alaskans' constitutional right to privacy. The rolls contained sensitive personal information, including partial Social Security numbers, addresses and driver's license numbers.

As part of the agreement, Alaska Division of Elections Director Carol Beecher signed a memorandum of understanding stating that the division would remove voters from the rolls within 45 days of a DOJ request if the department deemed them ineligible.

In Monday's statement, Dahlstrom said the agreement was “no longer operative” and could not be used to share voter information in the future. She said the division entered the agreement in good faith to cooperate with federal law enforcement partners as permitted under Alaska law. “The circumstances have now changed, the election has concluded, and the agreement no longer serves a continuing purpose,” she said. Dahlstrom also said Alaska has strong list-maintenance procedures and will continue to protect the accuracy of its voter rolls and the rights of eligible Alaska voters.

According to the division's statement, the division provided the confidential voter list to the DOJ in December 2025. The statement said the DOJ did not flag anyone to be removed or raise concerns regarding Alaska's voter-registration records, and no voters were removed from the state's rolls as a result of the agreement.

Separately, the division removed more than 3,000 voters from its rolls in July, saying they could be eligible if they provided additional proof of U.S. citizenship. The Alaska Beacon reported that some of those removals were erroneous.

The division said it had directed the DOJ to destroy Alaska's confidential voter information in accordance with the agreement. Its announcement said the termination did not constitute an acknowledgment of wrongdoing.

The division's announcement noted that a U.S. District Court had vacated the DOJ's voter-removal program in October, saying the department did not have the authority to obtain and review state voter lists. The division said Alaska entered the agreement in good faith under state law before any court had ruled that the program was unlawful.

After a review of the changed circumstances, the division determined that Alaska's interests were best served by ending the agreement and relying on established voter list-maintenance procedures. “Alaska will continue to consider credible information, apply the law, and make its own decisions,” Dahlstrom said.

The privacy lawsuit remains open in Anchorage Superior Court. The League of Women Voters, Alaska Black Caucus and Alaska Public Interest Research Group brought the challenge.
