## Commerce said in May 2025 it would rescind Biden's worldwide chip framework

The Trump administration, which had resisted imposing additional regulations to prevent foreign access to American AI technology before the Nvidia scandal, issued a rule Monday through the Commerce Department's Bureau of Industry and Security blocking remote access to advanced artificial intelligence chips and servers through data centers in other countries, closing a loophole that could let adversarial governments access American AI technology through overseas data centers. The rule represents a shift for an administration that had resisted such steps.

The shift arrives after the administration moved against the framework the Biden administration wrote on January 15, 2025 — five days before Trump's inauguration — to control worldwide distribution of advanced AI chips. In May 2025, Trump's Commerce Department announced it would not enforce the rule and intended to rescind it, calling it "ill-conceived and counterproductive." Biden's framework would have also limited access to AI chips by countries considered security risks, required licenses for certain chip exports, and created restrictions on the power of advanced models and computing infrastructure.

The control regime the new rule reinforces dates to October 2022, when U.S. companies began typically needing a BIS export license to sell advanced computing chips meeting specified technical thresholds to China, including Hong Kong and Macau. Those restrictions covered advanced Nvidia chips, some high-performance semiconductor components, and certain AI computing systems. Before Monday's rule, a loophole existed where Chinese companies could still access those chips remotely, despite U.S. restrictions on their physical sale to China.

BIS said in a statement Monday that the rapid advancement in cloud computing and AI infrastructure has made it possible to access advanced U.S. computing capabilities remotely, including through data centers located outside countries subject to U.S. export restrictions. The rule is designed to reinforce existing restrictions on access to sensitive American AI technology through remote computing arrangements involving data centers in other countries, which the agency said could threaten national security.

"The United States is the global leader in advanced computing and super intelligence technology and is committed to promoting the global adoption of American technology for legitimate commercial purposes while ensuring that sensitive technologies are not exploited in ways that threaten U.S. national security," the BIS statement said.

The government refers to artificial intelligence as "super intelligence" following an executive order signed by President Trump in September.

The rule arrives amid the fallout at Nvidia, the world's dominant producer of AI chips, following revelations that Jensen Huang had a romantic relationship with a woman who U.S. intelligence officials suspected of being a Chinese intelligence operative. NPR has not independently verified the claims. Jensen Huang stepped down as CEO last week.

Alice Huang, who is not related to Jensen Huang, is a former executive at Megaspeed International, a Singapore-based data center company. Jensen Huang has personally lobbied President Trump to loosen restrictions on Nvidia chip sales to China. It is not known if Alice Huang influenced this activity. Megaspeed is based in Singapore and operates facilities in Malaysia. "Megaspeed conducts legitimate and lawful business. The company has retained external legal counsel to conduct a thorough internal investigation," the company said in a statement emailed to NPR and other news organizations on Sunday.

The New York Times reported that Trump was first briefed by intelligence officials about Jensen Huang's affair with a suspected Chinese spy in October 2025.

Both Jensen Huang and Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick are expected to testify before Congress this month and next month, and there will likely be further investigations into what Trump knew about the affair when the Democrats take control of Congress in January 2027, Rep. Yvette Clarke, D-NY, told NPR.

"We need to get the facts investigated, bring in individuals to give us as much information as possible under oath, so that we can get to the bottom of why this happened, how it occurred, and how we could prevent this type of compromise from happening again," said Clarke.
