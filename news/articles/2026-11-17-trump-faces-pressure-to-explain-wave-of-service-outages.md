## Companies and agencies linked disruptions to upgrades, bugs and maintenance

As the technology failures continued, political criticism focused on what the White House would disclose. Trump, who arrived in Asia on Tuesday at the start of a three-day trip, faced questions from political allies and critics alike as the outages affected work, travel, finances and government communications. Even staunch Trump allies accused the administration of lying to Americans or hiding information about the true cause of the disruptions.

The series of outages began Thursday, when Microsoft’s email and other tools went down, paralyzing millions of workers who rely on them. Microsoft traced the issue to “an earlier network capacity upgrade.” Flights across the country were grounded the next day in the longest so-called ground stop since 9/11, and the Federal Aviation Administration blamed a router upgrade.

By Monday, customers reported difficulty accessing online accounts at several major financial institutions and the money transfer service Zelle after a technical issue at JPMorgan Chase caused ripple effects throughout the financial system. Chase said it was caused by “a software bug” and was “not cyber-related.” The same day, United Airlines said it had resolved an internal system outage that delayed departing flights.

On Tuesday, an internal network used by the National Security Agency to communicate classified information was offline after the agency described the disruption as “routine maintenance.” Microsoft, Chase and the government agencies involved each pointed to internal technical work and denied that cyberattacks or rogue artificial intelligence systems were responsible.

Asked whether he was concerned about the Chase outage, Trump said, “It’s life. When you’re big and powerful and you have all the money, things like that happen. It’s fine,” while in Manila, Philippines. He also said “things like that happen” after arriving in Asia on Tuesday. His response did little to calm the country.

Karoline Leavitt, Trump’s former press secretary, advised her former colleagues on Fox News: “What I would say to the White House is transparency is key.” She said Trump or another White House official should explain what they knew after his return from Asia “to really calm the temperature.”

Leavitt praised the White House for having Director of National Intelligence Jay Clayton address the outages in an interview on Fox Business Network. In a clip she shared on X, Clayton said, “Looking very diligently across the landscape, we are not seeing this as a cyber attack incident,” and said the U.S. government believed the incidents were caused by organizations making security upgrades that were taking longer than planned and encountering unexpected hiccups.

Tricia McLaughlin, a former Department of Homeland Security spokesperson under Trump, questioned the federal government’s limited public explanation. “Americans aren’t stupid,” she posted on social media. “We want and deserve to know what’s really going on.” Meghan McCain, who has sometimes differed with Trump and his allies, wrote on X: “Someone high in the administration needs to get on tv and tell the American people NOW what is going on,” and added, “No one who lived through Covid is believing this is all a coincidence.”

Laura Loomer, a prominent figure in Trump’s Make America Great Again movement, alleged a coordinated attack without presenting evidence. “Our leaders are lying to us. This is very clearly a coordinated cyber attack,” she wrote on social media. She also wrote, “President Trump should come back to the United States.” The White House did not respond to an Associated Press request for comment.

Conservative commentator Kylie Jane Kremer also pressed for disclosure on X. “Where is the transparency? Where is the accountability?” she wrote. “And why does it feel like we’re being told everything is fine while the very systems our country depends on are experiencing serious failures?”

The response cut across political lines: Trump allies joined administration critics in demanding more information, while people already distrustful of government and large institutions did not share Trump’s nonchalance. The series of outages also underscored the risks posed by interconnected technology networks linking communications, workplaces, air travel, banking and classified government systems. The accounts left the White House facing calls for a public explanation even as each provider and agency described an internal cause.
