## Carney targets doubling non-U.S. exports by 2035

Canada and China agreed to ease selected mutual tariffs. China will roll back tariffs on $280 million worth of Canadian pork and seafood exports, while Canada will offer relief on some Chinese steel and aluminum products. The Wall Street Journal reported that Canada’s relief would apply where Canadian supply is “insufficient or unavailable.”

Carney and Xi met in Shenzhen ahead of the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation summit. Carney said, “Canada and China have recalibrated our relationship to the benefit of citizens in both countries.” He said Canada is “diversifying our relationships abroad so that we have the freedom to live our lives as we choose.”

The agreement expands a pact from January but covers a small portion of the $88 billion in merchandise trade between the two countries. Since becoming prime minister last year, Carney has traveled internationally to seek trade partners as Canada’s ties with the U.S. fray. He has set a goal of doubling Canada’s non-U.S. exports by 2035. That has meant drawing closer to allies in Europe and mending rocky ties with countries like China in what he has called “principled and pragmatic” foreign policy.

China is Canada’s second-largest trading partner, with two-way annual trade of roughly $98 billion in 2025, a fraction of the nearly $872 billion in bilateral trade between Canada and the U.S. last year. Xi has sought to present China as a reliable alternative to the U.S.

Ties between Ottawa and Beijing deteriorated sharply in 2018, when China detained two Canadians in an action widely viewed as retaliation for Canada’s arrest of a Chinese executive pursuant to a U.S. extradition request. In recent years, Canadian security officials accused China of harassing critics in Canada and seeking to interfere in Canadian elections.

After Canada joined the Biden administration in imposing a 100% tariff on Chinese-made electric vehicles, China retaliated with steep tariffs on Canadian canola. A 2023 Pew Research survey found that only 14% of Canadians held a favorable view of China. During the federal election last year, Carney identified China as the “biggest security threat” to Canada.

But this year, Carney and Xi hailed a new “strategic partnership” during the first visit to China by a Canadian prime minister in nearly a decade. Canada said it would allow up to 49,000 Chinese electric vehicles into the country at a lower tariff rate, representing less than 3% of the country’s auto-sales market, while China eased levies on Canadian canola and other products.

The warming ties have drawn domestic criticism. Ontario Premier Doug Ford, whose province is the heart of Canada’s auto industry, warned that allowing Chinese electric vehicles could cost Canadian jobs and access to the U.S. market. Critics also warned that China has often used access to its market to pressure other countries and could do so again.

Vina Nadjibulla, a foreign-policy analyst and chief executive of the Canada-based Center for Strategic Statecraft, said in a post on X: “The bigger question is guardrails and how Canada will manage economic and national-security threats that come with this expanded economic engagement,” and “There were no details in the statements on that.”

The January trade pact drew disapproval from the Trump administration. President Trump initially told reporters that Carney should get a deal with China, then threatened to impose 100% tariffs on Canadian goods over the pact. Those levies never materialized, but the agreement has drawn repeated criticism from Trump administration officials.

Canada is also seeking relief from U.S. tariffs as high as 50% on autos, steel and aluminum. Those trade talks fell apart in August, though Carney said this month that negotiations have restarted. “We are making progress,” he said at a news conference last week in the Philippines. “There is the possibility of a bilateral deal, but we’re not yet there.”
