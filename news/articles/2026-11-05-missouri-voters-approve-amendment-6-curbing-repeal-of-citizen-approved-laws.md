## Democrats gain 16 state House seats; voters reject GOP abortion, map measures

Missouri voters approved Amendment 6 on Tuesday, a constitutional amendment that sharply limits the legislature's power to overturn laws and constitutional amendments enacted through citizen initiatives, and to revive laws voters rejected at the ballot box. The measure passed by a decisive margin in an election that also saw Democrats pick up 16 seats in the state House, though Republicans held on to every statewide office and control of the legislature.

Under the amendment, lawmakers now must win 80% support in both the House and Senate before they can propose changing or repealing measures enacted through citizen initiatives, or move to revive laws voters rejected through referendums. Even then, voters must approve any change in a statewide election. The restriction applies to measures approved since 2010, including the constitutional amendment protecting abortion rights that voters approved in 2024.

Amendment 6 also strengthens the referendum process. It restricts lawmakers' ability to reenact laws similar to those voters have rejected, explicitly protects the right of citizens to enact laws and constitutional amendments with a simple statewide majority — turning back the higher threshold Republican lawmakers had sought earlier this year — and safeguards the power of Missouri's courts to rewrite misleading ballot language, a power Republican officials have repeatedly tried to strip away.

The Missouri results came amid a broader election night on which voters across the country delivered a wave of Democratic victories, flipping seats in Congress and state legislatures and weakening a number of Republican majorities. The outcome in Missouri, the report noted, was more than a rebuke of President Donald Trump: it reflected long-simmering frustration with politicians who have repeatedly sought to override the will of voters, a practice that has intensified under years of Republican control.

Political observers said Missouri Republicans had only themselves to blame, pointing to lawmakers' resistance of a voter-approved Medicaid expansion, repeal of paid sick leave protections and attempt to reverse abortion rights — efforts, they said, that were sometimes packaged with unrelated culture-war provisions, such as limiting health care for transgender minors, intended to appeal to conservative voters. This summer, Republican lawmakers asked voters to surrender some of their own power by changing the constitution to make initiatives harder to pass in the first place. Voters rejected that proposal by more than a 4-to-1 margin.

Jayne Woods, a professor at the University of Missouri law school who has written about the tension between direct democracy and legislative power, said voters were pushing back. "I think the people of Missouri are trying to wrest control back from our legislature because our legislature at this point does not seem to really be representing the majority view in the state," Woods said.

Benjamin Singer, co-founder and executive director of the Respect Missouri Voters campaign, which helped organize the push for Amendment 6, said the frustration stretched back decades, including periods when Democrats controlled the legislature. When the campaign began organizing in late 2024, he said, many volunteers were still angry about lawmakers' 2011 decision to weaken restrictions on puppy mills that voters had approved just a year earlier. The campaign had already built a substantial volunteer operation before Republican lawmakers moved in 2025 to undo voter-approved measures intended to mandate paid sick leave and protect abortion rights, Singer said. Respect Missouri Voters went on to gather enough signatures to put Amendment 6 on the ballot, seeking not only to protect the initiative process but to strengthen the electorate's power over the legislature.

Abortion was before voters a second time on Tuesday's ballot. Republican lawmakers had placed a measure on the ballot to restore abortion restrictions, and voters rejected that as well. With Amendment 6 in place, abortion opponents lack a clear path to try again, the report noted.

Denny Hoskins, the secretary of state and one of Amendment 6's most prominent Republican opponents, acknowledged how profoundly the amendment would change the balance of power. Before the election, he told the Missouri Independent that if it passed, "it will basically cut our legislature off at the knees as far as being able to pass laws to reduce crime and cut taxes." In text messages on Wednesday, Hoskins said the amendment weakens the legislature and governor "regardless of party" and predicted it would be challenged in court. He said "giving power back to the people" might be a great slogan, but that the amendment's sponsors "have no idea what this actually will do."

Hoskins had tried to keep the amendment off the ballot. In August, he refused to certify the initiative petition, arguing that it violated constitutional restrictions on ballot measures, and rejected a separate petition seeking a statewide referendum on the newly gerrymandered congressional map that Republican lawmakers, working at Trump's behest, had approved to give their party another seat in Congress. The Missouri Supreme Court overruled him in both cases and ordered the measures onto the ballot. The court found Hoskins in contempt when he sought to use the map in spite of its ruling; he backed down, and the justices did not have him arrested.

Voters rejected the map on Tuesday as well. With Amendment 6 now in place, and Democrats significantly bolstering their numbers in the House, Republican lawmakers are unlikely to gather enough support to override the voters' rejection of the map, the report concluded.

Travis Crum, a law professor at Washington University in St. Louis and an expert on voting rights and election law, said he knew of no other state with a restriction quite like Missouri's 80% threshold. He called the amendment understandable given the legislature's repeated attempts to reverse voter-approved policies and its unsuccessful summer effort to make citizen initiatives harder to pass. "I think that there's just been this boiling frustration with how the legislature has ignored the initiative process and then sort of tried to rewrite it to its own ends," Crum said.

John Hancock, a former Missouri Republican Party chair and longtime GOP consultant, acknowledged that Republican lawmakers had brought some of the backlash on themselves. But he warned that Amendment 6 could make Missouri a magnet for well-financed out-of-state interest groups seeking to enact laws through ballot initiatives, possibly on taxation or gun policy. That concern about wealthy outside interests was also central to the Republican-backed proposal to restrict citizen initiatives that voters rejected in August: Gov. Mike Kehoe had spent months warning voters about out-of-state special interests trying to rewrite the state constitution, even as a campaign supporting his proposal to phase out the state income tax benefited from funding from a Delaware nonprofit with undisclosed donors.

Woods said the August defeat suggested voters were not persuaded by that argument. Regardless of who finances an initiative campaign, she noted, Missourians are the ones organizing, gathering support and ultimately deciding whether the measures pass. "I think that the people of the state pretty resoundingly said, 'No, we don't believe that that's true,'" Woods said. "We believe this is our citizens who are doing this. This is what we want as citizens."

The result, political observers said, could make the legislature less consequential than it once was. Republicans still have the votes to pass most laws, but when voters reject those laws at the ballot box — or enact their own measures or constitutional amendments through citizen initiatives — lawmakers will now face significant obstacles to overriding them.
