## Iran offers to reopen the Strait if talks resume

Iran is using the Nov. 3, 2026 U.S. midterms to pressure President Trump to return to negotiations, according to NPR. The newsletter said the election could signal a possible return to conflict with the United States and Israel. Iranian officials said they preferred negotiations, and NPR's Hadeel Al-Shalchi reported that Tehran was hoping it could wait out the election and possibly see Trump's party lose control of Congress.

Iran offered to reopen the Strait of Hormuz in two days, ahead of the seven days it proposed in September, if the United States re-engaged in talks, Al-Shalchi said. The Associated Press reported that the U.S. conducted defensive strikes on Iranian targets in the strait the previous night, including Qeshm Island, a key launch point for Iran's strikes. NPR said it could not independently confirm those details, and Iran reported explosions on the island. NPR said the pattern had occurred several times in recent months, coinciding with heightened Iranian attacks on shipping vessels in the strait.

Al-Shalchi also reported that Iran's inflation rate was around 90%, with residents facing dire economic conditions and fears of possible bombings. As winter approached, officials urged millions of Iranians to conserve energy to avoid soaring natural-gas prices, and they said the country's natural-gas networks were unstable because of damage from the war.

Just before Election Day, the U.S. Supreme Court rejected the Department of Justice's last-minute request to intervene in a dispute with California over voter rolls. The department sought to halt California from processing ballots for thousands of individuals identified as "likely" noncitizens.

NPR education correspondent Jonaki Mehta, who focuses on disabilities and learning differences, reported that America has not had enough special-education teachers for half a century. The shortage has worsened as the number of students with disabilities has grown rapidly; the most recent federal count put that number at 8.2 million, or about 1 in 6 students. Research offered some explanations for why the positions are difficult to fill: Special-education teachers enjoy working with students but spend more time on legally required paperwork and tasks other than teaching. They often do not receive more pay despite having more responsibilities than many other teachers, and those who remain in the profession are often handed a bigger load because of the shortage.

To go beyond the research, NPR senior producer Lauren Migaki and Mehta exchanged voice memos with more than a dozen teachers from large and small districts around the country. So far, the educators have described joy when a student found success, tearful moments when they felt like they had failed, and waking before dawn to make sure they were prepared.

Jeffrey Carroll, who teaches in Newport News, Virginia, said last year that he taught students across three grade levels and multiple subjects — second, fourth and fifth — and was constantly running from one classroom to the next. "I just didn't feel like I was teaching, I was never in a class long enough to be a meaningful part of it," he said. This year, he was relieved to teach across two grade levels. "It's been good to support my students in the classroom. I get to do some co-teaching. The lesson plans come to life." Mehta and Migaki said they would remain in touch with the teachers throughout the rest of the school year and visit some in their classrooms to better understand what the job requires.
