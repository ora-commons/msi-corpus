## Blue Ghost and Starship will dock with Orion in Earth orbit

NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman said Friday that the agency remains on track to launch Artemis III next summer, sending four astronauts into Earth orbit for a two-week docking exercise rather than a trip to the moon. He said Artemis IV remains scheduled for 2028 and would be NASA's first astronaut moon landing since Apollo. Speaking at a news conference at Kennedy Space Center in Cape Canaveral, Florida, Isaacman said NASA is moving fast on plans to build a base near the moon's south pole and return astronauts to the lunar surface in as little as two years.

Isaacman said Artemis IV remains scheduled for 2028 and would be NASA's first moon landing by astronauts since Apollo. NASA officials said the agency will keep its options open as long as possible before choosing between SpaceX and Blue Origin to build the Artemis IV lunar lander.

Isaacman's update came six months after the successful Artemis II flight around the moon by four astronauts, humanity's first lunar trip in more than half a century.

Both landers will be launched next summer for docking practice. NASA's Orion capsule will carry three NASA astronauts and one Italian into orbit, and the mission will require three launches in quick succession from Cape Canaveral. Orion will dock first with Blue Origin's Blue Ghost about 270 miles (435 kilometers) above Earth, then with SpaceX's Starship in a slightly lower orbit. The two landers come from the companies of Elon Musk and Jeff Bezos.

NASA's associate administrator, Amit Kshatriya, described the orbital test in informal terms. “As a rocket nerd, it’s going to be pretty awesome,” he said.

NASA officials said the planned moon base near the moon's south pole — nowhere near Apollo's more equatorial locales — should see its first launch by year's end. They said the Pittsburgh company whose first lunar landing mission did not succeed in 2024 will attempt a “take two,” this time aiming for the moon's south polar region with a rover and other experiments.

More landers, rovers and orbiters will follow, officials said, so many that the rover for the first Artemis moonwalkers will already be there waiting when they arrive.

To speed the program up and reduce risk, the initial moonwalkers will wear a lighter, less complex spacesuit than the one Axiom Space is still developing for longer, later excursions. Axiom Space will test the new so-called “sortie suits” on the International Space Station next year.

Isaacman said the “new space race” to land astronauts on the moon — racing against China now rather than the Soviet Union during the Apollo era — will be “much closer than it ever needed to be” because of slow going in past years. The agency is now accelerating, Isaacman said, eliminating what he described as endless meetings and bureaucratic inertia as SpaceX, Blue Origin and other companies scramble around the clock to meet deadlines and NASA bolsters its workforce.

Isaacman said NASA was drawing on Apollo's urgent and intense approach from 1968 through 1972, a period that saw 24 astronauts fly to the moon and 12 land on it. He said adopting that approach was not “tech industry pixie dust or some new management philosophy.” “It’s about our own history. It’s a playbook this agency knows really well, the same playbook that went from Apollo 7 splashdown to Apollo 11 in just nine months,” he said during the news conference at Kennedy Space Center.

NASA astronaut Frank Rubio, a member of the Artemis III crew, said establishing a long-term lunar presence is much more challenging than the Apollo-era missions. “It is incredibly ambitious,” Rubio told The Associated Press earlier this month. “One thing I’ve always believed and learned is don’t bet against America.”
