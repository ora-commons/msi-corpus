## Ontario, the Netherlands and Sweden provide extra support to solo-parent households

The Trump administration's proposal, initially reported by The New York Times, would move funds that subsidize childcare for low-income working parents, most of whom are single parents relying on childcare providers, to low-income married couples with one earner. Most single parents and stay-at-home parents are women, and both groups would apply for the same limited funds. The Guardian described the plan as part of the administration's latest pronatalist push.

When news of the proposal broke, policy experts and Reddit users bemoaned what they saw as a new front in the “mommy wars,” The Guardian reported. Given the predominance of women in both groups and their applications for the same limited funds, The Guardian assessed that the proposal “seemed almost designed to pit moms against each other.”

Details on the proposal remain scant. Eligibility would require a marriage, one person in the workforce and the other at home with the children. The Guardian described the criteria as “exceptionally narrow,” while family-policy experts said they were “highly unusual.” The Guardian reported that other countries offering a home-care allowance, or funding for parents to stay home with children, do not restrict those benefits to specific family forms.

Only about 20% of American families with children fit this specific household type, said Daniel L. Carlson, a family studies professor at the University of Utah and executive director of the Council on Contemporary Families, a research network. Jennifer Glass, a professor emerita at the Population Research Center at University of Texas, Austin, said she could not think of a single European country that conditioned childcare support or other family benefits on parents being married and one of them staying home full time.

The Guardian wrote: “However, like many of other nominally pronatalist policy proposals the administration has entertained so far, it appears this one is less focused on addressing the deeper challenges that face parents and aspiring parents as celebrating a throwback family structure.”

The Guardian wrote that “the new proposal from the Trump administration looks more like the government stepping in to hurt.” Because most of the money at stake flows to childcare centers and parents currently pay only a small additional amount out of pocket, reallocating it could force centers to raise prices or close. The Guardian said the transfer would likely amount to upward redistribution, moving money from the poorest dual-earner and single-parent families toward their married peers. A recent analysis of household incomes by Cuny policy scholar Sophie Collyer found that families and children in the new target group for the limited childcare funds were already relatively better off than those in single-parent families.

Many laws and policies already favor married, one-earner households. Vice-President JD Vance, a key champion of the proposal, has claimed that young children are “harmed” by spending long hours in daycare. Collyer found that high-earning married couples with one working parent receive a tax break averaging $8,000 per child — about 2.5 times the tax benefits received by low-income families of any type and four times the roughly $2,000 per child that high-income families with two equal-earning spouses receive. More than 1,000 U.S. statutory provisions confer different benefits depending on marital status.

Many family-policy experts, including some conservatives, were skeptical that the proposed shift would spur more marriages or additional births. Caitlyn Collins, a sociologist whose book *Making Motherhood Work: How Women Manage Careers and Caregiving* examined work-family policy in four western countries, said the proposal is “meant to encourage a particular family model.” She continued: “the 1950s Leave-It-to-Beaver family model, with a father who works outside the home and arrives back every evening to find a perfectly coiffed wife wearing pearls and heels setting a hot meal down on the table alongside two blonde, blue-eyed children waiting to greet him.” Otherwise, Collins said, benefits would be conditioned on the number of children and family income, as they are in many other places. She asked, “Why should marriage have anything to do with it?”

Shawn Fremstad, director of law and political economy and a senior adviser at the left-leaning Center for Economic and Policy Research, said, “It’s this weaving between wanting to give money to married mothers who are stay-at-home mothers, but not wanting to give money to single mothers.” He added, “Other countries have figured out that you can do something for everybody, but we’ve never really figured that out.”

In one comparison of 15 developed countries, single parents with one child under six received an average of $3,000 and $5,000 annually, with payments ranging from $1,500 to more than $20,000. In Ontario, Canada, the province provides an additional benefit of $147 a month per child to low- and moderate-income households on top of the national child benefit. Many countries also heavily subsidize childcare for all families, capping what high-earning families pay and often charging low-income families nothing. In Sweden, the most a family can pay for childcare for its first child is $180 a month, less than an American family's weekly grocery bill.

Sweden has long promoted gender equality through its family policies: parental leave is available to all while men are required to take several months off, and it provides heavily subsidized childcare to make working parenthood a norm. For eight years, Sweden experimented with payments for parents to stay home in a program similar to the Trump proposal but not linked to marital status. Critics called it a “housewife trap,” and the benefit was scrapped in 2016; conservatives are seeking to revive it. Glass said, “The general attempt to raise births by providing more benefits to stay-at-home mothers has clearly failed historically in EU countries.” Countries whose policies supported women in continuing to work through their childbearing years maintained higher fertility on balance, she said.

Hungary, under former prime minister Viktor Orbán's conservative Fidesz party, launched some of the most generous family benefits in the world in an attempt to boost fertility and is an outlier among countries that condition some family benefits on marriage. In 2019, Hungary announced an interest-free loan of about $31,000 to young first-time wives that is partly forgiven if they have two children and fully forgiven if they have three. Home-buying grants and mortgage deductions for couples also increase in value with additional children.

Patrick Brown, a family-policy expert at the conservative Washington, DC, think tank Ethics and Public Policy Center, observed that those policies had not produced the baby boom pronatalists had sought but had increased the share of children born to married couples and halted within Hungary an otherwise continent-wide rise in single motherhood. Brown said, “If you really lean hard on making benefits much more generous to married couples as opposed to single moms, you see that trend reverse.”

Even Hungary does not condition eligibility on one parent leaving the workforce. Many of its most generous benefits, including elimination of income tax for mothers with two or more children, are available only to mothers who work.

When European countries single out specific family types, they typically do so to extend extra help to solo-parent households, The Guardian reported. In the Netherlands and Sweden, single parents receive additional monthly payments on top of universal child benefits. Some countries assume responsibility for paying child support to single parents rather than requiring them to pursue former partners in court. Germany pays 259 euros a month to all parents and can provide more to single parents when a child's other parent does not contribute enough. Denmark's package includes additional child allowances and 22 extra weeks of paid leave. The Guardian described Denmark's support for single parents as “so robust” and said Denmark had a large cadre of “solomor,” or single mothers by choice.

The Guardian reported that the United States had a higher-than-average rate of single motherhood among wealthy countries and attached the highest penalty to having a single mother. A 2024 study found that the probability of poverty increases by nearly 10 percentage points when a child resides with a single mother, compared with a coupled household. In 24 of 30 rich countries, there was no such penalty at all. Among the six countries that applied one, The Guardian wrote that “the US is by far the most egregious offender.”

David Brady, a policy scholar at the University of Southern California and a co-author of the study, observed that the divergent outcomes suggest family structure per se does not cause poverty. He said, “If it’s just the case that single mothers fundamentally are disadvantaged, and there’s nothing we can do about it, then you would expect in other countries single mothers would be poor too, right?” In the Netherlands and Denmark, children in single-mother households were significantly less likely to be poor than children in coupled households, despite significant barriers facing single mothers in the labor market. “The difference is the government steps in and helps,” Brady said. He also said, “We make a choice in America to punish children in single-mother families.” He contrasted that with other countries' approach: “We’re going to take care of all children.”
