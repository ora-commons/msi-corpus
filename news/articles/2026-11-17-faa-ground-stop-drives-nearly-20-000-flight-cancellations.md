## Raymond James analysts estimate industry losses of $430 million to $1.1 billion

Friday's ground stop continued to affect airlines through the weekend, requiring carriers to rebuild aircraft and crew schedules while travelers handled canceled trips and refunds.

According to Cirium, airlines had scrubbed more than 9,000 flights by Friday evening, about one-third of all trips scheduled to, from and within the U.S. Planes, pilots and flight attendants missed later flights; some crew members timed out and needed rest, and aircraft were left out of position for maintenance.

Friday's halt came after a newly installed router disrupted air travel nationwide. Ground stops can result from weather or traffic backups, but FAA officials said Friday's stoppage was a safety precaution and an extraordinary and unusual step the agency had taken only a few times. The halt was also one of the longest and most severe nationwide groundings, and airlines and travelers faced hours of uncertainty while waiting to hear when flights could resume.

Friday's action was only the second major nationwide ground stop since the Sept. 11, 2001 attacks. The previous came in January 2023 after a system that provides critical information to pilots went down.

It took airlines the rest of the weekend to piece their operations back together, and the percentage of cancellations steadily declined each day from Friday's peak. Through Monday morning, airlines had scrubbed close to 20,000 flights. No carrier was unscathed.

Without knowing how long the ground stop would last or how quickly air traffic control could restart, particularly in congested East Coast airspace, JetBlue said recovery was difficult to plan. The airline took a conservative approach and pulled down flying in advance.

JetBlue said more than 92% of its weekend cancellations were made at least four hours before departure, giving it the industry's lowest rate of last-minute cancellations. “We’d rather tell customers early that their flight isn’t going to operate than have them show up at the airport only to find out it’s canceled,” the airline said. “That meant more cancellations upfront.” By Sunday afternoon, JetBlue said it had restored the bulk of its schedule.

American Airlines said late Sunday it was set up for a good start to the week and did not anticipate additional broad cancellations “related to this incredibly disruptive industry-wide event.”

Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy said Monday on CNBC that the economic cost of the disruption could tally in the billions of dollars. Although airlines did not cause the disruption, they lost revenue and had to refund customers who canceled trips and did not travel. Many carriers also offered extra pay to pilots and flight attendants to encourage them to pick up additional flying during the recovery, while high fuel costs were already eroding industry profits.

Data accompanying the cancellation figures said the rates cover marketing airlines, including mainline carriers and regional partners. Nov. 15 figures were current as of 8 p.m. EST. The analysis listed Diio Mi, FlightAware and Deutsche Bank Airline Research as sources.

By Monday, the immediate operational challenge had eased as cancellations fell and airlines rebuilt their schedules, but the financial accounting was still underway. Bernstein Research estimated $345 million in pretax income losses for the four biggest U.S. airlines—American, Delta, United and Southwest—while Raymond James estimated about $430 million to $1.1 billion for the airline industry.
