## Exports have shifted as tariff rates are often open to negotiation

There was none of last April's fanfare, the Rose Garden reveal, the gameshow blue board or "Liberation Day" rhetoric surrounding the Trump administration's tariff announcement this week. Instead, the latest exercise carries a tentative quality and includes talk of further negotiations. The measures are not due to take effect until January or possibly February after a period of consultation. Eight months of process and hearings have already occurred, and the announced rates are upper ceilings that are often up for negotiation.

US Trade Representative Jamieson Greer told CNBC, "We expect to have a broad, even a plurilateral negotiation to talk about excess capacity and how to resolve it."

Around the world, the announcement was viewed as an attempt to shore up the domestic legal underpinning of the tariffs more than as a fundamental change in the Trump trade agenda. Faisal Islam contrasted this week's rollout with last April's Rose Garden "Liberation Day" announcement, described as an overnight shock.

The administration is arguing that large swathes of the rest of the world, including allies, harm the US goods trade position by supporting their own industries. That argument forms the basis for the tariffs in large part, replacing the previous tariffs justified under forced-labor provisions. After the Supreme Court struck down the original emergency basis earlier this year, many important countries now have two legal grounds on which the Trump tariffs rest.

The UK was unaffected by the new tariff announcements but remains covered by forced-labor provisions at an extra 10% on global tariffs. The administration made no move in response to the UK's digital services tax. Separately, a group of 25 states has taken the Trump administration to court to strike down the forced-labor tariffs.

The tariffs must be finalized under a Democrat-led Congress, which has influence but not power over the outcome. Speaker-designate Hakeem Jeffries said this week that the new House would in its first 100 days "move aggressively to… repeal the Trump tariffs."

China's 20% rate is the truce rate from last year's Trump-Xi deal. The truce is due to expire in mid-January. Beijing's response was muted: it did not retaliate, called industrial overcapacity a "false issue" and asked for the Xi-Trump deal to be implemented. The US share of China's exports has fallen to around 10% this year, from 15% just two years ago and 19% in 2018. Islam assessed that the leverage over China had weakened.

More than half of Canada's tariffs are authorized under Section 338 of the 1930s Smoot-Hawley Act. A Senate bill seeks to repeal them, and Faisal Islam said there could be enough rebel Republicans in the House and Senate to threaten to override any presidential veto. Incoming Senate Finance Committee chair Ron Wyden said last week that Democrats were focused on "figuring out a way to beat Trump and his veto."

An interim deal is being sought first. Democrat Sen. Chris Coons, appearing on Canadian TV, told CBC, "Frankly, if you can get an interim deal, I'd take it, and then put it to us in the new majority to legislate an even better deal."

Trading partners have rerouted trade flows around the US tariff wall, a shift Faisal Islam said proved more resilient than expected. In cash terms, China's exports so far in 2026 are down about 8% from 2024, but the increase in China's exports to everywhere else was more than 25 times the amount of that decline. Canada has seen flat exports to the US since the tariffs began, with fewer cars and lumber and pricier flows of energy. Its exports to the rest of the world, especially commodities, have risen sharply.

The rest of the world is watching the political maneuvering in Washington and looking beyond Donald Trump's tenure. Next month's G20 summit at Trump's Miami resort is an important moment. US allies generally have not challenged the lower tariff level, accepting it as part of the new reality. Islam framed the summit as a test of whether middle powers might challenge their host in Florida.

The larger unresolved question, Islam said, is how much of the America First trade agenda Democrats decide to keep. His assessment was that the result would be neither all nor nothing, and he said economists now expect some tariffs to remain in place for years.
