## Connecticut touts AI workforce training as states announce similar efforts

**Subtype:** fact

- Gov. Ned Lamont joined university leaders, state officials and business representatives at Goodwin University in East Hartford on Wednesday to promote Connecticut's AI workforce training programs.
- The Tech Talent Accelerator 3.0, announced in February, supports AI pilot courses at Connecticut College, the University of New Haven and Quinnipiac University.
- Connecticut is partnering with RAISE US to pilot programs training workers displaced by AI, informing a national workforce strategy.
- The state has committed $20 million to an applied AI workforce center in Hartford and launched the free Connecticut AI Academy.
- New York, Arkansas and Maryland have announced similar efforts, including New York's Pursuit AI Jobs Institute targeting 40,000 workers.

## Lamont says state will lead in applying AI, not developing it

Connecticut Gov. Ned Lamont joined university leaders, state officials and members of the business community at Goodwin University in East Hartford on Wednesday to lay out state workforce development programs aimed at easing the transition to artificial intelligence and supporting workers. The event came as some economists and labor leaders raise broad concerns about AI's potential to upend the nature of employment, and as state leaders frame the technology as a source of new opportunity.

Much of the event focused on the importance of Connecticut's Tech Talent Accelerator, a four-year-old workforce development program that facilitates partnerships between higher education institutions and businesses. In February, the state announced that it was launching Tech Talent Accelerator 3.0, a more artificial intelligence-oriented version of the program. The Office of Workforce Strategy is supporting several AI pilot programs — an AI accounting and entrepreneurship course at Connecticut College, an AI for Cybersecurity program at the University of New Haven, and an online course on AI in healthcare business innovation at Quinnipiac University.

"We're putting our employers first and helping pull students through that process in order to build the skills, knowledge and abilities that they need to be successful," said Kelli-Marie Vallieres, the state's chief workforce officer. The goal, Vallieres and others said, is to work collaboratively with businesses in the state to connect students and companies well before graduation day.

Nicholas Schilling, a software engineer at IT and business consulting firm CGI, described how that sort of relationship helped him. Schilling entered the University of St. Joseph with few ideas of the career he wanted, then was connected with CGI at an event; he went on to study computer and data science, completed an internship with CGI, joined the company full-time after graduation, and now provides mentorship to students. "Because I had spent so much time with CGI as a student, the transition to full-time employment was seamless," he said. "I was able to focus on growth rather than trying to find my footing."

In Lamont's view, AI brings the potential for new opportunities and increased economic opportunity, and it fits into a broader push from state leaders to shift Connecticut's economy in a tech-oriented direction. "I'm pretty bullish because I think the world is moving in our favor in the sense that much more knowledge-based, skilled-based is where our future is," Lamont said. "A lot of that will be better directed, better guided, and much more effective for people learning how to use artificial intelligence."

The state has paired those arguments with a series of programs. Over the summer, Connecticut celebrated a grant award from the National Science Foundation that will help build out its quantum computing sector, a project also slated to receive $121 million in funding from the state. Around the same time, Lamont's office announced a partnership with RAISE US, a nonpartisan organization that offers workers displaced by AI a mix of retraining and upskilling; as part of the collaboration, Connecticut will pilot programs training workers to interact with AI, and lessons from Connecticut and other partner states will inform a national workforce strategy. "States are where the rubber really hits the road," Dan O'Keefe, commissioner of the Department of Economic and Community Development, told CT Mirror in July. "I think the federal government has been a bit hands-off on things, and we felt the need to fill the gap."

The state has also launched other efforts, including the Connecticut AI Academy, a free online school that offers certificate programs in AI literacy and AI fluency and is expected to expand to offer AI learning for teens, teachers and small businesses. In August, DECD announced a $20 million investment into an applied AI workforce center in Hartford, a project that will help train workers in new AI skills and technologies. The relationship between workforce development and artificial intelligence is also being examined by the state's Career Pathways Commission, which is developing a plan for work-based learning and career exposure to create pathway opportunities beginning in kindergarten through adulthood. "AI is not a major by itself, but it tends to be a minor in all the things you're doing," Lamont said. "So you learn how to apply it. That's where Connecticut's lead and its strength is going to be."

Connecticut is not alone. In recent months, several states have announced efforts to arm workers for the potential impacts of AI, many of them revolving around helping those most likely to be displaced develop new skills working with the technology. New York Gov. Kathy Hochul announced the Pursuit AI Jobs Institute, a Queens-based workforce center that aims to train 40,000 workers in work-relevant AI skills by 2032; the center, expected to be completed in 2027, is supported by $10 million in public investment and a National Advisory Council chaired by Miguel Cardona, the former U.S. Education Secretary and previous Connecticut Education Commissioner, who also chairs Connecticut's Career Pathways Commission. In Arkansas, the state is partnering with RAISE US on a career navigation program called ArkansasLAUNCH. In Maryland, RAISE US is supporting career transition programming and other support for displaced workers.

The variety of state efforts suggests there could be numerous ways to approach workforce development and skill training as AI technology develops; the coming months and years will test which efforts turn out to be most fruitful. As the event at Goodwin University came to a close, Lamont argued that Connecticut's initiatives, while multifaceted, all support the same goal: good jobs and economic stability for workers. "We're not going to be the R&D for AI, that's happening elsewhere," he said. "But we are going to be leaders when it comes to how to apply AI in the next generation of professions."
