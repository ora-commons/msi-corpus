## Councilwoman Tupola seeks a timeline of who knew what and when

At a Thursday press conference, Honolulu Mayor Rick Blangiardi vowed to be transparent about a fuel spill at Oʻahu’s H-Power incinerator and disclosed that more than 400,000 gallons leaked from a hole in an underground pipeline between January and May. Blangiardi said he had learned the full scope of the incident Tuesday and called the delay in notifying the public a major communications failure.

Honolulu city officials assured the public that drinking water would be safe from the contamination. It is not known what caused the hole in the underground pipeline, exactly how far the fuel plume has spread, how long it will take to fully remediate or why the issue was kept quiet from the public for so long. Blangiardi said shoreline impacts are not expected, but officials did not provide details about how they know that.

Honolulu City Councilwoman Andria Tupola, who represents the Westside, said she would press for answers, including “a complete timeline of who knew what and when,” and information about when workers were notified and how they would be protected. She said she would advocate for independent public testing of soil, groundwater and air in the area. Tupola also called for “full accountability” from Reworld, H-Power’s contractor, including coverage of long-term cleanup costs and payment of penalties under its contract. She said public notification of such incidents should be required.

“Westside families learned today about a fuel leak roughly 20 times the volume of the 2021 Red Hill spill, six months after it was discovered,” Tupola said. “That is unacceptable.”

Councilman Tyler Dos Santos-Tam, who was recently voted by his peers to be the next chair, said he would ask the Department of Environmental Services to brief the council and the public in the coming weeks. In a Thursday evening statement, Dos Santos-Tam said he wanted independent environmental testing to back officials’ claims that the water was safe, with the results made public. Communication problems also needed to be addressed, he said. “Fixing this one leak is not enough,” he said. “We need to make sure problems like this reach the Mayor, the Council and the public in days, not months, so we can address them head on.”

The U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, which is in charge of regulating aboveground fuel tanks like those at H-Power, recorded its last inspection of the facility on July 30 in its online enforcement data, which does not list any fuel storage-related violations. The EPA had not answered questions from Civil Beat about when it became aware of the spill or whether it was conducting its own investigation into the H-Power leak.

There have been three formal enforcement actions against the H-Power facility since 2019 for breaches of the Clean Air Act, according to the EPA database. The violations, which are not outlined in detail online, resulted in $23,900 in fines. The largest fine of $19,700 was for air pollution that contained particulate matter. The plant also was cited seven times for air pollution violations in 2017, but no fines were issued.

The Occupational Safety and Health Administration cited Reworld in March of this year for two violations that the agency classified as “serious,” resulting in fines totaling $29,790. One violation concerned the failure to “provide safe work places and practices by elimination or reduction of existing or potential hazards.” The other required supervisors and managers to be trained to recognize potential hazards, maintain safety and health protection in the workplace and reinforce employee safety training. OSHA’s website did not disclose the specific circumstances that led to the violation notices, and an inquiry to the Hawaiʻi Department of Labor and Industrial Relations had not received a response as of Thursday afternoon.

Reworld had been the city’s H-Power contractor since the early 1990s, when the facility opened, and the contractor and the city’s management had faced some scrutiny during that period. The city said Thursday that the current contract extends through 2032. A 2015 city audit said the company occupied “a unique role” as a longtime no-bid contractor with “extraordinary latitude to operate without much city oversight.” The audit found that the city had failed to provide proper oversight of the contractor, then called Covanta, and had not assigned the needed resources to monitor the company, instead relying on consultants. Reworld has been cited with multiple violations related to pollution and worker safety in recent years.

The audit said the city’s use of no-bid contracts gave the company “a monopoly on H-Power operations” and extended its right to run the facility for decades “without realizing” the city was doing so. Most localities only do contract extensions of five to 10 years, the audit found.

Covanta’s contract terms also obstructed the city’s ability to oversee the contractor, the audit said. Its contract limited the city’s access to company records, restricted the city’s ability to conduct audits and allowed for the destruction of records in violation of state law. The audit said, “Based on the unfavorable contract terms, the lack of staff in the energy recovery branch, ENV’s failure to assign the resources, needed to administer the complex H-POWER contracts, and other factors,” “we believe contract administration problems will recur.” Auditor Edwin Young made several recommendations, including not letting the company “write one-sided contracts that favor the contractor.”

Civil Beat reported at the time that then-Mayor Kirk Caldwell’s administration, through then-Managing Director Roy Amemiya, disputed the audit findings. The audit report said the administration agreed to most of the audit recommendations. According to the audit, city managers said the contracts were justified and in the best interests of the city and said the existing contract was a good contract. The auditor wrote, “We respectfully disagree.”

Civil Beat asked Blangiardi’s office on Thursday which audit recommendations, if any, had been followed in the last decade, and the office could not provide an accounting. The audit was conducted more than five years before the current mayor took office. In a statement, communications director Scott Humber said the mayor’s office recognized the importance of the findings and recommendations. “Our administration is reviewing the historical record to better understand what corrective actions were taken in response to the audit and whether any of those findings remain relevant to current operations,” Humber said. “We are not going to speculate about actions taken under a previous administration or characterize the current status of those findings without first verifying the facts.” He said Blangiardi expects “accountability, transparency and effective oversight” of city operations and contracts, including H-Power, and that those expectations remain unchanged.

Thursday’s disclosure established the spill’s volume and duration but left its cause, plume extent, cleanup timetable, regulatory response, months of delayed notification and how the leak went undetected for so long unresolved. Tupola is seeking coverage of long-term cleanup costs and payment of penalties under the contractor’s contract, while Dos Santos-Tam is seeking independent testing, public results and a disclosure process that reaches city officials and the public within days rather than months.
