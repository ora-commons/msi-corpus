## FEMA directs residents within 20 miles to shelter in place

The Environmental Protection Agency had collected more than 1,200 air, land and water samples as the U.S. Army prepared Wednesday to detonate the nation’s entire stockpile of degraded lead azide at the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant in Middletown. The controlled detonation, involving roughly 5 tons (4.54 metric tonnes), was scheduled for around 10 a.m. EST, with the Army saying the deteriorating bomb-making chemicals could explode at any time.

The agency said it would test lead levels during and after the detonation, monitor the air and report data every five minutes. No lead was detected in the air before the blast, and the agency expected only a brief increase afterward.

Residents and environmentalists have raised concerns about potential health and environmental effects. Officials on Tuesday warned residents near Middletown to be ready to evacuate to avoid toxic dust. The forecast called for southwest winds, away from Burlington and the Mississippi River, and the Army said it had placed barriers around the blast zone and applied ballistic gels to absorb lead dust.

The Federal Emergency Management Agency (FEMA) issued an emergency alert about an hour before the planned detonation. It said evacuation was no longer safe or advised and that all residents within 20 miles (32 kilometers), including Middletown, Augusta and Burlington, must shelter in place. The alert said, "STAY INDOORS with windows closed to block lead dust," and said evacuated residents would be notified by phone when it was safe to return.

Earlier, FEMA recommended that all residents in Middletown and others within a 5-mile cone of the plant evacuate. People outside the recommended evacuation zone were encouraged to shelter in place, close windows and doors, and set their heaters to recirculate rather than pull in outside air. Schools in the projected plume area and some businesses and government buildings closed. Some residents sealed windows and doors, while boxes of painter’s tape and masks were placed outside government buildings on Tuesday.

The blast was originally planned for last Friday. According to court documents, the Army rescheduled it to allow more time for extra safety measures and more favorable wind conditions, the third time it changed the date. After environmentalists sued to stop the detonation, a federal appeals court approved the operation.

Rob Cekada, director of the Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, Firearms and Explosives, said the country’s stockpile had been brought to the plant from bases around the country in 2009. The chemicals, used in munitions production, were stored in barrels in case the military needed to use them again. During a routine monitoring inspection in March, plant staff found that the lead azide had partially crystallized, making it sensitive to friction and cold temperatures and creating a risk of spontaneous detonation.

The Army assembled experts to formulate a plan and found that alternatives such as thermal treatment and neutralizing the drums’ contents were not as safe as a controlled detonation. The Army also said it was reviewing its storage facilities to ensure a similar situation did not arise again.

"It is the top priority of this agency to ensure impacted residents can swiftly be and feel safe and comfortable in their homes, at their schools, in recreational spaces, and in their community," said Tom Croci, the agency's associate administrator for national security and operations coordination. Officials also said they would take samples to measure lead levels in soil, air and water after the detonation. Experts and officials encouraged residents to clean outdoor surfaces children might touch, including outdoor play equipment, patios and decks.

Richard Fordyce, undersecretary of agriculture for farm production and conservation at the U.S. Department of Agriculture, said about 350 farms could be affected by the projected plume. The Iowa Department of Agriculture and Land Stewardship encouraged livestock farmers to move their animals indoors, shut windows and barn doors, contact the U.S. Army to have baseline soil samples taken from their farms before the detonation and monitor their animals after the blast for any changes in appetite or behavior.

Doug Seyb, a 71-year-old resident of Donnellson, Iowa, said his farm is 18 miles (29 kilometers) southwest of the plant. His cattle are on pasture and cannot be brought indoors, while ponds fill the farm’s water tanks. Seyb said, "We can just cross our fingers," and planned to head inside for a few hours until it was time to feed the cattle in the afternoon.

Seyb said he feared his calves could be born with deformities in the spring and was unsure how next year’s soybean and corn crops would fare if lead contaminated the soil. He also said, "Farm's been in the family over 150 years, and I've spent my whole life building it and making it what it is."

The Army kept media outlets and others more than 4 miles (6.4 kilometers) away from the blast site. In an advisory to news outlets, it warned, "Please, do not put yourselves in harm's way." The Associated Press livestreamed from a location about 5 miles (8 kilometers) away and upwind from the plant. The Army said it would film Wednesday morning’s controlled detonation and make the footage and photos available as soon as possible while continuing updates on its social media accounts.

Although a federal appeals court had approved the operation, potential health and environmental effects remained the focus of federal monitoring and local concern. The planned blast also remained contingent on changing weather patterns, with the Army saying it would issue a more precise detonation time after evaluating them.
