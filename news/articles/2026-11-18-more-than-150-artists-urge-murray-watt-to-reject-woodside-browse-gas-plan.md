## EPA decision clock paused pending further technical advice

More than 150 Australian artists have signed an open letter asking federal environment minister Murray Watt to reject Woodside Energy's proposal to develop the Browse basin, about 300 km off the Kimberley coast. The Greenpeace-led campaign targets a plan to drill more than 50 gas wells about 3 km from the Scott Reef area, where more than 1,500 species live, including endangered pygmy blue whales.

Actors Hugh Jackman, Rebel Wilson, Richard Roxburgh and Mia Wasikowska were among the prominent Australians who opposed Browse because they believed the proposed gasfield could damage the reef's unique ecosystem. Musicians Jimmy Barnes, Paul Kelly, Tame Impala's Kevin Parker, Tina Arena, Jessica Mauboy, G Flip, Vance Joy, Missy Higgins and Baker Boy also signed, along with authors JM Coetzee, Helen Garner, Anna Funder and Diana Reid.

The campaign followed a release by a supergroup of more than 20 Australian artists, including King Gizzard & the Lizard Wizard, Jimmy Barnes, John Butler and Montaigne. Their song, *If Not Now*, was released as a musical call to arms about Scott Reef. It reworked King Gizzard's 2020 track *If Not Now, Then When?* in a collaboration among Greenpeace Australia Pacific, Green Music Australia and p(doom) Records.

Browse was first proposed by Woodside in 2018 and would send gas about 900 km to the company's ageing North West Shelf gas export plant, adjacent to the world heritage-listed Murujuga rock art. Woodside, which The Guardian describes as Australia's largest fossil fuel company, calls Browse “Australia’s biggest undeveloped offshore gas resource.”

The open letter says Browse “would turn Scott Reef into an industrial gas zone with decades of deafening seismic blasting [and] carbon dumping beneath the ocean floor.” It also warns that drilling could cause an oil spill up to 30 times bigger than the 2009 Montara disaster, polluting Scott Reef with oil condensate, impacting the Kimberley coastline and potentially reaching Indonesia.

Woodside disputed the oil-spill framing. A company spokesperson said, “Browse is a natural gas and condensate development, not an oil development. References to a potential ‘oil spill’ from Browse are inaccurate.” The spokesperson said a potential well blowout was highly unlikely and that the longest credible duration would be around 12 hours.

The company also said feedback had helped reduce the infrastructure footprint around environmentally sensitive areas such as Scott Reef, which “will not be built on, drilled or mined.”

The Guardian reported that Western Australia's independent environmental watchdog had sharply criticized the proposal, saying plans to protect turtles and blue whales near Scott Reef would be ineffective. A draft letter from the Environmental Protection Authority, written in 2023, advised that reducing the risk of an oil spill to an acceptable level might be impossible. Greenpeace also nominated Scott Reef for national heritage listing, citing its ecological and scientific values.

Modelling by the Institute for Energy Economics and Financial Analysis suggests Browse would generate up to 6.8 million tonnes of carbon dioxide emissions yearly at peak production. The institute estimated that the emissions could account for between 2.9% and 3.7% of Australia's emissions in 2035. IEEFA's cost analysis found last year that Browse gas was likely four times more expensive than existing domestic gas and that the project “is unlikely to be competitive either domestically or internationally, based on IEEFA cost estimates”.

Matt Roberts, executive director of the Conservation Council of Western Australia, described the proposal as “extremely concerning.” Roberts said Western Australia had enough gas available to support the transition to a renewable future and that regional demand for LNG was declining. He said the federal and state governments had enough evidence to reject the proposal and save Scott Reef and that he expected them to do so.

A spokesperson for the national EPA said the development was being assessed under the national Environment Protection and Biodiversity Conservation Act. The spokesperson said the “decision clock” had been paused while the agency waited for “further technical advice.” A Woodside spokesperson said the proposed development “remains subject to a detailed, public and transparent assessment by state and Commonwealth regulators.” Woodside is still seeking federal approval, while the artists and conservation groups have asked the government to reject the proposal.
