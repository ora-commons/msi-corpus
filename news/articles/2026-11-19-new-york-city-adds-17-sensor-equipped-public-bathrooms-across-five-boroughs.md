## City pays $225,000 per unit for maintenance during two-year trials

New York City has brought 17 modular public bathrooms into service across all five boroughs over the past two months. Christopher Mims, a technology columnist for The Wall Street Journal, visited the units on a day-long tour this week and found them remarkably tidy, as did many New Yorkers he spoke with before and after they used the facilities. The bathrooms have logged nearly 40,000 uses and are operating as a two-year trial.

Mims wrote that public toilets in America’s major cities have a reputation for being “a cross between a public health hazard and a crime scene.” The bathrooms are kept clean through a blend of sensors and user-accountability mechanisms. Mims reported that Throne's network had recorded 15 million visits to date and that the Washington, D.C.-based company already maintained facilities in some of the roughest neighborhoods in America. Mims said he covered the company a year earlier when it was attracting attention in other cities, then returned after hearing it would come to a city with massive public-restroom needs and a history of failed attempts to solve the issue.

At North Shore Esplanade Park on Staten Island, the city's southernmost borough, a solar-paneled unit stands beside an accessibility ramp and is the only public toilet within a 15-minute walk. Sized like a modest camping trailer and painted in blue, white and green New York City Parks livery, the restroom requires no sewer, water or power connections. The modules nevertheless include a flush toilet, running water and touch-free sensors. They are built in Indiana, and Mims compared them more closely to upscale recreational-vehicle toilets than music-festival portable toilets.

Entry is free. Users can scan a QR code to send Throne a text, download its app or get an entry card from the city or a local business, providing an alternative for people without smartphones. The app asks users to rate cleanliness and smell as soon as they enter. Throne uses the ratings to identify users who leave a unit in poor condition; repeated problems can bring a warning and eventually an app-account ban. Accounts link to a phone, not a person. Mims said most users appeared to treat the bathrooms better than other public facilities because they knew they would be rated, while a small fraction of users caused the biggest problems.

Angel Garcia, a lifelong New Yorker using a unit for the first time, said, “It’s my first time using it, and it’s actually very clean.” He would give the bathroom an eight on a 10-point scale.

Jessica Heinzelman, Throne's co-founder and chief operating officer, said, “One of the big reasons why cities don’t have more public restrooms is that about 1% of people will ruin it for the other 99%.” Workers inspect each bathroom every 10 to 15 uses, or when a bad user rating triggers a special visit. Heinzelman also said the units are designed to maximize uptime and availability, with easily replaced wallpaper and generic music that research has shown keeps people from loitering.

At Cooper Square in Manhattan, Mims met Julia Kerson, New York City's deputy mayor of operations. Her portfolio includes making sure the city's bridges do not fall down and providing New Yorkers a place to relieve themselves other than the nearest subway elevator. The city currently has around 1,100 public bathrooms, mostly in parks and libraries with limited hours, and has set a goal of doubling that number, though the timeline is not fixed. Kerson said, “We know that a thousand public bathrooms for 8½ million people just isn’t enough,” and added, “There have been a bunch of different attempts to solve that problem, and some of them are really expensive.”

New York City is paying Throne $225,000 per bathroom for the two-year residence, including all maintenance and cleaning. Previous restrooms cost the city $3 million apiece, and many residents reported that those facilities were borderline unusable.

At Columbus Square in Brooklyn, Mims found that first-time users might not understand how to enter the units. Most people have never scanned a QR code or downloaded an app to access a public building, he wrote, and he found himself explaining the process to quizzical onlookers on several occasions. Jules Gillman, a New York City influencer who reviews public bathrooms, told him the QR codes were her least favorite part of the Throne facilities, although she rated them highly for cleanliness and accessibility. “The bathroom is very tech-y, which is cool in a way, but I don’t know if we should have to rely on our phones to use the bathroom,” she said.

Some New Yorkers have begun calling the facilities “Mamdani toilets,” and an Astoria unit appears under that name on Google Maps with a 4.9-star rating.

At his next stop in Queens, Mims met Heinzelman and another Throne co-founder, Chief Executive Fletcher Wilson. Wilson is a Stanford-trained mechanical engineer who said his chronic gut issues motivated him to start a bathroom company. Throne has deployed about 165 units in seven markets, including Washington, D.C., Los Angeles, Ann Arbor, Mich., Seattle, the Bay Area and Atlanta. Wilson said none of those markets were saturated, and he said the network benefits from higher-density areas because cleaners can move quickly around as needed.

By the eighth hour of his five-borough crawl, Mims was across the street from Yankee Stadium, at a unit in the Bronx. Locals said they appreciated having the restroom there, as did a bus driver with a nearby route. No one objected to the 10-minute time limit or to the lights flashing with 30 seconds remaining, soon followed by the door sliding open on its own.

Gillman, who has Crohn's disease, said she has out of necessity tried many available bathrooms across the city. She avoids some park facilities because they are so poorly designed, even in moments of dire need, and said, “If I had to choose between a Throne bathroom and almost any other public bathroom I’ve used in NYC, I would choose the Throne.”

The design makes the trade-offs explicit, Mims wrote: ease of use against ease of vandalism, availability against time limits, and cost against permanence. Mims wrote that many cities have found that decades of failed alternatives make these trade-offs worthwhile. The current program remains a two-year trial of 17 units, while New York City works toward its goal of doubling a public-bathroom system of around 1,100 facilities without a fixed timeline.
