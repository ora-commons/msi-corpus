## Oregon ethics commission reviews Bell's disclosure as Aloha 209th remains unfunded

When Oregon’s Housing Stability Council met by Zoom in early October, Andrea Bell told the oversight board she wanted to address a ProPublica story published that morning. The story said Bell, the executive director of Oregon Housing and Community Services, had not clearly disclosed her husband’s work for JH Brawner, a consulting firm that helps housing authorities develop apartments, sometimes with money from Bell’s agency. Nor had she notified Gov. Tina Kotek, as Oregon law requires of a governor’s appointee when an actual or potential conflict arises. Council members, who award hundreds of millions of dollars in housing subsidies, affirmed their support for Bell. She told them, “None of the projects that are coming from the council impact my household income.”

ProPublica’s newly obtained county contracts and emails show a financial connection between state housing decisions and JH Brawner. The records also show Bell participating in meetings and internal discussions about state funding for Aloha 209th, a large apartment project in Portland’s suburbs that JH Brawner was helping develop. Backers were seeking $17.5 million from the Housing Stability Council, which receives funding recommendations from Bell’s agency.

Washington County’s housing authority, which is seeking state funding for Aloha 209th, would owe JH Brawner a $2 million consulting fee if the transaction closed. The county contract said that no fee would be due if funding fell through for any reason and the project did not move forward. JH Brawner employs Bell’s husband, Joshua Crites, as director of strategic initiatives. Crites did not respond to an emailed request for comment.

The Park Place apartments in Clackamas County already received money through Bell’s agency. Oregon awarded $36 million to the project in May 2025, and five months later Clackamas County’s housing authority paid JH Brawner a $2.4 million consulting fee. The Clackamas County contract likewise said that if funding fell through for any reason and the project did not move forward, “no fee shall be due” to the firm. JH Brawner said Crites has not worked on Aloha 209th but was one of five staffers assigned to Park Place. The firm pays employees, including Crites, a fixed salary not tied to a project closing, according to managing director Zak de Gorgue. De Gorgue said Clackamas and Washington counties selected JH Brawner as a consultant years before the company hired Crites in 2023.

Bell said in a statement that she was “committed to transparency, accountability, and compliance with state law.” She added, “At no point in my tenure as executive director have I used my position to influence or benefit myself or anyone related to me.” Bell said she made no efforts to influence funding and that Aloha 209th had not received funding from Oregon Housing and Community Services and remained on the waitlist.

Bell’s public agency sits in the middle of Oregon’s effort to combat a homelessness problem that is among the nation’s worst. Gov. Tina Kotek has made building more housing a pillar of her time in office. Oregon Housing and Community Services has distributed billions of state and federal dollars to subsidize the construction of apartments intended to have affordable rents.

De Gorgue said the Aloha 209th project remained in development and JH Brawner had “no knowledge of Andrea Bell taking any action to move the project forward, nor has JH Brawner & Company engaged in any advocacy to secure funding approval” from Bell’s agency. Funding applications were submitted by the local housing authorities, not by JH Brawner. De Gorgue also said, “We have no indication, nor any reason to believe, that any project on which we have consulted has been the beneficiary of inappropriate influence.”

Norman Williams, a law professor at Willamette University College of Law who teaches about government ethics, said Bell should have reported to the governor that she had a possible conflict and stayed out of discussions about projects her husband’s employer helped develop. “It may all be an innocent mistake on her part,” Williams said, “but she knows who her husband is working for and she doesn’t report that. It makes it look like she’s trying to conceal this.”

After ProPublica asked Bell about her husband’s employment in September, she amended three years of ethics disclosure forms, explicitly naming JH Brawner as his employer. Before the amendment, one 2025 filing alluded to Crites’ job with the misspelled phrase “Spouse serves in an advisory role for a developmen.” It showed only the company’s street address, misspelled, and not its name. Bell also asked the Oregon Government Ethics Commission for a formal opinion about her potential conflict at the direction of Kotek’s staff. That evaluation was paused after the commission opened a preliminary review in response to a citizen complaint about her lack of disclosure. The review is ongoing.

ProPublica obtained the new details through county contracts and emails to and from Bell that were released under Oregon’s public-records law. Bell’s agency initially said the emails would cost $612.50 to produce but eventually provided them for free. Washington County charged $364.25 for contracts, invoices and other documents.

Backers say Aloha 209th is ready to be built, but the project remains on the state waitlist, and its turn is not likely to come until at least late 2028. Local officials worry that the delay could jeopardize other funding sources and that the project may need an even larger subsidy. Since 2024, Oregon has awarded the money on a first-come, first-served basis. When funding runs out, Bell’s agency places applications on a waitlist in the order received.

The new records contain Bell’s recollections of a June phone conversation with Molly Rogers, the head of the county housing authority seeking money for Aloha 209th. Rogers had spoken publicly about contacting Bell and said the state agency director was receptive to talking further. The same morning the two spoke, Bell emailed another department official that Aloha 209th had “a unique dimension of urgency and angst” because it was one of the last projects funded by a 2018 voter-approved housing bond administered by the Portland area’s Metro regional government. Natasha Detweiler-Daby, who leads the state housing agency’s affordable rental housing division, responded that it could be “responsive and logical” to have lawmakers “add nuance” next year by dedicating some portion of funding to housing-authority projects on Oregon’s waitlist. Detweiler-Daby did not respond to an email from ProPublica seeking comment.

Records show Bell spoke with Washington County officials about Aloha 209th during a 45-minute Zoom meeting in August. Internal notes said the county would send an updated report to the state agency to “inform movement of 209,” and the groups would have a follow-up conversation. Bell discussed the project with regional government officials in early September. About two weeks before that meeting, she emailed her deputy with two possible ways to get the project funded: a legislative “carve-out” or Metro consolidating its subsidies so it could fully pay for the project. Bell wrote, “I want to do everything we can to help the metro projects be successful and the resources, like ours, are nearly depleted,” and acknowledged that Metro might not find the solution appealing.

Bell told ProPublica that Washington County and Metro requested the meetings. She said her agency “does not have and is not pursuing a ‘legislative carve out’ for any project.” Bell added, “My approach is to listen to partners’ concerns and explain our funding process,” and, “I have no role in deciding whether projects should receive funding as part of this process, and I do not advocate for individual projects.” Bell said, “Characterizing these routine discussions and legitimate concerns as something improper fundamentally misrepresents their purpose and context.” A Washington County housing authority spokesperson said Bell made no commitment to alter Aloha 209th’s prioritization; another spokesperson said the August meeting focused mainly on “clearer criteria” for how waitlisted projects receive state funding. A Metro spokesperson said the agency requested the September meeting to strengthen its working relationship and coordination with the state. Metro “does not, and did not, advocate for any specific project,” the spokesperson said. “We clearly indicated this at the meeting and in writing ahead of the meeting.” The agency has advocated for a group of remaining projects, including Aloha 209th, so Metro can fulfill commitments from its affordable housing bond.

The Oregon Government Ethics Commission has until early December to complete its preliminary review of the complaint, which will help the agency decide whether to open a full investigation. Bell said she would “continue to remain responsive to the Ethics Commission” as the agency completes its examination. Kotek’s office, given ProPublica’s latest findings, said the governor “holds all agency leadership to consistent and high ethical standards” and that her office “is closely following” the ethics panel’s review.
