## Project-certainty rules draw opposition as Democratic divisions persist

At a press conference at Confluence Park in downtown Denver, Environment Colorado and The Wilderness Society urged Congress to reject the Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act, or BAAJA, which would change federal permitting laws. The groups’ objections came as BAAJA cleared several additional procedural Senate votes, with about 15 to 20 Democrats, including Sen. John Hickenlooper, continuing to vote in favor. BAAJA has divided Democrats, while some climate and clean-energy advocates support it and many environmental groups criticize it.

Environment Colorado’s Ellie Andrews called BAAJA “a Trojan horse” that would undermine “bedrock environmental laws that have protected our land, air and water for over 50 years.” “BAAJA would send us backwards,” Andrews said. “It may help some renewable energy projects move forward, but it accelerates mining, it accelerates drilling, it will accelerate building pipelines.”

Taylor Luneau, Colorado conservation manager for The Wilderness Society, said, “This bill is not the way forward to a clean energy future. It’s a destructive path that will cause irreversible harm to our public lands and the communities that depend on them.” Luneau added, “We’re grateful to Sen. Bennet for opposing this bill, and we urge Sen. Hickenlooper to do the same.”

Among opponents’ leading concerns are provisions relating to “project certainty,” which would curtail federal agencies’ and courts’ authority to revoke or modify project approvals after they have been granted. Supporters say the changes provide stability across administrations and prevent a repeat of former President Joe Biden’s cancellation of the Keystone XL pipeline in 2021 or President Donald Trump’s cancellation of offshore wind projects.

Many environmental groups view the regulatory and legal procedures under laws such as the National Environmental Policy Act, the Clean Water Act and the Endangered Species Act as central to their work since the laws passed in the 1970s. Those groups contend the changes go too far and could “lock in” major fossil fuel projects while climate scientists are urging policymakers to do the opposite.

“To stop (a project) because somebody discovered something, that no doubt is frustrating,” Andrews said. “But the things that get discovered matter, and there’s a process for that, and it’s been hammered out over 50-odd years.”

Colorado’s Senate delegation remains divided. Hickenlooper cast multiple votes this month in favor of advancing the measure, while Bennet remained opposed. In a Nov. 9 statement, Bennet said he was “concerned that not all perspectives were sufficiently heard during the drafting of this far-reaching piece of legislation.”

BAAJA was negotiated by Republican Sens. Shelley Moore Capito of West Virginia and Mike Lee of Utah, together with Democratic Sens. Sheldon Whitehouse of Rhode Island and Martin Heinrich of New Mexico. Capito chairs the Senate Environment and Public Works Committee, Lee chairs the Energy Committee, and Whitehouse and Heinrich are the ranking members on their respective committees.

BAAJA has emerged as a top legislative priority for the current Republican-controlled Congress after years of failed negotiations in Washington over how to speed approvals. Developers say the process can bog down major energy and infrastructure projects in years of study and litigation. Trade groups representing fossil fuel and pipeline companies have lobbied for BAAJA. Some climate and clean-energy advocates also support the legislation, arguing that it would accelerate the buildout of large renewable projects and transmission lines needed for the energy transition.

Critics also charged that BAAJA would accelerate data center development, including on public lands, and further enable the rapid expansion of Immigration and Customs Enforcement detention capacity envisioned by architects of the Trump administration’s immigration program. Tribal groups separately faulted what they described as the bill’s narrowing of tribal consultation requirements and protections for sacred and cultural sites.

The measure cleared several more procedural votes Wednesday, with a group of about 15 to 20 Democrats, including Hickenlooper, continuing to vote in favor. It could be amended further before final passage in the Senate sometime this week. BAAJA would then need approval from the House of Representatives before reaching Trump’s desk for a signature.

Following the midterm elections, some BAAJA supporters said the bill could deliver on the affordability message that drew many swing voters to their side. Those supporters also questioned whether a future compromise could win support from the next Congress’s Democratic majorities while remaining acceptable to Trump and the handful of Republican senators needed to break the filibuster.

In a statement earlier this month, Hickenlooper said, “This bill isn’t perfect. No bill is. We should continue working with Tribal Nations and environmental groups to strengthen it as it moves through the legislative process.” He added, “But we have a golden opportunity to make good on our promise to voters to make energy more affordable. We cannot afford to let it slip away.”

Some opponents argued that Democrats could take up the issue again in January, rather than fast-track BAAJA through Congress in the final weeks of Republican control. “The midterms sent a pretty clear message,” Andrews said. “I think there is definitely hope in the future for a stronger political coalition that can keep more of those voices at the table.”
