## Educators describe IEP caseloads, staffing pressures and student progress

Special education teachers from Florida, California, South Carolina, Virginia and Utah responded to NPR’s reporting on educator shortages and workload. Their messages described student progress while detailing staffing gaps, legally required individualized education programs, or IEPs, long hours, burnout and physical or emotional strain. NPR’s education team plans to follow about a dozen special educators across the country during the school year and visit some in their classrooms.

NPR launched the series to answer half-century-old questions about why special educators are always in short supply and what the work requires. NPR said it wanted to go beyond research and hear directly from teachers about their jobs each day. Special education reaches more than 8 million public school students in the United States, a number that continues to grow, while educators in the field remain among the hardest to hire and keep.

The teachers’ accounts so far include joyful moments when students met goals sooner than expected, as well as challenges such as classrooms without solid walls and the ripple effects of disruptive student behavior. Almost every teacher has wrestled with IEPs, which involve legally required paperwork. Examples of that work include creating IEPs, setting goals, tracking progress, writing assessment reports and holding at least one annual meeting.

Emily Ellis, an elementary school teacher in Cayce, S.C., whom NPR is following, sent NPR a voice memo on her way home from work after wrapping up her first quarter of teaching. She described a student entering kindergarten unable to count who later could count to 10 independently and had learned the sounds of the alphabet. “A big happy moment was just seeing how much growth my students have had,” Ellis said. Some of her students were on their way to mastering their IEP goals. Ellis said, “Just seeing where they started and where they are now.” Her data showed progress even when she felt as though nothing was working.

Kathleen Tejeda, a veteran teacher in Riverside, Calif., said special education teachers have two jobs: academic instruction and case management. She described creating and implementing IEPs, setting goals, collaborating with teachers and helping students with social and emotional needs. Tejeda said the work can consume a teacher’s entire life and that it took her years to find a balance. “It is a fine line between both parts of the job, and then yourself,” she said. “You don't want to lose yourself in the job.”

Wendy Campbell, a special education teacher in Jacksonville, Fla., wrote on Instagram after reading NPR’s story. She wrote that special educators were “in the trenches,” overworked, understaffed and “GROSSLY underpaid”. Campbell said Florida’s general teacher shortage is tenfold for special education, and teachers carry caseloads while supporting general education colleagues. Her message ended, “I stand with each of you, I'm glad I'm not alone.”

Jacob Meraz, who has taught special education in Southern California for almost 9 years, said NPR’s reporting matched his experience. Based in Montclair, Calif., he connected particularly with the balance among paperwork, meetings and teaching, and with how little teaching sometimes gets accomplished in a day. Meraz also described isolation from other teachers because of the workload, saying it rang so true that he began to tear up.

Russell Fung, a first-year elementary special education teacher in Los Angeles, said the comments accurately described a relentless “onslaught of chaos.” Fung said he initially thought he was a failure but concluded that he was not incompetent. He also said he was grateful for the support he received from his team.

Emily Clausen of Alexandria, Va., said, “It's no secret why there is a shortage of SPED teachers.” She called the job “an impossible job to do well if you want to have a life outside of contract hours.” Clausen said, “Pretty much every SPED teacher I know puts in long hours before and after school, on weekends, over breaks, etc and still feel like they can't get it all done.” She taught special education at the preschool and elementary levels for nine years before getting “so burned out and disillusioned” that she almost left teaching altogether. She switched to general education, which she loves, but said, “Don't get me wrong, it's still hard, but special education is a whole other beast.”

Erica Otero, a second-year special education teacher in Sacramento, Calif., described the work as an emotional roller coaster from minute to minute and day to day. Otero said she loves her students and resumed therapy during the school year because of the secondhand trauma in her classroom. She said concrete examples and advice from peers and colleagues helped her see changes that supported students, while regulating her own emotions after work helped her carry the daily workload.

Natalie Jeffs of Provo, Utah, said she feels excited to work with special children but cited a high burnout rate associated with IEP workload, scheduling, curriculum planning, data collection and physical trauma from students. Jeffs said a teacher she works with had taught special education for 18 years and “just last year suffered her 6th and 7th concussions due to student violence.” Jeffs said teachers cannot remain in such an environment indefinitely without harming their health, adding, “when we get hurt by them, our bodies and brains register it as trauma.”

Kara Grabendike of Ventura, Calif., described duties beyond lesson preparation, grading, classroom management and behavior. She also listed managing special education services, working with providers, tracking goal progress, assessing students, writing assessment reports and holding at least one yearly IEP meeting. Grabendike said, “Most of us have 25+ students on our caseload.” She said, “We feel like terrible teachers because our priority has to be the legal aspects of the IEP.” Grabendike said, “I am truly grateful for an incredible team of caring people that make it bearable,” and added, “We all love the students or we wouldn't do this job.” She concluded, “it's simply too much most of the time.”

Emily Zacarias of Santa Barbara, Calif., said she left special education after 12 years to maintain and preserve her mental health and well-being. She said the rewards of the work can be offset over time unless teachers have supportive administrators and parents who work with them rather than against them. Zacarias said she supports anyone entering the field and added, “they are not doing it for the money, I can assure you that.”

Former student Andrew Regopoulos emailed NPR after the story “hit home” for him. He had been a special education student from second grade through high school and said his teachers made a world of difference while he was living with ADHD and dyslexia. “I overcame so much with them. I'm now the Deputy Fee Manager at Grand Canyon National Park,” he wrote. Regopoulos, who is 38 and has a family, said that if it weren’t for his special education teachers “knowing how to care and support me I don't think I ever would have made anything of myself.”

Across the responses, teachers repeatedly described both progress and strain. They cited support from colleagues, in some cases from an experienced teacher or a team, alongside long hours, burnout, trauma and decisions to leave the profession. Many special educators say that while the work begins in the classroom, seeing their students succeed beyond school is their guiding star. NPR said its education team would remain in touch with about a dozen educators through the school year and visit some classrooms.
