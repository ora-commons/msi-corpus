## Army bars three Fort Hood survivors from Hasan execution

**Subtype:** fact

- The Army barred three Fort Hood survivors from witnessing Nidal Hasan’s firing-squad execution because none was a direct victim of the charges for which he was convicted.
- Military officials rejected the survivors’ request for reconsideration, citing security and space restrictions.
- Nidal Hasan sought a stay of his December 3 execution, according to U.S. court records.
- The survivors urged the Army to apply the Uniform Code of Military Justice’s broader definition of victim, which includes direct physical, emotional or pecuniary harm.
- A previous plan to livestream the military execution was rescinded after bipartisan backlash.

## Hasan seeks a stay while the Army rejects reconsideration

The Army announced on Nov. 10 that three survivors of the 2009 Fort Hood shooting at the Texas Army post would not be allowed to attend Nidal Hasan’s scheduled firing-squad execution. The Army said the three were injured in the attack but were not direct victims of the charges for which Hasan was convicted. The decision cited regulations governing who may witness a military execution, and the execution was scheduled for the afternoon of Dec. 3.

On Tuesday, military officials rejected the survivors’ request for reconsideration. An email sent Tuesday and shared with the Associated Press by recipients said the denial reflected security and space issues.

Hasan was found guilty of multiple counts of murder and attempted murder arising from the attack, which killed 13 people and wounded more than 30 by gunfire. On Tuesday, Hasan sought a stay of the execution scheduled for the afternoon of Dec. 3, according to U.S. court records.

The survivors argued that the Army should apply the broader definition of victim in the Uniform Code of Military Justice, which says a victim can be someone who suffered “direct physical, emotional or pecuniary harm.” They said the Army’s refusal to let them attend implied they had not been victims because they had not been struck by bullets.

A letter signed by the commandant of Fort Leavenworth, the military prison where Hasan is being held, said the three could not appeal again. The letter said: “Please understand that this is not meant as a judgment on any emotional injury you or others suffered as a result of CP Hasan’s crimes.”

An Army statement said regulations specify who can witness a military execution and reiterated the size and space restrictions. The statement added: “Efforts have been made over the years, from the trial to notifications to memorials and now to the execution, to recognize the impacts and support the victims.”

Retired Col. Kathy Platoni, who would have been Hasan’s supervisor during a planned deployment to Afghanistan, said the denial felt like a betrayal. Speaking from her home in Ohio, she said, “I would desperately like to attend,” and added: “To look Hasan in the eye and say: ‘You didn’t get me.’”

Platoni said the survivors from her unit deployed to Afghanistan 29 days after the attack, because, “we still served our country, and if we didn’t, [Hasan’] would have won”. She said the building where Hasan initially opened fire had been crowded with returning soldiers and those preparing to deploy. She had been moved to another building when the attack began.

Platoni described the response to the attack: “The looks on the faces of my friends and colleagues as they carried in the dying and wounded that day … it will haunt me for the rest of my life,” she said. A badly wounded captain who had rushed Hasan with a chair to try to stop the shooting was among those carried in.

Platoni described the lack of supplies as she tried to help the wounded. “I was throwing computers to the ground trying to get the tablecloths because we needed blankets. We didn’t have medical supplies or even water,” she said. “I still have that blood-stained uniform, from kneeling down next to that young man, trying to get him to stay with us. That will be with me forever.”

Julia Wilson, who was nine months pregnant when Hasan opened fire, said her request to witness the execution was also denied. She said the Army’s handling of the case had caused her anguish. “We have faced institutional betrayal after institutional betrayal,” Wilson said. “We remain on that battlefield because of that … if they don’t fix it we’re never going to leave that battlefield. We’ll never heal.”

An Army official, speaking on condition of anonymity to discuss sensitive plans, said the U.S. military was not planning to release details about the firing squad or protocols, including the number of service members involved.

Retired Maj. C. Alan Hopewell testified at Hasan’s trial and confirmed Hasan’s identity after Hasan was shot by two service members attempting to halt his rampage. One of those officers had asked Hopewell to accompany her to the execution. Hopewell said on Tuesday that he would accept the denial but with mixed feelings. “I’m disappointed that our status as victims is not recognized,” Hopewell said. “A bullet missed me and hit my car. I guess if the bullet had hit me … they would consider me a victim and let me attend.”

A previous plan to livestream the military execution was rescinded after bipartisan backlash. The execution would be the military’s first since 1961 and the first by firing squad since the second world war.
