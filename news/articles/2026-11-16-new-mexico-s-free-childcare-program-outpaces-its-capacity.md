## State has added more than half of its 12,000-spot childcare goal

New Mexico's free childcare program faces one catch roughly a year after the state became the first to offer free childcare to every eligible family regardless of income: the state doesn't have the capacity to meet the demand. After about a year, parents say the free childcare is life-changing. Reporting from Santa Fe published by NPR on Monday found long waitlists at providers even as state officials say capacity is growing.

At Kids Campus, a sought-after bilingual childcare center on the campus of Santa Fe Community College that serves children from 2 months old to 5 years old and is now free, Judyn Martinez enrolled her 3-year-old daughter after a long wait. Martinez, a single mother, described the center: "It's an amazing school so far. She loves it. I love it." She said she is grateful for the universal program. "I'm really grateful for the universal childcare 'cause I wouldn't have been able to send her," she said.

Martinez also warned other families about the wait: "So I waited about a year and a half on the waitlist for her to get in." The center's waitlist holds almost 700 names, she said. Childcare providers across New Mexico told NPR they also had long waits. One reason is a nationwide shortage of childcare workers, driven in part by low pay.

Magali Molina, the lead teacher at Kids Campus, said she loves her job but the compensation falls short. "I love my job, but it's still I think the pay is really low," Molina said.

The shortage is not confined to New Mexico. Nearly half of all children in the U.S. live in a licensed childcare desert where demand outpaces availability, according to the Center for American Progress, a left-leaning think tank. Rural areas struggle especially, and New Mexico is a rural state.

Catron Allred, who oversees the Early Childhood Center at Santa Fe Community College, said the state is building something new from scratch and that she is not discouraged by the rollout. "I think it's hard because we're all building a new system," Allred said, adding: "I think New Mexico has identified how to get there, but building supply takes time and it takes a lot of funds."

New Mexico set a goal to create 12,000 new childcare spots in the program's first few years, and according to the state's Early Childhood Education & Care Department, it is more than halfway there. "We need to keep growing," Governor Michelle Lujan Grisham said. The Democrat signed the program into law and described a build-out that began well before it launched. "I started in 2019. The second I got elected we listened to the industry. We talked to parents. We created a trust fund. We started to build the infrastructure," Lujan Grisham said.

She acknowledged the pace of the rollout, saying it feels a bit like the state is "building and launching a rocket ship all at once." She called the program foundational. "I think childcare is a game changer for every single thing that a state ought to deliver for its families," Lujan Grisham said.

Money is the other constraint. Much of the program's almost $600 million budget comes from New Mexico's oil and gas revenue. Even so, there is concern that it is projected to be tens of millions of dollars over budget this year.

Steve Lanier, a Republican state senator who unsuccessfully sued to stop the governor's universal childcare initiative, said the current trajectory does not look sustainable. "It doesn't look like it's going to be sustainable where we're at right now," Lanier said. He said the price tag is why he supports childcare assistance only for some families, while noting that his daughter, a schoolteacher with two young boys, receives free childcare. "So from a personal view, I see where it is needed. I mean, that helps her and her family immensely. But somebody that's making 450,000, 600,000, do they really need that assistance?" Lanier said.

Allred framed universal early childhood differently. "I think it's a mentality of early childhood is a public good. Nobody questions a wealthy family sending their kids to public school. It should be the same for early childhood," Allred said.

Parents waiting for slots describe the gap between the program and its rollout. Lilia Doyle, who is raising her 2-year-old son in Santa Fe, said she believes in the program but not in its rollout. "I have great belief in this program, but I think the rollout needs a lot of help," Doyle said. When NPR first spoke with her, she was on six different waitlists, and Doyle said she is selective when it comes to childcare but still thinks it should be easier: "I'm 97th on a multi-hundred-person waitlist. I'm 75th on something. And some I'm not even getting calls back."

When NPR checked back with Doyle, she had secured a childcare spot for her son, but it does not start until August of next year.
