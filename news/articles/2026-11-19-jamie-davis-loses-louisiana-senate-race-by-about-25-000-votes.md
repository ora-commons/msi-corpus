## Turnout in Louisiana rose eight points from four years earlier

Jamie Davis lost his bid for the U.S. Senate in Louisiana to Republican U.S. Rep. Julia Letlow, 49.1% to 50.9%, The Guardian reported. Davis was about 25,000 votes short in a state that cast about 1.5 million votes. Louisiana has not elected a Democrat to the Senate since 2008.

Democrats had officially secured 56 Senate seats after Mary Peltola’s victory in Alaska. A Davis victory would have raised the total to 57. Davis said the margin pained him, but he said the larger frustration was that some people did not recognize how close the race had been.

“Truthfully, I mean, it kind of stings to come close, but what hurts more than anything else is the fact that, still today, people don’t realize and know what we did,” Davis said. He estimated the margin at 1.1 or 1.2 points.

Across the South, other results also drew Democratic scrutiny. In Alabama, Barry Moore defeated Everett Wess by nine points, a margin The Guardian contrasted with Donald Trump’s 30-point victory in 2024. In a Florida special election, Angie Nixon came within three points of unseating Republican incumbent Ashley Moody. The Guardian said Nixon’s progressive politics and opposition to the Israeli government appeared poorly matched to Florida’s conservative electorate.

Democrats across the South questioned which states might have been winnable had the national party provided more support, and many focused on Louisiana. The Guardian said that, unlike Florida, Louisiana was not an expensive media market and that Louisiana did not have the depth of partisan political identification to be overcome in a place like Alabama.

Turnout nationwide ran about three points higher than four years ago. Louisiana’s turnout ran eight points higher, in line with competitive U.S. Senate elections in Michigan and Maine.

Republican incumbent Bill Cassidy and Trump had long been at odds. Cassidy voted for Trump’s impeachment and asked him not to run in 2024, while Trump targeted Cassidy in the Louisiana Republican primary and endorsed Letlow. The Callais decision from the US supreme court originated in Louisiana and prompted the state to postpone primary elections and set off redistricting battles this year.

Combined with Cassidy’s third-place finish in the Republican primary, Davis said those events should have alerted Democratic fundraisers and operatives to the potential effect on Black voters. “When it became open, that’s the time when Republicans and Democrats and independents all should have been on notice that there’s an open seat in the Senate,” Davis said. “And not just automatically think that the seat is going to turn to be Republican.”

Letlow raised and spent almost twice as much as Davis, according to spending totals The Guardian described as about $13 million so far. That total was low by historic standards in Louisiana and nationally this cycle. Per resident, Letlow spent about $1.44 and Davis about $0.82.

For comparison, Troy Jackson raised and spent about $25.2 million in Maine, a state with about 1.4 million residents, to unseat Susan Collins—about $18 for every Mainer. Abdul El-Sayed spent about $5 per person in Michigan, buoyed by fierce national attention. Roy Cooper spent about $3.66 per person in North Carolina’s blowout race.

Davis said he was hurt by a last-minute campaign advertisement that used AI to depict him slowly walking toward the camera while a narrator falsely described his positions on abortion, gun rights, crime and immigration. “I did not have enough campaign funds in my account to answer that ad,” he said, adding that he regretted allowing it and vowed never to let it happen again if he ran for statewide office.

Davis stopped short of saying the advertisement alone decided the race. “I’m not saying that single-handed thing ended the election for me, but I will say not being able to answer it didn’t help me at all,” he said. “But I also say that my dad always told me that you cannot depend on somebody to fight your fight for you. You can’t depend on somebody to plant a seed and you harvest it. If you plant the seed, you should harvest it.”

A photograph published by The Guardian showed Davis in Baton Rouge on election night. A political unknown a year earlier, Davis lived on acreage between Red Gum and Ridgecrest, about 10 miles from the Mississippi River, in black dirt, row-crop, river-bottom country north of New Orleans. He farms corn, soy, sorghum and cotton.

“I am that moderate Democrat that comes from real rural Louisiana,” Davis said. “That rural Louisiana part that most of the Democrats felt like, hey, they done forgot about us. We’re Democrats too. We don’t live in an urban area, but we still get up and go to work in the morning, we still farm, we still raise chickens, we still do country things like old-school Democrats used to do.”
