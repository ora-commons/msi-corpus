## Seattle closure plan draws resistance after two earlier reversals

Seattle Public Schools is facing renewed opposition to a proposal to shutter nearly a dozen campuses, with the district back in the same financial bind. State auditors warned nearly four decades ago that the district needed to close several low-enrollment campuses to avert a deepening budget crisis. The recommendations drew fierce neighborhood resistance, and Seattle officials set them aside.

Thirty-four years later, in 2024, the district's then-superintendent proposed shuttering up to a fifth of its schools because there were not enough students to fill them. The district scrapped that plan after community resistance.

Seattle's repeated reversals fit a broader pattern across U.S. school districts, where leaders have largely hesitated to downsize despite shrinking student populations.

Thomas Dee and Jaymes Pyne, Stanford University researchers, found that public school enrollment has fallen by more than 1.4 million students since the pandemic. The decline is primarily due to declining birth rates and expanding school choice. Their study found one out of every 4 U.S. public schools has lost 15% or more of its student population since 2019. Seattle's enrollment has fallen roughly 8% since 2019 to below 49,000 students.

Despite the decline, the overall number of schools in operation remains virtually unchanged, and teacher headcounts have grown by 1% nationwide. Dee said, “School districts have been playing Hamlet on this for a while now and dithering on this question,” adding, “We’ve never had more teachers per student in our nation’s history.”

The budget troubles can be attributed, at least in part, to more than $190 billion in federal pandemic relief aid. That one-time cash allowed superintendents to maintain staffing levels even as student bodies shrank. With the federal funds exhausted, many districts now confront structural deficits. Disability diagnoses are rising and costly, and many students have not caught up academically after the pandemic.

Research from Arkansas, California, Chicago and elsewhere suggests closing a school yields only modest and temporary budget relief. Liza Rankin, a Seattle school board member since 2019, said, “The conversation really should be about the educational experience: How do we better serve the students with the resources we have left?” She noted that employee pay and benefits account for roughly 80% of a district's budget and said, “Closing schools doesn’t save much money to begin with. Payroll is where the big money is.”

District leaders have often preferred one-time reserves or small administrative cuts. In San Francisco, where enrollment fell 10% since 2019, a $100 million-plus deficit prompted a late-2024 proposal to shutter more than a dozen schools. That plan was shelved following the superintendent's resignation.

Earlier this year, a new superintendent reached a $183 million contract with the United Educators of San Francisco, or UESF. The district's most recent budget projects that it will close its structural deficit next school year, mostly by relying on early retirements and cuts to central office to avert closures. The current budget also projects a $26 million deficit by the following year.

Labor leaders insist any panic is unwarranted. A UESF spokesperson argued that the district's substantial cash reserves could stabilize the budget. The district said its rainy day fund stood at nearly $139 million last year, though it expects to draw down its reserves to just $52 million over the next two years. Jason Willis, a school finance expert with research group WestEd who trains K-12 budget officers, warned against tapping rainy day funds for ongoing expenses like salary: “That could buy them more time, maybe provide another year of planning, but it doesn’t come close to resolving the structural issues that San Francisco has.”

The consequences may reach students. Research shows that closing schools can set students back academically, while teacher layoffs may be even more disruptive to student learning. Dee warned that if local leadership fails to act, other political actors might step in.

State takeovers of school districts have been rising, with at least 21 happening since 2023, according to a tally from nonprofit news outlet Chalkbeat. Under state takeovers, school board members and superintendents often have much less flexibility to determine how to cut budgets or whom to lay off. Dee said, “If they don’t manage their finances well, there’s always the specter of some really disruptive state oversight.”
