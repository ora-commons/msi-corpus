## AR-15 popularity drives the challenge to state bans

The U.S. Supreme Court will hear arguments on Dec. 2 in a case challenging Connecticut's and Cook County's bans on AR-15-style semiautomatic rifles. The question is whether the Second Amendment guarantees a right to possess the AR-15, or rifles like it. Gun-rights challengers representing people who want to own AR-15s in Connecticut and Cook County, Ill., are relying on the standard the court set out in District of Columbia v. Heller, which bars governments from prohibiting weapons "in common use." Estimates put the number of AR-15s in civilian hands at 20 million to 30 million, giving the challengers a central argument that the rifle has become mainstream in American life.

How the court applies that language will determine whether Connecticut's post-Sandy Hook ban and Cook County's similar ban remain in force. The argument also tests how far the conservative six-justice majority's expansion of individual gun rights reaches: since 2022, the court has struck down New York state's strict rules for issuing concealed-weapons permits and issued two other major rulings broadening gun rights this year. The AR-15 case puts a semiautomatic rifle that gun makers built to be cheap and easy to manufacture before that majority.

The transformation began in the 1990s, when AR-15 makers were mocked at the National Rifle Association's annual convention. Many Vietnam veterans recalled malfunctions in battle with earlier military models, and the rifle's military look and plastic and aluminum parts put off sportsmen accustomed to polished wood and gleaming steel. Gun maker Randy Luth recalled, "We'd have NRA members walk by and give us the finger." By the time the Supreme Court took up the rifle's constitutional status, AR-15s were a mainstay at gun shows and shooting ranges and a symbol of resistance for their owners.

The rifle traces to Eugene Stoner, a little-known designer who created it for the U.S. military in the 1950s at ArmaLite, a subsidiary of an aircraft company. The military adopted the design as the M16 in the 1960s as the Vietnam War ramped up, and that version could fire fully automatic. A semiautomatic version, which fires a round each time the shooter pulls the trigger, first reached civilians in the 1960s, but sales were weak. In the late 1980s, police chiefs worried about gang members armed with AR-15s and joined gun-control groups in advocating "assault weapons" bans. "There's no need for it," said former Arizona Sen. Barry Goldwater, the founder of modern American conservatism, of the rifle at the time.

Americans owned about 400,000 AR-15s when Bill Clinton signed the Federal Assault Weapons Ban in 1994. Clinton said, "We will finally ban these assault weapons from our streets that have no purpose other than to kill." In some respects, the ban backfired: it sparked new interest, and manufacturers soon figured out how to make AR-15s without prohibited features. Post-ban rifles had a different look and were sold with 10-round magazines instead of 30-round magazines, but they still fired as fast as a shooter could pull the trigger. By 1999, gun makers were producing more AR-15s than ever before. Mark Westrom, who revived the ArmaLite brand to sell the rifles, said, "If you want to sell something to an American, just tell him that he can't have it."

A 2004 report for the Justice Department found that the federal ban's "effects on gun violence are likely to be small at best and perhaps too small for reliable measurement." The authors warned that if the ban were lifted, it was "possible, and perhaps probable" that the guns would "eventually be used to commit mass murder." That year, the Republican-led Congress let the ban lapse without much debate. After the Sept. 11 attacks, returning veterans from Iraq and Afghanistan wanted civilian versions of the M16, and American consumers wanted them too. Private-equity firms entered the market with sophisticated marketing campaigns, and new gun conglomerates ramped up production. Calls for new gun control after mass shootings and the issue's prominence in U.S. presidential elections also created surges in demand.

The AR-15 was far more profitable than traditional rifles, and gun owners found it easy to shoot and modify. They called it "Legos for adults." Gun maker Randy Luth, who now serves on the NRA's board, said, "I call it the Corvette of rifles because it's cool looking, you can modify it, accessorize it: It's the All-American rifle." Some Republican politicians embrace the gun, wearing AR-15 pins and posing with the rifle in Christmas photos.

Gun-rights supporters point out that rifles of all kinds account for only a tiny percentage of the nation's gun homicides, and that handguns are responsible for many more deaths. Opponents answer that AR-15s allow gunmen with minimal firearm training to inflict a lot of damage quickly, and the rifles have been used in some of the deadliest mass shootings.

Connecticut enacted its AR-15 ban after the 2012 shooting at Sandy Hook Elementary School in Newtown, Conn. A 20-year-old armed with an AR-15 and two handguns killed 20 first-graders, six educators and himself. David and Francine Wheeler, who lost their 6-year-old son Ben and had never before been involved in politics, gave testimony that persuaded Connecticut's legislature to tighten its assault-weapons ban. "The liberty of any person to own a military-style assault weapon and a high-capacity magazine and keep them in their home is second to the right of my son to his life," Wheeler said as he sat before state lawmakers.

Congress did not act on assault weapons after Sandy Hook, but states passed bans of their own. About a third of Americans now live in places that prohibit AR-15-style rifles, including about a dozen states and the District of Columbia. The political movement behind those laws also produced a bonanza for AR-15 makers as customers rushed to buy rifles before bans took effect: American gun makers made 1.9 million AR rifles for sale in the U.S. in the year after Sandy Hook, the most they had ever manufactured in a single year.

Briefs filed in the Supreme Court also contain first-person accounts of mass shootings at Sandy Hook, Las Vegas, Parkland, Fla., and Uvalde, Texas. One brief quotes a doctor in Uvalde who testified that two children murdered in the school shooting were mangled so badly by an AR-15's high-velocity bullets that the only way to identify them was the bloody cartoon clothing they were wearing.

The Supreme Court's own jurisprudence changed during the rifle's rise. In District of Columbia v. Heller in 2008, a 5-4 court declared for the first time that individuals have a right to own guns unconnected with military service. The ruling dealt only with handguns kept in the home for self-defense, and the court said legislatures can ban "dangerous" and "unusual" weapons but not weapons "in common use." For the next 14 years, the court said little more about the constitutional status of military-style weapons or the right to carry guns outside the home.

Beginning in 2022, the court's new six-justice conservative majority sent a different message, striking down New York state's strict rules for issuing concealed-weapons permits and, earlier this year, issuing two other major rulings broadening gun rights. Scholars and advocates fiercely debate the meaning of the common-use test, and the disagreement now centers on whether a weapon's popularity can control its constitutional status.

Connecticut's defense is led by Elizabeth Prelogar, the U.S. Solicitor General under former President Joe Biden. Prelogar's side argues that accepting a popularity-based reading would mean Congress had the authority to enact the 1994 assault-weapons ban when AR-15s were relatively uncommon, but lost that authority once the ban expired and the weapons became widespread. "That result is nonsensical," Prelogar wrote. The challengers' lawyers are leaning on a literal reading of "common use" and have filled their legal brief with statistics about the tens of millions of AR-15s in circulation. "The AR-15," they wrote, "is the paradigmatic American rifle."

The court will decide whether the AR-15 receives constitutional protection under the "common use" standard and whether the Connecticut and Cook County bans survive. The case comes after more than a decade in which a rifle once derided in the 1990s reached the point where its popularity in American life is itself the argument before the justices.
