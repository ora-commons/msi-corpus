## Texas and Iowa Senate winners echoed the cost-of-living argument

Zohran Mamdani wasn't running in the midterm elections. In races across the country, however, the New York City mayor's influence could be seen, as Democrats adopted a laser focus on affordability and downed establishment opponents, The Guardian reported Monday. From Texas to Kansas, Democrats ran by pointing out the high cost of living and, frequently, by linking that to billionaires. It was similar messaging to the one Mamdani used during his upset victory in New York's mayoral election last year. It made sense for Mamdani, a self-described democratic socialist, but even centrist candidates in traditionally Republican states borrowed from his playbook.

*Zohran Mamdani speaks at an election-night party for Democrats last week. Photograph: Jeenah Moon/Reuters.*

James Talarico, the moderate Democrat who won a Senate seat in Texas, put the argument in top-versus-bottom terms on his campaign page. "The biggest divide in this country is not left vs. right. It's top vs. bottom. Billionaires want us looking left and right at each other instead of looking up at them," Talarico wrote. Josh Turek, who was elected to the US Senate seat in Iowa, had a similar message: "Life is harder for working-class people because Washington is riddled with corrupt politicians who are out of touch and focused on enriching themselves."

The messaging tracked with the results. As of Thursday, Democrats led Republicans by 255 to 168 seats in the House of Representatives and have flipped eight seats in the Senate — votes are continuing to be counted in some races. In Kansas, Adam Hamilton's campaign centered on affordability, and at campaign rallies and in interviews Hamilton linked the war in Iran to the higher costs Kansans faced. "There's a whole lot of people in Kansas, hundreds of thousands of people are living paycheck to paycheck, and this is not OK," Hamilton said in his victory speech, blaming "Decisions made in Washington that are hurting people in our own state who are struggling already."

In New York City, Mamdani's influence was direct: Darializa Avila Chevalier, Brad Lander and Claire Valdez all won their primaries in safely Democratic districts after being endorsed by the mayor, and all three won election to the House on 3 November. Mamdani's campaign was renowned for using social media to communicate — something the mayor continues to do — and Joseph Geevarghese, executive director of Our Revolution, a progressive organization, saw that used by others. "He had an influence on the medium. He was able to communicate his affordability message using new media, through his social media platform and creative videos, and he was able to do creative campaigning to move that message online," Geevarghese said. Mamdani's campaign website carried a simple line: "New York is too expensive. Zohran will lower costs and make life easier."

How much credit Mamdani deserves was itself a subject of debate. Geevarghese said he saw a Mamdani-inspired movement in the midterms. "He was able to recognize that the issue was about populism, not partisanship. He understood that people were voting for Trump because Trump said he would make their lives economically better. And Mamdani was then able to effectively campaign on that issue," Geevarghese said. Geevarghese said Mamdani helped define a central issue of the midterm — affordability — and that, along with corruption, was the central narrative of the midterm election.

Trip Yang, a New York-based Democratic strategist, said Mamdani wasn't the only cause of the affordability message. "Mamdani's race got the most attention of any race, obviously, so his campaign popularized it, but campaigns by and large were talking about affordability," Yang said, noting that Donald Trump had argued affordability himself in 2024, promising that egg and gas prices would go down. "If you view it holistically, he had a large impact that was very positive for Democrats," Yang said.

Yang said that a year ago national Republicans were saying they would tie Mamdani, "this new democratic socialist mayor of the nation's largest city," to national Democratic candidates everywhere and make them all defend his policies. The plan failed, Yang said: Donald Trump met with Mamdani many times this past year, which Yang said prevents Republicans nationally from really tying Mamdani to Democratic candidates. "Mamdani's approval rating is pretty good. He's actually one of the more popular national Democrats," Yang said. "They had planned to use Mamdani as a boogeyman, as a liability for national Democrats. They could not," Yang said.

Richard Florida, a distinguished professor at Vanderbilt University who studies US class structure, said Mamdani had a "big effect" on the demographic shifts that moved Americans toward Democrats in November. Florida's work has focused on how class structure has changed in the US, developing beyond the traditional blue-collar and white-collar, working-class and middle-class framework. He divides most US workers into three categories: the creative class, meaning people in knowledge-based jobs such as educators, doctors and lawyers and managers; the service class, which includes carers or those with jobs in retail stores and restaurants; and the working class, which he defines as factory and construction workers.

The working class leans Republican and the creative class Democrat, Florida said. The service class, he said, is more likely to swing between parties. Those voters were enticed by Trump's economic populism as they struggled with low pay and economic uncertainty, but Mamdani managed to win some of those Americans in New York. Democrats have tried before to build a coalition of the creative class and the service class, Florida said, but no one managed to build such a "robust" coalition as Mamdani. "He built a coalition around affordability that brought these two groups, which most people never thought would go together, into alignment," Florida said, by leaning into high prices and inequality. "And I think the Democrats kind of did that in the midterms," Florida said. "I think they saw that the issue of affordability resonated, and they saw that charismatic young, vital candidates resonated."

Since elected, Mamdani has been an almost inescapable presence in New York, repeatedly touting his administration's successes, in areas like winning money for delivery workers and managing to implement his much-publicized rent freeze. Asked in a CNN interview in the wake of the midterms what Democrats should prioritize, Mamdani pointed to costs. Democrats need "Change that is urgent, it has to be change that speaks to the crisis in working-class Americans' lives. And it has to be change that can be measured in the day-to-day lives of those same Americans, something that isn't just an ideal or a value, but something that is also what they're struggling to afford every single day, be it rent, be it childcare, be it groceries," Mamdani said.

Geevarghese said the same pressure now falls on Democrats in Washington. "Democrats have to deliver on what they ran on, and they ran on issues of affordability. They committed to pursue policies that would raise the standard of living for everyday Americans. And they committed to taking action on corruption, to expose how Donald Trump and his contributors, his campaign donors, have benefited off the presidency at the expense of the American people," Geevarghese said. Democrats, he added, have to "deliver legislation and put it on the president's desk and force the question: 'Are you with the American people who want lower costs, or are you with your oligarch donors?'"
