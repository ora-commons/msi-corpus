## New ACA identity checks slow enrollment as groups seek deadline extension

**Subtype:** fact

- The Hispanic Leadership Fund, Americans for a Balanced Budget and health insurance broker firms asked CMS on Friday to extend ACA open enrollment from Dec. 15 to Dec. 31.
- CMS canceled 760,000 ACA enrollments after issuers determined the enrollments were unauthorized.
- Dee Stewart said new identity-verification requirements left applicants waiting in queues, with some abandoning enrollment.
- Emily Trevino said completing an ACA enrollment application can now take an hour, compared with 15 minutes before the new checks.
- The Texas Department of Insurance said 375,363 of the 760,000 people whose ACA enrollments were canceled lived in Texas.

## CMS says it canceled 760,000 ACA enrollments as unauthorized

The Hispanic Leadership Fund, Americans for a Balanced Budget and leaders of 11 health insurance broker firms sent letters to the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services on Friday asking the agency to extend ACA open enrollment from Dec. 15 to Dec. 31. The groups said the enhanced identity-verification process introduced after 760,000 enrollments were canceled is adding processing time, causing online applications to time out, forcing applicants to start over and prompting some to abandon enrollment.

Customers seeking coverage that starts Jan. 1 have until Dec. 15 to renew or enroll in an ACA health plan. A separate final deadline of Jan. 15 applies to enrollment for coverage beginning Feb. 1.

CMS said it identified 1.18 million potentially unauthorized enrollments that were all agent- or broker-assisted, lacked a verified Social Security Number or immigration document number, and had premiums fully covered by advance premium tax-credit payments. The agency had insurers review whether enrollees had filed claims or contacted their insurers, then contacted each enrollee multiple times and gave the person 30 days to respond. CMS said it had used the same verification method for unauthorized enrollment since 2019. After applying those criteria, issuers determined that 760,000 enrollments were unauthorized, and CMS canceled them. Fewer than 1% of the people removed have asked for reinstatement.

Two months earlier, Vice President JD Vance and CMS Administrator Dr. Mehmet Oz announced that the government had flagged 315,000 ACA health care plans covering 760,000 people as potentially fraudulent. Officials said some enrollees were renewed or signed up without their consent, resulting in taxpayer-funded subsidies paid to insurers and payments from insurers to brokers. Vance said two months ago, “We’re actually making sure that the people receiving Obamacare subsidies are actually entitled to receive them.” Few specifics had been offered since the announcement.

Dee Stewart, president of Americans for a Balanced Budget, an advocacy organization focused on fiscal responsibility and government accountability, said, “We are hearing significant concern from eligible Americans trying to enroll and encountering technical glitches and administrative obstacles that prevent them from completing enrollment.” Stewart also wrote that the requirements were “adding significant processing time and leaving applicants waiting in queues, with some abandoning the process altogether.”

Mario H. Lopez, president of the Hispanic Leadership Fund, said, “With all the changes that they’ve made, people are having a hard time to get re-signed up.” He added, “The easy solution is to just give people more time.”

A Nov. 20 letter signed by leaders of 11 health insurance broker firms said, “Our teams are all ready to help, but new ID proofing requirements are making it nearly impossible for some people to finish their applications. A link expires, or the screen says it can’t confirm who they are, and neither of us can see why.” The brokers asked CMS to give families more time to enroll and protect their coverage while they verify that they still qualify for help paying for it.

A CMS spokesperson said the agency would not speculate on potential future actions. The spokesperson said enrollment assistance remained available through HealthCare.gov, a call center and local enrollment partners. “The agency is focused on helping eligible Americans complete enrollment and obtain coverage,” the spokesperson said. CMS did not say whether it had taken action against brokers who enrolled people erroneously.

For Houston broker Emily Trevino, CEO of Wise Partners, the new system has changed the time required to complete an application. “Completing the application used to take 15 minutes but can now take an hour,” Trevino said. Brokers must repeatedly verify an enrollee’s identity and consent to the broker processing the application. “To the client, it can feel like saying yes three times, and they become frustrated with the constant authorization,” she said.

The Texas Department of Insurance said about half of the 760,000 enrollees whose plans were canceled were from Texas. The agency provided no details about whether the 375,363 Texas ACA enrollees were mistakenly enrolled automatically or whether fraud had occurred.

Texas has become heavily reliant on brokers to enroll people in ACA coverage. More than 4 million Texans signed up for an ACA plan a year ago because they did not have access to health insurance through a workplace. A September study by the Episcopal Health Foundation found that four out of five Texans applied for ACA health coverage through a broker, who are paid by the insurance companies.

The Episcopal Health Foundation reported that broker-assisted enrollment rose from 53% of all ACA signups in Texas in 2020 to 83% in 2026. “Brokers have become the backbone of Texas Marketplace enrollment,” the study found. “In practice, most individual coverage in Texas now runs through this one channel,” the report concluded.

Publicly funded health-insurance navigators have also lost staff as enrollment assistance has become more important. At the ACA’s 2014 debut, nearly $11 million was spent hiring workers stationed with nonprofit organizations to help people sign up. That grant funding still exists, but it has fallen sharply after the federal government slashed the program a year ago.

Dr. Ann Barnes, president and CEO of the Episcopal Health Foundation, which commissioned the study on brokers’ impact on the ACA, said, “This report shows how important enrollment assistance is for Texans seeking health insurance coverage, but also where gaps exist, particularly for rural and low-income communities.” With a new Democratic government about to take over in Texas, renewed discussion has focused on increasing funding for nonprofit navigators who help people sign up if they earn too much to qualify for Medicaid but lack workplace insurance, but no bill had been filed on the issue.

Oz said 35% of the people cut from the ACA rolls had never used their health plan at a doctor’s office. “That just is not possible,” Oz said. “So we know there is fraud.” Health experts and brokers said the absence of medical use is not an immediate sign of fraud because younger people tend to use coverage less and people with high-deductible plans may use it infrequently. “It is not unusual for people not to use their insurance, especially with high deductibles,” Trevino said.

Trevino also doubted that 300,000 Texans were defrauding the government and said she believed the cancellations were primarily caused by data matching issues, mainly missing Social Security numbers from the ACA app. She said Social Security numbers had never been required on the app up until a year ago. Trevino noted that many ACA enrollees automatically renew their coverage after speaking with an insurance agent and taking no further action, which could explain a missing Social Security number on a 2026 plan.

Trevino and Austin insurance broker Rachel Elsberry believe brokers could have helped investigate suspected “ghost” accounts if CMS had contacted them directly. “I would like to see more emphasis on investigating the people responsible for unauthorized enrollments rather than continually adding administrative requirements for legitimate agents and consumers,” Elsberry said. “We should be able to protect consumers from fraud without making it harder for them to obtain and maintain health insurance.” It remains unclear what role, if any, Texans’ reliance on brokers played in the 2026 removals. ACA customers do not have to use brokers and can enroll themselves on HealthCare.gov.

Texans are facing their second consecutive year of double-digit ACA price increases. Average premiums this year will rise about 13%, following a 35% average increase last year after pandemic-era subsidies were allowed to expire. Income-based subsidies remain available, but not at the levels seen during the COVID-19 years.

Austin customer Kathleen Cullather said keeping her current $1,200-a-month ACA plan for herself, her husband and their newborn would cost more than $2,000 a month next year. “That is not feasible. I’m still paying hospital bills from earlier this year,” she said. After meeting with Elsberry, Cullather was considering a plan with a health savings account. The account would let her save pre-tax dollars for care and lower the family’s household income, qualifying them for larger subsidies. The plan would cost a little more than $600 a month. “It’s an option that is unreal to me, like it’s too good to be true,” she said.

The Texas Tribune describes itself as a nonprofit, nonpartisan news organization funded in part by donations from members, foundations and corporate sponsors, and says financial supporters do not play a role in its journalism. CMS has not said whether it will grant the requested two-week extension.
