## Security experts say single arrest likely reveals broader pattern

Alfozan, a Saudi national, was charged Tuesday after investigators accused him of placing the recording device in a company conference room, according to the Journal. In announcing the arrest, Patel said the FBI considered protecting AI technology secrets among its highest counterintelligence priorities.

Alfozan's attorney told the Journal the allegations were "wholly out of character" and emphasized that his client was charged only with violating a federal recording statute. The attorney said Alfozan was not charged with stealing trade secrets or with acting as a foreign agent.

The OpenAI case fits a pattern that corporate security specialists say extends beyond any single incident. "Catching one guy means there are half a dozen out there," said Randy Watt, president of leadership consulting firm SRW, which conducts security and threat assessments for companies. Watt said malicious actors may be after technical and strategic information, but also personal secrets that can be used to exploit or blackmail individuals.

OpenAI has worked to raise employee awareness for years. In training sessions, the company has warned employees that outsiders — including competitors and state-sponsored actors — could try to obtain company information through cash offers, eavesdropping in cafes, or attempts at seduction, the Journal reported, citing people familiar with the matter. Employees at Anthropic and Google have received similar warnings.

In one training scenario described to the Journal, a stranger approached an OpenAI employee at a San Francisco playground, befriended the employee and the employee's child over repeat visits, and used small talk to ask about the employee's work. The stranger was a spy, according to the company.

Corporate training materials instruct OpenAI employees to avoid wearing company-branded clothing outside the office, to use screen protectors on public transit, and to wipe down whiteboards when finished in case kitchen and cleaning staff are compromised, the Journal reported. Employees receive recurring reminders of these precautions, including via Slack.

OpenAI has also briefed workers on so-called honey-pot operations, in which agents use the offer of sex or romance to get close to a target. One anecdote OpenAI shares: foreign intelligence operatives sent a blonde woman with champagne to a U.S. diplomat's hotel room in Russia; after he declined, they sent a redheaded woman, then a young man. The implication, according to trainers, is that the same tactics could be aimed at OpenAI staff.

The industry's rapid expansion has multiplied the potential entry points. "AI companies are scaling their workforces at unprecedented speed. The question is whether their ability to manage insider risk is scaling at the same pace," said Aviv Nahum, chief executive of Above Security, a startup that uses artificial intelligence to help companies detect insider risks. Nahum said the traditional approach relies on verifying identity and then trusting that what an employee does afterward is legitimate, which can miss threats that develop over time.

The concentration of value in a handful of AI companies has made them larger targets, said Phil Venables, a partner at Ballistic Ventures and former chief information security officer for Google Cloud. OpenAI and Anthropic are each expected to seek valuations of more than $1 trillion in their initial public offerings, the Journal reported. "The resources and money required to train foundation models makes them even more valuable intellectual property," Venables said.

Recent court cases underscore the risks. In 2022, a former Twitter employee, Ahmad Abouammo, was found guilty of spying for Saudi Arabia and sentenced to 42 months in prison; prosecutors said he passed along private user data tied to critics of the Saudi government in exchange for hundreds of thousands of dollars. In January, a former Google software engineer was convicted of stealing thousands of pages of confidential information about the company's AI work and providing it to China.

The companies themselves employ large numbers of foreign nationals, with China supplying a substantial share of the world's leading AI researchers. Trump administration officials have identified employment of Chinese nationals by AI and technology firms as a national security vulnerability, alleging that some share secrets with Beijing.

Insider-threat concerns have also surfaced in the industry's routine job mobility. OpenAI and Apple are engaged in litigation over claims that poached employees took confidential files with them to their new jobs. Elon Musk's xAI filed a similar lawsuit against an engineer who joined OpenAI.

An OpenAI spokesperson said the company maintains a security team with experience in physical security, insider risk, information security and related disciplines.
