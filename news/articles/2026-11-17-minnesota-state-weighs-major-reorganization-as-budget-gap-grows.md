## Trustees seek $43 million over two years for struggling institutions

The Minnesota State board will vote this week to ask the Legislature for $43 million over two years to assist financially challenged institutions, along with $25 million for demolitions and $15 million for systemwide reorganization. The 26-college and seven-university system, the state's largest higher education system, faces a current $31.5 million budget gap, and projections suggest the shortfall could reach nearly $100 million by summer 2028. Officials are weighing what would be the system's most substantial reorganization in three decades, including possible campus mergers or closures and centralizing services such as IT or financial aid, with initial proposals expected as soon as January.

While addressing employees at Century College last month, Chancellor Scott Olson said, “We can see it coming,” and warned that without action Minnesota State “will find itself in insolvency.” Minnesota State officials attribute the shortfall to insufficient state funding, declining enrollment and inflation. A Minnesota law says the Legislature intends to provide two-thirds of funding for public higher-education institutions, but Minnesota State has not received that share since 2002. Without that money, system officials have relied on tuition hikes that risk making schools unaffordable. In an interview with MinnPost, Olson said the system has no option but to find a solution for its 270,000 students, about two in three resident undergraduates in the state, adding, “Minnesota fails if Minnesota State fails.” He joined the system in 2003 and became chancellor in 2023, and said targeted consolidations and mergers in the last decade avoided worst-case scenarios such as school closures.

Last month, trustees approved moving up to $25 million from most schools, especially community colleges, to financially struggling four-year schools, including St. Cloud State and Bemidji State. Officials considered the decision relatively unprecedented. Olson said those two universities are not the lone drivers of the system's financial problems but are the “canaries that are coughing the most in the mine.” College presidents, students and some system leaders said shifting money away from other campuses could harm otherwise stable institutions. “I never, in 38 years in the Legislature, would have thought we would have had to have done that,” said Gene Pelowski, a former Democratic state lawmaker and Minnesota State trustee.

Trustees also approved pulling millions from school equipment budgets to raze 12 buildings, as officials lack the upfront cash to demolish structures even when demolition would save money. The action came as the system's physical footprint remained unchanged despite enrollment measured by credit hours falling nearly 18% over a decade. The footprint spans nearly 500 football fields, while the bill for maintaining its buildings, including fixing boilers and roofs, has more than doubled in 10 years to $2 billion.

The Legislature created Minnesota State in 1991 by merging public technical colleges, community colleges and four-year universities, a move that was controversial even among some lawmakers. A 2000 audit found the merger improved educational options, including students' ability to transfer between schools, but it was unclear whether the changes saved money. After a 2010 audit documented outsized administrative spending, system leaders pledged to cut dozens of central-office jobs and consider consolidating administrative services. The audit said some system leaders suggested merging entire campuses, but more than half of campus presidents agreed leadership had not thoroughly considered that option. Earlier efforts largely stalled because of conflicts over leadership and faculty outcry over proposals they said would unnecessarily cut academics.

A 2016 internal report compiled by a workgroup on long-term sustainability projected a shortfall of $66 million to $475 million in 10 years if Minnesota State did not make massive structural changes. A group of administrators, with help from faculty and students, concluded that state funding and tuition would not cover future costs. The report recommended reorganizing the system, sharing administrators across schools and shrinking an “overbuilt” physical footprint. It also suggested standardizing curriculum, unifying metro-area schools in a way that required faculty travel between campuses, and making changes to faculty unions that professors saw as attempts to weaken organized labor. “We cannot and must not wait until 2025 to begin to make changes or we will only compound the challenges of achieving financial sustainability,” the report said, adding, “We must act now.”

Those proposals drew immediate resistance. “Overall, the report implies that faculty and staff are worker bees on an assembly line making widgets (in this case, making rubber-stamped students) in more cost-effective ways,” Richard Jewell, a now-retired professor at Inver Hills Community College, wrote in response to the 2016 report. When the 2016 report was released, the Minnesota State College Faculty union called the projected crisis a “manufactured crisis that, while possible, is extremely unlikely” and said, “It is simply an excuse to implement an agenda not directly related to financial sustainability.”

Employee support had already been low before the report. In 2014, unions voted no confidence in then-Chancellor Steven Rosenstone after he quietly signed a $2 million consulting contract to assess the system for a separate potential overhaul, and faculty refused to participate until mediators stepped in. At the time of the 2016 report, 19 of 31 institutions were under monitoring for poor financial health, compared with eight today. Then-Chief Financial Officer Laura King told lawmakers in 2017 that at least 200 employees had been laid off.

Since then, Minnesota State has combined administrators at two Anoka-area colleges, is merging two more colleges in the Twin Cities east metro and merged five far-northern community colleges into one school in 2022. The changes did not close the widening gap, and college and university presidents report years of layoffs, hiring freezes, spending down reserve funds and academic cuts. Board of Trustees Chair Dawn Erlandson, who joined in 2012, said at a meeting last month, “I feel like we’ve been cutting – death by 1,000 cuts – since I joined the board.”

Four-year faculty union president Alec Sonsteby said administrators' struggle to work with unions, students and staff a decade ago prevented wholesale change, but warned that a major overhaul would be difficult for a public system the size of Minnesota State. “I think it’s a really hard ship to steer,” said Sonsteby, who teaches library studies at Metro State University. He said obtaining broad buy-in is difficult whenever fundamentally large changes are proposed.

Faculty skepticism persists, especially over system spending and the quick timeline to share ideas that could have major implications for all schools. Many faculty and staff agree the status quo is not working, but some worry system leaders are moving too quickly and question whether the system has been transparent about what led to the budget problems. At an October board meeting, Jennifer Erwin, who represents system staff, said, “We are told there is no money, yet new costs continue to be introduced,” and, “We are told employees should trust the process, yet timelines continue to shrink.”

Kevin Lindstrom, president of the Minnesota State College Faculty union, said, “I've been in the system for 37 years, and 36 of those years there was a budget crisis.” He is unconvinced that consolidating campuses will save enough to make a dent in the current budget gap. The ongoing project to share administrators at Anoka Technical College and Anoka-Ramsey Community College will save $700,000 per year, and Lindstrom said he worries academics could be next on the chopping block, similar to 2016.

Unions also question how much the ongoing Workday technology overhaul has drained the system's finances. The $290 million project costs nearly $48 million more than originally approved and is several years behind schedule, according to a 2026 state audit. The four-year faculty union is urging Minnesota State to disclose how much Workday has affected the budget before it blames the financial problems on campuses. Olson has previously said the project's costs were anticipated.

Olson described the reorganization as a group effort involving dozens of faculty, staff and students. “When we give them an opportunity to debate, to challenge, to come up with alternative ideas, we get smarter,” he told MinnPost. He acknowledged that issues raised in the 2016 report should have been addressed earlier and said the report was “put on a shelf.” Tyler Treptow-Bowman, president of the administrative and service faculty union at four-year universities, said many faculty accept that systemwide changes are needed but that a decade of concern about layoffs and academic cuts has dragged morale “in the gutter.”

The Legislature has warned state-funded systems not to count on more money as Minnesota contends with its own budget pressures. For decades, the state spent a smaller share of its budget on higher education as other needs such as healthcare and K-12 schools took priority; in 2026, higher education received 6% of the state budget, compared with nearly 15% in the ’90s. Gene Pelowski said lawmakers and the governor are the “final arbiters” of Minnesota State's response. “This is a creature of the Legislature: The MinnState system, and the trustees,” he said. “Once the Legislature is in session, the trustees are not in control. The Legislature and the governor is.”
