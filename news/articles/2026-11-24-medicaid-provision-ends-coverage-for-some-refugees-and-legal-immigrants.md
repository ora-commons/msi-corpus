## Medicaid provision ends coverage for some refugees and legal immigrants

**Subtype:** fact

- PBS NewsHour reported that a Medicaid provision in President Donald Trump's signature tax and spending law has left some refugees, asylum recipients and survivors of human trafficking and domestic violence without coverage.
- The Congressional Budget Office estimated that 100,000 more immigrants will be uninsured by 2034.

## Refugee patients face out-of-pocket costs and more emergency-department visits, doctor says

PBS NewsHour's Geoff Bennett said hundreds of thousands of immigrants living illegally in the U.S. had recently lost Medicaid coverage under the provision. The report also noted that undocumented immigrants are not eligible for federally funded Medicaid coverage. Its detailed accounts involved refugees and other immigrants with legal status. The law is projected to reduce federal Medicaid spending by $900 billion over the next decade.

Al-Omar is among the 100,000 more immigrants the Congressional Budget Office estimates will be uninsured by 2034. KFF Health News reported that more than 280,000 immigrants were expected to lose Medicaid coverage in October alone.

Dr. Alan Bock, a physician at Lower Lights Health in Columbus, said many of his refugee patients are struggling to pay for medical visits out of pocket. He said he has had multiple patients whose Medicaid coverage was mistakenly cut. "To have so many patients suddenly lose access to health insurance, I mean, this is new," he said. Bock said the loss of coverage affects his work serving vulnerable families.

Bock said: "When these patients feel like they can no longer get care, they're just less likely to have controlled blood pressure, controlled diabetes. And so, in the short term, that can lead to complications. These patients will end up in the E.R. more." He also said patients who do not receive primary care or regular cholesterol medications face increased risk.

Hasan Al-Omar, 46, takes every opportunity to get outdoors near his apartment in Columbus. Last month, he lost Medicaid coverage and, with it, the caregivers who helped him leave home and live more independently. Through an interpreter, Al-Omar said: "If I'm not able to get back on Medicaid, what will happen to me? Who will help me with the bathroom, with eating, with drinking, with medical treatment? I can't afford to buy medical supplies or fix my wheelchair if it's broken, or get a new bed if it breaks. Medicaid helped me with all of those things too."

Al-Omar now relies entirely on his family for care. His wife works to pay the rent and bills, and his children are in school. Al-Omar said he recently had an accident because he "couldn't hold it anymore." He called his three sons home from school; they took him to the bathroom and gave him a bath. "If they don't return my Medicaid, death is better for me," he said.

In 2012, Al-Omar fled war in Syria. After more than a decade living in a tent in Lebanon, his family of seven entered the United States in 2024 through the U.S. Refugee Admissions Program. Through an interpreter, he said: "I said to myself that I will go there and live in safety and stability and dignity. But, right now, my life is at risk because there is no one to consistently take care of me."

Hawo Jimale, 79, has lived with fear since losing her insurance. A doctor's visit now costs up to $150 out of pocket. She relies on a community member to cover that cost and buy her blood-pressure medication. Jimale was originally from Somalia and lived in an Ethiopian refugee camp for 20 years before coming to the United States in 2024 through the U.S. Refugee Admissions Program. Through an interpreter, she said: "I am asking the government to give back what they used to give me, my Medicaid. They were the ones who brought us here as refugees."

Refugees in the United States face a backlog for permanent residency, with nearly 150,000 pending green-card applications. Alvare Tyunibyan has been waiting for her green card for more than a year. During that time, she was diagnosed with bone marrow cancer. Last month, she received the same letter as thousands of others saying her Medicaid coverage had been revoked. Through an interpreter, Tyunibyan said: "I was in shock. With such a disease and with my financial means, if my chemotherapy treatment stops, my doctors say my life will be shortened."

Tyunibyan entered the United States in 2024 through a program that accepts minority groups from former Soviet Union countries, including her home country of Armenia. She is able to continue blood transfusions for now with financial aid from her university hospital, but her chemotherapy has been postponed. Through an interpreter, she said: "I hope that the insurance will be restored, because I have no other opportunities. And there is hope in God. We hope that God will arrange everything for us."

In an email to PBS News, the White House pointed to two memos listing examples of Democrats expanding health care access to undocumented immigrants. The White House alleged that the Biden administration and some Democratic-led states had "abused their power." PBS NewsHour also reported that President Trump had repeatedly made false claims that undocumented immigrants exploit taxpayer-funded health care in large numbers, including Medicaid. Trump said: "But our country can't handle people that come into our country illegally, and they want to give them full health care benefits."

Former President Jimmy Carter said: "Ours is a nation of refugees, a nation of immigrants." PBS NewsHour said Carter's 1980 Refugee Act created a federal framework for refugees to access medical care. The report noted that in 1996, when Congress barred many legal immigrants from Medicaid for five years, it exempted refugees and asylums from that waiting period.

Steve Camarota, director of research at the Center for Immigration Studies, a think tank that supports lower immigration levels, said cutting Medicaid for legal immigrants is not cost-effective. He described the policy as: "This is a way of the administration, you might, say nibbling at the edges, trying to curtail welfare use, in this case Medicaid, among a subpopulation." Camarota said the best solution would be an immigration system that selects people unlikely to need welfare, with greater weight on education, skills and earnings potential than on whether someone has a relative here.

Al-Omar said he hopes to regain coverage and return to physical therapy. Through an interpreter, he said: "I hope to get Medicaid back and to return to doing physical therapy, and to one day get back to walking and working. I hope that my children will continue to study and will not be stopped from going to school to take care of me. And I hope that government officials will consider my health and financial situation and return my Medicaid coverage."
