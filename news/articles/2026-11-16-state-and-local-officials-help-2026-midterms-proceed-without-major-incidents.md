## Courts blocked ballot challenges as wide margins limited post-election scrutiny

Nearly two weeks after Election Day, a virtual panel of researchers and former local government officials hosted by the Safeguarding Democracy Project at the University of California, Los Angeles said Monday that the election system had held. Panelists expressed relief but warned against complacency about future contests. No major incidents occurred, and voting proceeded without federal interference. Panelists attributed that outcome to the resilience of state and local officials, the preparation of election workers and Democrats' sweeping victories.

Election officials and voting rights advocates had braced for a chaotic post-election period, including federal agents appearing at polling places or trying to disrupt vote counting. Election workers across the country gameplanned how to respond. Bill Gates, executive director of Arizona State University's Mechanics of Democracy Laboratory, which supports efforts to combat election misinformation, said, “It didn’t happen magically.” The panelists credited that preparation, including for possible disruption, with helping ensure smooth voting.

Tammy Patrick, chief programs officer at Election Center, an operation of the National Association of Election Officials, said workers were deeply concerned about federal intervention. “They were really concerned about federal intervention. It was their top concern at every single one of our events in the last year,” she said.

Under the U.S. Constitution, states administer federal elections; Congress may override state election regulations, but the president has no unilateral authority over how elections are run. Trump nevertheless made repeated attempts to assert that authority, including by signing executive orders that sought to impose a federal proof-of-citizenship requirement and directing the U.S. Postal Service to restrict ballots sent by mail. Federal courts blocked major portions of both orders. The U.S. Supreme Court halted the mail-ballot restrictions for the midterms, but their legality for the 2028 election remains unsettled.

The Trump administration also pursued legal challenges before the election. For months, the U.S. Department of Justice sought sensitive voter data from states through dozens of lawsuits, none of which succeeded. Weeks before Election Day, the department sued California to force it to set aside ballots from suspected noncitizen voters, preemptively sued Alaska over certification of a U.S. Senate race and demanded that Washington set aside ballots. Federal judges blocked the California and Alaska challenges, and a judge barred the department from forcing Washington election officials to segregate ballots.

Guy-Uriel Charles, a professor of law at Harvard Law School, said the pre-election litigation was, in many ways, a good development. “Instead of going and putting troops or agents at the polls … the Trump administration litigated their disputes,” Charles said. Charles Stewart III, a professor of political science at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, called federal efforts to bend the election system often “feckless.” He said the decentralized system, in which states run elections, helped protect it against a federal government that lacked the capacity to carry out many of the actions people feared.

Democrats' broad wins also reduced the potential for a chaotic period after the votes were counted, the panelists said. At least at the national level, few high-profile contests were extremely close, leaving less room for legal fights or fraud claims. Wide margins let news organizations call enough races that Democrats' control of Congress became clear within hours of the first polls closing. The close Senate races in Alaska and Mississippi therefore did not determine which party controlled the chamber, reducing scrutiny of those states.

Mississippi nonetheless produced one of the closest results. Democrat Scott Colom led Republican Sen. Cindy Hyde-Smith by only a few dozen votes on election night. The Associated Press did not call the race for Colom until more than a week later, when he held a lead of 4,669 votes, or about 0.5 percentage points.

Rick Hasen, director of the Safeguarding Democracy Project and the panel moderator, said a Mississippi result that decided control of the Senate could have drawn a national news response to the state's election administration problems. “We would be in big trouble,” Hasen said. Some panelists indicated that a tight Mississippi election could otherwise have spiraled into a national controversy.

Mississippi has no recount law, so a recount would occur only if ordered by a court. Election officials used data from the credit agency Experian to list voters as inactive, and those voters had to sign affidavits when they voted. Mississippi Secretary of State Michael Watson, a Republican, defended the affidavit system in an opinion column posted to his official website on Monday. Watson said approximately 75% of affidavits were approved and counted.

Charles said the midterms reflected an electorate that wanted to send a “very clear message.” He said the message might not always be so clear. He warned: “We can’t count on that next time around, and I think there’s some warnings here that we must heed,” Charles said, “notwithstanding the great work by a number of people trying to assure that this election went as smoothly as possible.”

Taken together, the panel's assessment was that state authority, worker preparedness and decisive congressional margins helped keep the 2026 midterms orderly. The unsettled status of the mail-ballot rules for 2028 and Mississippi's narrow result showed why the speakers stopped short of treating the outcome as a guarantee for future elections.
