## Colom told Hyde-Smith his personal views would be irrelevant to his daily work

In 2023, then-President Joe Biden nominated Colom for a federal judgeship, and the nomination stalled in the U.S. Senate. Mississippi's Sen. Roger Wicker, a Republican, indicated he would support the nomination. Hyde-Smith, the state's junior senator, blocked it.

Hyde-Smith cited donations from billionaire philanthropist George Soros around Colom's run for district attorney — donations that Colom said in 2023 went to an independent PAC, not his campaign. In a statement provided to the Mississippi Free Press at the time, she also said Colom opposed legislation to protect female athletes.

In a letter to Hyde-Smith, Colom said he did not know what she was referring to and noted that he had signed a statement saying he would not use law enforcement resources to criminalize doctors who offer gender affirming care to transgender youth. He stated in the letter that, if confirmed as a judge, any of his personal views would be irrelevant to his "daily work," and he asked the senator to reconsider. She did not.

Three years later, Hyde-Smith was defending her seat from the man whose nomination she had blocked. She told her supporters on election night she was confident she would beat him: "I blocked Scott Colom from being a federal judge, and you blocked him from being U.S. senator." She lost instead, and on Wednesday night she issued a statement conceding the race and saying she respects the decision of Mississippi voters.

Democrat Scott Colom has defeated Republican Sen. Cindy Hyde-Smith in Mississippi's U.S. Senate race, according to a race call by The Associated Press on Wednesday evening. Colom won by less than five thousand votes, with a small fraction of the vote still left to be counted.

The victory stands as one of the most stunning upsets of this year's midterm elections, according to NPR. When Colom announced his candidacy late last year, it seemed like a long shot in a ruby red state — one that had not sent a Democrat to the Senate since 1982 and where an incumbent has not been defeated since 1942. Colom also becomes the state's first Black U.S. senator since Reconstruction.

Colom swept areas of Mississippi that have traditionally leaned left, including Hinds County, home to Jackson, the state capital. He also picked up places that have traditionally voted Republican, like Forrest County, which President Trump won in 2024 with about 58 percent of the vote.

Colom has been the district attorney for the state's 16th judicial district since 2016. The district includes his hometown of Columbus in Lowndes County, an area with a right-leaning voter base.

He ran on accessible health care, saving rural hospitals and affordability, and said that working in a Republican-majority area informed the need for bipartisanship in tackling those issues. "I believed that the majority of the Mississippians love our state more than they hate the Democratic party or love the Republican party," Colom said. "They want their kids to have good schools. ... they want to be able to make sure that young people, if they want to stay in Mississippi, can have a job. They want the hospitals to stay open."

By October of this year, a month before votes came pouring in for him, Colom was confident. "We didn't have hundreds of million dollars to come in to spend – we did it going door to door, festival to festival, earning support," he said at a canvassing event on the Mississippi coast. "We're gonna shock a lot of people, but I'm not gonna be shocked. The people of Mississippi are behind this campaign: they understand Mississippi matters."

Attention from national Democratic leaders and his party's campaign funds came late this year, Colom acknowledged, but he said it was the attention his campaign received from voters within the state that made the difference. About two weeks before the election, the PAC associated with Senate Minority Leader Chuck Schumer donated $1.2 million for advertising. Former Vice President Kamala Harris, former Transportation Secretary Pete Buttigieg, former President Bill Clinton and U.S. Sen. Raphael Warnock of Georgia were among the prominent Democrats who joined Colom on the campaign trail.

"They understand the power of their vote in ways that we really haven't seen in a long period of time, because if you look at these margins, certainly everything that everybody did made a difference," Colom told reporters in Jackson just days before the election was called.

The race also drew attention on social media. In an interview with WLOX, Hyde-Smith responded to questions about beef affordability by suggesting folks pick a different protein. Colom responded with a campaign ad suggesting folks pick another senator instead.

Colom said he is proud to hold the distinction of being the state's first Black senator since Reconstruction, but he is not resting on it. "My job is to be a senator that makes it easier for the next African American that has the work ethic, the vision, the values, and they want to be United States senator, make it easier for them," Colom said. "I am a person that is continuing to show up in the community and take the tough questions and explain my, you know, live my values. And if I do that, it makes it easier for the next person to be judged by their character and not their skin color."

Colom traveled the whole state during the campaign, from the coast to the capital and up to the Tennessee border. Speaking in the Mississippi Delta, he said what he wants to do in the Senate all goes back to his home of Columbus. "I've always been committed to trying to make my community better, trying to make my city better, trying to make my state better, and that's what running for United States Senate is all about for me," Colom said. "It's a continuation of a service that runs in my family and I was raised [by] my community and my church to give back and that's why I'm doing it in this moment."
