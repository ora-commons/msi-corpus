## Pennsylvania Republicans debate direction after Election Day rout

**Subtype:** fact

- Pennsylvania Democrats expanded their one-seat state House majority to 13 and flipped the state Senate, which Republicans had controlled almost continuously since 1978, after Gov. Josh Shapiro won a second term on Nov. 3.
- Four Republican incumbents lost U.S. House seats in Pennsylvania.
- Democrats picked up at least 40 House seats and eight Senate seats nationwide.
- Former U.S. Rep. Charlie Dent called the election a referendum on President Trump and said the “MAGA movement is badly damaged by this.”
- Dent said Republicans need a deep debate over the party's future, framed as a battle between legacy Republicans and MAGA Republicans.
- State Treasurer Stacy Garrity, the Republican nominee for governor, raised $9.2 million through mid-October, compared with the $66.3 million that Shapiro's campaign spent.

## Shapiro wins second term as Democrats flip Pennsylvania Senate

The rout unfolded amid turmoil in Washington, another Middle East war and price hikes at home. The defeats extended from the top of the ballot down. Shapiro won a second term, Democrats grew their state House margin to 13 seats, and four Republican U.S. House incumbents fell in a year when the party lost at least 40 House seats and eight Senate seats across the country. House Minority Leader Jesse Topper, a Bedford Republican who was reelected to his leadership post last week, said the results were a clear message.

“Every couple years, the voters have a chance to basically tell you how they’re feeling, and they spoke loud and clear that they’re unhappy with Republicans,” Topper said. “And I think there are Republicans who are unhappy because they feel we didn’t do what we said we were going to do, particularly at a national level.”

Former U.S. Rep. Charlie Dent, who represented the Lehigh Valley for seven terms, placed the vote in the tradition of midterm elections. “This election was a referendum on the president of the United States,” Dent said, and argued that this year's backlash was notably severe because of what he called “the six Cs”: cost, conflict, corruption, chaos, cruelty, “and sometimes crazy.” Dent said Republicans across the country need to have a deep debate over the future of the party, adding that it will be a battle between legacy Republicans and MAGA Republicans. “I do think the MAGA movement is badly damaged by this,” he said.

Dent said his use of “cruelty” went beyond the treatment of immigrants. “When I talk about cruelty, I’m not just talking about treatment of immigrants,” he said. “I’m talking about the treatment of Canada, of [Ukrainian President Volodymyr] Zelensky in the White House and … the name calling, the never ending discourteous behavior,” he said.

Prices were a constant on the campaign trail. As inflation and the war against Iran pushed up petroleum costs this year, Trump dismissed the affordability crisis as a “Democrat hoax” and told rallygoers in Nebraska last month that higher gas prices were a small price to pay for a non-nuclear Iran. Former House Majority Leader Kerry Benninghoff, a Centre County Republican who won reelection, said it was clear to those campaigning that rising prices were the primary cause of voters' frustration. “I hear from my daughters who do the primary amount of grocery shopping. So a lot of us knew that those were going to be some of the driving force issues, which we don’t necessarily have a lot of control over,” he said. During the campaign, Trump implored Republicans to vote like he was on the ballot, and the Capital-Star reported that the president appeared indifferent to the effects of his policies on regular people.

Pennsylvania Republican Party Chair Greg Rothman, a state senator from Cumberland County, declined an interview. Spokesman James Markley said in a statement that Garrity “worked as hard, if not harder, than any candidate across the country.” Markley added that “as the party in power during a midterm election, Republicans around Pennsylvania and the nation put forth a full and organized effort to defy history and hold the line, but sadly that did not materialize,” and said the party would analyze the results, talk with voters and prepare to come back stronger. “The Republican Party of Pennsylvania remains focused on growing our movement across the Commonwealth, supporting conservative leadership, and fighting for policies that return power, freedom, and hard-earned money back to the people of Pennsylvania,” Markley said.

Dennis Roddy, a former journalist and media strategist who worked as an aide to Republican ex-Gov. Tom Corbett and said he is no longer a party member, said the party also failed to adequately back its top ticket. He said state Treasurer Stacy Garrity was a strong candidate but was not able to tell her story. “If Josh Shapiro outshone Stacy Garrity, it was because he had the money to pay the electricity bill, you know, to illuminate,” Roddy said. “She was without funds.” Garrity, a businesswoman and U.S. Army veteran, raised $9.2 million through mid-October, compared with the $66.3 million that Shapiro's campaign spent.

Charlie Gerow, a conservative commentator and a candidate for governor in the 2022 GOP primary, placed some of the blame on state party leaders, saying they need to return to traditional campaigning. “Rather than having paid door knockers and a bunch of digital gobbledygook, they need real campaigns run by real people, the old-fashioned way,” Gerow said.

Topper said the party's first order of business is “to pick a direction.” He said voters who formed Trump's America First coalition are confused about what the label means now that there is a war in the Middle East and gas prices rising in its wake. Trade policy, Topper said, is equally unsettled. “Are we a party of free trade? Are we a party of tariffs? Are we a party of building things in America? Are we a party of making sure that we are able to build things abroad and make things cheaper?” he asked. “Right now, we’re trying to do a little bit of both, and I just don’t believe that works, and I don’t believe that's a clear message.”

Topper said winning back independent and young voters who swing elections depends on advocating for policies that create opportunities for people of all walks of life. “We have to outline what the American dream is right now,” Topper said, adding that homeownership, higher wages and upward mobility seem out of reach for many voters who turned to Democratic social-welfare policies for help. “That’s not the American dream.”

Former U.S. Rep. Charlie Dent, who served for 14 years in the General Assembly, agreed Pennsylvania Republicans need an agenda based on ideals and principles. He said the party for too long focused on “fealty to the president,” whose goals were unclear. “The party really became less of one based on ideals and principles, and more focused on following a man who has unfixed policy positions, and they shift constantly,” Dent said.

Gerow said the party must define itself on the issues that matter to blue-collar and working-class people. Roddy said the party needs to return to its past as the centrist party he joined after growing up in Cambria County in the 1960s, where he said Democrats were known for having a socially conservative wing. “The Democrats were not a progressive party. They were not a forward-thinking party. They were not a party known for tolerance and acceptance,” Roddy said. He said that branch of the Democratic Party was eradicated as the parties took sides on issues like abortion, guns, God and gay rights. “But right now, the Republican Party is the one that is in most need of remediation,” Roddy added, “because racism, religious bigotry, and just plain bloody mindedness have found their way into too much of the dialog.”

Dent noted that the party also suffered from a degree of complacency, saying more than two dozen of the Republicans defeated in the U.S. House were in districts Trump won in 2024 by 10 to 20 points. He said incumbents in those districts tend to focus only on the 20% to 30% of the electorate that votes in primaries. “Well, I got news for folks: the other 70, 80% showed up, and you know you have to speak to a much broader audience,” Dent said. “Politics is a game played between the 40-yard lines, and for a lot of folks in both parties, this game is playing closer to the end zones, staying hard in the base.”

Dent said the Republican Party has a deep hole to get out of in Congress and the Pennsylvania Legislature, while it remains to be seen whether Democrats will be able to consolidate their gains. “Democrats need to be somewhat humble and recognize that this remarkable victory for them might be a temporary phenomenon, and that they should govern accordingly, carefully, and you know not go too far left,” he said. He noted that a wave election can take out strong incumbents while suboptimal candidates who ride the tide may prove vulnerable in later elections. “It throws up a lot of driftwood on the beach, and you know there are going to be several loose logs close to the shoreline,” Dent said. “They’re going to be pulled back in the rip tide, which will come in a cycle or two.”

Pennsylvania Republicans also face two state Supreme Court seats next year and the U.S. Senate seat held by Democratic Sen. John Fetterman in 2028, with Fetterman widely expected to face primary challengers. U.S. Rep. Madeleine Dean (D-4th District) told Politico she is “considering” a run for Senate against Fetterman.
