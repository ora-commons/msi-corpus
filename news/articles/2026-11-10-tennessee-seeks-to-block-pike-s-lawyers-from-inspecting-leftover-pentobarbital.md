## Tennessee seeks to block Pike’s lawyers from inspecting leftover pentobarbital

**Subtype:** fact

- Tennessee asked Judge I’Ashea L. Myles to block Christa Pike’s attorneys from handling leftover pentobarbital from the failed execution.
- A Tennessee Department of Correction employee said handling the vials or reviewing their labels could identify the state’s lethal-drug supplier.
- The employee said Tennessee would likely be unable to find other providers if it lost its current pentobarbital source.
- Attorney Luke Ihnen said Pike’s legal team needs the drug’s labeling and storage to assess mixing, transport, handling and expiration.
- Tennessee law bars public disclosure of people or entities involved in procuring lethal substances or carrying out executions.

## State argues supplier secrecy protects continued access to lethal drugs

In a Friday court filing, Tennessee asked Judge I’Ashea L. Myles to rescind the part of her order that allowed Pike’s legal team to inspect, collect and test any pentobarbital left from the attempted execution. The state said it was complying with Myles’ broader order requiring preservation of evidence in the lawsuit filed by Pike’s attorneys. Tennessee’s filing argued that the remaining drug could expose the supplier and that public disclosure would violate state law.

Pike, 50, survived two doses of pentobarbital in September. Her attorneys said the doses probably did not enter her bloodstream correctly and instead pooled in her arms, leaving them burned and blistered. She was hospitalized on a ventilator for days after the failed execution, then returned to prison, where she says she remains in constant pain.

The dispute reflects the secrecy surrounding execution drugs, which states often argue is necessary to protect access to the drugs and the safety of suppliers. In an affidavit cited in Tennessee’s filing, an unnamed Department of Correction employee wrote that the supplier would become identifiable if Pike’s lawyers handled the vials or reviewed their labels. The employee wrote that residue from the attempted execution was “most likely to be found” in the vials.

The employee said the supplier was concerned that even with redactions on the labels, the source of the pentobarbital would be identifiable. The employee also said another supplier stopped communicating with the department in 2024 after its identity was exposed. The employee said Tennessee would likely be unable to find other providers if it lost access to its current source for the drug.

“Pike’s counsel and experts cannot be allowed to see, handle, or otherwise get the information on the leftover vials of pentobarbital from Pike’s attempted execution,” Tennessee Attorney General Jonathan Skrmetti wrote in a court filing. Tennessee law prohibits the public disclosure of any individual or entity involved in procuring lethal substances or carrying out executions.

Luke Ihnen, a lawyer representing Pike, said publicly identifying the state’s lethal injection drug supplier “has never been the goal” of the litigation. He said the legal team needs to review how the pentobarbital was labeled and stored to understand whether there were issues with how it was mixed, transported or handled and whether it was expired. “We still don’t know exactly what happened that night,” Ihnen said. “Those types of things are all valuable pieces of information about what went wrong.”

Tennessee Department of Correction spokesperson Dorinda Carter declined to comment, citing the pending litigation.

Pike and her boyfriend tortured and stabbed 19-year-old classmate Colleen Slemmer to death in 1995. Pike was 18 at the time, making her one of the youngest women sentenced to death. Slemmer’s mother had traveled from Florida to witness Pike’s execution.

Frank Strada, commissioner of the Department of Correction, announced his resignation in the wake of the failed execution. Gov. Bill Lee named Christopher Hansen as interim commissioner.

Lee also tapped a former U.S. attorney to conduct an independent review of the failed execution and postponed the state’s other execution by lethal injection scheduled for this year.

Pike’s attorneys want Republican Gov. Bill Lee, who is set to leave office in January, to change her death sentence to life in prison. He has said that she will remain on death row unless a court determines otherwise.

U.S. Sen. Marsha Blackburn, the state’s Republican governor-elect, has called for the state to explore alternative methods of execution, including the electric chair.
