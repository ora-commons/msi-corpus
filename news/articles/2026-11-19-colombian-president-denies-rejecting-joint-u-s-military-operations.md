## U.S.-Venezuela military cooperation ended in 2005

From Venezuela, Secretary of Defence Pete Hegseth travelled on to neighboring Colombia, where President Abelardo de la Espriella, a close ally of the Trump administration, denied a New York Times report that he had rejected carrying out joint operations. Colombia is already part of the U.S.-led Americas Counter Cartel Coalition, and in August Hegseth had floated the possibility of joint U.S.-Colombian military operations against armed groups. De la Espriella took to X on Wednesday to make the denial. In his post, he stressed that any decision would be taken while respecting Colombia’s constitution and the country’s laws and sovereignty. Hegseth reposted the tweet, commenting that the U.S.-Colombian partnership “is stronger than ever before.”

The BBC’s wider account traced the restored U.S.-Venezuelan military relationship to Hugo Chávez, Venezuela’s left-wing president at the time, who terminated U.S.-Venezuelan military cooperation in 2005. Chávez alleged that U.S. military personnel sent to Venezuela to train their counterparts had made disparaging remarks about his government. He also accused the U.S. military office located in Fuerte Tiuna, Venezuela’s largest military complex, of being “an organ of the CIA... conspiring against the government.”

After Chávez died of cancer, Nicolás Maduro succeeded him, and relations between the two countries deteriorated further. The BBC said relations reached “rock bottom” late the previous year when the United States doubled its reward for information leading to Maduro’s capture to $50m (£38m). U.S. forces eventually seized Maduro on 3 January in a military raid. Maduro and his wife, Cilia Flores, are now in custody in New York on charges of drug trafficking and conspiracy to commit torture, and both deny the charges.

After that rupture, the interim government led by Delcy Rodríguez, Maduro’s former vice president, signed a military co-operation agreement with Hegseth on Wednesday, re-establishing military ties less than a year after the raid on the South American capital, Caracas. Few details about the agreement had been released. Hegseth said Venezuela had “gone from our most aggressive regional threat to a compliant security partner” in less than 12 months, while Rodríguez said the agreement would help the two countries confront “common threats.”

Rodríguez described the agreement as “extraordinarily relevant for our bilateral relations.” Addressing Hegseth during a meeting at the presidential palace, she said: “In a matter of months, secretary, we have turned very difficult moments for our people into a space for co-operation and mutual benefit for both of our countries.” In her speech, Rodríguez mentioned the training of Venezuela’s armed forces, improving its “operational equipment” and access to new technology “to confront shared threats.” No other details of the agreement had been published.

Hegseth described the deal signed by him and Venezuela’s defence minister on Wednesday as a “new chapter for the United States, for Venezuela, for the Venezuelan military and for the Western Hemisphere through the Americas Counter Cartel Coalition.” He made the remarks in a speech at the U.S. embassy in Caracas about the change in the two countries’ relationship since Maduro was seized.

At the embassy, Hegseth said: “It’s a great honour to be here as the first United States Secretary of War this century to set foot in Venezuela. The weight of this moment does not escape me.” He added: “Just one year ago, my presence here, our presence here would have been deemed impossible, almost unimaginable,” recalling a public appearance by then-President Maduro that had angered the Trump administration.

In a longer passage, Hegseth said: “Exactly one year ago today, the indicted narco-terrorist fugitive Nicolás Maduro, the man who spent more than a decade handing Venezuela’s borders and territory to terrorists from all over the world, stood on a stage, not too far from here, and yelled mockingly: 'Come and get me!'. Well, we answered that call.” He added that the result of the U.S. military raid on 3 January had been as Maduro predicted, quoting the deposed leader’s words: “No war, yes peace.”

In the same speech, Hegseth addressed “the narco-terrorists who abuse this [Venezuela] as a safe haven,” warning that “we will hunt you down, we will find you, and we will kill you.”

Hegseth’s remarks linked the Venezuela agreement to the Americas Counter Cartel Coalition, while his trip to Colombia and de la Espriella’s post concerned the possibility of joint military operations against armed groups. With no detailed terms of the Venezuela accord published, the training, equipment and technology priorities Rodríguez described and Hegseth’s characterization of Venezuela as a compliant security partner were the clearest public descriptions of the deal.
