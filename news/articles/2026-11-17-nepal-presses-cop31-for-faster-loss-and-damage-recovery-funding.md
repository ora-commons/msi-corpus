## Aid pledged to Nepal has no disbursement date

At COP31 in Turkey, Nepal and other mountain countries are pressing negotiators to accelerate funding for recovery from climate-fueled disasters and make Himalayan risks a lasting priority in international climate action. Other climate activists are calling for broader discussions about the dangers communities face as the planet warms. Their intervention comes as flood survivors in and around Nuwakot remain in temporary shelters after deadly August floods washed away entire villages and left thousands dead or missing.

Along the Trishuli River, power lines, solar panels, cars and homes remain partly buried in mud. Broken bridges leave communities cut off from neighboring towns, and survivors in makeshift metal shelters rely on donations for drinking water and food. Farmers who once grew rice for their families and livelihoods now wait to learn whether they will own land again. Villagers also gather around the mound of dirt where a temple where families had prayed for generations once stood.

Lakchin Bahadhur Tamang, 45, a hydropower plant worker, described the uncertainty facing displaced families. “God knows how long we will have to stay here,” Tamang said. “I fear that once these aid organizations and helping hands leave, we will be left helpless, without even enough drinking water.”

At the start of the summit, Nepal and Bhutan sought a dedicated negotiating agenda item on warming in mountain regions, but their proposal was not included. Foreign Minister Shisir Khanal said Nepal wants a dedicated program on mountains and climate change so those risks shape international climate action, including funding decisions. “After the disaster, we received an outpouring of sympathy. This is the time to put sympathy into the system,” Khanal said during the climate talks last week.

The stakes extend beyond individual villages. According to the Kathmandu-based International Centre for Integrated Mountain Development, or ICIMOD, glaciers and snow in the Hindu Kush Himalaya feed rivers on which nearly 2 billion people depend for drinking water, farming and electricity. Warming can destabilize mountain slopes as glaciers retreat and frozen ground thaws. Floods carrying rock, ice and debris can cross borders within minutes, leaving little time to warn downstream communities, particularly as early warning systems face funding cuts.

The warning centers on warming caused mainly by burning gas, coal and oil. Over the next years, global average temperatures are set to surpass 1.5 degrees Celsius, or 2.7 degrees Fahrenheit, compared with the early 1800s. Delegates set that threshold during the 2015 Paris Agreement. Returning the global average below it would require massive reductions in greenhouse-gas emissions that the world has not been able to achieve.

Pam Pearson, director of the International Cryosphere Climate Initiative, warned against assuming the rise could be easily reversed. Snow and ice can disappear rapidly, while recovery can take centuries or millennia, she said. “There is no negotiating with the melting point of ice,” Pearson said.

Climate experts at COP31 called for monitoring unstable slopes and dangerous glacial lakes, sharing information across borders in real time, coordinating early warning systems and identifying global funding. Pema Gyamtsho, director general of ICIMOD, said Himalayan disasters repeatedly had been treated as warnings. “Every time there’s a disaster in the Himalayas, we tend to call it a wake-up call,” Gyamtsho said. “The question before us is: What will it take for us to wake up and take action?”

The U.N. Fund for Responding to Loss and Damage was established in 2022 to provide financial support to vulnerable developing nations suffering the impacts from climate change. Its creation followed years of debate over how much developed nations, including the United States — historically the No. 1 polluter — should pay to help poorer nations adapt to a climate change those countries had little role in causing. Experts say more funding and faster disbursement are essential to prepare for more efficient recovery as climate disasters become more frequent and severe, but the fund has been slow to turn promises into payouts.

For Nepal, the fund pledged about $20 million, only a fraction of the estimated $2.5 billion in losses. Khanal said rebuilding after the disaster would cost about one-tenth of Nepal's economy. Manjeet Dhakal, a member of Nepal's delegation and lead adviser to least developed countries at COP, welcomed the initial response but said “the scale of the resources needs to increase.”

Countries have pledged more than $820 million to the fund, which faces 176 requests for support from developing nations totaling about $2.8 billion. The fund's board members will meet in December to consider more support requests. Money earmarked for Nepal remains subject to a formal funding proposal and final board approval, with no disbursement date announced.

The Stockholm Environment Institute estimated that by 2030, lower-income countries would need between $400 billion and $1.6 trillion per year for loss-and-damage finance. At COP31, protesters repeatedly called “climate justice now,” calling for help for Nepal.

For survivors in Nepal, the gap between what has been offered and what is needed is huge. Back in Nuwakot, Dhurba Bhandari, 41, a small store owner, said Nepal had contributed very little to global carbon emissions yet had been devastated. “The international community must take responsibility and help people who have been displaced, as well as support reconstruction,” Bhandari said.
