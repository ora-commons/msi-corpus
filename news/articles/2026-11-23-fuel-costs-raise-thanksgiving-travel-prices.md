## Fuel costs raise Thanksgiving travel prices

**Subtype:** fact

NPR reported that the biggest Thanksgiving expense is the cost of travel, not the turkey or cranberry sauce. U.S. Thanksgiving travelers are facing higher gasoline and airfare costs this year. AAA forecasts 81.5 million people will travel at least 50 miles from home, while Hopper says Thanksgiving tickets are about 30% more expensive than last year. Gasoline averaged 56 cents more per gallon Monday than a year earlier, according to AAA, and Hopper economist Hayley Berg said jet fuel prices were almost 90% higher than at this time last year.

The cost increase follows a tightening of oil supply since the U.S. began its war in Iran in February, NPR reported. The Strait of Hormuz has been effectively throttled, leaving less crude oil for refineries to process and pushing up prices for gasoline, diesel and jet fuel.

AAA said Monday's average gasoline price was 56 cents above the year-ago level, a few cents short of the most expensive gas prices ever recorded around Thanksgiving. Most Americans who travel at Thanksgiving drive, and AAA forecasts a near-record 81.5 million people will travel at least 50 miles from home. AAA estimates that 200,000 drivers will skip a long-distance road trip this year because of fuel prices.

Patrick De Haan, head of petroleum analysis at the fuel-savings app GasBuddy, said most drivers would still take the road even if they were unhappy about gasoline prices. "Thanksgiving is a very big holiday to spend with friends and loved ones," he said. "I don't think that fuel prices are going to be really impeding many people."

A gallon of regular gasoline now costs $3.63 on average, after hovering above $4 for much of the year, NPR reported. Gas tends to be cheaper in the winter because people take vacations in the summer, when demand and prices tend to rise. Refineries also make two blends of gasoline. The summer blend produces less smog but is more expensive, while the winter blend uses butane to help cars start in freezing weather and is cheaper.

Geopolitical supply is also easing pressure. More oil is reaching the market from the Middle East through pipelines rather than the Strait of Hormuz, increasing available supply and lowering crude prices, NPR reported. Market watchers had also been bracing for an escalation just after the U.S. midterms, but it had not materialized. Oil traders were showing cautious optimism that the conflict would eventually end and the strait would reopen, helping crude prices come down from earlier highs and driving down gasoline prices.

Airline fares have also risen. Hopper says Thanksgiving tickets are about 30% more expensive than last year, while AAA puts the average round-trip U.S. ticket at around $800 this season. NPR attributed the increase to jet fuel, one of airlines' biggest expenses, and said carriers are paying billions of dollars more for it this year.

The industry trade group Airlines for America said carriers are absorbing some of the increased costs, while Hayley Berg said carriers also are passing some of them on. Berg said, "Jet fuel prices are almost 90% higher than they were at this time last year, and airlines have been managing that major cost increase by passing some of those costs onto travelers in the form of higher airfares."

At the Fortune 500 Innovation Forum last week, Delta CEO Paul Bastian said Delta's ticket prices are up 15% to 20% this year and customers are still buying. "Obviously, fuel prices were the trigger that enabled it, but we would not be able to generate that type of pricing if we didn't have a consumer that was willing to pay it," he said.

Berg said people were willing to pay more because many Thanksgiving trips are tied to family routines. "A lot of Americans see their holiday travel as inflexible," she said. "It's either a long-awaited, long-planned vacation or a trip home for the holidays to see friends and family."

The TSA expects to screen 17.6 million passengers between tomorrow and next Monday. On Sunday alone, the agency said it could screen more than 3 million air travelers. The projection comes despite recent U.S. air-travel disruptions, including a systems issue that briefly grounded Delta planes in Atlanta over the weekend and a nationwide ground stop earlier this month caused by an FAA technical problem.

Thanksgiving is Amtrak's busiest season. The rail service broke ridership records during last year's Thanksgiving travel period, rising nearly 6% from the previous year, and Amtrak predicts 2026 will be another record year.

Bus travel is also busy. Data from Flix North America, which owns Greyhound, show bookings for the week up 17% compared with last year. Flix said bus fares have a competitive edge over other modes of transportation, attracting travelers concerned about affordability.

Fred Ferguson, president and CEO of the American Bus Association, told NPR that Peter Pan Bus Lines is adding more than 300 departures in the Northeast for Thanksgiving. "The company expects to carry more than 160,000 passengers over the holiday, up 7% from last Thanksgiving," he wrote.

December is another major U.S. travel month, and gasoline remains more expensive than it was at this time last year even as its price declines. The war in Iran is not over. Analysts said that even if a peace deal were reached tomorrow, the oil supply chain would need many months to recover and fuel prices would take that long to reach pre-war levels. Prices remain volatile and could rise again if the war escalates. Many travelers have already booked December trips, so their travel costs already reflect the higher autumn prices.
