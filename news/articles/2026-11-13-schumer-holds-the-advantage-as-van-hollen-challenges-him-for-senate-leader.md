## Schumer lacks a publicly pledged majority ahead of December leadership vote

Senate Democrats won back the majority last week. Their next fight is over who will lead it. Maryland Sen. Chris Van Hollen has entered the race against longtime leader Chuck Schumer, telling PBS NewsHour he spoke with Schumer before officially launching his challenge.

"We understand that this will be a competition," Van Hollen said on the program. "I think it's important to give Senate Democrats a choice. That's what we just gave voters all over the country, a choice. They chose change." He added that the caucus "needs to be open to new ideas, a different way of governing, and a different way of conducting and approaching politics."

The contest will be settled in December, when Senate Democrats vote on their leader for the new majority. Introducing the segment, PBS NewsHour anchor Amna Nawaz described Van Hollen's bid as the first serious challenge Schumer has faced to his party leadership.

Van Hollen, Desjardins noted, is 67 and has served in Congress since 2003. Taking on Schumer is by far his most assertive move, she said. The Maryland senator argues the race is specifically about the election results: the newly elected senators, in his telling, were part of a movement about new leaders altogether, not just new lawmakers.

The pressure on Schumer predates the challenge. Desjardins told anchor Amna Nawaz that a groundswell has been developing outside Washington for some time, including from parts of the Democratic base, especially progressives.

Schumer has led Senate Democrats since President Trump first took office in 2017. A New Yorker who knows how to use "the strength of leadership," in Desjardins's description, he has been lining up votes for some time — while Van Hollen announced relatively late in the game.

The two men are not opposed on everything. Desjardins noted that Schumer holds some of the same positions as Van Hollen, including that both want to end funding for the Iraq War, perhaps even risking a shutdown. The difference, she said, is that Van Hollen is more progressive — and is openly saying it is time to end the filibuster.

Desjardins then walked through the arithmetic. Eleven new senators have been elected so far, with a 12th possible if Mary Peltola is confirmed in Alaska. The majority of those new senators oppose Schumer. They are weighed against the 44 current members of the Senate who will vote in the leadership election, a group that includes Van Hollen's own vote.

Is the challenge real? Yes, Desjardins said — but Schumer "has a much greater advantage." "The math favors Schumer," she said, because the incumbent leader has spent months working that same group of senators for their votes. Desjardins said Schumer had already locked up all of those commitments. And timing, she said, "is everything."

For now, Schumer does not have a majority of the caucus publicly pledged to him — but he says he has the votes. The answer comes in December, when Senate Democrats elect their leader.
