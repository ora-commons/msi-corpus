## Motown nonprofit sells $15 dog tags for homeless women veterans shelter

## About 2,000 sold; goal is 1 million for shelter

The Motown Women Veterans Association has sold about 2,000 of the military-style dog tags it is selling for $15 through its website, founder Alexis Derriso said. The tags carry the inscription “Millions of women have served this country. I am one” and are intended to raise funds for a Detroit-area homeless shelter for female veterans and their children. Derriso said the nonprofit’s goal is to sell 1 million tags.

Derriso enlisted in the U.S. Army at 19 in 1980, following a father and brother who had served in the military. She went through basic training and spent six years in the Army, mostly handling personnel management and other administrative duties. As founder and chief executive of the 640-member Motown Women Veterans Association, she wants others to understand that women like her sacrificed as much as men, and sometimes more, in service to their country.

Women have a long history in the U.S. military, most of that service spent as nurses or in administrative and support roles for male troops. Thousands also served in the Women’s Army Corps, which was created during World War II.

In 1948, President Harry S. Truman signed the Women’s Armed Services Integration Act into law. It allowed women to serve in the regular Army and Organized Reserve Corps, which later became the U.S. Army Reserve and Air Force Reserve. Women were restricted to 2% of service members in the Army, Navy, Air Force and Marines. Those ceilings were removed in 1967.

In 2013, the ban on women in direct ground combat roles was lifted. Three years later, the ban was removed for all military roles. Women currently comprise about 17% of the more than 1.2 million active-duty military personnel and more than 21% of troops in the National Guard or reserves. More than 2 million women are U.S. military veterans, according to the Department of Veterans Affairs.

Even as women have reached high-ranking command roles and fought alongside men on the battlefield, Derriso said they often receive less public honor than their male counterparts. “We’re treated as if we’re invisible,” she said. She described being overlooked while wearing an Army cap: “If I just have a cap on that says ‘U.S. Army,’ I’m pretty much ignored. In the civilian world, they will look at me as if I’m wearing my husband’s cap.”

The dog tags are designed to bring women veterans “out of isolation,” Derriso said. Susan McCain, a National Guard veteran from St. Clair Shores, northeast of Detroit, enlisted in 1985 and served until 1993.

“The military is still a man’s world. All we can do is show up and do our duty and pay our respect to our country like we signed up to do,” McCain said. She initially struggled with the little recognition her own service brought but said the Motown Women Veterans Association and its programs allow her to be there for others.

“When I finally owned my veterans’ status I became a much prouder person to myself,” McCain said. “It really lifted up my self-esteem. Going into the military was very empowering to me as a woman back in the ’80s because we were just getting started in filling in some ... traditional male roles.”

Anitra Grant, 68, enlisted in the Army in 1982 and retired in 2007 from the reserves as a master sergeant. Grant, who lives in Hampton, Virginia, said people who have not served often find it difficult to see a woman as a veteran. “Did you actually have to learn how to fire a weapon, get in a foxhole?” she said. “Yep, I did all that,” she laughed. “Yep, women fly the same airplanes.”

Grant also said she experienced discrimination from some of the people she served with. “I was told by our lieutenant that he didn’t believe females should be in the service,” she said. “Once again, you have to prove yourself.”

Historically, military identification tags were small, thin metal plates worn on chains around soldiers’ necks to identify them if they were killed or wounded during battle. A soldier’s name, branch of service and blood type were stamped into the metal. The Motown Women Veterans Association’s tags are also metal and can bear the names of the Marines, Air Force, Navy or Army. They are made in Mount Vernon, New York, by LogoTags, a division of Ball Chain Manufacturing that supplies stamped dog tags and ball chains to the U.S. military.

“We want to have something we’re proud to give to our customers, especially our veterans,” said Bill Taubner, who runs the family-owned company. “You wear them. They’ve got your name on it. It’s tangible and it’s physical and they’re always with them.”

Derriso said the nonprofit’s goal remained 1 million tag sales, with the money intended for the Detroit-area shelter.
