## Tariffs, AI rules and immigration enforcement shape California industry risks

A Democrat-controlled Congress gives federal lawmakers a route to revisit Trump administration policies affecting California’s agriculture, wine, technology sector and smaller businesses, News From The States reported. Business leaders said the election result could change policy because President Donald Trump had lost the Republican-majority Congress that gave him outsized power. The outlet’s analysis says any changes would depend on compromise with Trump, and business owners said not every possible change would be welcome. It examines trade and agriculture, AI regulation, federal lending and immigration enforcement.

California’s agricultural and wine industries were hurting from inflation brought about by tariffs and the U.S.-Israeli war in Iran and were hoping for some relief. California Farm Bureau President Shannon Douglass said tariffs have affected agriculture differently depending on what farmers grow and sell. “Tariffs have had a tremendous impact on agriculture, depending on what people are growing and selling,” Douglass said. “We need consistency in our trade relations. We want more markets and more ability to sell our products.”

The International Trade Administration data cited in the analysis showed California’s vegetable and melon exports to Canada dropping about 27% in 2025 from the previous year, falling from $1.14 billion to $837 million.

California winemakers have also lost overseas business. Matt Licklider, owner of LIOCO in Healdsburg, a winery that gets its grapes from small vineyards around the state, said, “The American brand was so damaged by tariffs and the toxic politics of the day.” He said LIOCO had done almost no export business since Trump took office the second time.

Not all of Licklider’s fears about tariffs materialized, and he did not have to reduce staff. The lost export business had represented about 10% of LIOCO’s business, and he shifted his focus to the domestic market.

But tariffs — some of which were struck down by the U.S. Supreme Court — combined with the Iran War led to higher costs for just about everything, such as transportation and supplies. According to a new study by the Port of Long Beach and an institute at the University of Southern California, the next “tariff shock” could come after the holidays, after the suspension of a U.S.-China trade truce expires.

All of it has cut into LIOCO’s bottom line. “As good of a year as we had from a revenue standpoint, from a profit standpoint, the champagne is not on ice,” Licklider said. “It’s pretty heartbreaking after having worked so hard all year.” Licklider also worried about the long-term effects on global trade. “I think the rest of the world is watching what happens in the next election, and wondering ‘can they trust America’?” he said.

A separate risk hangs over California’s technology sector. The analysis described uncertainty over whether the artificial-intelligence boom was about to burst, which it said could affect the state budget. Wall Street had a mixed reaction to the congressional shift. Before Nvidia Chief Executive Jensen Huang’s resignation and reinstatement unsettled the chip company at the center of the AI boom, stock markets saw large declines amid fears that Democrats would enact tougher AI regulations. The analysis also said opposition to data centers influenced voters around the country and contributed to Democrats’ midterm gains. It noted that California laws tend to become models for the rest of the country and that the technology industry is doing its best to advocate for itself in its home state.

Silicon Valley Leadership Group CEO Ahmad Thomas said confusion around data centers had not given community members an accurate picture of responsible development and that the group had participated in legislative efforts on the issue. The group’s members include the world’s biggest tech companies. Thomas pointed to data centers in San Jose that use renewable energy and recycled water and include community incentives and utility-cost goals. Yet San Jose paused data center development the previous week. Thomas said Democrats were more inclined to pass laws reining in AI, including rules related to how the technology affects and sometimes eliminates jobs. He said it was important to train today’s workers for jobs with the new technology reality in mind. “I think it’s vital to be optimizers rather than obstructionists,” he said.

Small businesses are dealing with increased costs, and the analysis says other Trump administration policies are affecting their ability to expand or start. Carolina Martinez, CEO of CAMEO Network, a national association of organizations that support small businesses, said a new Congress could restore access to Small Business Administration loans for green card holders, which the agency revoked earlier in the year.

The administration also withheld funding for community development financial institutions, or CDFIs, which lend to underserved communities that may not be able to reach traditional banks and services. CAMEO and others sued, forcing the federal government to release $289 million in appropriated funds, but more than $1 billion remained locked as the administration proposed additional cuts. “It is really important for lawmakers to focus on restoring and protecting the federal CDFI fund to ensure that capital keeps flowing to small businesses in rural and urban communities alike,” Martinez said. “This actually needs to be very clear: Immigrants start businesses at twice the rate of the U.S.-born population.”

President Donald Trump, almost immediately after the election, said of the new Democratic majority: “Maybe we can work something out.” Trump also acknowledged that his approach to immigration, which has included sweeping raids and deaths of detainees and others, may have been “too tough.” He then appointed Stephen Miller, widely known to be behind the administration’s hardline immigration policies, as chief of staff. UC Davis professor of international economics Giovanni Peri said, “Trump has responded to these midterms in some schizophrenic and kind of incoherent ways.” Peri said the big question was whether immigration raids that upended communities and reduced California’s workforce would be scaled down.

California was one of six states and Washington, D.C., where the total workforce decreased by more than 338,000 workers from April to December 2025, according to a UC Merced study. During the same period, the private-sector workforce in the rest of the nation increased by more than 1.6 million. A separate UC Merced analysis warns that counties facing sustained raids should expect hiring and payrolls to decline, affecting families, local sales and property-tax revenue. The analysis reported that ICE had stepped up immigration arrests in California since the election. Peri said if there is an eventual curtailment of Trump’s immigration crackdown, it would help the agricultural, construction, hospitality, food and other important sectors, plus California universities.

Peri said the administration’s other immigration-related policies had affected universities, research laboratories and the technology and biotechnology industries by slowing the arrival of new students and scholars, which would hurt those entities’ ability to hire and innovate. The administration raised fees for H-1B visas and proposed higher fees for the optional practical training program that allows international students to work in the United States after they graduate. “It’s not just the deported,” Peri said. “A few were, but a lot of people left and a lot of people didn’t come. Those are real consequences for these industries.”

The sector-by-sector picture is not uniformly negative or positive. Agricultural and wine businesses want relief from tariffs, technology companies want workable AI rules and preparation for job changes, small-business advocates want federal lending restored, and immigrant-dependent industries want clarity on enforcement. The analysis tied each possibility to compromise with Trump, leaving the timing and scope of change unresolved.
