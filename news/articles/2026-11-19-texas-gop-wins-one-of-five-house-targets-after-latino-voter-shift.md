## Democrats won about 53% of the House vote but only 14 of 38 seats

Texas Republicans' 2025 congressional map delivered one House pickup in the Nov. 3, 2026 election rather than the five the party sought, while Democrats won six of seven seats considered in play and left Republicans with a net loss of one seat. Democrats received about 53% of the U.S. House vote in Texas to Republicans' 47%, but the shape of the districts limited them to 14 of the state's 38 seats. Seven Republicans held on by less than five points. Texas' 2025 mapdrawer Adam Kincaid said an initial review found that all Republican statewide nominees except one carried all 24 congressional districts that elected a Republican to Congress. The party's only successful pickup was the 32nd District, reshaped to connect Dallas with East Texas, which flipped to Republican by a narrower-than-expected three-point margin.

The six Democratic-won seats were the 9th and 35th; Democratic Rep. Henry Cuellar's 28th; Democratic Rep. Vicente Gonzalez's 34th; the 15th, then held by Republican Rep. Monica De La Cruz; and the open 23rd, most recently held by former Republican Rep. Tony Gonzales. Democrats retained Cuellar's and Gonzalez's redrawn South Texas seats. Democrat Johnny Garcia prevailed in the San Antonio-area 35th, redrawn to favor Trump by 10 percentage points, and Democrat Leticia Gutierrez won the Houston-area 9th, which Trump had carried by 20 points. All six had majority-Hispanic voting populations, though some narrowly. Strong Latino turnout and a large shift toward Democrats helped the party prevail in majority-Hispanic districts that had appeared safe, including the 15th, where Trump had won by 18 points.

News From The States described Cuellar as a longtime moderate, and his 28th District had been considered the toughest of the five targets because he could overperform statewide Democrats. As Election Day approached, an internal Democratic poll put him 20 percentage points ahead of his Republican challenger. “I said, I’ll take 12-14 points,” Cuellar, a Laredo Democrat, said. “I’d be happy with that. 20 points? Eh, that’s a little high.” The poll underestimated his eventual margin: on Nov. 3, Cuellar won by 36 percentage points in a district President Donald Trump would have carried by 10 points in 2024 under its new lines.

Republicans drew the map in 2025 to pick up five seats and protect incumbents from the party's typical midterm losses. The new map passed at the request of Trump's political operation and over Democrats' objections; Democratic lawmakers left the state to delay the process. State Rep. Todd Hunter stated the goal plainly: to “improve Republican political performance.”

Beyond picking up seats, Republicans sought to protect incumbents by ensuring Trump carried every district drawn for the GOP by at least 10 percentage points. That required carving out territory in places shifting toward Republicans — southern Bexar County, southeastern Harris County and along the South Texas border. Adam Kincaid, executive director of the National Republican Redistricting Trust and Texas' 2025 mapdrawer, said drawing secure seats was impossible and that the objective was to make them “winnable under the right circumstances.” As the cycle went on, concern grew that drawing lines based on Trump's historic 2024 performance in Texas had been a miscalculation. A House Republican strategist who worked on Texas races said, “I think there was a bit of a mirage in how they redistricted, and should’ve gone back further to 2022 [or] 2020 years in order to redistrict, and look at those lines.” “That’s the one criticism that I’ve heard over and over again.” Kincaid testified that the map was not based solely on Trump's 2024 performance and that mapmakers used electoral data going back to 2012. The map also faced what Kincaid called a 26-point swing at the top of the ticket, from Trump's nearly 14-point win in 2024 to Republican Senate nominee Ken Paxton's loss by more than 12 points.

The map assumed that the Latino voters who moved toward Trump in 2024 would continue voting Republican down the ballot. “As I said at the time, the maps would perform for Republicans if existing trends continued,” Kincaid said in a statement to The Texas Tribune. Cuellar described the result differently: “They based everything on a premise that turned out to be a false premise,” he said. “And that premise was: Hispanics in South Texas voted for Trump, therefore they’re going to vote for Republicans down ballot.” Kincaid said four of the five targeted seats were majority Latino because Republican support was growing in Latino areas and political data, rather than race and ethnicity data, drove the map. He said Republicans could not create other pickups elsewhere in Texas without violating other mapping criteria. Interviews with nearly a dozen candidates and operatives in both parties described the political environment continuing to improve for Democrats as the fall progressed despite the new district lines, while Republicans' 2024 gains among Latino voters faded.

Democratic candidates reported growing enthusiasm on the ground, particularly among Latino voters. In 2024, Cuellar recalled being in previously heavily Democratic areas such as Starr County and repeatedly hearing, “El Trumpo va a ganar” — Trump is going to win. In 2026, local Democrats told him they were hearing the opposite: that voters would support Democrats up and down the ballot. Katy Padilla Stout, the Democrat who flipped the 23rd District, said her confidence grew on each trip into the South and West Texas counties in her district. “It was older women, who would come up to me, and say, ‘Mija, we’ve got you,’” she said. “‘We are going to make sure you get this.’” People in both parties said the economy was by far the top issue in the election, but some Democrats and some Republicans believed the redistricting fight had given new energy to Democratic opposition. “When you come and you redraw our maps … cuando vienes a Tejas, nos respetas and no te metas,” said Garcia, the Bexar County sheriff's deputy who held the 35th District. “When you come to Texas, you better respect it, and don’t mess with it. And guess what? Texans woke up. Latinos woke up.”

For Democrats, the goal became recruiting strong candidates and developing messaging that resonated with Latino swing voters. Bobby Pulido was the first Democratic House recruit of the cycle, said CJ Warnke, communications director at House Majority PAC. Democratic groups in Washington began talking to him in December 2024 and remained confident that the right candidate could win the district even after a Republican rout there. Pulido's near-universal name identification from his music career gave him an advantage, particularly after redistricting pushed his opponent, De La Cruz into territory where she had never run. The Democratic Congressional Campaign Committee began recruiting in Texas before the map passed, especially in the 35th District. Its conversations with Garcia began early and became more detailed as the seat took shape, even while Democrats were fighting the redraw in court, and Garcia's early willingness to run encouraged the group. “Even as we were fighting it legally, we had an idea of the kind of candidate we wanted in that district,” said Madison Andrus, the DCCC's southern regional press secretary. “We knew we wanted someone with a law enforcement background, and Johnny fit it so perfectly.” A House Republican strategist said Garcia had little to attack: “Having a law enforcement member — there was very little to attack him on,” the strategist said. “I think he, out of everybody, was the best recruit in Texas.”

House Majority PAC, Democrats' biggest outside spender, announced its Lone Star Fund in late July, a $20 million fundraising effort for Democratic recruitment and ad support with an eye toward Hispanic voters. The $20 million figure proved to be an underestimate: HMP ultimately spent $30.2 million on advertising alone in five districts Democrats won. By the end of 2025, as the U.S. Supreme Court cleared Texas to use the new map, Democratic officials described Cuellar and Gonzalez as proven incumbents, Garcia as a strong recruit and Pulido's popularity as an opportunity to compete beyond the 15th District. After the primary, Democrats renewed their interest in the 9th and 23rd districts. In both, more Democrats than Republicans voted in the March 3 primaries, which party officials treated as encouraging; Democrats won their nominations outright while the Republican contests continued into runoffs.

The 23rd District was open because Republican Rep. Tony Gonzales announced he would drop his reelection bid after admitting an affair with an aide who later died by suicide. The DCCC contacted Padilla Stout early, before adding her to its “districts in play” list in August. After Republican Brandon Herrera became the nominee, Democrats paid closer attention, including polling the race. As rumors swirled around Gonzales in the weeks before he dropped out, an HMP staffer spent hours upon hours combing through Herrera's YouTube history. In the immediate aftermath of Gonzales' announcement, HMP began circulating clips online of Herrera discussing owning a copy of *Mein Kampf*, goose-stepping to a Nazi song in a parody video and making Holocaust jokes. “That was weeks of planning to get ready for that moment, because we knew to be competitive in a district like this, [for] a candidate who would not get outside help until the last two weeks, we were going to have to make Brandon Herrera a thing,” Warnke said. Herrera pushed back during the campaign against allegations that he was a neo-Nazi, calling the idea bizarre and desperate.

In the 15th District, responding to Pulido's primary victory, De La Cruz said in a video that the election was not about “who you want performing at your niece’s quinceañera.” Pulido later quipped that it was the best campaign advice he received all cycle. He performed at dozens of quinceañeras across the district over the course of the campaign and made direct contact with thousands of voters. “It gave him a free campaign opportunity once a week,” Warnke said. Building a large crowd once a week for months was difficult, but the events gave Pulido a recurring opportunity to meet voters and explain his presence at each quinceañera. “Oh right, because the Republican congresswoman dissed quinceañeras,” Warnke said. Republican operatives said attacks highlighting Pulido's employment of a musician who was a registered sex offender and crude comments he had made on social media did not move voters.

In late April, HMP made early advertising reservations in five districts, including the 23rd, while Congressional Leadership Fund, the main House Republican outside spender, placed its first Texas buys only in Cuellar's and Gonzalez's districts. In mid-October, CLF pulled out of Cuellar's district, which Republicans had long regarded as the most challenging, and a handful of Texas Republicans in safer seats began advertising. No Going Back PAC, connected to Trump's political operation, and Elon Musk's America PAC made late-breaking October buys in the 9th and 23rd districts. Republican candidates and strategists were also concerned about the governor's race. They had expected Gov. Greg Abbott to help down-ballot candidates because of the size of his war chest and his record of double-digit wins in three prior gubernatorial runs, but they saw little early spending or campaigning from his office. “One thing that was concerning to us was the lack of spending and the lack of campaigning, early, from the governor’s office,” the House Republican strategist said. “Nobody really knew why the governor wasn’t so gung-ho, and probably started a little too late … That was always a concern that I kept hearing about.”

As fall advertising accelerated, Democratic internal polls became so favorable that party officials hesitated to believe them. “We got some polls out of DCCC analytics, internal polls, that we saw and we were like, ‘There’s no way, right? That’s not possible,’” said Andrus, who attributed the large margins to South Texas's volatility and difficulty polling. The polls proved accurate or underestimated Democrats: races that were close in August and September showed Democratic leads in October. In the 35th District, once Democrats began airing positive Garcia ads, Warnke said, Garcia's numbers improved immediately. By mid-September, another Republican strategist who worked on Texas races said the most competitive races felt increasingly wobbly. Some Republicans continued to assume that undecided voters would break Republican as they reliably had in the past. “Down the stretch, it was, well, it’s going to be tight,” the strategist said. “Then it became: it’s not looking good. Then it became, we really need something to break for [Republicans] at the end here. Then ultimately, you could just tell, once early voting started, it was just not hitting the numbers.”

Democrats were outraised and outspent in the Houston-area 9th District, but they had kept an eye on the race. The DCCC added the seat to its target list in early October and polled it in the middle of early voting, finding Republican Alex Mealer ahead by one percentage point. Both parties were surprised by the result — a 15-point victory for Gutierrez. “We thought that was a safe one,” said a national Republican strategist who had not been tracking the 9th District. “And it turned out, it wasn’t.”

Republicans lost one seat net. A national Republican strategist contrasted Texas with North Carolina, where a map drawn to give Republicans 11 seats to Democrats' three was spread so thinly among incumbents that Democrats won all 11. The strategist said the Texas map's main effect was to create more competitive seats rather than seats designed solely to protect incumbents. “In Texas and a lot of these states, you created a lot more competitive seats where, for a long time, it had been inelastic — you had a lot of seats that were just red or just blue,” the strategist said. “And you just created a bunch of competitive, in a good environment, we can run it up. And in a bad environment, like you saw, it can get real bad.” The strategist characterized Democrats' House gains as more of a rental than a permanent realignment and expected the redistricting strategy to pay dividends for Republicans in the long run.

Kincaid said a more aggressive Republican map “would have endangered too many seats.” He said the 15th and 23rd districts “would have unfortunately been lost … in this environment” whether Republicans had used the lines drawn in 2021 or the new ones. Kincaid argued Republicans could win both districts back and “make real inroads in the other pickup seats” in 2028 if the party selected the right presidential nominee, candidates and message. With Democrats holding the governor's mansion and the Texas House, another Republican-led redistricting round — which some Democrats feared — was off the table, and both parties were likely to use the current map next cycle.
