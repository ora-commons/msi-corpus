## Federal election grants remain limited as counties face equipment costs

Democrats who take control of Congress in January are poised to influence federal spending on election administration and security, raising hopes among election officials and experts for steadier aid. The party will lead the appropriations process, but the Senate’s 60-vote threshold for most legislation could produce friction. The current annual grant is $45 million, while state and local officials describe needs running far higher.

In Hardin County, Kentucky, Republican county clerk Brian Smith is preparing for a larger electorate by the 2028 presidential election. The county south of Louisville includes Fort Knox, has more than 110,000 residents and is growing as Ford builds a battery plant expected to bring thousands of jobs. “I am confident that by the 2028 presidential election, I (will) have huge equipment needs in order to have enough polling locations operating to serve the number of voters I have,” Smith said. Without a major change, Smith expects those costs to fall largely to the county.

Federal election assistance has moved sharply from year to year. Over the past decade, lawmakers approved more than $400 million in election security grants to states in some years and zero in others, while experts say billions of dollars are needed for election infrastructure upgrades. Former President Joe Biden unsuccessfully proposed $10 billion over 10 years to bolster election security.

Democrats have called for substantial new investments and are poised to play influential roles over the next two years as the party takes control of both chambers, giving them more say over spending even though Republicans have backed election aid in the past. California Democratic Sens. Alex Padilla and Adam Schiff introduced legislation this year to authorize $10 billion in federal election aid over three years. Padilla will chair the Senate Rules & Administration Committee, which oversees federal election policy. “Senator Padilla will continue to prioritize election administration and security funding and hopes to see bipartisan support for the issue going into the 2028 election cycle, especially if he becomes the Chairman of the Rules Committee in the 120th Congress,” Padilla spokesperson Danny Rodriguez said in a statement.

The institutional questions surrounding election funding extend beyond appropriations. President Donald Trump fired two commissioners of the U.S. Election Assistance Commission in July, and a third resigned, leaving the bipartisan independent agency without members. The EAC is the only federal agency dedicated solely to helping states and localities run smooth and secure elections. Operating on a roughly $24 million budget, it distributes grants, develops voluntary security and design standards for voting machines and trains election officials.

The EAC administers the Election Security Grants program, which was created in response to foreign interference in the 2016 election. The grants fund technology upgrades, including cybersecurity, physical security improvements at election sites and efforts to combat voter misinformation. States had spent about $343 million in election security grants on voting equipment and cybersecurity as of 2024, according to a Bipartisan Policy Center report that year to the EAC.

Thomas Hicks, one of the two Democratic EAC commissioners Trump fired, said states do not have adequate resources to continually upgrade voting equipment and argued that delaying improvements would worsen the problem. “If we neglect doing this, we’re going to have some sort of catastrophic event where we say, oh, we should have put money in,” Hicks said. The other fired Democratic EAC commissioner, Benjamin Hovland, said in an interview: “Even in the best of years, the federal government is paying a very small portion. But it’s an important piece.”

Funding for the grants peaked early in the program. Congress approved $380 million in 2018 and $425 million in 2020, and election officials also received $400 million in pandemic relief aid that year. The grants then fell to $75 million in both 2022 and 2023, $55 million in 2024 and $15 million in 2025. The 2026 grant is $45 million. Grants are divided among states and territories by formula, and most states receive the minimum: the 2026 amount leaves most with $819,000 each and requires a 20% match.

The Project for Election Infrastructure, a nonpartisan advocacy group, has called on Congress to provide $400 million a year. A letter it released in May carried signatures from nearly 90 state and local election officials, who warned that inadequate funding can produce long lines at the polls and delays in processing mail ballots. Executive director Sarah Hague called the $45 million amount “pennies” for the needs of election workers and said it “only scratches the surface.” She said, “We have a lot of catch-up to do.”

In Washington state, Clark County Auditor Greg Kimsey, whose county includes Vancouver, asked county leadership in March for approval to spend nearly $900,000 on new mail-sorting machines, equipment he said was critical for processing ballot envelopes in a state that conducts elections by mail. He also bought cameras for about $600,000 to monitor ballot drop boxes after a box was bombed in Vancouver on Oct. 28, 2024, burning hundreds of ballots. Officials later identified voters for 488 damaged ballots, and a similar incident occurred in nearby Portland, Oregon. Until a few years ago, Kimsey said all of his office’s election capital expenditures were funded through grants. “Looking forward, we made the assumption that these federal grant dollars were not going to be available,” Kimsey said.

Federal support has had bipartisan backing, but the Trump administration and some Republicans in Congress have sought to condition future grants on election policies favored by the president. In July, the U.S. Department of Homeland Security announced that states seeking some DHS grant dollars would have to use a federal computer program to check registered voters’ citizenship status and meet other election requirements. A federal judge blocked the change in September.

The Republican-controlled House Appropriations Committee approved a bill in April that would have cut EAC salaries and expenses from $23.86 million to $17 million and reduced the election security grant program from $45 million to $15 million. The bill also included a prohibition on using funds to register noncitizens to vote, although noncitizen voting was already illegal. Congress did not pass the reductions and instead maintained current spending for the EAC and grants, while the Senate did not pass the SAVE America Act, Trump’s signature elections bill, which would have required voters to prove their citizenship.

A spokesperson for Rep. Dave Joyce, the Ohio Republican who chairs the House Appropriations Financial Services and General Government Subcommittee, did not answer an interview request. The subcommittee oversees spending levels for the EAC and the election security grants, and several other returning members did not respond or declined comment. A spokesperson for Washington Democrat Rep. Marie Gluesenkamp Perez referred States Newsroom to her previous remarks about election funding. Gluesenkamp Perez represents the southwest corner of Washington, including Clark County. “Last general election, my ballot boxes were firebombed. They have never caught the person who did it,” Gluesenkamp Perez said at a 2025 subcommittee meeting. “If you think this can’t happen all across the country, we are setting ourselves up for a very dangerous situation.”

The White House did not answer questions about whether Trump continues to support linking grant funding to requirements such as proof of citizenship or whether he plans to nominate new EAC commissioners. After Trump fired the commissioners, the White House said the administration had been working to “safeguard elections from fraud and abuse” and investing in “strong infrastructure” to sustain that mission. Tim Harper, a project lead focused on elections at the nonpartisan Center for Democracy & Technology, said the dismissals threaten the independence of what is supposed to be a neutral agency providing nonpartisan guidance and help to election officials. Harper said the EAC’s independence allows states to trust that its recommendations are not influenced by partisan goals. “That independence is essential to its core mission,” Harper said.

Trump last year attempted to use the EAC unilaterally to require voters to offer proof of citizenship, but a federal court blocked the move. The administration argues that the president enjoys sweeping control over the executive branch and that Congress has limited power to place agencies beyond that control. In June, the U.S. Supreme Court at least partially affirmed that view by ruling that Trump could fire members of the Federal Trade Commission without cause. Hicks said he is still considering a legal challenge to his dismissal and warned that allowing it would open the door to future presidents, including Democrats, also removing the EAC’s leadership. “It goes beyond this administration,” Hicks said.

Democrats enter the incoming Congress with proposals for a larger federal role, but legislation must pass a Senate where most measures need 60 votes. The EAC also remains without members, while the grant program still operates with uneven funding and policy requirements. In Kentucky, Hardin County Clerk Brian Smith is preparing for 2028; in Washington, Clark County Auditor Greg Kimsey is protecting current operations after assuming federal grant dollars would be unavailable.
