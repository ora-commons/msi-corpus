## George Washington’s 5,000-person crew consumes 48,000 sandwiches monthly

The Wall Street Journal reported strong demand for Uncrustables among American servicemembers on Navy ships around the world. The report said fighter pilots would not go on missions without the snacks, supply officers could not stop thinking about them, and sailors of all ranks were convinced their peers were hoarding them.

Aboard the USS George Washington in the Arabian Sea, the 5,000-person crew eats 48,000 of the peanut butter and jelly snacks each month, a number short of actual demand, according to Lt. Cmdr. Brian Lasley, the carrier’s principal assistant for services. “Uncrustables have taken up more brain space in my career than I care to admit,” Lasley said with a laugh. “We absolutely have to distribute them like rations.”

Culinary specialists passed out the sandwiches on mess decks and in other locations, according to the report. Morgan said word travels fast when the sandwiches are being offered, and sailors who obtain one are stopped and asked where they got it. The sandwiches are sometimes traded for other valuable items.

The Navy’s decision to make ship versions whole grain did not reduce demand. Lasley said, “The Navy decided at some point to at least make the ones on ships whole grain, but even making them ‘healthier’ hasn’t blunted demand,” adding, “Our sailors swarm the service line anytime we have them out.” Public affairs officer Lt. Cmdr. Bobby Dixon said the sandwiches are devoured so quickly that they are often called “un-crush-ables.”

On the front lines of the Iran war, pilots and aircrew are known to pack Uncrustables in their go-bags for seven-hour missions to strike Iranian targets or protect commercial ships transiting the Strait of Hormuz, according to The Wall Street Journal. One fighter pilot said he was far more anxious about forgetting an Uncrustable than he was about getting shot down by Iran. The pilot said the sandwiches help prevent him from crashing out during long missions.

Mass Communication Specialist 2nd Class Bruce Morgan said, “It’s quick, no mess, and it reminds me of home.” He added, “On long days, it’s nice to have something simple and familiar.” Beyond Morgan’s remarks, the report said the sandwiches can be stuffed in cargo pockets and enjoyed later after a long shift.

The report said the plastic-wrapped PB&Js had become even more coveted during this year’s deployments in the Middle East, where sailors were operating in an intense environment with little fresh food or certainty about when they would be back on land.

For months, the USS George Washington had operated as the late-night carrier in the Arabian Sea after arriving in August to replace the USS Abraham Lincoln. The Lincoln had made headlines because of concerns about living conditions and mental health. Supply issues affecting crews earlier in the Iran war were smoothed out after the Navy started routing goods through the island base of Diego Garcia in the Indian Ocean, according to Navy officials. Even with more food options on board, the popularity of Uncrustables remained high.

The crustless, crimped sandwiches follow in the footsteps of Pop Tarts, ramen and cheese balls that served as deployment staples for older surface-warfare officers. Retired Capt. Michael Stewart, a former Navy aviator, also coveted the sandwiches and recalled eating boxed-lunch turkey sandwiches on missions over the Indian Ocean in 1993. A fellow pilot once pranked Stewart by loading his sandwich with mayonnaise. “I would have appreciated the sealed packaging after the mayonnaise incident,” Stewart joked.

Adm. Daryl Caudle, the chief of Naval Operations and a former submarine commander, recalled how comfort foods such as pizza and burgers kept crews going during long days at sea. “If there’s one thing I’ve learned in more than 40 years in the Navy, it’s that you never underestimate the importance of good chow,” he said. Caudle added: “If Uncrustables are helping fuel the world’s most powerful Navy, then I’m all for looking at how we can keep more of them coming.”

Mark Smucker, CEO of Uncrustables maker J.M. Smucker, comes from a family of servicemembers. His paternal grandfather, Paul Smucker, served as a U.S. Navy line officer aboard the destroyer USS Cassin Young during World War II before becoming the company’s CEO. His maternal grandfather, Sam Coddington, was also a fighter pilot on an aircraft carrier during the war, flying Vought F4U Corsair fighter planes. After hearing about the sandwich’s popularity on the carrier, Smucker said, “To be part of those missions, and to be part of the sailors’ mealtime, it’s pretty special.”

J.M. Smucker said its Uncrustables business across all branches of the military is up 60% from last year. Smucker said the snack is “part of culture, and it does bring nostalgia,” adding that it has the right combination of carbs and protein for active servicemembers.

Caudle addressed sailors enjoying an Uncrustable between watches, flight operations or maintenance shifts: “To all our sailors out there enjoying an Uncrustable between watches, flight operations, or maintenance shifts, keep doing what you do best.” He added: “If a little PB&J helps keep you ready to fight and win, then by all means, carry on.”
