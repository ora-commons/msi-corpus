## Clermont County lawsuit follows heavy metals in at least 20 wells

Down winding rural roads in Clermont County, dump trucks head toward a 1,400-acre landfill owned by Dynegy Zimmer LLC, a Vistra Corp subsidiary. The site has served as a dumping ground for waste from coal-powered plants around the Cincinnati region for decades, and The Guardian described it as containing coal ash and other hazardous materials. Brian and Rhonda Brittain said the sight of the trucks fills them with fear and anger.

In consecutive rounds of testing conducted this year and for several previous years, Dynegy Zimmer's own reporting showed “statistically significant levels above the applicable groundwater protection standards” for lithium at the landfill. The Brittains' property abuts Little Indian Creek, the waterway on which the landfill was constructed and which flows into the Ohio River four miles downstream. Millions of people in Cincinnati and Louisville depend on the Ohio River for drinking water.

Brian Brittain said, “Everything that’s coming out of [the landfill] is coming right through this pipe and into this pond,” pointing to a neighbor's watering hole surrounded by a half dozen livestock. He said, “These people raise cattle to feed their family.” Brian and Rhonda Brittain have been battling sulfuric odors and concerns about environmental hazards associated with the landfill. “The smell is only the warning system,” Brian Brittain said.

The Guardian said emails it sent to the Ohio Environmental Protection Agency asking whether the agency would facilitate independent testing of water inside the landfill were not answered. A Vistra spokesperson declined the same question but said coal ash was not currently being dumped at the site. Jenny Lyon of Vistra said, “Since 2007, the site has accepted inert, limestone-based byproducts from the Miami Fort power plant, which are classified as non-hazardous waste under applicable regulations.” She said the materials consist primarily of naturally occurring substances, including silica and river sediment. “We recognize residents’ concerns about odors and take them seriously … Vistra has invested in multiple systems and processes to manage and minimize odors,” Lyon said.

The Guardian reported that coal-powered electricity plants have been phased out in recent years, but their closures have left a legacy of coal combustion residual, or CCR, landfills. The residual material typically consists of ash, boiler slag, silica and other waste substances. The report said dangerous constituents in the waste can cause cancer and neurological illnesses, and communities along this stretch of the Ohio River have dealt with the fallout for years.

The report cited a national lung and bronchus cancer incidence rate of 51.8 per 100,000 residents, compared with 69.6 in Clermont County and 62 in neighboring Hamilton County; much of the waste being dumped at the landfill came from Hamilton County. The defunct William H. Zimmer power plant was a five-minute drive from the landfill, on the banks of the Ohio River. It was shuttered in 2022 after taking 20 years to build and operating for three decades. A further five-minute drive down the river, the Beckjord coal-powered electricity plant operated for 75 years before closing in 2014.

Clermont County administrators are suing Duke Energy, Beckjord's former operator, and Missouri-based Commercial Liability Partners, a company that specializes in tearing down former industrial plants and now owns the facility. The lawsuit followed 12 years of pollution, during which heavy metals were detected in at least 20 water wells. According to local media, unlined holding ponds at Beckjord contain billions of pounds of toxins, and residents stopped drinking tap water as a result. County authorities face a $30m bill for a new water-treatment plant capable of removing the pollutants and have already spent tens of thousands of dollars on legal fees.

Commercial Liability Partners is Clermont County's largest property taxpayer, but authorities said earlier in the year that the company owed the community more than $640,000 in delinquent taxes.

Thom Cmar, an attorney at Earthjustice, an environmental protection nonprofit that tracks coal ash contamination across the country, said: “The Zimmer landfill, because of its status as a legacy landfill, hasn’t had to meet the same safety and design standards that landfills being built today [have].” Cmar said no barriers were installed to ensure coal ash disposed at the site would not contact groundwater. He also said, “Unfortunately, states like Ohio have always been deferential to the utility industry and a lot of states where coal-burning is an industry have been willing to let [them] take the lead in setting the safety standards and best practices that they follow.”

The Guardian reported that Texas-headquartered Vistra Corp, described as a $44bn energy company, has faced fines and lawsuits across the country for market manipulation, chemical fires and explosions and other pollution violations. In 2023, Vistra subsidiary Dynegy Midwest Generation settled for $400,000 in a case in which it was accused of dumping coal ash near a river at an Illinois facility.

The report also said Vistra had received billions of dollars in federal government grants. Trump and former House Democratic leader Nancy Pelosi were among the company's high-profile purchasers of its shares this year. Vistra settled for $38m the previous year following charges of market manipulation.

The Guardian noted that silica and many other naturally occurring substances can be dangerous to human health in certain quantities. It cited the deaths of at least 764 West Virginia miners in the 1930s after exposure to high levels of silica dust.

According to the Clermont County auditor's website, Vistra, through Dynegy Zimmer, has amassed dozens of parcels totaling more than 3,400 acres in the Brittains' Washington township, an area equivalent to around one-quarter of the island of Manhattan. Locals said corporate land ownership caused financial strain in the community and restricted access to a local cemetery because of the landfill. “When they devalued their property, the township had to pay money back to Dynegy Zimmer. We lost a lot of money that was already in the budget,” Rhonda Brittain said. She said the $139,000 that schools, libraries and other Clermont County entities had to repay to Vistra in 2023 was money already included in their budgets.

Brian Brittain, a diesel engine mechanic, believes the land purchases were intended to keep the public away from the landfill and hide its dangers. “It seems like nobody wants to spend any money to cure this problem, but they were just given a ton of money [a $4.2bn loan from the department of energy] for a plant outside Cleveland,” he said. “But you can’t spend money here to try to fix it?”

Environmental groups and residents are also fighting back. In Illinois, a lawsuit forced Dynegy in 2023 to move around three million cubic yards of coal ash that was leaching into a major waterway, although the project was still awaiting approval from the Illinois EPA. On the banks of the Ohio River, Brian and Rhonda Brittain are taking on the fight. “I got into this issue because I have grandchildren [and] four of them live with us,” Brian Brittain said. The family asked the company to address the contamination: “We are just asking that they spend the money to fix it. We know they have it.”
