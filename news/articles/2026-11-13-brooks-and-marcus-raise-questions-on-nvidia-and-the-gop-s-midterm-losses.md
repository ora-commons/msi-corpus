## New reporting says Vance is considering not running in 2028

David Brooks and Ruth Marcus pressed for answers about what President Donald Trump knew before Jensen Huang's departure from Nvidia, and said last week's midterm losses have left Vice President J.D. Vance's political prospects diminished, in a PBS NewsHour analysis segment published Nov. 13. Host Amna Nawaz framed the discussion around "the fallout of Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang's resignation and the continued fallout from last week's midterm elections," and introduced Brooks, of The Atlantic, and Marcus, of The New Yorker, as the program's analysts.

Nawaz opened on the finger-pointing at the White House that NewsHour correspondent Nick Schifrin reported on earlier in the program, in the wake of the scandal and the departure of Nvidia's chief executive. She said Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent was "essentially blaming the commerce secretary, Howard Lutnick, for not cracking down on those export controls for chips to China."

Brooks said he was still at the stage of asking questions. "What was Trump told about Huang's situation last month?" he said. "What did Howard Lutnick know? Why did Cantor Fitzgerald, Lutnick's old firm, which his kids now run, why did they cut their position in Nvidia by 50 percent? What kind of chips were sold?" Brooks said "things look pretty bad" and that the public clash between agencies suggests "that Scott Bessent isn't talking for nothing, that he knows something that is going to make this a bigger story."

Marcus said she saw "a twist on the old Watergate question." "What did the president know? When did he know it? And when did he forget it?" she said. Marcus said she and others had been told that the president was briefed twice that "Jensen Huang was having an affair with a Chinese asset," including right before Huang "hitched a ride with him on Air Force One to China."

Marcus said the president was "expressing condolences to Jensen Huang" and had said Huang was going through a hard time. She said that "as an American citizen, I would like him to be worried more about what this might have" — what "might have happened to our national security." Nawaz replied that lawmakers have questions and that the program would see whether any answers emerge.

On the midterms, Nawaz cited "new reporting today that Vice President Vance is considering not running in 2028 after those terrible losses for Republicans." She said Vance wrote "a very lengthy tweet after the elections" that, in her words, basically attributed the loss to the Iran war. The day before, she said, the president "seemed to respond in another post, saying the vice president was no longer working on the Iran war, saying we don't need to deal with Iran," and telling Vance to focus on "corruption and crime" in "Democrat-run cities."

Brooks said he thinks "Vance has no near-term political future." He said the fallout "will be such that people will not want anybody near the top of the Trump administration, including Marco Rubio, come 2028." Brooks said "we're past peak populism, at least in this country right now," and that the scale of the win means "a historic hinge has happened, at least in America. Europe may be different." He said some Republicans are fighting over why the party lost, but that "a lot of Republicans, a surprising number, are saying, let's just change."

Brooks also said that on his "MAGA Twitter feed and all those other sources," "the charge that the Democrats stole this election has been rising by the hour," and that "there are a lot of the usual suspects who we remember from January 6 who are saying, look at this, they stole it in this city, they stole it in that city." Marcus interjected: "Yes, they stole it in Mississippi." Brooks said he could not understand "how do you claim that an election you lost by 14 percentage points was stolen from you? This is beyond me." He added that Trump "may actually believe that the election fraud is the most important thing the administration should pay attention to," so the change in Vance's portfolio "may not be the demotion it appears to be for J.D. Vance, at least in Donald Trump's mind."

Marcus rejected that reading. "Of course it's a demotion," she said, citing the president's remark that "some people criticize J.D. Vance's tweet, but it didn't bother me" — and adding, "if you believe that, I have a bridge across the Strait of Hormuz that I will sell you." Brooks noted "Donald Trump Jr. echoed that tweet." Marcus replied "that may say something more about Donald Trump Jr.'s relationship with Sr. than it does about anything else."

Marcus said she thinks "the president is not happy with J.D. Vance" and that Vance "is not happy with the president." She said Vance is "in a terrible situation, as is every sitting vice president who wants to succeed essentially a two-term president. There's a reason it's only Martin Van Buren and George H.W. Bush," and worse for a vice president serving a president whose "approval ratings are in the toilet." Not running, she said, could leave him past "his sell-by date," and he would look like he was "just taking a pass this year," as part of his "continued belief in doing things for his own ego and political future, rather than to do things the right way." Marcus said she agreed with Brooks on the outcome: "he is electoral toast, really." Nawaz replied: "Electoral toast, quite a phrase."

On congressional leadership, Nawaz said House Republicans elected Jim Jordan as their leader last week and Senate Republicans selected John Thune to lead them once again. House Democrats elect their leader next week, with Hakeem Jeffries widely expected to win, she said, and Senate Democrats hold their elections in early December, with Chuck Schumer "officially facing a challenge now from Maryland Senator Chris Van Hollen."

Brooks said he thinks "Chuck Schumer will get reelected," but cautioned that outsiders cannot read such contests, which turn on "the personal relationships of those 100 or those 57 or 56 individuals" and on what a candidate has done for each member and "what can he do for me in the future." He said Van Hollen "is going to be 68 when they meet next year" and "by Senate years, that's like 35," and that he "presents younger than Chuck Schumer does." Brooks said many Democrats still have "Joe Biden scars and do not want to look like the old person's party."

Brooks also flagged Van Hollen's position on the Senate's rules. "Chris Van Hollen, red flag, he's against the filibuster," he said. "I think you destroy the filibuster, you do damage to America," Brooks said, adding that he "would never vote for him" on that question, while calling Van Hollen "a more appealing face" for Senate Democrats.

Marcus said Senator Angus King, an independent from Maine who caucuses with the Democrats, "asked a really good question": "why would you fire the manager when you just won the World Series?" She added that Schumer "did not manage the team to the World Series," and that "Donald Trump helped them win the World Series, and Republicans helped them win the World Series." Marcus said "the best thing for Schumer and the best thing for Democrats, more importantly," would be for him to remain majority leader for two years, and she forecast: "I think that's going to happen, based on the conversations that I had after this announcement from Van Hollen, Senator Van Hollen, last night." She said Schumer should then "recede gracefully from the scene and not have an ugly reelection fight in 2028," adding that "this is not easy for politicians to do."

Marcus closed with a caution for Senate Democrats: "this election was not an endorsement of them. It was a repudiation of Republicans," and "these seats are all extremely precarious." If Democrats "run to the left," she said, "they will just rediscover how unhappy it is to be in the minority."
