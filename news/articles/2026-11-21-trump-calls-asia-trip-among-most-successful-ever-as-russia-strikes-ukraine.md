## Zelenskyy calls Putin 'a killer using terrorism against civilians'

President Donald Trump returned from his trip to Asia this week and described it as one of the most successful presidential visits to that continent, or anywhere else, in American history.

Trump's account came at an hour-and-a-half-long press conference at the White House, where he took questions on a trip that included the first-ever trilateral meeting with the leaders of China and Russia.

In China, Trump met Chinese President Xi Jinping and Russian President Vladimir Putin around what NPR senior contributor Ron Elving described as an enormous circular table. Elving, speaking with host Scott Simon, said the arrangement was "to some degree, a symbol of equality among the three men, but still staged to place Chinese President Xi as host."

"We know that President Trump cares a lot about visuals and staging, and here we had perhaps the most elaborate photo opportunity in history," Elving said.

Simon noted that some commentators compared the meeting to the 1945 Yalta Conference, where post-war leaders divided Eastern Europe. Trump said the parallel had not been on his mind. "I didn't think about it. I just went saying, I get along great with these two people. We'll work out our differences. We've been able to work them out," Trump said, adding that "then I realized how big it was. It was an amazing event."

Elving said the week's claims did not match what the meeting produced. "If you watched the news conference yesterday as a Trump admirer, you got plenty to feed that," he said. "Otherwise, you could have felt the claims of peacemaking and dealmaking in Asia were long on promise and woefully short on details."

Elving said that when Trump was asked whether Putin was a friend to America, the president said he could not speak to America, which Elving called "a remarkable thing for a president to say." Elving said Trump then returned to the 2016 episode, when Putin denied colluding with Trump's first campaign, and said how much he had appreciated Putin's support in that episode.

Ukraine's leader, Volodymyr Zelenskyy, responded hours later, saying that for Ukraine, Putin was not a friend but "a killer using terrorism against civilians."

Elving said the summit could carry real-world consequences "if these various vows of peaceful cooperation bear fruit, or if these vaunted trade deals come to pass." What is known, he said, is that Russia this week launched "one of its most massive assaults against Ukraine since it first invaded that country in 2022," with assaults against civilian targets that also struck Poland, a NATO ally of the U.S.

Elving said it also made a difference that "Trump was more interested in meeting with Chinese President Xi than he was in confronting that country's efforts to obtain the most advanced American computer chips."

On the Democratic side, Elving said Hakeem Jeffries is now the official Democratic nominee for House speaker, with 251 votes out of 262 cast — "way more than he'll need to be formally elected in January." He contrasted that with Nancy Pelosi's last nomination for speaker, when she "barely got over 200 votes and had to do some heavy lifting to bring her dissidents on board." Jeffries, Elving said, "can concentrate on Trump and the Republicans from a secure position."

Senate Democrats have yet to vote on their leadership, Elving said, and the presumed leader there, Chuck Schumer of New York, "has a long history in leadership and perhaps too much baggage for younger Democrats." Chris Van Hollen of Maryland has challenged Schumer and promises a fresh face, Elving said, but "if there's a big move to Van Hollen happening among Senate Democrats, it remains below the surface."

Turning to Elon Musk, whom Elving described as the richest person in the world and the biggest spender of the midterms, the commentator noted the red ball cap that said Trump was right about everything. "So maybe not so right about Elon Musk," Elving said. He said Musk's promise to cut trillions from the federal budget fizzled, and that Musk then spent something approaching half a billion dollars on the midterms and got shut out.

"He is a brilliant engineer and businessman, to be sure, but that does not always translate to success in politics," Elving said. "Republicans won't miss him on the campaign trail if he's really through with it, but they may well miss that money."
