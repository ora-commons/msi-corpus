## Pentagon calls damaged artifact a replica; no one was hurt

Photographs published Thursday by The Washington Post showed the shattered 30-inch globe. The Guardian described the object as dating from before the Second World War. A U.S. Army spokesperson confirmed in an email to the newspaper that damage occurred during “an attempted move.” The February move was intended to place the object in Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth’s office in the Pentagon.

One photograph showed fragments of the globe beneath its wooden frame on a blue hallway carpet. Another showed an inches-wide gash in the bottom and the base punched through. “The spherical portion of the globe was detached from the metal mounting that allows it to rotate,” the Army spokesperson said.

The Washington Post, citing people familiar with the February incident, reported that Hegseth’s aides moved quickly to enact the relocation. The Post’s sources said the aides did not wait to engage specialist movers, who would have packed the globe with precision and care, and instead dispatched their own team.

One source said the aides “lost control of the artifact and dropped it, sending the globe crashing on to its base and impaling the fragile shell near Antarctica,” and blamed an “apparent lack of patience.”

According to the Post’s account, Hegseth asked for one of two similar artifacts dating to the 1930s and housed in the Pentagon. The Post reported that the globe’s historical significance attracted him and that the artifact was believed to have been used by Dwight D Eisenhower, the 34th U.S. president, while he served as allied supreme commander and planned the D-Day landings.

The Guardian reported that Hegseth has an interest in military history and that he said he had been instilling a “warrior ethos” in the U.S. military since his January 2025 confirmation.

A Defense Department spokesperson referred to the agency as the “war department” during Donald Trump’s second term, according to The Guardian. The department offered a different description of the object: “An accident occurred while staff were transporting a replica of the Eisenhower globe as part of its relocation,” an official statement said.

The department added, “Accidents happen, we are grateful no one was harmed, and the globe is being repaired.” The statement said, “It is unsurprising that the Washington Post is once again dedicating time to another inconsequential non-story.”

Ultimately, the globe was not relocated and is undergoing repair. The Post attributed its account of the aides dropping the globe to people familiar with the February incident, while the Army spokesperson confirmed the attempted move and resulting damage.

Craters & Freighters posted about a successful 2022 relocation of an Eisenhower globe between two Army facilities in Alabama. The company said: “Since the globe is considered priceless, we knew its value was much greater than financial, and that our team was heavily relied upon for a safe delivery.” It described how the company designed and precision-built protective crating and highlighted the expertise and care required for the undertaking.

The competing accounts do not resolve whether the damaged object was the globe associated with Eisenhower’s D-Day planning or a replica of it. The Army acknowledged the attempted move and resulting damage, while the Defense Department said the globe was being repaired and no one was harmed.
