## Senate Democrats and Republicans ask what officials told Nvidia's board

President Donald Trump said on Tuesday that he would be open to pardoning Nvidia chief executive Jensen Huang for potential crimes, though he said he did not expect a pardon to be necessary. Speaking to the New York Post, Trump said, "It's always an option, I guess," adding, "But I don't think it'll be necessary."

In the same interview, Trump said he wanted Huang to account for his conduct. "I think I want [Huang] to just tell the truth, and I mean, his lawyers are going to have to advise him," he said, adding that "I've always found him to be 100% aboveboard, and you know, a very smart person."

The remark came after Trump intervened in Nvidia's leadership turmoil, pressing the company's board to reinstall Huang as chief executive despite other lawmakers' concerns over whether Huang posed a national security risk for having an affair with an alleged Chinese spy.

Huang has not been formally accused of any crime. Attorney General Todd Blanche told reporters on Tuesday that no evidence indicated Huang had committed a crime, saying that if the Justice Department held information showing Huang was committing crimes, "that's something that I would have certainly told the president had it been true."

Blanche did not confirm whether the Justice Department had conducted a thorough investigation into Huang, saying only that law enforcement agents "looked into" the situation. Days earlier, Trump told CNBC that his administration had done "a pretty good investigation" to exonerate Huang.

Huang is expected to testify to congressional committees in the coming weeks about Nvidia's chip exports, his alleged affair and the US government's role in having Nvidia reinstate him as chief executive.

Republican Senator John Curtis, who sits on the Senate commerce committee, questioned what senior US officials told Nvidia's board. "Nvidia's board says it relied on input from senior U.S. officials to conclude that bringing Jensen Huang back posed no national security risk," he wrote on X. "I have some questions. Who in our government weighed in? What did they tell the board? And on what basis did the board conclude there was no risk?"

Republican Senator Pete Ricketts, who sits on the Senate foreign relations committee, wrote on X on Monday that "Nvidia's decision today doesn't excuse Jensen Huang from testifying before Congress. It also doesn't absolve Nvidia of answering for national security concerns and unaccounted-for chips."

Republican Senator John Kennedy, who sits on the Senate banking committee, told Fox News that he wanted to know more about Huang's alleged affair for national security purposes, saying: "It is my interest, though, if he was having sex with a Chinese spy."

Elizabeth Warren, the top Democrat on the Senate banking committee, wrote on X on Monday that "The Trump Administration needs to explain what 'input' was given and by which senior officials to support Huang's reinstatement." Democratic Senator Elissa Slotkin, who sits on the Senate homeland security committee, raised the question of whether Trump "knew about the potential compromise with the alleged Chinese spy influencing Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang" and "did nothing about it."

Lawmakers from both sides of the aisle have questions about the US government's determination, and their questions converge on the same subjects: which senior officials provided input to Nvidia's board, what they told it, and on what basis the board concluded that reinstating Huang posed no national security risk. Two matters remain unresolved in public — whether the Justice Department opened a thorough investigation into Huang and what it found, and the content of the government's advice to Nvidia's board.


Trump's defense of Huang has run through the same period as the reinstatement fight. [MSI previously reported](/articles/2026-11-24-trump-says-the-country-needs-more-geniuses-as-nvidia-reinstates-huang/) that Trump defended Huang's return by saying "He did nothing wrong" and arguing "I believe that you have to protect our geniuses." The pardon option carries that advocacy into the legal questions now before Congress.
