## Defense counters that much of the disputed material was already public

An indictment in Huawei's New York trial laid out a long pattern of alleged technology thefts by a company that grew into one of the world's biggest suppliers of gear for wireless telecommunications networks. Prosecutors argued that Huawei repeatedly stole technological secrets from competitors and partners in the United States and elsewhere.

Prosecutors said Huawei once sold internet routers containing source code copied from a competitor. The indictment said that, in another episode, a person working for Huawei slipped into a competitor's booth at a Chicago trade show to surreptitiously photograph the circuits inside another company's networking device. Prosecutors said that on another occasion, a Huawei worker removed a robotic arm from a competitor's lab so it could be photographed and measured.

The victim companies included Cisco Systems, Motorola Solutions, T-Mobile, Quintel USA and Fujitsu, according to court records. The indictment also accused Huawei of doing business in countries subject to U.S. sanctions, including Iran and North Korea, and making false statements to U.S. government officials and banks as part of an effort to hide its misdeeds.

Telling the jury at the trial, prosecutor Meredith Arfa said: “The tone was set at the top by Huawei leaders and executives who themselves engaged in criminal conduct, like lying to Congress, lying to banks, violating U.S. sanctions and trying to interfere with the very criminal investigation that led to this case.”

Huawei's lawyers said U.S. investigators had used isolated episodes of wrongdoing by a few of the company's more than 200,000 employees to spin a false narrative about a grand conspiracy that did not exist. In his closing argument to jurors, Heberlig said prosecutors relied on “innuendo and facts ripped out of context,” along with testimony from former employees “with a bone to pick,” to pursue a company that had succeeded through “hard work, innovation and competition.” “Huawei didn’t steal its way to success,” he said, noting that much of the material the U.S. alleges was stolen was already public. “You can’t steal a secret that isn’t a secret,” he said.

Heberlig said Huawei paid billions of dollars in licensing fees over the years, with 75% of that money going to U.S. companies. He also said the company had reprimanded or fired employees found to have engaged in wrongdoing.

Heberlig further said lines of Cisco source code turned up in Huawei software more than two decades ago. When the copying was discovered, he said, Huawei recalled routers sold in the United States, offered to replace them with Cisco routers and created a “compliance culture that permeates Huawei today.”

On Tuesday, a U.S. jury in New York returned a split decision after a lengthy trial. It convicted Huawei of conspiracy to steal trade secrets and bank fraud but acquitted the company of engaging in a criminal racketeering conspiracy, the most serious charge it faced.

That charge typically is used in organized-crime and drug cases, but prosecutors used it to accuse Huawei of operating in an illicit fashion to steal intellectual property from American companies.

Outside court, Heberlig said Huawei would appeal the convictions and described the racketeering acquittal as a vindication. “That was the only count that brought all of the allegations together,” he said, adding that the verdict “repudiated the central government narrative in the case.”

It remains unclear what effect the verdict might have on Huawei's global fortunes. The company has not been allowed to sell equipment to U.S. carriers for years because of security concerns, and it has been blacklisted by other Western allies, including Canada and Britain. That has not stopped Huawei from doing a robust business elsewhere: its smartphones and other telecom equipment are sold globally. More recently, it has expanded into chipmaking as the artificial-intelligence boom fuels demand for microprocessors, becoming an important alternative in China to chips made by companies such as Nvidia.

The United States announced criminal charges against Huawei in early 2019 and later expanded the allegations. Chinese officials said the United States had engaged in “economic bullying” and improperly used national security as a pretext for oppressing Chinese companies.

U.S. prosecutors had originally also brought criminal charges against Huawei chief financial officer Meng Wanzhou, accusing her of misleading HSBC about the company's business dealings in Iran. Meng, the daughter of Huawei's founder, was arrested in Canada in 2018 but ultimately was allowed to return to China in 2021 in a prisoner swap that freed two Canadians held by China. As part of the deal, the United States dropped its case against Meng.

The New York trial overlapped with a summit meeting between President Donald Trump, China's Xi Jinping and Russia's Vladimir Putin in the technology-centric city of Shenzhen, China. The jury's split decision leaves Huawei's global position unsettled, and the company has said it will appeal the convictions.
