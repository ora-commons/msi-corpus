## Tesla reports more than 1.7 million FSD subscriptions without disclosing a senior count

Tesla reports more than 1.7 million active subscriptions to Full Self Driving (Supervised), an increase of 67% from a year ago. It is hard to know exactly how many older drivers use the system, though the numbers may be growing. Tesla has not disclosed how many of those subscribers are older or whether it tracks senior use. Tesla did not reply to requests for comment.

MIT research scientist Bryan Reimer, who studies how drivers interact with technology, said the system has safety gaps that make him uncomfortable. “It's not a technology that I would drive with. It's not a technology that I would suggest that my loved ones use. I think it has a few safety gaps that just make me uncomfortable,” he said. Tesla claims that FSD is up to nine times safer than human driving. Tesla does not release much data about FSD, so Reimer said it is impossible to independently verify whether its safety claims are true. Reimer said Tesla's technology is impressive and getting better all the time, but that it is not a replacement for a human driver, at least not yet. “Tesla has continually over-promised and under-delivered,” Reimer said. “Full self-driving is not full self-driving, but yet the name feels so real.”

Tesla's official communications always use the full title FSD (Supervised) to underscore that the system is not fully autonomous. The driver is supposed to pay attention at all times in case the car encounters a situation FSD cannot handle.

Alexandra Mueller, a senior research scientist at the Insurance Institute for Highway Safety, a nonprofit funded by the insurance industry, said the system's repeated successes can lead drivers to expect it to behave the same way in a similar situation. “It's good enough, often enough, where people go, 'The next time I'm in this similar situation, I know how it's going to behave,'” she said. Mueller worries that older drivers will not be ready or able to take control of the car in an emergency. “This can be a huge problem,” she said. “Ultimately it is very unsafe if you have drivers who are not fully competent due to age-related impairments or decline.”

There are more older drivers on the road than ever, and families often struggle with the question of when it is time to stop driving. Some seniors are using emerging technology to stay behind the wheel longer, specifically automated systems made by Tesla. Reimer said the system has safety gaps that make him uncomfortable, while Mueller raised concerns about whether older drivers could take control in an emergency.

Joan Spalding, an 89-year-old Colorado resident, has driven the steep, winding road to her house outside Denver for more than 50 years. These days, she barely touches the steering wheel or pedals of her Tesla Model Y. She tells the car where she wants to go and presses a button on the large touchscreen in the dashboard. “I just get in and go,” she said. For all practical purposes, the car appears to be driving itself, navigating confidently on twisting roads through postcard valleys full of juniper and ponderosa pine.

As the car pulls onto a dirt track that can barely fit two cars side by side, with a steep drop-off to one side, Spalding says, “Now this is probably one of the narrowest roads.” The Tesla handles the tight turns gracefully and stops to let an SUV squeeze by in the other lane.

Tesla's service is officially called Full Self Driving (Supervised), often abbreviated FSD, and drivers pay for it, usually $99 a month. Spalding was initially skeptical until she saw what it could do. “When I was driving,” she said, “I saw that the kind of things that it did were safer than what I would do.”

Before she bought the Tesla, Spalding had been in a number of minor fender benders. Two years ago, she was in a serious crash while trying to make a left turn. “I made a wrong decision. I wasn't focusing on what I should be doing … I was lucky,” she said. “But I realized then that my abilities were not where I wanted them to be.”

Her son, Mike Spalding, had known for some time that his mother's driving was getting worse. He already drove a Tesla, and it was his idea to get one for her. “It would be safer for mom if she was in a wreck because it's got all kinds of safety features. And then safer for people around her if we didn't have any more crashes. I've been driving one for years, and so I'd experimented with the Full Self-Driving and saw what it could do,” Mike Spalding said. “It's better than me.”

Tesla has not said exactly how many senior drivers use FSD to stay on the road longer. Its chief executive, Elon Musk, has encouraged the practice. In a recent post on X, Musk wrote, “Tesla self-driving gives mobility to seniors.” Several times, he amplified a video from a YouTube channel called TFLEV featuring a 98-year-old man who had just bought his first Tesla.

Musk reposted the video the previous month, writing, “At Tesla, we even care about the chipmunks.” The clip has been viewed more than 3.5 million times. The driver, Larry Dimmock, said in the video, “It has not made a mistake. And I've been driving it probably two or three months and it's not made an error in its driving.” He continued, “In fact, the other day it stopped. A chipmunk was crossing the road and there was no other traffic and it did not run over the chipmunk.” Dimmock joked in an interview with NPR, “My moment of fame, I guess.”

Dimmock says he has owned dozens of cars in his life, beginning with a Ford Model A. He says he is still “a pretty good driver” at 98, though not as good as his Tesla. “I feel safer riding with this auto system than I do driving or riding with anybody. It's just been flawless,” Dimmock said. He added, “I'll get in the car and go somewhere, otherwise, I probably wouldn't.”

Riding along with Joan Spalding, NPR reported that it was easy to understand the appeal for older drivers: the car gives her mobility and freedom she would not have otherwise. For Spalding in Colorado, it is more than just a question of convenience. Spalding says FSD is allowing her to live independently in the house she and her late husband built more than 50 years ago.

“I love the solitude of being in that place and being by myself,” she said. Spalding said that without the mobility FSD gives her, she would likely have to find another place to live where she would not need to drive. “I'd have to move,” she said. “This way, I think I can drive it for as long as I want to. I don't see any reason why not.”
