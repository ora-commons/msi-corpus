## A Myanmar judgment could shape the ICJ's later assessment of Israel

The International Court of Justice is expected to rule Thursday on whether Myanmar's government committed genocide against the Rohingya. The Guardian reported growing fears that the US could sanction ICJ judges, including ad hoc judge Navi Pillay, if the court finds genocide. US ambassador to the UN Mike Waltz said Washington would be watching the ICJ's handling of the proceedings closely, and the stakes extend beyond Myanmar because the judgment could clarify how genocidal intent is established in a later case involving Israel and Gaza.

A finding that Myanmar's government committed genocide would mark the first time the ICJ has held a state responsible for the crime. The court has never previously made such a finding. Proof of intent is central, with the genocide convention defining the crime through acts committed with the “intent to destroy, in whole or in part” a national, ethnic, racial or religious group. A significant factor in previous cases has been how to prove intent.

Lawyers representing Rohingya survivors say patterns of behaviour by Myanmar's military, including killing people as they fled, show clear attempts to destroy the group during assaults on Rakhine state in 2016 and 2017.

A 24-page submission jointly filed by the UK and five other mainly European states argued that the court should examine a range of “facts and circumstances” and assess them “comprehensively and holistically.” It stressed that genocide is not defined solely by killings, and that the treatment of children, gender-based violence and forced displacement are also relevant.

Independent judges have the role of interpreting the genocide convention. ICJ judges have taken advice on protecting their assets from possible US sanctions, The Guardian reported. The report said a genocide judgment could be interpreted by Washington as lowering the “legal threshold” for the crime. The Foundation for Defense of Democracies, a think tank close to the White House, predicted that a genocide ruling would lead the US to sanction the ICJ.

US ambassador to the UN Mike Waltz warned Pillay and the 15 ICJ judges that Washington “will not allow globalist, faux judges to weaponise the law against our troops.” Last month, Waltz asserted: “The ICJ ad hoc judge Navi Pillay is tipping the scales on the ICJ’s current proceedings on Myanmar, intending to lower the legal threshold for proving genocide so that weakened and false standard can later be weaponised against American and allied soldiers whenever they raise a finger against terrorists that hide behind civilians”. In remarks addressed to Pillay, Waltz said: “We see your agenda and we’ll be watching the ICJ’s handling of these proceedings very closely.”

In a speech last Friday, US secretary of state Marco Rubio said: “For this global elite, international law … is an instrument to advance their interests under the pretence of universal principles.” He warned the ICC against quoting laws to “men with swords.”

The Guardian reported that the US and Israel fear the Myanmar judgment may clarify how to establish a government's intent to commit genocide and set a precedent that makes it more likely the ICJ will later rule that Israel committed genocide in Gaza. Israel has previously criticised adverse advisory opinions issued by the ICJ and refused to implement 2024 orders requiring Israel to admit more aid into Gaza and cooperate with the UN relief and works agency.

The Gambia, which brought the case as a litigant, was entitled to appoint an ad hoc judge and chose Pillay, a South African lawyer who received the Nobel Peace Prize in October. Pillay is required to act as a jurist rather than an advocate for The Gambia.

The US had until recently taken a relatively benign view of the Hague-based ICJ, The Guardian reported. Under Antony Blinken as secretary of state, the US determined that Myanmar had committed genocide. Myanmar has denied carrying out genocide and says its actions targeted a military uprising by insurgents.

The ICJ and International Criminal Court have different mandates. Sometimes described as the UN's top court, the ICJ settles interstate disputes, while the ICC prosecutes individuals accused of war crimes and other international crimes. The US has already imposed sanctions on ICC judges after the court issued arrest warrants against Benjamin Netanyahu and has announced sanctions against the entire ICC as an institution, which it described as a “rogue court.” Washington fears the ICC could pursue US troops.

The 15 ICJ judges are elected for nine-year terms by the UN Security Council and UN General Assembly. Rubio has given the ICC until April to respond to his criticisms before he imposes sanctions. On Friday, the ICC formally decided to drop plans to prosecute US soldiers and intelligence agents for potentially unlawful acts in Afghanistan. The near-dormant inquiry was shelved because evidence would be hard to obtain, and the report said that could make the US more willing to abandon its sanctions plan.
