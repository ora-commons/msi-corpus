## Secret ballot could give Van Hollen a major upset

On Nov. 17, The Wall Street Journal reported that Sen. Chris Van Hollen (D., Md.) is challenging Sen. Chuck Schumer (D., N.Y.) in a secret ballot for the position that will make the winner next year's Senate majority leader. The outlet said the contest follows a midterm result in which Democrats picked up either eight or nine seats, depending on what happens in Alaska, giving them what it described as a "gigantic majority" next year.

The role gives its holder control of the Senate agenda, authority over who gets confirmed, a role in standing toe to toe with President Trump and responsibility for leading the Democratic Party into the future. The Wall Street Journal called it one of the most powerful jobs in Washington.

Leadership challenges are uncommon. Republicans have had their share of congressional leadership mutinies on the House side, but a challenge for the Senate Democratic leadership is rare. Schumer helped Democrats navigate the 2026 midterms and emerge with a large majority, making an attempt to unseat him after that result potentially counterintuitive, according to The Wall Street Journal.

The contest is not a simple clash between old and new. Schumer is 75, while Van Hollen is 67 and was first elected to Congress in 2002. Damian Paletta, the outlet's Washington coverage chief, wrote that "2028 has begun," even though 2026 was not technically over.

Democrats are still working through how to reconcile the party's position on Israel and how to stand up to Trump during his final two years in office. Paletta said the Democratic Party that won a couple of weeks ago may not be as beholden to the old ways of politicking as the party Schumer had long been part of.

The Wall Street Journal reported that Sen.-elect Abdul El-Sayed of Michigan has signaled that he would prefer Van Hollen.

It also reported that Sen. John Fetterman (D., Pa.) has hinted he would leave the party if Van Hollen gets the job. That hint widened the stakes described in the newsletter: the contest was presented not only as a choice of leader but as a test of the party's future direction.

The secret ballot leaves preferences hidden until the vote. Paletta said a Van Hollen victory would be a "huge upset." He also said the contest could create a moment for Democrats to try to extract promises from Schumer that would suit their home-state interests, setting up a choice between cut deals and the possibility of another large victory for Schumer.
