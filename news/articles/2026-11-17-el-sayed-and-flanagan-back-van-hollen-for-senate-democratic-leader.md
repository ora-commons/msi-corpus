## Schumer outlines Congress agenda as Van Hollen seeks to halt Iran war funding

Abdul El-Sayed of Michigan and Peggy Flanagan of Minnesota are the only incoming freshmen who have said how they will vote in the leadership election, and both have said they will support Chris Van Hollen. Both were backed by progressive groups in their campaigns, while Chuck Schumer supported each of their primary opponents; he eventually put $67 million into El-Sayed's competitive race.

El-Sayed said Tuesday that he likes Schumer and will work alongside him if he wins, but he thinks Van Hollen is the best person to address affordability challenges and liberal priorities such as Medicare for All. At The Atlantic Summit in Washington, El-Sayed said, "I do hope that I can have a voice in a large caucus, all of whom have their voices, but this is not about opposition to something."

South Carolina Sen.-elect Annie Andrews, a doctor, said Tuesday that she is in conversations with both candidates and had been "sharing my plans to fix our healthcare system." Incoming Iowa Sen. Josh Turek told reporters he had met both Schumer and Van Hollen. Turek wants more specifics on how they would help rural families, overhaul campaign finance and ban lawmakers from trading stocks. Turek also wants to assess "who is going to put forth the most robust anti-corruption agenda." Neither Andrews nor Turek has publicly announced a leadership choice.

For some of the other freshmen, many of them moderates from Republican-leaning states, the decision could be more difficult. Several said during their campaigns that they would not vote for Schumer, but it was unclear whether any would back Van Hollen. The eight incoming freshman Democratic senators — nine if Mary Peltola wins the Alaska Senate race, which has not been called — are set to alter the caucus next year.

Colorado Sen. John Hickenlooper, asked by reporters on Monday how he would vote, flipped an imaginary coin. "Leave it to the Democrats," he said. "We flipped nine seats, and the person who actually helps put it all together and do all that, and all of a sudden, there's a revolt."

Virginia Sen. Tim Kaine defended Schumer while saying he remained open to Van Hollen or someone else. Kaine said, "Chuck delivered an amazing win," and, "Chuck knows Trump, and that will be really, really important going forward." He added, "The voters are demanding change, so we have to figure that out as a caucus, and we're going to be able to. We're not afraid to have a discussion."

Georgia Sen. Jon Ossoff, who was just reelected, said he had not committed to either candidate. "I want to hear from any candidate what their vision is for the floor and managing the caucus," Ossoff said.

Michigan Sen. Elissa Slotkin had appeared at a press conference last week with Vermont Sen. Bernie Sanders, an independent who caucuses with Democrats, as Sanders declared that he would not support Schumer. Two days later, Sanders immediately endorsed Van Hollen when he entered the race, but Slotkin had not. Most Democrats who returned to Washington on Monday for the first time since Van Hollen entered the race would not answer questions about their support. That included prominent progressive Massachusetts Sen. Elizabeth Warren. Slotkin told reporters Monday, "I'm not saying anything."

Hawaii Sen. Mazie Hirono, who is backing Schumer, said the caucus needs to hear more from the new senators who "have been through the fire to get here." "I think that we have to make sure that all these voices are heard," she said, adding, "They've got a lot of talent to bring to the table."

Schumer has mostly kept quiet publicly while holding private conversations with the senators who will decide the race. At a caucus luncheon last week, he shared his agenda for the next Congress, laying out a plan to try to quickly pass legislation on health care and tariffs and to launch multiple investigations into President Donald Trump's Republican administration. Schumer said, "I'm proud of what we've accomplished together — 55 seats and counting is an amazing result — and I have the votes to continue serving as Democratic Leader." He added, "I look forward to leading our new Democratic majority and delivering for the American people."

The unusually public contest — the first contested election for Senate Democratic leader in decades — comes immediately after the major midterm victories and has forced more moderate incoming Democrats to take sides before they are sworn in. The party normally elects its leader every two years behind closed doors and on a secret ballot.

Schumer, who recruited several of the moderate Democrats who helped win the majority, says he has the votes to be reelected. Around 15 of the 55 elected Democrats in next year's caucus have said publicly that they will support him, while four Democratic senators have said they will support Van Hollen. The rest were staying quiet for now. The Dec. 1 contest will determine which candidate leads the 55 elected Democrats in next year's caucus.

Maryland Sen. Chris Van Hollen has been making his case publicly as he challenges New York incumbent Chuck Schumer for the leadership post. Backed by progressive groups, he says Democrats need new leadership after Schumer's nearly decade-long tenure. Although the two candidates align on most issues, Van Hollen says Democrats need to push more aggressively to end the Iran war and potentially shut down the government next month, if necessary, to stop the flow of war funding. He said, "To deliver, we need new leadership," adding, "This isn't about any one person – it's about answering that call."

Schumer has drawn intense criticism from the party's base. In 2025, some voters and progressive groups called for his removal after he voted with Republicans to keep the government open and as they called for stronger opposition to Israel's war in Gaza. Still, many in the caucus see the role not as a national figurehead but as an inside operator who can navigate intraparty politics and the 100-member Senate's arcane rules to pass legislation they like and block legislation they don't. Party leaders also spend much of their time fundraising and recruiting candidates — a particular interest for Schumer, who has been directly or indirectly in charge of the Senate Democrats' campaign arm for two decades.

Associated Press writers Joey Cappelletti and Kevin Freking in Washington, Meg Kinnard in Columbia, S.C., and Hannah Fingerhut in Des Moines, Iowa, contributed to the report.
