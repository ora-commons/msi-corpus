## Seven African countries expressly exempt husbands from prosecution for marital rape

The UN Population Fund (UNFPA) reported on 19 November 2026 that one in four women around the world have no power of consent if a husband or partner demands sex. The report found 45% of women in west and central Africa could not say no to sex within their own marriage, compared with 33% in east and southern Africa and in Arab states. In Latin America and the Caribbean, one in 10 women could not refuse sex.

UNFPA’s analysis of eight countries with comparable data found that younger women and girls aged 15 to 19 had even less power to say no than women aged 45 to 49. The countries were Angola, the Democratic Republic of the Congo, Kazakhstan, Malawi, Mali, Nigeria, Tajikistan and Zambia. The findings aligned with research showing that adolescent girls endure very high rates of intimate partner violence.

The Maputo Protocol, a landmark treaty adopted by the African Union in 2003, guarantees women’s rights and obliges states to protect women from violence. It affirms that marriage must be based on full consent and guarantees women’s right to health.

Jean-Paul Murunga, a lawyer with the global human rights organization Equality Now, said: “These are really sad figures.” He said: “Especially given that the continent of Africa as a whole has adopted the Maputo protocol, which sets [binding legal standards for] equality between women and men. We still have a lot of work to do to ensure women and girls benefit from these commitments.”

Murunga set out the legal principle: “The underlying principle is unambiguous: sexual intercourse without free and voluntary consent constitutes sexual violence, including within marriage.” He added: “Marriage does not create perpetual or implied consent. A woman retains the right to decide whether, when and how she engages in sexual activity”.

Murunga said changing rape laws across the continent was needed as a first step. According to his 2024 report, rape within marriage is not criminalised in some African countries. Seven countries expressly exempt husbands from prosecution for marital rape, including Côte d’Ivoire, Ethiopia and South Sudan. Some countries, such as Tanzania and Eritrea, only criminalise marital rape when the couple do not live together.

In countries where child marriage is permitted and marital rape is not criminalised, children are left unprotected. A legal loophole is created for conduct that would be classed as rape outside marriage, and young wives have few options for protecting themselves from sexual violence and exploitation. In Gabon, for example, when a man has married a child he abducted, he can only be prosecuted after the marriage is annulled.

The laws were described as resting on a mistaken belief that marital rape does not exist as a concept because wives are assumed to have permanently consented to sex with their husbands. Religion was said to play a key role in affirming these beliefs in many areas.

Alimatu Dimonekene, founder of A Girl At A Time, an organization supporting adolescent girls in Sierra Leone, said a conversation around consent in marriage had yet to begin in Africa. “We’ve not even started the conversation [around marital rape],” she said. “Women will never call it rape; they make excuses for it or even blame themselves.”

Dimonekene said, “In west Africa, sex is seen as an obligation in marriage. You must do whatever your husband demands.” She continued: “Once he has paid your bride price [a traditional marriage custom in which the bride’s family are paid money, livestock or goods by the groom], he owns you and you cannot reject his demands. Whatever he wants, he gets, including in terms of intimacy, finances and raising the children.”
