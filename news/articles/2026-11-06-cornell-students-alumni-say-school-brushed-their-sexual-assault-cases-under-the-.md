## Faculty pass no-confidence vote but appear split on whether Kotlikoff should step down

Students and alumni have come forward in recent weeks to say they were sexually assaulted at Cornell University and that the school "brushed their cases under the rug" in order to protect its reputation, according to Jeongyoon Han of WSKG in Ithaca, New York, whose report aired on NPR. Han reported that Cornell's faculty senate passed a one-sentence vote of no confidence in the administration in response to those accounts, with 75 votes in favor, 50 opposed and a few abstentions.

Faculty members drafted the resolution after a former student, known as Jane Doe, filed a lawsuit saying she was sexually assaulted by several men at a fraternity house in 2024, according to Han's reporting. Faculty who saw the news said they were alarmed by how the university handled the initial investigation.

The one-sentence resolution states that the faculty senate has no confidence in the administration to "effectively address the ongoing crisis of sexual assault at Cornell University."

It sets out no action steps, making it a symbolic measure, and faculty who support it said it is an important way to acknowledge the students, staff and alumni who feel that trust has been broken.

Anne Marie Brady, a faculty senator who cosponsored the resolution, said the vote sends "a important message to the Cornell community that we take these allegations very seriously and that we want to build trust both with the administration and with our colleagues and our students."

Not all faculty supported the measure. Some opponents said they wanted to see more concrete measures to address sexual misconduct on campus, and some wanted to delay the vote until an independent investigation into Jane Doe's case — one that Cornell requested — comes out with its findings.

The resolution does not take an explicit stance on the leadership of Cornell's president, Michael Kotlikoff, who has faced criticism in recent weeks for how he handled the initial investigation of Jane Doe's case. Faculty appear split on whether he should step down, Han reported.

Brady said that if Cornell really wants to address the problem, its leaders would need to be committed to making real systemic changes — and that she is not sure the current leadership is prepared to do that. "I suspect that at the end of the day, yes, you will need to remove key people in senior positions to advance real change," Brady said.

Kotlikoff declined to comment. But Cornell's board of trustees issued a statement saying it is confident in Kotlikoff's leadership as well as that of other senior officials.

In previous statements, Kotlikoff said he is committed to addressing the issue of sexual assault on campus. The school also announced recently that it is implementing stronger sexual assault support and prevention services.
