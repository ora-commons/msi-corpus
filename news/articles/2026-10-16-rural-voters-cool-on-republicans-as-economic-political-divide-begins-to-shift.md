A Wall Street Journal analysis documents an economic split that has come to define American politics — Democrats winning dense, high-output cities and inner suburbs, Republicans carrying land-rich, lower-output rural counties — and finds signs that map may be shifting ahead of November's midterms.

The 2020 presidential race illustrated the divide in stark numbers. Donald Trump won 83% of America's counties, but those counties accounted for only 29% of U.S. gross domestic product, according to GDP data produced by Mark Muro and colleagues at the Brookings Institution. Joe Biden, by contrast, won 17% of U.S. counties, but those places produced more than 70% of the nation's economic output.

The geographic split reflects two different economies. Democrats dominate in cities and populous inner suburbs, where the nation's professional class is clustered. Republicans hold sway in nonmetropolitan areas, where jobs in agriculture, mining, and basic manufacturing are more common. The party's footprint covers a lot of land, but a smaller share of the national economy.

Rural voters are now among those showing less enthusiasm for the Republican Party, the most recent Wall Street Journal poll shows, favoring a Republican for Congress over a Democrat by 18 points. In the 2024 House races, by comparison, rural voters backed Republicans by 34 points in the national popular vote, according to the AP VoteCast survey.

The shift is one reason Democrats see rising prospects in House races in Iowa, Michigan, and Wisconsin — states with significant rural communities and small towns — and in Senate races in North Carolina, Ohio, Kansas, and Nebraska.

If Democrats win those races and manage to dent the GOP's traditional lead in rural and small-town communities into the future, the party's Electoral College opportunities in the 2028 presidential election could expand. The change could also diversify the representation rural and small-town industries have in Congress.

The 2020 race was a recent high-water mark in the nation's economic divide, the Brookings data show. Trump-supporting counties accounted for 36% of GDP in 2016 and 38% in 2024 — both greater shares than the 29% recorded in the 2020 Biden-Trump election.

The Wall Street Journal's analysis combined its own polling with county-level GDP figures from the Brookings Institution and AP VoteCast survey data, offering a quantitative measure of how the political and economic geographies of the United States have diverged — and where the lines may be starting to bend.
