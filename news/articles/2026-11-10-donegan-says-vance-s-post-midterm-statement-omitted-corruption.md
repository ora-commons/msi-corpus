## Vance did not seem to walk away from enforcement conduct, Donegan wrote

Guardian US columnist Moira Donegan said Vice President JD Vance’s post-midterm statement omitted corruption, which a large majority of voters cited as one of their major concerns. In commentary published Nov. 10, 2026, Donegan said the statement signaled Vance’s continued commitment to the nationalist and nativist faction of the MAGA coalition, which she described as prizing a maximalist immigration policy complete with roundups, camps and “masked thugs” and as having objected to foreign wars. She also wrote that Vance did not seem eager to walk away from enforcement conduct that included disappearances into a network of gulags and deaths in custody. Donegan said the crackdown spread into American cities and paralyzed life in many communities, and that Republicans had faced dramatic losses among Latino and Asian American voters in 2026.

Donegan began with what she called the good news: the “dramatic results of last week’s midterm elections” would leave Donald Trump and his administration “much less able to immiserate and impoverish the American people for the next two years.” She said newly competitive states were likely to see an influx of investment from Democrats. She also said states once under seemingly unbreakable Republican control now had Democratic state governments that will hopefully pursue “more just, or at least less cruel” policy agendas. Her “bad news” was that “The bad news is that the 2028 presidential cycle has already begun.”

Donegan said that, ahead of the 2028 contest and in light of Donald Trump’s “collapsed approval rating” and the “decisive rebuke” Republicans faced at the polls in the midterms, Vance had set about trying to spin the “catastrophic” results for his party into a beneficial outcome for himself. She described Vance as the presumptive favorite for the Republican presidential nomination in 2028. In a lengthy statement Wednesday morning after Tuesday’s elections, Vance argued the results showed that Americans favored his worldview all along. Donegan said Vance called the notion that ICE crackdowns could have spurred the massive Republican losses “gaslighting,” then insisted: “The reason we lost is the war.”

Donegan said corruption was notably absent from Vance’s statement. She wrote that “Even for a craven spin doctor like Vance, trying to distance the Trump administration from that one would be hard to do with a straight face.” Her argument was that even Vance would have difficulty distancing the Trump administration from corruption.

Donegan said Vance’s insistence that immigration enforcement was not the cause of any of the electorate’s shifts to the left reflected a “profound misreading” of both the 2024 and 2026 cycles. She said Trump’s 2024 campaign built a victorious coalition in large part because of rightward shifts among men of color, particularly Latinos, who were drawn to the Republicans’ focus on economics and promises of an immigration crackdown presented as a fight against criminals. “But immigration enforcement against criminals is not what the Trump-Vance administration delivered,” she wrote.

Instead, Donegan said the American public endured months of a “grim spectacle.” She wrote that masked men snatched children from their parents, while ordinary people were kidnapped on their way to work or at their children’s school-bus stops and disappeared into a network of gulags. Some were sent to foreign prisons or shipped to third-party counties they had never set foot in, she said.

Donegan wrote that the crackdown “invaded American cities,” “paralyzed life in many communities” and “had a body count.” She said many immigrants had died in custody and that both foreign- and American-born people had been shot and killed by ICE agents. Donegan described the agents as “trigger-happy,” “high on malice” and “low on self-control.”

Donegan said all of this had Vance’s name on it and that none of it appeared to be something he was eager to walk away from. She said Republicans had faced dramatic losses among Latino and Asian American voters in 2026, particularly people who often had close ties to immigrants that the Trump administration “terrorized, persecuted and kidnapped.” She questioned how popular Vance thought ICE was with those voters and argued that he had to answer for the administration’s conduct while presenting himself as a break from its excesses.

Donegan was skeptical that Vance could credibly and meaningfully distance himself from the Iran war. “It is hard to credit the idea that Vance will be able to credibly and meaningfully distance himself from the Iran war; after all, it was launched when he was the vice-president,” she wrote. She noted that Vance had defended the war publicly as a surrogate for the Trump administration, “even if he is now condemning it.” Donegan said Vance’s theory of the case seemed to be that he could pitch Americans on the idea that he had always opposed the war even as his administration launched and continued it. She said that idea suggested no one in the Trump administration respected him enough to heed his advice.

The statement also deepened Vance’s rift with the party’s “neoliberal wing,” Donegan said. That faction championed foreign intervention in places such as Iran and Venezuela and encouraged ongoing aid to Israel. Donegan identified Secretary of State Marco Rubio as the faction’s de facto leader and said his presidential ambitions in 2028 were rumored.

Donegan said the statement perhaps unintentionally revealed Vance’s “profound weakness as a candidate” at the outset of the 2028 cycle. She said he would have to serve simultaneously as the new standard-bearer for the Trump movement and a departure from its unpopular excesses, cast himself against the Iran war while serving in the administration that prosecuted it, and make the case that “draconian immigration enforcement” was good for a diverse public that can no longer be deceived about what the policies meant.

Donegan characterized Vance’s strategy as a bet that the United States was an isolationist country with white-nationalist sentiments, that it would punish foreign wars and high prices, and that it would reward racism. Her contrary conclusion was that the midterm results showed a pluralist country with a limited appetite for public cruelty, growing impatience with the Trump administration and weariness of being lied to.
