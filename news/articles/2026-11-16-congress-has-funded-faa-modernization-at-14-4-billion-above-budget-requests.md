## OMB has paused $5 billion; GAO found nearly 12,000 unintegrated schedules

Congressional correspondent Lisa Desjardins reported that Congress funded $14.4 billion for FAA modernization across two different bills, far above the roughly $5.5 billion requested in President Trump's budgets over the past two years. She said she was still waiting for administration responses and had asked for comment and context, but her research and conversations with sources indicated that the modernization issue was not about congressional funding. "It is true that our FAA and our system needs more funding for some things, but the modernization effort, President Trump has gotten what he's asked for," she said.

Desjardins said some of the funding holdup came from the president's own Office of Management and Budget, which paused $5 billion of modernization funding in September that cannot be spent until next year. She pointed to the FAA's website map of modernization efforts and said it showed that current funding was flowing.

Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy is requesting another $30 billion to $50 billion. Desjardins said President Trump's budget last year requested $1 billion for the transition from copper wiring to fiber optics. A separate $30 billion proposal in Congress is called Modern Skies.

Desjardins said $10 billion of the Modern Skies proposal is for air traffic control, but most of that money is not for the fiber-optics transition. Another $10 billion is for terminals at three airports, while $10 billion more would go to control towers and other infrastructure. She said the overall proposal is not for the same modernization project and includes other things that might be necessary. "Secretary Duffy at best is conflating things," she said.

The September Government Accountability Office report warned that these kinds of upgrades need to be paced properly and staffed properly and raised concern that the FAA's plans were not specific enough. O'Brien said the GAO discovered a modernization campaign with no real central strategy and nearly 12,000 individual project schedules that were not integrated together.

O'Brien separately said the FAA's systems-engineering expertise had degraded steadily over roughly the past decade. He pointed to DOGE cuts a couple of years ago and additional workforce cuts to the agency's technical and engineering enterprises. "If you're really serious about modernizing, why would those things be cut?" O'Brien said, adding that the FAA needs to rebuild its engineering ethos and brain trust from the inside out.

Desjardins said Duffy had been on the phone and she had confirmed that he spoke by phone with Democratic House Leader Hakeem Jeffries that evening and planned to speak to all Senate Republicans at their lunch the next day. She said lawmakers had questions for Duffy, including why he was blaming Congress after Congress funded the projects and why he was seeking more money. Desjardins said there was general sympathy for the FAA in Congress, but she believed Duffy's request for other funds at that moment had undermined that sympathy.

O'Brien had been reporting on the episode since Friday, speaking with his contacts, and referred to the box problem on Friday the 13th and last week's nationwide ground stop. In O'Brien's account, a crew with contractor L3Harris was installing a new router as part of the effort to put fiber-optic cable into the system and the broader modernization program. O'Brien said, "Evidently, that router was not configured properly," and described the router as sending broadcast messages containing thousands of requests for information, or pings, every second.

O'Brien said the traffic caused the network to break down, leaving 17 of 20 en route air traffic control centers affected with static and potentially inaccurate information. He said controllers issued the ground stop out of an abundance of caution because clarity in life-and-death situations was necessary and the information was unreliable. Aviation is built on redundancy, O'Brien said, adding: "The fact that this could happen, this one box in one place could cause this, really speaks to a much bigger systemic problem, a systems engineering problem, which needs to be addressed."

O'Brien pointed to Canada as a comparison. In 1996, Canada moved its air traffic control operations separate from its regulatory safety body in an arrangement called Nav Canada. The government retains oversight authority and regulates aviation safety, while a nonprofit private-public partnership operates the air traffic system.

O'Brien said the structure enables Nav Canada to issue bonds, provides a steady stream of funding and allows the entity to charge a fee for aircraft using the system. He called the arrangement controversial and said it had been discussed in Washington in the past, with private pilots opposing it and airlines generally favoring it. O'Brien said he would be interested to see whether a healthy debate emerged and said he believed one should.
