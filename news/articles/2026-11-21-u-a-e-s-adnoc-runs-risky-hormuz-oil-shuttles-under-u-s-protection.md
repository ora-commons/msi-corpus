## Eleven crew injured and one missing after latest strike on an Adnoc ship

The United Arab Emirates' state oil company has quietly become a driving force behind oil shipments through the Strait of Hormuz, ramping up its tanker fleet and weathering Iranian attacks to keep crude flowing on a perilous route other operators are afraid to sail.

The high-risk operation by state-owned Abu Dhabi National Oil Company — with American military protection from Iranian missiles and drones — is a crucial link underpinning U.S. efforts to break Iran's chokehold on the strait. It also comes at a considerable cost to its ships and crews.

The dangers were clear this past week. Eleven crew members were injured and another was missing after a vessel operated by Adnoc was struck Tuesday night, the latest in a series of attacks. More than 20 Adnoc vessels have been hit since April, people familiar with the company's operations said.

The U.A.E.'s role started with its efforts to keep its own crucial oil-export industry running. It expanded into a shuttle service for others as its tanker fleet and experience on the route grew, putting it at the center of the Persian Gulf's wartime oil trade. Adnoc also buys Iraqi crude at a discount and resells it to buyers outside the strait.

"The Emiratis have proven to be the most aggressive and risk-taking of the Gulf states through the war," said Rory Johnston, founder of oil research firm Commodity Context.

Adnoc was founded in 1971, the same year as the U.A.E. itself, to manage Abu Dhabi's petroleum resources and has grown into a major supplier of global oil. The U.A.E. pumped around 3.4 million barrels of crude a day in 2025, while six publicly listed companies in the Adnoc group generated more than $51 billion in revenue. The company launched a crude-oil trading arm in 2020.

The war has accelerated Adnoc's longer-term push to exert greater control over its oil production, trading and transportation. This spring, the U.A.E. broke with the Organization of the Petroleum Exporting Countries after years of friction over production limits. It was an early mover in the wartime shuttle system, using its own and chartered tankers to move crude through Hormuz, sparing other operators the dangerous crossing.

In Adnoc's operation, tankers typically load Emirati or Iraqi crude inside the Persian Gulf before crossing the Strait of Hormuz. They make the transit at night in convoys coordinated with U.S. and Emirati military and security authorities, people familiar with the operation said. Once through the strait, they transfer their cargo to other tankers that take the crude to market while the Adnoc ships make the return trip to pick up another load.

The system addresses issues with insurance and private tanker owners' risk tolerance that allowed Iran to cripple traffic in the strait with relatively few strikes for much of the year. Despite more frequent Iranian attacks, crude is now moving regularly through Hormuz again, helping push overall Gulf oil exports briefly above prewar levels in recent weeks.

More than 70% of the crude that passed through Hormuz in August was subsequently transferred offshore near hubs off the U.A.E. and Oman used by Adnoc's shuttle service, according to data from ship tracker Huax. Iran stepped up its efforts that month to shut down shipments through Hormuz and regain a key source of leverage over the U.S. and the Gulf. By Aug. 7, Adnoc had reported attacks on 15 of its vessels that killed one sailor and injured 20.

Iran has placed Adnoc-linked vessels on a blacklist of ships it accuses of violating its transit rules. The U.K. Maritime Trade Operations Center, which is affiliated with the Royal Navy, has warned that many vessels struck in the region aren't reporting the attacks.

The danger is no longer confined to Hormuz. Attacks on Adnoc vessels have occurred near the Emirati coast, according to a senior company executive. Adnoc officials view them as a warning from Iran that their ships could be targeted even before attempting to pass through the strait. Across the Middle East, the International Maritime Organization has reported 138 strikes on ships and 25 seafarer deaths between March 1 and Nov. 17.

The U.A.E. quickly reversed the hit the war delivered to its own oil exports. Shipments of U.A.E.-linked crude and condensates through Hormuz plunged to just 11,300 barrels a day in March from roughly 2 million barrels a day before the war, according to Huax. Three months later, they had rebounded to 2.1 million barrels a day, and the U.A.E. had become the biggest shipper through the strait by volume. Overall U.A.E. crude exports, including oil piped across the desert and around the fighting in Hormuz, reached an estimated 3.74 million barrels a day in October, higher than prewar levels closer to 3 million barrels a day.

For Iraq, the new shipping arrangements with the U.A.E. have become an important way to sustain oil revenues. Its export terminals lie deep inside the Persian Gulf, leaving Baghdad struggling to move oil past the strait. Seeking to keep exports flowing, Iraq has offered steep discounts to buyers willing to collect crude at its terminals and shoulder the shipping risks; Iraqi officials familiar with the Iraq-Adnoc trade say discounts have exceeded $25 a barrel.

Adnoc lifted roughly 20 million barrels of Iraqi crude in August and another 14 million by mid-September, according to Huax data. Some Iraqi crude can be processed at the U.A.E.'s Ruwais refinery complex, freeing more valuable Emirati crude for export, while some has been sold to overseas buyers including Chinese refiners, Huax said.

Adnoc's strategy is part of a broader transformation of the Gulf oil trade, as the war makes control of tankers a new source of power. Owning tankers gives national oil companies more control over whether their oil reaches global markets, reducing their dependence on commercial operators at a time when shipping routes are under threat.

"Adnoc now has considerable flexibility, combining its own fleet, access to Iraqi crude, refining capacity at Ruwais and export infrastructure outside Hormuz," said Arsenio Longo, the founder of Huax.

The rapid expansion has also been lucrative for Adnoc's shipping business. Net profit from Adnoc's global tanker operations shot to $1.42 billion in the first nine months of the year from just $122 million a year earlier, fueled by the expanding fleet and soaring charter rates.

In August, Adnoc's shipping arm announced a $1.3 billion deal to acquire 11 more vessels, including six oil supertankers each roughly the length of three football fields. The deal expanded its fleet of these so-called very large crude carriers to 14.

Adnoc officials have also discussed with U.S. authorities possible licenses to buy or lease tankers that had been sanctioned for transporting illicit oil, people familiar with the talks said. No approval has been announced; Bloomberg earlier reported the possible deal. Last month, U.S. Energy Secretary Chris Wright said the administration was working with investors to buy and refurbish sanctioned tankers for legitimate oil shipments.

Beyond the tanker transits, the U.A.E. already has a pipeline capable of moving 1.8 million barrels a day across the desert, bypassing Hormuz, and is adding a second pipeline that is expected to be online in 2027. But those alternative routes can only go so far, leaving dangerous tanker crossings through Hormuz essential to keeping Gulf oil flowing.

Adi Imsirovic, an oil-trading veteran, likened Adnoc's voyages to rabbits racing past a predator where most get through, but some are caught. "This rabbit strategy seems to be working," he said. "But every now and then some ship gets hit. So there's a cost that we don't see."

Adnoc's convoy system is the arrangement that has kept Gulf crude moving since, at a cost of more than 20 of its vessels hit and seafarers killed and injured, according to the people familiar with its operations.
