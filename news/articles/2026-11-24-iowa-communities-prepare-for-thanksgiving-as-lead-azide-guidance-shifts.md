## Neighboring communities and Kim Reynolds learned of plans Nov. 15

Residents across Iowa, Illinois and Missouri spent the run-up to Thanksgiving preparing for a controlled detonation of hazardous bomb-making materials at the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant in Middletown. As the operation changed, school and business closures shifted and recommendations for reducing toxin exposure remained unclear, leaving many residents confused and frustrated.

Middletown residents had known about the plans for at least a month, when a public meeting was held on the subject. Neighboring communities were not informed until the Army publicly announced the operation on Nov. 15, five days before the initially scheduled detonation. Iowa Gov. Kim Reynolds and other politicians said they were first briefed that day.

Officials encouraged residents within five miles of the detonation site to evacuate and advised those within the immediate 20-mile radius to stay indoors with windows closed to avoid airborne dust. The Iowa Department of Health and Human Services recommended that residents of affected areas stay indoors for 24 to 48 hours because of the blast. The timing fell on the day before Thanksgiving, one of the heaviest travel days of the year.

The operation could release plumes of toxic chemicals across a 100-mile radius, but projected landing areas changed with each rescheduled date. EPA technicians took soil samples from around homes in Augusta, Iowa, near the detonation site on Tuesday.

Muscatine, a town approximately 60 miles north of Middletown along the Mississippi River, was initially placed in the plume path by prediction models. Rehana Pasha, an employee at Blain’s Farm & Fleet, said she had been fielding questions from concerned farmers since the plans were first announced the previous week. Containers for safely storing water, tarps to cover livestock feed and leaf-blowers for removing lead dust and other contaminants from surfaces were selling fast.

“Initially we had only five days of warning, and as a farm supply store we were overwhelmed with customers asking how to prepare,” Pasha said. She added that the customer base skews older and was struggling to order supplies on Amazon and other e-commerce sites on such short notice.

The most recent blast models predicted that the plume would travel southwest into southern Iowa and Missouri, where officials said approximately 350 farms could be affected. At Tuesday’s press conference, USDA official Richard Fordyce said federal agencies “have proper protocols in place to ensure there are no heavy metals, including lead, in the food that enters the supply chain.”

Some farmers were already taking precautions. Brandon Hanson, whose livestock and animal feed company Hanson Farms distributed more than 100 free lead-testing kits before the detonation, said the business was encouraging customers to test soil before and after the blast to monitor changes and protect livestock. He said the abrupt rescheduling left many locals frustrated.

“They’re upset because they had made plans – they had taped windows, come up with different locations for their animals, and dumped out water for the night just to find out that it wasn’t going to happen,” Hanson said. “Living nearby, I am concerned about waking up in the middle of the night and it having already exploded in my backyard.”

Behind the preparations, the Army said the controlled operation involving 68 barrels of lead azide was scheduled for 10 a.m. EST Wednesday. The material, stored at the plant, had been discovered to be dangerously unstable earlier in the year. At a press conference, officials said they could not find a “viable alternative.” Adam Telle, acting secretary of the U.S. Army, said, “There is consensus across experts across the country that a controlled, mitigated detonation of the lead azide here at Iowa Army Ammunition Plant is far preferable to an uncontrolled, spontaneous detonation of that substance.”

The operation was initially planned for last Friday but rescheduled three times as public concern grew and environmentalists sued to block the detonation. Donald Trump ordered the military to hold off pending a court ruling and the sign-off of Democratic governor-elect Rob Sand, who does not take office until January. The Army then announced that it would go through with the plan.

The handling of the explosive chemicals has raised questions about management and transparency. The United States phased out lead azide for munition production over the last decade because of its extreme volatility, but officials said the phaseout moved the majority of the nation’s stockpiles to Middletown. The chemicals should have been subject to regular inspections, and it remains unclear how they deteriorated to the point officials described them as dangerously unstable.

Iowa Sen. Joni Ernst raised those concerns in an X post on Tuesday. “Regarding the Army’s planned detonation in Middletown, at a time when trust in our institutions is already low, the military owes the people it serves more transparency, not less,” she said. “I expect clear leadership, real transparency, and accountability, and I will be pressing Army officials at every level for answers.”

The planned detonation also comes amid broader concern about pollution in Iowa, which The Guardian reported became a central pillar of the 2026 midterm elections. The Guardian reported that Iowa has the second-highest rate of new cancer diagnoses in the United States and is one of only three states where overall cancer incidence is rising.

Daisy Ramirez, an Iowan protesting outside a separate military manufacturing plant in Rock Island, Illinois, said the operation’s proximity to farms that provide food across the United States and to the Mississippi River, whose watershed is connected to 32 states, was of particular concern. Ramirez said she and others would push for both health and environmental testing after the incident to monitor the fallout.

“This isn’t just a Middletown problem, it isn’t just an Iowa problem or a midwest problem. It is a cross-nation problem,” Ramirez said.
