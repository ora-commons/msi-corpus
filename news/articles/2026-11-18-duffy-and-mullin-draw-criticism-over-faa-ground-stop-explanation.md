## Duffy seeks $50 billion for FAA upgrades; Mullin defends Louisiana flight

Democratic lawmakers and some prominent conservatives criticized Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy and Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin’s attempts to explain the abrupt and unusual nationwide ground stop that delayed flights across the country. Brian Harrell, who served as assistant secretary for infrastructure protection at the Department of Homeland Security during Trump’s first term, said the official answers were insufficient. Separately, Duffy sought $50 billion at the Capitol to update FAA technology, while Mullin defended a government-plane trip to a Louisiana college-football game one day after the ground stop.

Some people close to Trump privately and publicly complained that the administration’s response to the incidents had been halting, while some political allies said its initial response was not forceful enough. The outages also prompted concern about the country’s dependence on fragile technology. Trump told reporters Tuesday, “It’s life. When you’re big and powerful and you have all the money, things like that happen. It’s fine.”

At a Friday night White House news conference, Duffy and Mullin blamed the country’s outdated air-traffic-control system for the ground stop but stopped short of pinpointing the cause. Mullin said, “So far what we’ve seen, there is actually no cyberattack,” before adding, “There’s nothing completely certain right now.”

Harrell called the official response inadequate. “The bumbling answers that we have received thus far are insufficient,” he said. “The current media strategy is not working and creating less confidence.”

Former White House press secretary Karoline Leavitt called on the government she used to work for to do more. During a Fox News interview, she said, “What I would say to the White House is transparency is key,” then added, “It’s going to help the country grapple with what’s going on.” Leavitt was channeling a sentiment that several of the president’s allies expressed privately. She later praised the White House for putting Jay Clayton, the director of national intelligence, on television to discuss the outages, writing, “He is smart, professional, and a very credible voice to address Americans’ legitimate concerns about recent outages.”

On Fox News Monday night, Vice President JD Vance responded to friends who asked whether people should prepare for a possible crisis. “I’ve had a lot of my friends reach out and say, should we prepare…to build a bunker to stock up on food and water? And my answer to that is no,” he said. “We do not see some crisis coming down. But you have to be prepared for everything.”

The technological disruptions started last Thursday when a global Microsoft outage affected nearly half a billion people. The next day, the Federal Aviation Administration announced the ground stop that led to the cancellation of nearly 20,000 flights through Monday morning.

On Monday, JPMorgan Chase said a software bug was disrupting transactions, but customers still had access to their accounts and funds. The same day, United Airlines said an outage affecting tools the airline uses to communicate with pilots had delayed flights. On Tuesday, U.S. officials said a classified internal communications network was down at the National Security Agency. A person familiar with the incident said the NSA was simply updating its software to patch the system.

The outages led to widespread speculation on social media. On TikTok, creators posted thousands of videos with the hashtag “cyberattack” that have received more than 14 million views in the last week, according to data from analytics firm Exolyt. Most of the videos suggested the outages resulted from a coordinated hack, while some described the events as a doomsday event.

Social-media creator Amber DeVilbiss said in a TikTok video that accrued more than a half-million likes, “The government is saying, ‘Oh, it wasn’t a cyberattack,’” before adding, “I’m in cybersecurity, I’ve been doing this for 18 years. This feels like a cyberattack to me.” Former Department of Homeland Security spokeswoman Tricia McLaughlin wrote Tuesday on social media, “Americans aren’t stupid. We want and deserve to know what’s really going on.”

By Tuesday, Trump’s advisers said they realized they needed to aggressively push back against spreading online theories that the outages were part of a coordinated cyberattack. Clayton told Fox Business, “We do not see the indicia of a coordinated cyberattack—let me just be clear about that—and we do not see the indicia of state-sponsored activity here.” Clayton made similar remarks in an interview on CNBC and was slated to appear again Tuesday night on Fox News.

Duffy spent Tuesday at the Capitol seeking $50 billion to update FAA technology. Some senators balked at the amount, while multiple Republicans were supportive of additional funding. After meeting with lawmakers, Duffy said he was working to get to the bottom of what happened that led him to call for the ground stop. “I promise all of you that when I know what happened with that router, injecting that overload into our system, I will most definitely share that with you,” Duffy said.

One day after the FAA announced the ground stop and while travelers across the country were still dealing with flight delays, Mullin flew on a government plane to a college-football game in Louisiana. The move angered some White House officials, who called it an unforced error, according to people familiar with the matter. Mullin for months has faced criticism from some of the president’s advisers—and at times Trump himself—for his performance at the department, the people said, though they added there weren’t any plans to oust him.

Mullin told CNBC on Tuesday that he was required to travel on government planes and that the ground stop had been lifted by the time he traveled to the game on Saturday. “I would have never taken airspace if airspace wasn’t open,” he said. “And we still have got to live our lives at the same time.”
