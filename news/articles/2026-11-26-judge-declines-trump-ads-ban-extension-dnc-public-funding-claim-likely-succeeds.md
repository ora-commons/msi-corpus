## Temporary restraining order covered some challenged ads through Nov. 25

U.S. District Judge Loren AliKhan declined on Wednesday to extend a temporary restraining order that had blocked the Trump administration from developing, airing or distributing some government ads promoting President Donald Trump and partisan messages. The order had run through Nov. 25, and AliKhan declined to extend it after the November 2026 midterm elections.

The Democratic National Committee sued the Trump administration in October after the government began airing the ads. Court papers said the advertising was paid for at least in part by a $20 million Department of Homeland Security contract. The DNC challenged at least 11 government-created ads that either aired on television or appeared on Trump administration social media feeds.

Some of the ads presented Trump in highlight reels of scenic speeches, promoted his tax cuts and emphasized support for law enforcement. One reused a clip from Trump’s 2024 presidential campaign, showing him walking down a hallway as his voice could be heard warning of a “final battle” against “globalists” and “warmongers.” The DNC argued that the ads amounted to illegal government-sponsored propaganda intended to promote Trump’s campaign message just weeks before the November election.

AliKhan’s earlier order came when the election was less than a week away. It blocked the administration from developing, airing or distributing some of the challenged ads through Nov. 25. In a separate case in New York, another judge blocked a broader collection of advertisements, declined a long-term ban and required the government to pull three ads from official government websites and social media sites.

In Wednesday’s decision, AliKhan focused on the passage of the election. “But the November 2026 midterm elections have come and gone,” she wrote. The DNC argued that the party could still be harmed in December congressional runoff elections or because the 2028 presidential cycle had already begun.

AliKhan also rejected the DNC’s argument that the permanent nature of campaigns justified an injunction. “Such an argument, taken to its logical conclusion, would allow the DNC to seek preliminary relief in perpetuity because it could always point to the occurrence of some election somewhere in the country,” she wrote.

AliKhan nevertheless said the DNC was likely to succeed on its claim that the use of public money for the ads violated the law. Her assessment did not result in an extended temporary restriction against the advertising.

The White House had defended the ads as public service announcements akin to those made by past administrations to promote various policies. The plaintiffs argued that many earlier public service announcements directed the public toward benefits from specific government programs, while the challenged ads did not.

Ahead of the ruling, the Justice Department argued in court papers that there was no threat of harm because the government had ceased using public funds to air the ads and the midterm elections had passed.

DNC spokesperson Angelo Fernández Hernández said in a statement that the organization was “disappointed” by the judge’s decision and that the DNC “will fight on to put an end to the Trump administration’s attempts to undermine our democracy.” The White House did not immediately respond to a request for comment.
