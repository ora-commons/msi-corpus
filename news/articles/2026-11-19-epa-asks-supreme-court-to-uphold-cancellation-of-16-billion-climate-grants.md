## EPA asks Supreme Court to uphold cancellation of $16 billion climate grants

**Subtype:** fact

- The Trump administration asked the Supreme Court to uphold the EPA’s cancellation of $16 billion in grants for the Greenhouse Gas Reduction Fund.
- A divided federal appeals court in Washington ruled in August that the EPA improperly terminated the grants and paused disbursement for possible Supreme Court review.
- Climate United Fund and other nonprofit operators say the cancellations violated federal law and the Constitution and seek access to their grant money.
- The EPA says the Tucker Act gives the Court of Federal Claims exclusive authority over the contract dispute.
- The Supreme Court has not said whether it will take the case.
- Climate United Fund and other nonprofits could regain access to their grant money if the Supreme Court declines to take the case.

## Nonprofits could regain grant access if Supreme Court declines review

The Environmental Protection Agency has asked the Supreme Court to uphold the cancellation of grants awarded under the Greenhouse Gas Reduction Fund, a climate program commonly called the “green bank.” The $16 billion at stake was authorized to finance clean-energy projects and accelerate the transition away from fossil fuels. The agency describes the dispute as the largest grant-cancellation case ever, while the justices have not said whether they will take the case.

The 2022 Inflation Reduction Act created the green bank, and the Biden administration awarded the money to Climate United Fund and other nonprofits selected to operate the program. The EPA canceled the grants soon after Donald Trump’s second term began in January 2025. EPA Administrator Lee Zeldin accused the groups of mismanagement and potential fraud.

The nonprofits’ lawsuit says the EPA broke the law and violated the Constitution. It demands access to their grant money so they can run the program. The administration says it is immune from legal and constitutional claims and contends the groups filed their case in the wrong court.

In August, a divided federal appeals court in Washington sided with the nonprofits and said the administration improperly terminated the grants. The court paused the release of money to give the Supreme Court an opportunity to weigh in. If the justices decline to take the case, the nonprofit groups could regain access to their funds.

The case could also affect Republican President Donald Trump’s push to defund the priorities of prior administrations. In a filing this month, the administration said a favorable Supreme Court outcome could help it defeat other grant lawsuits. Trump has also targeted grants considered to support diversity, equity and inclusion. The EPA said lower courts had tried to thwart the administration’s priorities and cited the dispute’s scale as another reason for Supreme Court review. The justices recently ruled for the administration in separate disputes involving canceled National Institutes of Health grants and teacher training grants.

The administration built its Supreme Court argument around the Tucker Act, which gives the Court of Federal Claims exclusive authority over contract disputes. The claims court can award money but cannot direct the government to take certain actions in the way federal courts can. The nonprofits argue that the EPA’s cancellations did not upend a contract with the government but were illegal and undercut spending on entire programs Congress required, making them a constitutional violation. The government characterizes the matter as a contract dispute.

EPA Administrator Lee Zeldin last year told Fox News that he suspected the green bank “was a clear-cut case of waste and abuse.” U.S. District Judge Tanya Chutkan said that when the government was asked for evidence of fraud, the EPA did not provide it and shifted its position to more general concerns about the level of oversight over the program. Chutkan said the government cannot terminate the contracts in violation of law and that the groups should have access to some of their frozen money, which is held in an account at Citibank.

The administration has maintained that nothing requires it to make the grants to these groups. Trump’s tax and spending bill passed last year rescinded part of the law that established the green bank and the money that had not already been obligated to its recipients. The EPA said those changes mean it does not have to release any money to the groups.

Romany Webb, deputy director of the Sabin Center for Climate Change Law at Columbia University and a supporter of Climate United’s arguments, said a ruling for the EPA could have far-reaching implications for the government’s ability to reshape grant programs and terminate grants that do not advance a particular administration’s policy priorities.

Bob Wagman, president of the Court of Federal Claims Bar Association and a partner at White & Case, called the administration’s broad use of contract cancellations to enact policies a novel approach. He said, “I would hate to think that the only programs that could be supported have a four-year life,” mentioning cancer research as an example of a grant program that would need longer than a single administration to pay off. “It is just not good for the country.” The EPA and Climate United declined to comment.
