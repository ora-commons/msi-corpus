## UK courts prosecuted only three of at least 21 identified airmen

The Guardian, with additional reporting by Sophie Sullivan, reported on Nov. 28, 2026, that it had identified at least 21 American airmen in the UK who were convicted of child sexual abuse offenses in the past decade. Only three were prosecuted in UK courts; the others were brought before US courts martial. More than 12,000 US military personnel work at at least 15 bases and facilities across the UK.

A high-level internal inquiry involving at least four Whitehall departments and police chiefs is examining whether US military personnel stationed in Britain are being prosecuted properly. It was set up after the Guardian reported in June that UK police allowed US military authorities to take over the investigation of pilot Jacob Wulfson, who strangled a British woman in his Cambridge flat. Wulfson was tried at a court martial at RAF Lakenheath in Suffolk; Downing Street said it was “deeply distressing” and “very concerning” that UK authorities were not involved in prosecuting Wulfson.

The jurisdictional issue was illustrated by Jacob Ozbirn, an American airman stationed at RAF Mildenhall in Suffolk. One summer evening, he drove two hours to a country lane near a Derbyshire hotel, expecting to meet a 12-year-old girl he had groomed for sex near Burton upon Trent. In the preceding days, he sent explicit messages to the girl and two others, encouraging them to send naked photographs after all three said they were aged 12 or 13. He wrote: “There is nothing to be afraid of. I will take care of you! And please can I have one more picture.”

Ozbirn instead walked into a sting run by members of the public whom the Guardian calls “paedophile hunters” and who pose online as girls to lure suspected offenders to meetings and confront them. Ozbirn told them he was seeking to educate the girls about dangerous predators. The hunters notified Derbyshire police, which detained him. The Guardian said Ozbirn should have been investigated by local police and, if necessary, prosecuted in a British criminal court. Instead, Derbyshire police handed the investigation to US military police; Ozbirn was tried at a court martial held on the Mildenhall base and sent to a US military jail for three years.

Under the Status of Forces Agreement, an official arrangement dating back to the 1950s, any US military personnel accused of breaking the law should be investigated by a British police force unless the alleged offense was committed while on duty or against another member of the US armed forces, US military property, or a dependant such as a spouse or child. The Guardian reported a growing number of cases in which UK police forces acquiesced to US military requests to take control of investigations into potential wrongdoing by American personnel.

The Guardian reported critics’ argument that courts martial provide a more limited form of justice than open British courts. Military juries consist of the defendant’s peers and are sometimes all-male, while members of the public cannot easily attend proceedings held on US bases. Accounts of the proceedings are often too sparse to determine whether UK police were involved or should have been. Documents are published on a public register inconsistently, sometimes disappear without explanation, and describe charges in a limited way or not at all.

Rachel VanLandingham, a retired lieutenant colonel and former military lawyer, said military-justice transparency had been “woefully inadequate” for decades. “It’s been brought to the military’s attention time after time again,” she said. “It’s intentionally opaque. They want it to be opaque.”

The US military uses courts martial to prosecute personnel for offenses including sexual violence against women, assaults, drug-taking and failure to obey orders. The Guardian’s analysis found that 63 military personnel had been convicted at courts martial in the UK going back to 2016, including 18 convicted of child sexual abuse offenses — more than one quarter of the total. Those cases included rape and sexual abuse of children under 12 and acquiring large amounts of child pornography.

Two of the most recent convictions came in October in separate courts martial at RAF Lakenheath. Lucero, known only by that name because the US military has not published his first name, received 10 years in a military jail for sexually abusing a child and possessing and obtaining child pornography. William Berroa received two years for possessing and viewing child abuse images; the US Air Force published no details of either airman’s offending.

The Guardian asked the Air Force Office of Special Investigations whether it had asked British police to take over jurisdiction for the investigations. The OSI did not answer directly. In a statement, it said: “As in all our investigations, the Air Force Office of Special Investigations worked with our UK partners to determine investigative primacy. Extensive negotiations took place to establish jurisdiction. While we cannot give details, these were decisions that were not made quickly or spuriously. Ultimately, the local authorities have the right of first refusal on all investigative matters.”

The Guardian reported that the US military wants to handle as many prosecutions of its personnel as possible and frequently asks UK police to take over investigations even when they appear to fall clearly within British authorities’ responsibility; UK police often agree but sometimes refuse. In 2025, British police raided the Bury St Edmunds home of Jarik Lofthus, an airman at Mildenhall, and seized pornographic images of young children and bestiality. Suffolk police said they rejected a US military request to take over the case; the US Air Force did not respond when asked for comment. In July, Lofthus, 33, was sentenced at Ipswich crown court to eight months in jail, suspended for two years.

Sam Jarvis, 36, was convicted in June of sexually assaulting a 12-year-old girl at a social event in Oxfordshire. Jarvis was stationed at RAF Croughton in Northamptonshire and received an 18-month jail sentence at Oxford crown court, suspended for two years. The US Air Force would not say whether it had sought to take over the investigation. It said, “Criminal jurisdiction for this case was determined consistent with the status of forces agreement.” The service added that it “works in close coordination with local UK law enforcement agencies and respects the authority of UK court proceedings.” Thames Valley police, which investigated the case, could not say whether the US military had asked to take over jurisdiction. The force referred questions to the Americans and said, “Thames Valley police is not able to comment on behalf of partner agencies.”

Among the 18 cases handled through the military system, Brandon Justice, a US Air Force technical sergeant described by military investigators as a “multi-child molester,” received the longest sentence: 47 years in a military jail after a 2016 court martial at Lakenheath. Christopher Toolan, a staff sergeant, received 30 years at a 2024 Lakenheath court martial after multiple offenses over four years. Toolan had written on his phone that he had raped a child under 12, adding, “I was in heaven.” He was also convicted of sexually abusing another child and possessing child abuse images. His court martial heard that, late one evening, he approached a girl playing in a park at Lakenheath and showed her a photograph of a girl aged six or seven lying naked on a red blanket.

Garret Souders, a technical sergeant at Mildenhall, was convicted after his wife discovered his sexual fantasies about raping, torturing and killing their four young daughters. On New Year’s Day in 2019, she checked his phone and found graphic exchanges with other men on a website in which he described his “darkest fantasies” of “handing his very young children over to a group of men to use however they wanted.” His court martial heard that he imagined other men raping his daughters and that, when they did, it was always “hard and rough and violent, and sometimes they don’t survive.” He was jailed for nine years in 2021.

The Guardian also reported concern that the US Air Force was not doing enough to tackle child sexual abuse within its ranks. Don Christensen, a retired colonel who spent 23 years in the US military justice system as a judge, prosecutor and defense lawyer and later joined reform efforts, said the conviction rate for such offenses was low in his experience. He attributed that partly to juries composed of service members and said military personnel often assumed colleagues would not commit such offenses. “A lot of times, there is a natural tendency for military members to believe that other military members, the people they serve with, whether they know this person or not – you know, we’re still like the band of brothers – that they would [not] do something as horrific as child sex offences,” he said. Christensen also said relatives could face a financial disincentive to report an airman accused of rape because a conviction and dismissal from the military would leave the family at a disadvantage. A US Air Force spokesperson said: “There is no place in our ranks or in our communities for anyone who exploits or abuses a child. The United States Air Force is committed to the investigation and prosecution of anyone accused of these offenses and to maintaining good order and discipline within our forces.”
