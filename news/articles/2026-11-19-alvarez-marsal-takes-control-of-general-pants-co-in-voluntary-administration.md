## All 35 stores will remain open while administrators assess a sale

General Pants Co entered voluntary administration late Wednesday evening, placing nearly 500 jobs at risk. Alvarez & Marsal confirmed that it had taken control of the Australian youth fashion chain and nine related businesses after being appointed the businesses' voluntary administrator. The Guardian contacted the chain's parent company, Alquemie group, for comment on Thursday morning.

The administrators said they were working closely with General Pants Co's existing management team to undertake an urgent assessment of the business, including its trading performance, stores, stock and costs. All 35 General Pants Co stores and its online store would remain open and trading as usual while the administrators worked out whether they could sell or restructure the business to keep it afloat.

Administrator Jason Tracy described General Pants Co as “one of Australia’s best-known youth fashion brands, with a loyal customer base and a dedicated team.” He said the immediate priorities were to keep stores trading, support employees and customers, and pursue “a sale and/or recapitalisation” of the business. “With Black Friday next week, we are focused on maximising trading performance and expect strong demand over the period,” Tracy said. He also thanked the company's team members, landlords and suppliers for their support during the administration.

Alvarez & Marsal said the 500 people employed by General Pants Co across Australia and New Zealand would continue to be paid for the work they did during the administration period. The consultancy said staff entitlements accrued before the appointment remained legally protected under the Corporations Act and had priority over “most” other unsecured creditor claims.

Two Alvarez & Marsal managing directors, Jason Tracy and Glen Kanevsky, were appointed to oversee the administration. Their first meeting with creditors is scheduled for 30 November.

General Pants Co began in 1972 as a specialty denim store on Sydney's George Street and has traded across Australia and New Zealand since.

General Pants Co's administration followed David Jones entering administration last week with more than $400 million owed to creditors; David Jones is closing half of its 38-store network. Less than two months earlier, Cue Clothing Co, owner of Cue and Veronika Maine, entered administration and receivership in September. Last year, Mosaic Group, owner of Noni B, Rivers and Katies, was liquidated.

Australian retailers were dealing with higher operating costs as indebted households tightened their budgets during another bout of inflationary pressures. The Guardian described cash-strapped shoppers and online-only competitors as adding to the pressure on the sector. Credit reporting agency Equifax said the strain was driving a sharp contraction across the sector, marked by surging business closures and a slowdown in new brands.

Equifax's Brad Walters said the strain was not limited to large retailers such as David Jones. “Smaller retailers are also dealing with a difficult combination of softer discretionary spending, rising operating costs and increasingly narrow financial buffers,” Walters said. “Instead of funding long-term expansion, smaller operators are taking on credit simply to manage immediate overheads.”

Myer, meanwhile, faced its own financial stress and was forced to offer “higher than planned promotional activity to stimulate demand.” Four of Myer's five newly acquired apparel chains — Jay Jays, Portmans, Dotti and Jacqui E — recorded falling sales in the last financial year, leaving Just Jeans as the only one to post growth.
