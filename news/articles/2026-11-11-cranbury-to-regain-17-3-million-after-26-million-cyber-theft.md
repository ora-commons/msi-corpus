## Police chief delayed disclosure as residents questioned the timing

Cranbury, N.J., is expected to regain $17.3 million after thieves diverted $26 million intended for a land purchase through fraudulent wiring instructions, according to town officials. The money was seized before the thieves could move it out of reach, but nearly $9 million remains unaccounted for. Police Chief Giuseppe DeChiara said his team reached Town Hall within minutes of being alerted to possible fraud and that he called the Federal Bureau of Investigation.

The loss put Cranbury's financial management and public disclosure under scrutiny Monday night at the Cranbury Public Library, where residents packed the building and spilled into an overflow room. Residents, stepping to the microphone for three minutes each, asked how the town could transfer $26 million for land, what the theft meant for taxpayers, and why they were not told about the Sept. 28 incident until Friday, Nov. 6, three days after a Nov. 3 election for one of five township committee seats.

Mayor Lisa Knierim told the gathering, her voice catching, "My heart is simply broken for Cranbury," and said, "Your anger is valid, your fear is valid." Cranbury, a town of about 4,000 with a median household income of $217,000, was still confronting what the missing money would mean for taxpayers and public trust.

The payment was part of a $26 million bond-funded land purchase that the township pursued after abandoning a proposal to seize farmland through eminent domain to satisfy affordable-housing mandates. That proposal had fractured the community. The theft also landed in a village long steeped in political drama: Cranbury was established in 1697, its Main Street clapboard houses carry plaques marking their vintage — one reads "Built by John Nesbit 1804" — and locals proudly note that George Washington and Alexander Hamilton met there in 1778 ahead of the Battle of Monmouth. In 1804, Aaron Burr swapped horses in the village after killing Hamilton in their duel and fleeing south. Officials did not give specifics of the cyber theft at the meeting because a multiagency investigation was continuing.

DeChiara said the town received "fraudulent wiring instructions" before transmitting the money. Within minutes of being alerted to possible fraud, his team descended on Town Hall, and he called the FBI. He said he put a gag order on Cranbury staff and officials and repeatedly rejected requests by Knierim and her four fellow committee members to go public.

"If we tipped our hand too early, they could have liquidated everything," DeChiara said of the thieves. He said the chance to claw back money faded over time, and the township released a public statement on Friday, Nov. 6.

Some residents saw a possible political motive in the release by the all-Democratic township committee — that officials did not want bad news to hurt a Democratic candidate's chance to win on Nov. 3. Republican candidate Carol Haring asked, "We're expected to believe that it's a coincidence that you suddenly felt comfortable releasing this information at 3 p.m. last Friday afternoon, right after the election?" Haring lost by 27 points, and some villagers argued the contest was never close. DeChiara rejected the allegation: "I'm telling you right now, it had nothing to do with an election."

The financial loss remains unresolved. People cheered when DeChiara confirmed at Monday's meeting that the township would regain the $17.3 million seized before thieves could move it out of reach. Nearly $9 million remains unaccounted for, however, and the town attorney said it is not known how much Cranbury could recoup under its $5 million insurance policy.

The incident also brought scrutiny to Town Hall's longstanding reliance on a single individual serving simultaneously as chief financial officer, finance director and town administrator. Knierim announced at the meeting that the employee was retiring. The committee took a first step to create two new positions, and Cranbury hired an accounting firm.

Ben Bernstein, a cybersecurity expert at Huntress Labs, said the sheer size of the Cranbury theft makes it a major outlier. "Grabbing $26 million in one shot puts this in the upper tier of public-sector thefts," he said. The Journal reported that local governments are increasingly lucrative targets for cybercriminals who use malicious software, phishing emails or subtly altered digital addresses to infiltrate government business.

Residents offered different reactions. Fran McGovern questioned the sequence of events and suggested that municipal incompetence had left the village caught out of its depth. He said, "The world is perhaps too sophisticated for Mayberry." Afterward, residents lingered, trying to make sense of the lapse. Catherine Colaizzo, a 66-year-old chiropractor, accepted the police chief's explanation for the delayed notification but remained baffled by the theft itself. "I can't even get on my iCloud account without a two-factor identification," she said, adding, "I don't know how you would transfer $26 million. That seems a little bit absurd." Tom Beatty, 55, who works in banking, said, "I believe they were asleep at the wheel and they were complacent."

Jim Grossnickle, 56, who works in telecommunications, directed his criticism at the township committee. "I respectfully want to tell the township committee that the buck really should stop with you all," he said. He later vowed to recruit candidates to challenge the incumbents up for re-election next year.

Officials moved the meeting from Town Hall to the library because they anticipated high turnout. Technical glitches disrupted the feed shown on a television in an auxiliary room and on the Township of Cranbury YouTube channel. One attendee told the committee, "We can't hear a thing out there. We can't see anything," adding, "So I mean, I can see how the money got stolen."

All weekend, the news consumed the town and dominated chatter at places like Cranbury Pizza on Main Street. Former mayor Jay Taylor, whose ancestors settled locally in the 1700s, when the community's name was spelled Cranberry, described the mood: "Fear and angst, those are the two biggest things." Knierim, who was chosen by the committee to be mayor, said it stung to lose the trust of friends and neighbors but that she isn't going anywhere. "I'm not hiding in my house," she said in an interview Tuesday. "I'm happy to meet with a resident one on one. And if I have to spend the rest of this year and into next year doing that as a committeewoman, then I'm going to do it."
