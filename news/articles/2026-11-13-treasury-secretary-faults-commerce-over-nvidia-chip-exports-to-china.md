## Treasury secretary faults Commerce over Nvidia chip exports to China

**Subtype:** fact

- Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent accused Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick on November 13 of failing to keep Nvidia's most advanced chips out of China.
- Bessent said Treasury, War, USTR, and State backed adding Singapore-based Megaspeed to the Commerce entity list "for months," but Commerce controls the list.
- Chris McGuire of the Council on Foreign Relations said a Commerce decision to stop enforcing a foreign-buyer chip rule two days before its May 2025 start let Megaspeed buy chips without U.S. government approval.
- Independent researchers discovered this week that China's AI models were all developed thanks to smuggled Nvidia advanced chips, PBS News reported.

## Bessent says four agencies backed Megaspeed entity listing for months

The public dispute erupted as the fallout from former Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang's departure expanded into open finger-pointing between two of President Trump's top advisers. Amna Nawaz of PBS News introduced the segment by noting that the treasury secretary had accused the commerce secretary of failing to prevent China from obtaining Nvidia's most advanced chips, and that Huang had played an outsized role in President Trump's technology policy.

Bessent made his case on CNBC. "Treasury, War, USTR, and State, they were in favor of putting Megaspeed on the entity list for months. But Commerce has the pen," Bessent said. The Commerce Department controls the entity list of countries and companies banned from buying U.S. technology.

Bessent said the leakage contradicted the president's stated policy. "I am frustrated when President Trump made it very clear that China should not receive any Nvidia chips above H100s. And so any leakage is very frustrating because it hurts the -- our -- hurts our great companies in the AI race, gives the Chinese an advantage," he said. Bessent also turned his scrutiny on Nvidia itself, asking: "Did Nvidia know their client? And was there a robust effort at Nvidia to make sure that there was no leakage and that they were selling to what were obviously pass-through shell companies?"

The dispute traces back to how the restrictions were built. Chris McGuire, a senior fellow at the Council on Foreign Relations and former director of technology on President Biden's National Security Council staff, said the effort to restrict Chinese access to chips began when the first Trump administration blocked Chinese telecommunications giant Huawei from buying semiconductors with U.S. technology. McGuire said the United States concluded that export restrictions were among the best ways to constrain Chinese military and AI advancements, and the restrictions continued into the Biden administration.

In early 2025, the Biden team pushed through new regulations requiring most foreign companies to get Commerce Department permission to buy advanced chips. "It was slated to go into effect in May 2025, but two days before it went into effect, the Commerce Department said they're not enforcing it," McGuire said. "If the Commerce Department had enforced this, Megaspeed would not have been able to get chips unless the U.S. government green-lighted it. But because they didn't enforce it, Megaspeed could just buy whatever they wanted completely uninterrupted."

Last year, Commerce and the Trump administration decided to let China buy some less advanced Nvidia chips. International companies believed to collaborate with Beijing, including the Singapore-based Megaspeed, the company whose leader Huang had an affair with, reportedly gave China access to the most advanced chips, PBS reported.

Huang, a celebrity in China, helped push President Trump to allow sales of U.S. chips to China in the first place. "Why is it that we don't come up with a regulation that's more balanced so that Nvidia can win around the world, instead of giving up the world? We want United States to win at every single layer, including the chip layer. And conceding the entire market is not going to allow the United States to win the technology race long term," Huang said. Howard Lutnick, whose agency runs the entity list, had earlier said: "We want to keep having the Chinese use the American technology stack." Huang separately described Lutnick as "the person I talked to most in the administration."

McGuire disputed the premise that chip sales serve U.S. interests. "Well, Jensen certainly wins if they if they sell chips to China, but I would argue America does not win," McGuire said.

Independent researchers this week discovered that China's AI models were all developed thanks to smuggled Nvidia advanced chips, PBS reported. McGuire said the stakes extend beyond market share: "If we did prevent them from getting these chips, they actually would be, instead of months behind us in AI, they would be years behind us in AI."

McGuire argued that the United States can still preserve its advantage in artificial intelligence. "While I think the poor implementation on export controls has probably cost us years of advantage, we still -- doubling down now will still give us years, even potentially decades of advantage in the future, as long as we actually manage it right. But if we don't, China is going to stay close and that will have catastrophic impacts for U.S. national security," he said. PBS reported there is a debate over that assessment, which will continue.

Huang's departure is expected to shift the internal balance of that debate. PBS reported that his exit will empower advocates of stricter export controls, including a push on Capitol Hill to further restrict the world's leading technology from going to the U.S.' chief international competitor.
