## Asia-Pacific data-center investment reached a record $11.6 billion in 2025

Asia-Pacific data-center investment reached a record $11.6bn (£8.85bn) in 2025, CBRE said, as a market largely powered by Nvidia chips experienced what the research group called an "unprecedented boom," with the volume of investment rising steadily in recent years. Google, Microsoft and Oracle plan to boost infrastructure spending in the region by roughly 60% this year, and demand for neoclouds, specialized data centers that power AI, has risen rapidly. The BBC's captioned reporting also notes that data centres worth billions of dollars are being built in Malaysia.

Jensen Huang's return as Nvidia's chief executive sent shockwaves through the global artificial intelligence industry. Tech-heavy indexes in Japan, South Korea and Taiwan moved on a roller coaster this week: they soared after a near-trillion-dollar surge in Nvidia's share price, then slumped as concerns about political and regulatory scrutiny hit investor confidence.

The market response reflected the scale of Asia's exposure. From chip manufacturing and data centers to foreign investment, AI has become a strategic priority for economies across the region, leaving them increasingly dependent on Nvidia's fortunes. Nvidia's unrivalled power in cutting-edge chips, the financial clout that turned it into "the bank of AI," and Huang's role as a high-profile evangelist have placed the company at the heart of the AI ecosystem. Dylan Loh, an international studies lecturer at Nanyang Technological University, called Nvidia a "symbolic bellwether" of the AI trade, particularly important to South Korea, Taiwan, Singapore and Malaysia.

Loh said Asia was not merely a customer base for Nvidia but "an indispensable engine of the company's AI dominance" because of East Asia's semiconductor manufacturing and memory ecosystems.

Nvidia was able to grow rapidly by outsourcing chip production rather than spend heavily on its own manufacturing plants. The chipmaker contracts most of its production to Taiwanese company TSMC. Paul Cavey, founder of Taipei-based research firm East Asia Econ, said that makes Taiwan vulnerable to changes in AI technology demand. He attributed the surge in Taiwan's real economic growth since 2024 to the big increase in AI-related exports.

Huang said earlier this year that Nvidia plans to invest around $150bn (£114.4bn) a year in Taiwan at a launch to celebrate the firm's planned headquarters on the island. South Korea and Japan are also exposed, though to a lesser extent, as suppliers of memory chips and testing equipment, with firms such as Samsung and SK Hynix making processors. Nvidia's chips fill data centers, power AI models and feed companies that make cars and electronics.

Singapore this week further raised its growth forecast for this year as the technology boosts its economy. Loh said Nvidia had become "hugely important" to economies that had bet heavily on AI, with vast sums being spent on its chips and factory activity becoming increasingly dependent on its technology.

Nvidia also acts as a financial backstop and major lender to companies in the AI supply chain. Singapore-based AI company Megaspeed had become South East Asia's biggest buyer of Nvidia chips before it was accused of smuggling them to China. Megaspeed said it "conducts legitimate and lawful business" and has "retained external legal counsel to conduct a thorough internal investigation". The United States said it intends to ban Megaspeed Ltd from doing business in the country.

Asia's technology industry has come under increasing US pressure as the Joe Biden and Donald Trump administrations tightened rules on exports of Nvidia's most advanced chips to China. Last week, Washington announced new rules that appear to renew those efforts, increasing scrutiny of data-center operators in Singapore, Malaysia, Thailand, Indonesia and Brazil. The rules restrict exports of high-end chips to China and access to computing power that Chinese firms rent abroad to train AI models.

The changes are expected to hit South East Asian countries that have invested billions in data-center infrastructure to serve Chinese firms. The Financial Times reported that Nvidia had paused shipments to the region, although it was unclear whether the pause was temporary while the company determined how to comply. Nvidia did not immediately respond to a BBC request for comment.

In many economies, news of Huang's return to Nvidia was met with jubilation. In Taiwan, where Huang was born and where the phenomenon is known as "Jensanity," investors joked that Huang's return was a "one man saves the village" story. A Getty Images caption accompanying BBC report imagery says Jensen Huang signed a Taiwanese banknote for a visitor at Computex 2026 in Taipei. Posts on social media and the Line messaging platform included "Old Huang is back," "Daddy is back to save my life" and "the humanitarian corridor is open."

Some investors said the episode should prompt a closer examination of how effectively Taiwan had built a "non-China" semiconductor supply chain and how risks from the industry's heavy reliance on Nvidia could be managed better.

The political backlash is growing as Huang is due to testify before Congress next week. President Donald Trump said he "made the deal" to "convince" Nvidia's board to reinstate Huang, and the episode has produced growing bipartisan backlash against the president and the chipmaker. As Democrats prepare to take control of the US House and Senate in the New Year, Huang is likely to face further risks.

Charu Chanana, chief investment strategist at Saxo Bank, said, "Jensen's return may resolve the immediate leadership crisis, but it does not resolve the legal and political overhangs." Investor concerns about trillions of dollars being poured into AI and Nvidia's pivotal role were already sending tremors through global stock markets. Some investors fear the concentration of influence in one company also poses a threat.

Ray Wang, founder of San Francisco-based Constellation Research, said the President and White House would like to brush the issue over but believed Democrats in Congress would keep probing. "The story is not over yet... Jensen coming back is probably the biggest, baddest return ever. The question is what will happen next?" Huang's future remains uncertain, and the episode could affect economies heavily exposed to the AI industry.
