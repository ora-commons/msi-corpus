## Gulf states push for talks as Trump weighs U.S. options on Iran

Roman Gofman, who leads Israel's Mossad and is a close confidant of Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu, spent part of last week in Washington pressing U.S. officials for a new, multiday round of airstrikes on Iran. People familiar with the matter said the spy chief met Trump administration officials, including CIA Director John Ratcliffe, on Friday, presented them with Israeli intelligence about Iran's nuclear program and made the case for renewed U.S.-Israeli strikes.

The request came amid an Israeli push for renewed attacks. Netanyahu has been pushing for renewed operations against Iran for months and says Israel's goal remains toppling the Iranian government. Netanyahu's office did not respond to a request for comment, and the Central Intelligence Agency declined to comment.

The decision about whether to renew attacks against Tehran lies with Trump, who has oscillated in recent months between threatening renewed intensive strikes and wanting to reach a deal with Iran's leaders. Many analysts said renewed strikes are unlikely to push Iran toward a deal or to topple the Iranian government.

Gulf governments are pushing in the opposite direction. Gulf countries led by Qatar, which worry about retaliatory Iranian attacks against their infrastructure, are pressing for negotiations and a deal, though those efforts have so far remained stuck. A U.S. official said the economic pressure campaign on Iran had been so effective that the president does not need to conduct major combat operations, though the United States retains all its options to ensure Iran does not acquire a nuclear weapon.

Even as the debate runs on, Iran and the United States are trading fire on land and sea, though at a lower intensity than in the early months of the war. Iran is firing at ships trying to pass through the Strait of Hormuz, and American forces have quietly carried out a series of strikes in recent weeks on Qeshm, Iran's largest island, from which the Revolutionary Guard has launched drones and missiles. Israel has not been targeted by Iranian missiles since June.

U.S. forces have expanded their presence in the region. The aircraft carrier USS Theodore Roosevelt arrived in the Middle East and sailed alongside the carrier USS George Washington in footage released by the U.S. military, bringing the number of carriers in the region to three. According to U.S. and Israeli officials, many U.S. assets have been moved to Israel, which is considered safer after Iran damaged U.S. bases, radars and early-warning systems in the Middle East. The U.S. military also sent an additional Patriot system to Israel to reinforce missile defenses in the country.

Dozens of U.S. tankers are parked at bases in the south and at Israel's Ben Gurion International Airport, and U.S. military forces can be seen across Tel Aviv, where they are staying in apartments and hotels.

The Wall Street Journal reported that the United States and Israel launched the war in Iran on Feb. 28, with both countries initially speaking of toppling the Iranian government. Their goals have since diverged, with Trump seeking a deal and Netanyahu seeking to topple the Iranian government; analysts said that divergence remains a problem for future joint kinetic operations between the two countries. Israel has been working to build up its target bank in Iran since the end of the joint bombing campaign with the United States, and Israeli officials remain concerned about Iran's rebuilding efforts.

Netanyahu is now Israel's caretaker prime minister and is in a vulnerable political position. His grip on power weakened after he lost his majority in parliament in national elections last month, which allowed the opposition to vote in a new Knesset speaker this week. Gadi Eisenkot, a former Israeli general who heads the opposition, is working to build an alternative ruling coalition to oust Netanyahu, who has led the country for around 19 years across multiple terms.

The heads of Israel's opposition parties have said they worry Netanyahu could launch military operations for political gain rather than for national security needs, in order to stay in power. Netanyahu has rejected the allegations. Some current and former Israeli security officials told The Wall Street Journal that they share those concerns about political motivations for an attack.

Netanyahu previously tried to push for renewed operations against Iran before Israel's elections last month and faced opposition from Israel's chief of staff, Eyal Zamir, who worried about the timing and the potential for escalation, according to people familiar with the matter.

The decision on whether to resume large-scale strikes remains with Trump, who is weighing the Israeli request against the administration's economic-pressure strategy and the Gulf states' push for a negotiated agreement.
