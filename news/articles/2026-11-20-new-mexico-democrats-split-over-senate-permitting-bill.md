## Stansbury and Leger Fernandez seek delay amid objections to safeguards and tribal input

New Mexico Democrats divided over the Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act as Sen. Martin Heinrich supported and cosponsored the federal permitting bill, Sen. Ben Ray Lujan voted against it, and Reps. Melanie Stansbury, Teresa Leger Fernandez and Gabe Vasquez criticized, opposed or sought delay. The Senate passed the measure 62-33 Thursday evening and sent it to the House after objections from environmental groups and tribes in New Mexico and elsewhere.

New Mexico's two senators took opposite positions when the Senate voted 62-33 Thursday night to pass the BAAJA. Sen. Ben Ray Lujan (D-N.M.) voted against the legislation. He said after the vote that he supports permitting reform but “I could not support legislation that fails to address the concerns of our Pueblo and environmental leaders.”

Sen. Martin Heinrich (D-N.M.), who cosponsored the bill, welcomed its passage in a Thursday statement despite steady criticism from tribal and environmental leaders and members of his own party. He said the legislation would provide more affordable, clean energy on the grid faster, create jobs and lower costs for ratepayers. “Americans sent Congress a clear message in this historic election: lower costs,” Heinrich said. “We don’t have to wait another year to deliver relief. We can act right now.”

Heinrich described the legislation as a “hard-fought bipartisan agreement.” Most Senate Republicans voted in favor, along with 14 Democrats. “It wasn’t easy. But we proved that Democrats and Republicans can put politics aside and deliver meaningful results for the American people,” he said. “I’m proud of what we’ve accomplished. Now let’s get this bill across the finish line and signed into law.”

The bill now heads to the House, where Democrats are largely split on the issue. President Donald Trump has said he supports the legislation. Stansbury and Leger Fernandez, both New Mexico Democrats, have repeatedly criticized the bill and urged Heinrich and other sponsors to wait until January to bring it up for consideration. January is when Democrats will retake both chambers of Congress following the Nov. 3 election.

Vasquez, who had previously declined to weigh in on the legislation, issued a statement Thursday morning opposing it. “I will continue to work with my House colleagues to improve the existing Senate bill,” he said, “but as the bill is currently written, I cannot support BAAJA at this time.”

Heinrich and bipartisan cosponsors introduced the legislation at a news conference roughly two months ago. The BAAJA aims to hasten federal reviews of a wide variety of energy projects, including gas pipelines and solar arrays. It also aims to ensure electricity costs from data-center development do not fall on ratepayers.

Since its introduction, the bill has drawn widespread criticism from environmental groups in New Mexico and elsewhere. The groups said the legislation weakens laws meant to prevent energy projects from polluting water, harming endangered species or causing other environmental consequences.

Tribal and pueblo leaders in New Mexico and elsewhere also pushed back against provisions they said would weaken tribal input in developments, including on sacred or culturally meaningful sites. Some leaders traveled to Washington, D.C. in recent weeks to lobby against those provisions. The All Pueblo Council of Governors, which represents 19 New Mexico pueblos, has been among the most outspoken tribal opponents.

New Mexico Mining Watch, a coalition of groups concerned that the bill could hasten uranium mine proposals across the state, urged House members to address concerns raised by tribal nations and groups like the coalition. Ava Curtis, coordinator of the Multicultural Alliance for a Safe Environment, said in a statement: “Communities in New Mexico are still living with the consequences of uranium mining that took place decades ago. Families have spent generations fighting for cleanup, accountability and protection from further contamination. Instead of learning from that history, Congress is advancing legislation that could expose more communities to the same risks.”
