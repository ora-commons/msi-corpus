## States face administrative hurdles enforcing Medicaid work requirements

**Subtype:** fact

- The Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services extended the deadline for state Medicaid programs to impose work requirements to June 30, 2027.
- Nebraska state data showed at least 956 people lost Medicaid by August 2026 because they did not provide required documentation.
- Nebraska had denied 557 new applicants and 533 renewal applications under the new rules by August 2026.
- KFF data show 63% of adults on Medicaid work 80 hours a month or attend school.
- D.C.'s Department of Health Care Finance estimated nearly one-third of about 75,000 adult enrollees without children subject to the rules appeared compliant through household income.

## Nebraska state data show at least 956 coverage losses tied to missing paperwork

The six-month reprieve gives states time, but not relief from the administrative machinery needed to enforce Medicaid work requirements. The Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services extended the deadline to June 30, 2027, after state Medicaid programs said the former Jan. 1 target would be too challenging and expensive. In a Nov. 13 letter, Daniel Brillman, a CMS deputy administrator and director, called the added period a “hold harmless” period in which states would face no penalty. States still face tasks including explaining exemptions, overhauling eligibility systems, collecting income documentation and hiring staff to review documents.

The federal requirements apply to adults under the age of 64 who are able-bodied and earning up to 138% of the federal poverty line, around $22,000 for an individual. Those adults must volunteer, go to school or work for at least 80 hours a month, or meet a minimum household income of $580 a month, or $6,960 for a household year, to be eligible for Medicaid. Among the 41 states plus the District of Columbia that expanded Medicaid to lower-income adults, and states that partially expanded through waivers such as Georgia, Tennessee and Wisconsin, 31 have minimum wages above the federal $7.25 minimum and would require fewer than 80 hours. Across 20 states plus the District of Columbia, enrollees are eligible if one person in their household works at least 40 hours a month. State administrators therefore must account for minimum-wage standards each year, and many states adjust those standards annually.

Some states moved ahead of the deadline and encountered problems. Jeremy Nordquist, president and CEO of the Nebraska Hospital Association, said communication had proven to be the biggest barrier: “It’s really important to try to get down, you know, as deep into the community as possible. Typically these folks aren’t watching local news as much as the general population. They’re not following healthcare policy.”

Nebraska began enforcing the work rules on May 1. State data showed that by August 2026, around four months later, the state had denied 557 new Medicaid applicants. Of those denials, 466, or 83.7%, involved people who had not provided documentation proving compliance or an exemption. Another 533 people seeking to renew Medicaid were denied, and 490 of them, or 91.9%, also had not supplied the required documentation. Overall, at least 956 people lost Medicaid because of paperwork.

Nordquist also criticized Nebraska's initial explanation of the rules. “Early on, we saw the state send a letter to Medicaid recipients with a high-level overview of what would be needed, and it was really written at a 14th-grade reading level,” he said. “That’s really not something that’s easily comprehensible or easily followed up on by Medicaid populations.” In Nebraska, Nordquist said his biggest takeaway for states and hospitals is to start early. Nordquist said the Nebraska Hospital Association created Spanish-language resources that hospitals could use for community outreach, explaining the requirements and telling recipients to keep their information current. He said members of the Medicaid expansion population move more frequently than the general population, making physical mail less reliable. “That’s why it’s so important we find ways to break down those barriers and get people’s contact information updated as frequently as possible.”

Dr. Aaron Wernham, CEO of the Montana Healthcare Foundation, said Montana already had difficulty helping enrollees complete paperwork before imposing work requirements. “We have long wait times in the Medicaid call center, high rates of call abandonment by people trying to call in to get help and also very high rates — among the nation’s highest — of renewal applications taking more than 45 days to process, that was before all the work requirement stuff,” Wernham said.

The documentation process also extends to determining whether a person works, attends school or qualifies for an exemption. Amaya Diana, a health policy analyst at KFF, said states might check pay stubs, other data sources or connections with education hubs to verify hours worked and education status. “And then, if they don’t have data on file, that’s where they may ask for additional documentation from an enrollee, and that is where they might be submitting extra documents to the state,” Diana said.

Rebecca Kellenberg, a principal at Health Management Associates, said states expect many beneficiaries already to be working or to qualify for exclusions, making documentation the central challenge. “I think states are anticipating that the majority are already working or will meet some exclusions based on their circumstances, and that the challenge is documenting that work,” Kellenberg said. “It’s a communication challenge to figure out what the documentation requirements are … that will be the biggest challenge for ensuring individuals don’t lose coverage when they are in fact eligible and meeting requirements.”

Jennifer Evans, an attorney at Polsinelli working with health care providers and a former Medicaid director for Colorado, said states already check income for Medicaid eligibility, but requiring proof of income twice a year creates a significant and unnecessary burden. KFF data show 63% of adults on Medicaid work 80 hours a month or attend school. Evans said many beneficiaries are low-wage earners whose circumstances mean the dollar threshold may have little practical effect. “It’s just that they don’t make enough money, or they don’t work in a location that offers health insurance. … These are low-wage earners, and so I don’t think that the fact that the dollar number is less is going to have a material impact,” Evans said. She described the process as an additional administrative burden for both Medicaid agencies and providers helping beneficiaries submit evidence. The report also said doctors and hospitals in three dozen states would see lower Medicaid payments.

In Washington, D.C., the Medicaid program must distinguish District workers from federal workers. Federal workers are paid at least $7.25 per hour, while District workers earn $18.40, almost three times as much. Melisa Byrd, senior deputy director of the Department of Health Care Finance, which administers D.C.'s Medicaid program, wrote: “The biggest challenge will be ensuring that District residents are reporting all their income so that DHCF (Department of Health Care Finance) can accurately determine their total income and subsequently, their compliance with the work requirements.”

Byrd wrote that the department's goal was to use electronic sources for income verification whenever possible, while accepting other documentation showing earned income. The department estimated that among approximately 75,000 Medicaid adult enrollees without children who would be subject to the work requirements, nearly a third appeared compliant based on household income of at least $580 a month.

Jennifer Wagner, director of Medicaid eligibility and enrollment at the Center on Budget and Policy Priorities, said many beneficiaries have little or no income when they apply for coverage. “Many people on Medicaid have little to no income, and little to no taxable income,” Wagner said. “Many people turn to Medicaid in a time of crisis, such as when they’ve lost a job and are in between jobs, or they’ve had a medical crisis that’s caused them to take time off of work,” she said. “Their annual income might be higher, but when they apply for Medicaid, they may be very low income.” Wagner said states will face confusion and paperwork issues involving people in school, medically frail enrollees, caregivers and others covered by exclusions. Enrollees taking care of children under the age of 14 are also exempt.

In June, 25 Democratic-led states plus the District of Columbia sued the Trump administration over the work requirement, largely over changes to “medically frail” guidelines that asked enrollees to prove they were too sick to work. In late October, a U.S. district judge struck down that part of the rule, saying it would cause significant harm to enrollees and create costs for states, while granting some concessions to the federal government in other areas.

The court upheld a separate rule requiring states to look back 12 months of claims data when determining whether an enrollee qualifies for the medical-frailty exemption. States also must look back at least one month at an enrollee's work history before the person can apply, a requirement Wagner said had not previously applied. KFF reported that 31 states had opted to examine one month of work history, five planned to examine two to three months, and another two planned to examine three months after a quarterly compliance check.

“This idea of looking back at a month instead of what your current circumstances are is not a concept in Medicaid,” Wagner said. “It’s not the way applications are structured. It’s not the way eligibility systems are structured, and so this is a totally new thing.”

The reprieve changes the calendar, but not the need for accurate records, clear notices and current contact information. Medicaid enrollees may work inconsistently, face unemployment or sudden changes in their circumstances, or qualify through school, caregiving, medical conditions or other exclusions. Until states can distinguish noncompliance from missing paperwork, eligible beneficiaries remain at risk of losing coverage.
