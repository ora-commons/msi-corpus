## Expired Tomahawks and submarine command firings emerged at the hearing

Hung Cao, President Trump's nominee to become Navy secretary, faced questions from Republican and Democratic senators at his confirmation hearing Tuesday about the Iran war, military resource strains and the living conditions and mental health of sailors on extended deployments in the Middle East. In answer to a question about the high numbers of munitions used in Iran, the retired Navy captain said the Navy had been using expired Tomahawk missiles in the Middle East because of the military's shortage of critical munitions and had recoded them for Operation Epic Fury. He also disclosed that the Navy had fired the three top leaders of the USS South Dakota because of a near mishap and faced questions over eight reported suicide attempts aboard the USS Abraham Lincoln.

Cao, whose family fled Vietnam during the war, was confirmed as Navy undersecretary in 2025. Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth tapped him to lead the service on an acting basis in April after firing John Phelan as Navy secretary. Senators would need to approve a waiver for Cao to serve permanently because he left the military less than seven years ago. His confirmation as undersecretary allows him to continue as acting secretary through the remainder of the Trump administration.

Cao said the Navy had been using expired Tomahawk missiles in the Middle East because of a shortage of critical munitions. He made the disclosure in answer to a question about the high numbers of munitions used in Iran. Operation Epic Fury is the name for the U.S. war with Iran. He compared the Navy's workaround to eating expired bread.

“I came from a poor family, right? You know, bread—things like bread, doesn’t expire. You just rub some water on and microwave for 15 seconds, and it’s good to go,” Cao said. “And so for some of these munitions, we recoded them.”

Mark Montgomery, a retired rear admiral and expert with the Foundation for Defense of Democracies, said the Navy sets expiration dates because after a certain age, some percentage of missiles have technical issues. Montgomery said the Navy can recode missiles as acceptable to fire, usually after an inspection verifies that performance has not degraded.

The United States also used expired Tomahawks during last year's Christmas Day strikes against Islamic State militants in Nigeria, according to a U.S. official familiar with the operation. Several of those missiles did not explode and were found in civilian areas.

Lawmakers repeatedly pressed Cao about living conditions and mental health for sailors on extended deployments in the Middle East. Asked about reports of eight suicide attempts aboard the USS Abraham Lincoln, Cao said the Navy employs chaplains to help with sailors' mental health and pointed out that none of the attempts succeeded.

“Unfortunately this is the fight we’re dealing with right now,” Cao said.

Sen. Kirsten Gillibrand, a Democrat from New York, criticized Cao for calling the attempts “suicide mitigations,” saying the phrase was “an insult to these men and women who are devoting their lives to the armed services.”

Sen. Mark Kelly, a Democrat from Arizona and a retired Navy captain, said he had heard from the parent of a sailor serving in the Middle East that the sailor had lost more than 45 pounds in six months because of insufficient food and lacked basic hygiene supplies.

“These are servicemembers deployed in support of combat operations, and they’re not even being given the basic things to stay healthy and carry out their missions,” Kelly said. “This is completely unacceptable.”

Cao said sailors had “adequate” rations and compared reports of servicemembers losing weight to “my kids opening up the refrigerator door and saying there’s nothing eating here.” Kelly rejected the comparison.

Cao also said the Navy had taken the unusual step of firing the three top leaders of the Virginia-class attack submarine USS South Dakota because the boat experienced a “near mishap.” The Navy was still investigating the incident, he said, and he declined to provide additional details. Sen. Tim Kaine, a Democrat from Virginia, said the incident happened in September.

Cao discussed the incident in response to a question from ranking member Sen. Jack Reed, a Democrat from Rhode Island, who rebuked the acting secretary for failing to inform Congress, which the Navy normally does.

“The squadron commodore felt that they didn’t live up to their honor, courage, and commitment ethos,” Cao said. He also said he initially withheld the information from lawmakers because he did not want it to leak to the media.

Cao said “there’s no excuse” for not informing Congress and promised to brief lawmakers in depth about the incident.

Lawmakers from both parties expressed frustration over the Pentagon's lack of communication with Congress. Sen. Mike Rounds, a Republican from South Dakota, chastised Cao for not inviting Democrats to high-level briefings. Rounds said the Pentagon leadership's lack of engagement with Democrats had made it more difficult for Congress to fund the Pentagon's full budget request.

“Pretty tough to ask all of my colleagues to support that if they’re not even invited in to hear the information in the first place,” Rounds said.

Cao said he was committed to “being open and honest with the entire Senate” and called national defense a “bipartisan issue.”

Sen. Roger Wicker, a Republican from Mississippi, said Trump's desire to put steam catapults on future aircraft carriers was a nonstarter. “Returning to steam is a nonstarter, and everybody needs to understand that,” Wicker said.

Trump has long opposed the electromagnetic catapults used on the Gerald R. Ford-class aircraft carriers and pushed the Navy to revert to steam catapults on a new carrier already under construction.

Kaine, who defeated Cao in the 2024 race for Virginia's Senate seat, told Cao that sailors had complimented the new electromagnetic system during his recent visit to the Ford, which was deployed at the beginning of the Iran conflict. Trump administration officials have argued that carrier technology needs to be simplified so ships can be built faster.

Cao said installing the new electromagnetic catapults and elevators had delayed carrier construction. Kaine said retrofitting carriers with steam catapults would lead to even more cost increases and diminished capability.

“I do hope you’ll consider the experience of those who’ve been operating these launches on the Ford…before you decide to go down a path that will be more expensive and also less capable,” Kaine said.

The Navy was still investigating the South Dakota incident, and Cao promised lawmakers a more detailed briefing. His appointment still requires a Senate waiver because he left the military less than seven years ago, though his 2025 confirmation as undersecretary allows him to keep serving in an acting capacity for the remainder of the Trump administration.
