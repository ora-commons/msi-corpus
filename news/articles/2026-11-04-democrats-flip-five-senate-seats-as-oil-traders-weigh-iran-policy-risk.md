## Jan. 3 start leaves Trump’s Iran diplomacy and strike options open

Democrats flipped five Republican-held Senate seats in the midterm elections, giving the party control of the chamber, according to The Wall Street Journal. The result could add constraints on President Trump’s agenda, particularly funding for overseas military operations and other foreign-policy commitments, and complicate his efforts to pursue his foreign-policy priorities. The changed balance of power would take time to translate into policy.

“The new Congress will not take office until Jan. 3, leaving nearly two months for the administration to pursue further foreign policy initiatives under the existing congressional composition,” said Michelle Brouhard, head of policy and geopolitical risk at Kpler.

## Oil traders weigh diplomacy against further strikes

Wednesday’s oil advance came as investors assessed how the midterm result could shape Trump’s approach to Iran. Front-month Brent crude, the global oil benchmark, rose 0.3% to $103.58 in European trading. West Texas Intermediate, the U.S. benchmark, gained 0.2% to $94.99 a barrel. High energy prices were adding to inflation and weighing on consumers worldwide.

For oil traders, the immediate question was whether Washington would pursue a diplomatic agreement with Tehran or launch another round of U.S. military strikes. Prices had been volatile that week as fresh U.S. military action was weighed against diplomatic efforts to end the conflict with Iran.

Warren Patterson and Ewa Manthey, analysts at ING, said the oil market had increasingly priced in the possibility of further hostilities between the U.S. and Iran. If the elections passed without either a new military escalation or a diplomatic agreement, traders would have to assess whether the geopolitical risk premium in oil prices could be sustained or begin to ease, the analysts said.

Negotiation progress brought some relief, but uncertainty over regional developments and persistent threats to shipping continued to support prices. A U.S. attack on Iran’s Qeshm Island fueled fears of renewed escalation in the region.

## Shipping and consumer risks broaden the stakes

Under a broader military campaign, Iran could retaliate against economic and civilian infrastructure across the region. Such retaliation could further disrupt energy supplies and threaten shipping through the Strait of Hormuz, which The Wall Street Journal described as a critical artery for global oil and gas trade.

The conflict had put energy prices at the center of the midterm political debate as Americans confronted higher costs for fuel, groceries and utilities. Inflation remained above the Federal Reserve’s 2% target, while the economic fallout from the Iran war added to price pressures and uncertainty about the outlook.

“I think the starting point is that Americans vote with their pocketbooks, which is nothing new,” said Brouhard. “Affordability and inflation are central concerns, and energy prices are one of the most visible ways consumers experience those pressures.”

Analysts also identified negotiations involving Russia and Ukraine as another key development for energy markets. Progress could affect U.S. sanctions on the Kremlin and Russian fuel exports, as well as the security of energy infrastructure at a time when refined-product markets, particularly diesel, remained severely constrained.
