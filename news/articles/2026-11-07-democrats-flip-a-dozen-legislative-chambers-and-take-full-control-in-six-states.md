## Early sessions next year would mark Democrats' first trifecta lead since 2009

Democrats flipped a dozen state legislative chambers in Tuesday's elections and took full control of state government in six states, the party's best statehouse showing in more than four decades, according to The Wall Street Journal. The party captured both legislative chambers and the governor's office — a trifecta — in Arizona, Michigan, Minnesota, Nevada, Pennsylvania and Wisconsin.

The results carry practical weight. With Congress often gridlocked and court rulings returning more authority to the states, legislatures play an increasing role in Americans' daily lives, setting policy in areas including education, healthcare, labor, abortion and gun laws, the Journal reported.

## The trifecta count and its historical benchmarks

Republicans headed into Election Day holding trifectas in 23 states, Democrats in 16, and the two parties shared power in 10 states. Nebraska's officially nonpartisan unicameral legislature was outside the count. Democrats emerged from Tuesday's elections with 22 trifectas. Alaska's races had not been called and the state was excluded from the historical totals.

When state legislative sessions begin early next year, Democrats will have more trifectas than Republicans for the first time since 2009, the year after Barack Obama won his first term as president. The breakdown then was 17 to nine in Democrats' favor. The Journal said all of that changed in 2010, when a Republican wave flipped more than 20 chambers and gave the GOP 21 trifectas, a tally that would reach 23 in subsequent years.

The Journal's trifecta chart labels each year by the beginning of the legislative term. A separate state-control chart noted that, after President Nixon's resignation, no states were controlled by Republicans, and that Democrats fully controlled more states than Republicans twice in the last three decades.

The historical data cited the Council of State Governments, the National Conference of State Legislatures, the National Governors Association, the Wyoming Almanac, the Idaho State Historical Society, the South Dakota State Historical Society and Ballotpedia; the Associated Press supplied the 2026 results.

## Where control changed

Democrats picked up trifectas in Michigan, Minnesota, Nevada, Pennsylvania and Wisconsin, in addition to Arizona. Republicans lost trifectas in Georgia, Iowa, New Hampshire, Ohio and Texas. In Texas and Georgia, Democrats retook the House after more than two decades. In Arizona, they will lead both chambers for the first time in 60 years.

Texas had been a Republican trifecta for more than 20 years. The Journal described Tuesday's result as a surprise: Republicans lost two of their three governing legs, the governor's office and the House. “This is a revolution in Texas,” said Brandon Rottinghaus, a political-science professor at the University of Houston. “The slow progress that Republicans made from the ’90s to the mid-2000s let them piecemeal and gradually pick up seats. Democrats did it all in one night.”

## State leaders set out policy agendas

Democrats in some states have waited decades for the chance to pass a raft of policies.

In Wisconsin, where Democrats claimed both chambers for the first time in 16 years and won the governorship, the to-do list was long, said Greta Neubauer, the state Assembly's Democratic leader, who is poised to become speaker. Goals she ticked off in an interview included easing the pinch of high grocery costs by giving free school meals to every child; stepping up regulation of prescription-drug costs; increasing public-education funding; and repealing the state's right-to-work law, which bars making union membership or paying dues mandatory to get or keep a job.

After Minnesota Democrats picked up a trifecta in 2022, they passed a flurry of bills that prompted Republican accusations of liberal overreach. Neubauer said she wasn't worried about backlash. “I think we’re very attuned to the challenges that people are facing and the things that they’re looking for us to do,” she said.

Pennsylvania Democrats last held a trifecta in 1993, when a split in the state Senate made the Democratic lieutenant governor the tiebreaker; before Tuesday, voters had not given the party outright control since the 1970s. Democrats had held a narrow state House majority while Republicans controlled the Senate.

The Journal described the outcome as “a boon” for Democratic Gov. Josh Shapiro and said he was “widely seen as eyeing a 2028 White House run.” “Here’s what I can guarantee you we will get done,” Shapiro said at a celebratory postelection news conference. “We will fund mass transit. We will raise the minimum wage. We will make sure that women have a right to reproductive freedom here in this commonwealth. We will build more housing.”

Republican state Sen. Kim Ward, who has led Pennsylvania's Senate, said she respected voters' will but warned of what the results would bring. “There’s no question the results of this election will have significant consequences for our commonwealth in the form of higher taxes, increased electric rates, power grid challenges, stagnating our energy potential with net-zero policies and government interfering in our personal lives,” she said.

Arizona Democrats will be fully in charge for the first time since 1966, after a two-chamber flip and the re-election of Democratic Gov. Katie Hobbs. Veteran political consultant Chuck Coughlin said the realignment puts the state in uncharted territory. “None of them have ever been in a majority leadership position,” he said of Democrats. “This is going to be a real experiment in terms of democracy and leadership.”

Hobbs, who vetoed many bills passed by the Republican-led legislature in her first term, outlined plans for her second term in a postelection speech. She promised to “crack down on the waste, fraud, and abuse in the out-of-control” school voucher program while delivering $1 billion to public schools without raising taxes. She pledged to cut starter-home costs, expand apprenticeship programs and protect Arizona's water resources.

“When I got to the legislature in 2011, there were so few Democrats in the Senate that we joked the caucus could share one pizza,” Hobbs said. “We’ve now gone from a one-pizza caucus to a majority in both chambers.”

## National politics and the durability of gains

The late House Speaker Tip O'Neill quipped that all politics is local. But for at least the past century, statehouse elections have closely tracked U.S. House races, according to data analyzed by Steven Rogers, a political-science professor at Saint Louis University.

“State legislative elections are dominated by national politics,” Rogers said. “Presidential approval is one of the best predictors of how someone’s going to vote.” Many voters do not know who their state representative is, he noted, and he argued that voters' ire at President Trump drove this year's Democratic statehouse wins.

The Wall Street Journal said the party's gains might be fragile because many Democratic candidates won in historically Republican districts. “Democrats are going to play so much defense in 2028,” Rogers said.
