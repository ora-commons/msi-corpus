## A majority of midterm voters say AI will hurt society more than help it

President Donald Trump's push to reinstate Jensen Huang as Nvidia's chief executive comes as Congress prepares to investigate whether the president and Nvidia officials skirted U.S. rules designed to keep advanced chips out of China's hands. The scrutiny arrives at a moment when voters are worried about AI-related job losses and political self-dealing, and Nvidia, the chip maker that is a pillar of the AI economy, has become the focus of congressional attention to the administration's dealings with it.

Congress is preparing to investigate the national-security risks of the administration's interactions with Nvidia. Many Republican lawmakers who have overwhelmingly supported President Donald Trump in the past are now saying they plan to press his administration and Nvidia officials on whether they skirted U.S. rules designed to prevent China from obtaining the company's most sophisticated chips, and on why President Donald Trump intervened on Huang's behalf. On Capitol Hill last week, lawmakers offered little support for the president's decision to intervene with Nvidia on Huang's behalf.

Huang's own record is scheduled to come before Congress. The Nvidia board fired Huang after he admitted to two extramarital affairs, including one with a suspected Chinese intelligence asset, only to bring him back 11 days later at President Donald Trump's urging. Huang is scheduled to be interviewed by three House committees privately Dec. 1, then in public by the Senate Banking Committee on Dec. 3.

The money ties run alongside the policy questions. President Donald Trump owns Nvidia stock that at one point in October was worth $83 million, and Nvidia committed $25 million to the president's White House ballroom project and gave $1 million to his second inauguration. White House spokesman Davis Ingle said the president's stock-and-bond investments are managed by independent financial institutions. "Neither President Trump nor any member of his family has any ability to direct, influence, or provide input regarding how the portfolio is invested or when investments are bought or sold," Ingle said. At Huang's urging, President Donald Trump approved several deals to send Nvidia chips to China and other countries posing security concerns, and he adopted an AI policy that is friendly to the industry.

The political space opened after the midterms. The party's losses have weakened President Donald Trump's standing, leaving Republicans feeling more free to investigate whether the president's personal interests have been intertwined with government policy. President Donald Trump is increasingly building his second-term legacy on a bet that what is good for artificial-intelligence companies is good for America, and persuading voters of that bet is where the difficulty is showing up. His push to reinstate Huang was one of several actions allying him with AI-industry leaders who are building enormous wealth, and those leaders face a backlash from voters who worry that AI will undercut their job prospects and that politicians are giving special deals to themselves and political insiders.

Voters have supplied the other half of the political problem. The midterm elections turned up significant opposition to data centers and anxiety about AI. Democrats such as Sherrod Brown, the party's Senate nominee in Ohio, won election after making opposition to data centers a prominent part of their campaigns. Voters in 13 states this year sided against data centers in 53 of the 54 ballot measures decided so far, according to a tally by Ballotopedia, an election-tracking site. A large poll of midterm voters nationally found that 57% believe AI will do more to hurt society than help it, with about 25% saying they saw more benefits than harms. Three-quarters said they would oppose construction of a new data center in their community, according to the poll, called The Voter Poll by SSRS.

The administration's answer is that American leadership on AI pays. White House officials say that maintaining U.S. leadership on AI will fuel revenue growth, innovation and productivity across the economy. Stocks initially rallied on President Donald Trump's success in persuading the Nvidia board to rehire Huang, which the president has called a vote of confidence in his actions. President Donald Trump said in a press conference last week that "We have record numbers of jobs in construction, because they're building all of the new plants" and "we're going to fight very hard for the data centers." The president also promised to impose "tremendous penalties" on companies that build data centers in other countries rather than the U.S.

Conservative and libertarian voices say the president's case has not been made. Scott Lincicome, vice president of economics and trade at the libertarian Cato Institute, said "The president has done a pretty lousy job of explaining the potential benefits of this technology for the U.S. economy and national security." Lincicome said that as a result, it is harder for President Donald Trump to argue that Americans benefit from reinstalling Huang as CEO. He also said, "Rightly or wrongly, it shows a president that cares more about billionaires than average people," and added, "People turn on the TV, and Trump is intervening on behalf of his billionaire buddy."

Douglas Heye, a Republican strategist and former congressional staff member, said that because President Donald Trump hasn't persuaded Americans that they will benefit personally from AI technology, voters might see his efforts to help Nvidia as a form of self-dealing. "Trump hasn't put in any work to convince the American people of why this is important," Heye said of the advancement of AI technology. "And I don't think there's a Republican on Capitol Hill who can tell you what the administration's AI policy is in any coherent way."

Conservative commentators see a significant risk to President Donald Trump in the hearings. Jesse Waters, a Trump ally, said on his Fox News program that "This is going to get ugly fast," connecting the Republican midterm losses to the challenge in the coming hearings. Waters said that as a billionaire "chip king," a Republican donor and admitted adulterer, Huang "isn't the most sympathetic character for the president's party to defend." "That's going to be a hard argument to make on TV for senators who just got their heads kicked in for data centers and the cost of living," Waters said.

Ben Shapiro, a conservative commentator, warned on his podcast this week that the Nvidia matter mixed a set of ingredients Democrats could make politically toxic: accusations of self-dealing, a weak national-security approach to China and public anxiety over artificial intelligence. "What if you wrap that all up into a ball and just threw that like a bomb into the middle of the American public discourse?" Shapiro asked. "Because that's what's about to happen."

One Trump ally outside Congress defended the decision. Dan Bongino, who served for nine months as deputy FBI director in President Donald Trump's current term, said on his podcast that Nvidia was right to rehire Huang, though he knew many in his audience disagreed. "If Jensen Huang were to leave and take his talents to another company that would benefit a foreign government, folks, we're going to be screwed," Bongino said.
