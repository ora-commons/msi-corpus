## USDA proposal would end automatic SNAP eligibility tied to TANF

**Subtype:** fact

- The U.S. Department of Agriculture proposed a rule change Tuesday that would end automatic SNAP eligibility for people who qualify for Temporary Assistance for Needy Families benefits.
- USDA estimates the change would affect over 17% of SNAP participants, about 3.2 million households or 6.3 million individuals, by the 2029 budget year.
- The proposal says the change would reduce SNAP spending by $44.6 billion over five years and ensure the program benefits the truly needy.
- Jessica Dietrich of Hunger Free Oklahoma said reinstated asset limits would hit Oklahoma families hardest and would set a hard income cutoff at 130% of the poverty line.

## Oklahoma agency has no estimate of residents affected by proposal

The proposal, posted Tuesday by the U.S. Department of Agriculture, would end a current practice that allows automatic eligibility for the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program, or SNAP, for people who are meeting the requirements and receiving benefits from Temporary Assistance for Needy Families, another federal aid program. The posted rule is open for public comment until Dec. 10.

SNAP, commonly known as food stamps, provides aid to more than 36 million people across the country, including over half a million Oklahomans. USDA estimates the rule change would impact over 17% of participants, which is about 3.2 million households or 6.3 million individuals by the 2029 budget year when the rule would be fully implemented. The change would reduce SNAP spending by $44.6 billion over five years and ensure the program benefits the truly needy, according to the proposal.

SNAP has already been subject to a series of congressional changes that made millions ineligible, and the rule change posted Tuesday could add to this number.

Jessica Dietrich, director of government relations and public policy at Hunger Free Oklahoma, called the proposal "terrible news" for Oklahoma and all states. Hunger Free Oklahoma works to ensure all Oklahomans have access to affordable and nutritious food.

Dietrich identified two aspects to the proposed change. The one that would most impact Oklahoma families, she said, is reimplementation of strict asset limits to be eligible for SNAP. That means assets that are available and could be used to purchase food, like a savings account, could count against a family's eligibility.

Dietrich said that under current policy, if someone is slightly over income limits but qualifies for the Temporary Assistance for Needy Families Program, or TANF, it doesn't matter, but the rule change would create a hard cutoff at 130% of the poverty line, which equates to an income of $42,900 for a family of four.

"If you don't use SNAP or have a loved one that uses the program, it might be easy to think that it doesn't affect you but that's less money flowing into our local grocery stores," Dietrich said. "And it could be the difference between a grocery store, especially in a rural area, staying open or having to close because they don't have enough income."

Oklahoma is already one of the most food insecure states, Dietrich said, with 1 in 5 people and a quarter of children in the state facing food insecurity.

Dietrich said the change would also burden state agencies and make SNAP less efficient. "Our state, and every state, is already facing so many new rules that they have to implement into very antiquated technology that doesn't pivot as easily as someone in the public might think," Dietrich said. Adding another step where paperwork needs to be gathered will overload agencies and overwhelm families, she said, and it will likely also impact the state's error rate, which could cost Oklahoma more money.

SNAP has already been subject to a series of changes under the Big Beautiful Bill passed last year by Congress. This includes work requirements to maintain eligibility for SNAP as well as requiring states to lower their error rates or pay a larger share of the program's cost. Error rates refer to payment inaccuracies, which are not necessarily fraud and can be underpayments or overpayments.

The Oklahoma Department of Human Services, which administers SNAP, does not yet have a reliable estimate of how many Oklahomans would be impacted by the rule change, said Nazarene Harris, an agency spokesperson. Harris said the agency is still reviewing the proposal to understand how it would impact eligibility for the program in the state.

Since the passage of the Big Beautiful Bill, the Oklahoma Department of Human Services has worked to lower its error rate to 11.04% through additional training, oversight and quality control to improve payment accuracy, Harris said. An error rate of 6% is needed to prevent Oklahoma from shouldering a larger share of the program's cost.

Since November 2025, Oklahoma's SNAP participation has declined by about 70,000 participants, which is consistent with national trends, Harris said. While Congress made changes to eligibility, Harris said participation can change for many reasons and cannot entirely be attributed to the Big Beautiful Bill.
