## Attorney says officers pointed weapons at family after fake tip

The woman known as Jane Doe in the Cornell seven case is receiving threats from people who have discovered her identity, according to her attorney, Thomas Giuffra. Guardian columnist Arwa Mahdawi detailed the threats in a column published Oct. 10, writing that Giuffra described his client as terrified for her safety.

Mahdawi wrote that Doe "was even the victim of a swatting episode this week," a form of harassment in which someone places a false call to emergency services with the goal of sending armed officers to the victim's location. Giuffra told CNN that officers pointed weapons at Doe's family on Tuesday evening after a fake tip about someone "killing people in the house."

"If you had a trigger-happy cop, or somebody had done the wrong thing, somebody could have ended up dead," Giuffra said.

Mahdawi wrote that nobody "ended up dead on that particular occasion," but that Doe's "character is systematically being assassinated." The column argues the response to Doe repeats a pattern that follows other women whose sexual assault accusations become national news.

The column traces that pattern to Christine Blasey Ford, who accused Brett Kavanaugh, then a Supreme Court nominee, of assaulting her when they were both teenagers in the 1980s. Ford and her family reportedly had to flee their home because of threats and harassment, and, according to her lawyers, her email was hacked and she was impersonated online. Kavanaugh, the column noted, was praised by Donald Trump and now holds a lifetime appointment on the Supreme Court.

The column draws a similar parallel to E Jean Carroll, who accused Trump of sexually assaulting her in 1996. Trump said Carroll was not his "type" and was "totally lying." His lawyer asked Carroll why she did not scream during the alleged attack and why she did not go to the police at the time. Carroll's life was upended, and the Trump administration opened a criminal investigation into her, Mahdawi wrote.

Mahdawi also cited recent commentary about Doe. Caitlin Flanagan wrote in the Free Press: "What attracts women to those parties – late at night, like Jane Doe – to walk alone to a fraternity house and take her chances?" and "What she's really looking for is a man. And she has no idea how to find a good one." Mahdawi wrote that the underlying message was that Doe "should have known better than to go to a frat party" and was "asking for it."

Rightwing commentator Megyn Kelly, who has been vocal about her experience of sexual harassment at Fox News, went further and attacked Doe's looks, Mahdawi wrote. "No one will even consider the possibility that this woman made a decision to get it on with four men total, not seven, and enjoyed it because, I'm just going to be honest, she's not the most attractive gal," Kelly said on a recent episode of her podcast. "She's extremely beefy in like her arms and her legs. She has a whole video online talking about her cankles. And she possibly enjoyed being admired. This is how women make these mistakes."

Mahdawi wrote, "It should go without saying that being raped is not a 'mistake'." She wrote that "a woman should be able to go to a party and have a drink and walk home at night and wear whatever she likes without it being an invitation for men to rape her."
