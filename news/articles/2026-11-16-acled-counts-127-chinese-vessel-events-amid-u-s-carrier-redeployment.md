## Three incidents brought Chinese and Philippine forces into direct contact

Armed Conflict Location & Event Data, or Acled, a U.S.-based nonprofit that monitors conflict, counted 127 events involving Chinese military, coast-guard, maritime-militia and government vessels in and around the South China Sea between late July and the end of October. The tally covered movements of forces and incidents of harassment. The total was 94 between mid-April and late July and 55 between January and mid-April.

Philippine Defense Secretary Gilberto Teodoro Jr., speaking in an interview, said there had been an “increase in the more aggressive forms of maritime activities.” The reporting period included three confrontations. In October, a Chinese jet fighter buzzed a Philippine military plane with a series of dangerous maneuvers. In September, a Chinese coast-guard ship collided with a Philippine government vessel that was bringing fuel to fishermen. In July, when a rubber boat from the Philippine navy approached a small boat carrying Chinese coast-guard personnel, the Chinese personnel used wooden batons to strike Philippine sailors.

The Wall Street Journal described frequent clashes with Philippine vessels in areas claimed by both China and the Philippines. Such incidents create risks for the United States, which has a mutual-defense treaty with Manila and would have to decide whether to come to the Philippines' aid if the clashes developed into actual fighting. The risk coincided with the U.S. moving important military assets, including aircraft carriers, from the Pacific to support operations in the Middle East. That transfer raised concern among some U.S. allies about Washington's ability to respond to a crisis in Asia.

President Donald Trump's planned visit to Manila for Tuesday's summit of Southeast Asian leaders is the immediate setting for these security questions. The Wall Street Journal described the Philippines as a key U.S. ally that has been “on the front lines of geopolitical tensions with China and of America's efforts to project power in the Pacific.” The report said the administration had stayed close to Manila, contrasting that relationship with what it called President Donald Trump's “strained relations with other longtime allies,” particularly in Europe.

The Philippines is a nation of 7,641 islands perched along some of the world's most strategic waterways. The report said the country has taken a leading role in challenging China's claims to much of the South China Sea, which it calls a crucial trade artery. The Philippines would be a central staging ground for any U.S. defense of Taiwan, the self-ruling democracy claimed by Beijing.

Trump has also worked to build closer ties with Chinese leader Xi Jinping, whom he is due to meet in Shenzhen later in the week. The two leaders are also due to meet Russian President Vladimir Putin in what the report called an unprecedented three-way summit.

Joseph Wu, a Taiwanese national security official, said on social media that a Chinese carrier strike group had been operating east of the Philippines while Chinese coast-guard vessels were sailing in the waters east of Taiwan. Laura Sorica, an Asia-Pacific analyst at Acled, said, “Beijing is appearing to be seeking to gradually change the operational status quo in its favor, while also making sure that all these different actions and incidents remain below the threshold of an open conflict.”

The Wall Street Journal assessed that “Controlling the South China Sea and the waters to the east would give the Chinese navy a major advantage in a blockade or invasion of Taiwan.” The report said China's new aircraft carriers and quieter submarines could help block U.S. warships and resupply efforts.

Joshua Kurlantzick, a senior fellow for Southeast Asia and South Asia at the Council on Foreign Relations, said China appears to be pushing deeper into the Pacific at a time when the U.S. is distracted by its war with Iran and other Asian countries have been hurt by the subsequent energy crisis. He said Beijing's goal is “making the idea of a U.S. intervention harder and harder to imagine.”

A Chinese foreign ministry statement this year said Chinese actions in the South China Sea have been “reasonable, legitimate, professional and restrained” in response to what it described as provocations by certain countries. The ministry said China remained committed to resolving disputes through negotiation and consultation.

Scarborough Shoal is a reef around 120 nautical miles west of Luzon, the main Philippine island. The Wall Street Journal reported that China had “effectively controlled” the shoal with navy and coast-guard ships since a 2012 standoff between Beijing and Manila and had “tightened its grip” over the past year. China declared a nature sanctuary in the area and temporarily deployed a floating platform. China said the platform was for scientific research and increased its coast-guard patrols around the shoal.

The report warned: “Any attempts by China to build permanent structures on Scarborough Shoal would be a major escalation and a test for the U.S.-Philippine alliance.” The report said Beijing had built a series of large artificial islands in the South China Sea that can host military assets such as aircraft, missiles and ships.

Asked about China's actions in the South China Sea, Teodoro said, “I have always made no bones about it, that we are in for a long struggle in this.” Teodoro also said, “But it's either you struggle for your national territory and dignity, or you don't and suffer the consequences.”
