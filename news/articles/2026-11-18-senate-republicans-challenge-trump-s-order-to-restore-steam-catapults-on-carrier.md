## GOP lawmakers cite faster launches and fewer sailors on USS Gerald R. Ford

Republican senators are seeking to reverse President Donald Trump's August order to retrofit new aircraft carriers with old steam catapults to launch fighter jets, a directive that, they say, would cost billions of taxpayer dollars and could blunt the lethality of America's most advanced warships. The challenge surfaced on two tracks this week: a 2027 defense appropriations bill released Monday by Republican members of the Senate Appropriations Committee that would direct the Navy to compare the effectiveness of the new magnetically powered catapult system with the old steam-powered one, and a Tuesday Senate Armed Services Committee hearing on Trump's nomination of acting Navy Secretary Hung Cao to permanently take the top civilian job.

The dispute comes as Trump tries to convince Congress to pass his $1.5 trillion defense budget before the end of the year. Democrats will take control of the House and Senate on Jan. 3, setting the stage for spending disputes in Washington, and the pushback has arrived in the waning days of the Republican-controlled Congress.

Sen. Roger Wicker, the Mississippi Republican who chairs the Senate Armed Services Committee, said at a Tuesday hearing: “Returning to steam is a nonstarter, and everybody needs to understand that.” Republicans on the Appropriations Committee made the same opposition clear in their 2027 defense appropriations bill, released Monday, which would direct the Navy to provide a report on the effectiveness of the new, magnetically powered catapult system that shoots fighter jets off the deck of carriers versus the old steam-powered one.

In the explanatory statement accompanying the spending bill, the Republican lawmakers wrote: “The Committee is concerned that such a major redesign would result in many billions of dollars in additional costs and many years in program schedule delays.” The statement warned against “such significant programmatic shifts without substantive analysis, justification, and Congressional approval.”

The statement also relayed what lawmakers and experts have been saying about the fighting in Iran: the new electromagnetic system on the nation's most advanced aircraft carrier, the USS Gerald R. Ford, appeared to show that it launched more planes in less time and with fewer sailors than the steam catapults of older carriers. The new technology also appeared to demonstrate greater “combat lethality than previous generation carriers,” the GOP lawmakers said.

Under questioning from Sen. Tim Kaine, a Democrat from Virginia, at the Armed Services Committee hearing, Cao appeared to stand behind Trump's directive, arguing that including the new system, along with electromagnetically driven elevators, was “delaying our carriers by seven years.” “I’m trying to accelerate that,” he added. His support for the steam order appeared in contrast to his arguments earlier in the hearing, when he touted his decision to halt all engineering changes to the Navy's new frigates to keep that program on track. Asked about the pushback and the contradicting testimony, Lt. Cmdr. Adagray Willis, a spokeswoman for Cao, said Cao would work with the White House, Pentagon, Congress and the defense industry “to ensure ours remains the fiercest Navy in the world.”

The new systems did experience development delays during the USS Ford's construction, but Navy leaders have praised their performance and maintenance following the carrier's recent deployment to the Middle East.

Kaine spoke against the directive at the hearing, noting his conversations with sailors on the flight deck of the USS Ford who spoke “very highly” of the new system. Kaine represents Norfolk, the Ford's homeport, and Newport News, the city where Newport News Shipbuilding, the only company building U.S. aircraft carriers, is headquartered. “I do hope you’ll consider the experience of those who’ve been operating these launches on the Ford before you decide to go down on a path that will be more expensive and less capable,” Kaine told Cao.

Trump's August directive to remove the electromagnetic system and return to steam raised concerns about costs as well as the difficulty of maintaining the older systems, which need more sailors to operate them. His criticism of the electromagnetic system dates to at least 2017 in his first term, when he claimed the new system was “not good” and “doesn’t have the power” of the older, steam systems. “You’re going to goddamned steam,” Trump said in an interview with Time magazine. The topic has frequently come up since then at rallies and other events.

The Republican opposition runs against a position Trump has held since at least 2017: he said in Oval Office remarks last year that the magnetic catapults “didn’t work,” and in August he issued the memo directing a return to the old steam system.

In April, Cao's predecessor, Secretary John Phelan, announced that the Navy was reviewing the design and costs of the Ford-class aircraft carrier and was not ruling out canceling future versions of its design. The newest Chinese aircraft carriers employ electromagnetic catapults, and France has said it plans to use the system on its new carrier.
