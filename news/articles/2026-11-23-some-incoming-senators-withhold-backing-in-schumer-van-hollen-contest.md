## Some incoming senators withhold backing in Schumer-Van Hollen contest

**Subtype:** fact

- Senate Democrats will choose their leader by secret ballot on Dec. 1 as Chuck Schumer of New York faces Chris Van Hollen of Maryland.

- Adam Hamilton said he would not back Schumer but declined to endorse Chris Van Hollen or another candidate.

## Secret Dec. 1 ballot will test incoming senators' promises of change

The Democratic Party's incoming senators have not yet been sworn into office, but the choice of who will lead their new majority has already tested the promise of change they made as candidates. The 13 new Democrats are part of the largest incoming Senate class in decades. Several won in Republican strongholds by promising to shake up Washington and be independent voices.

The incoming senators are now engaged in an intra-party leadership fight over whether to stick with longtime Senate leader Chuck Schumer of New York or back challenger Chris Van Hollen of Maryland. Newly elected progressives, including Abdul El-Sayed of Michigan and Peggy Flanagan of Minnesota, have backed Van Hollen, who is challenging Schumer with promises of a more aggressive legislative approach. Sen.-elect Sherrod Brown of Ohio, who is returning to Washington after losing his seat in 2024, supports Schumer.

Incoming senators from traditionally Republican states such as Texas, Mississippi, Iowa and South Carolina, whose victories helped deliver Democrats the majority, have so far largely avoided taking sides publicly. Annie Andrews of South Carolina has said she is talking with both candidates. Scott Colom of Mississippi says he wants to get to know them better before deciding and describes himself as being in "listening mode."

Kansas Sen.-elect Adam Hamilton said Thursday that he would not back Schumer, but he declined to endorse anyone. "I'm looking for someone who is going to bring people together," Hamilton told MSNow. "Right now, in places like Kansas, there's a lot of people who don't feel that in Chuck Schumer."

Schumer's longstanding relationships and knowledge of Senate machinery are a significant advantage heading into the Dec. 1 secret ballot. He oversees the Senate Democrats' campaign spending group, which has funneled millions into many races. Schumer has also recruited candidates to run in some cases. The reluctance of many incoming senators to commit has put an unusual spotlight on first-term lawmakers who could help shape the party's direction in the new Congress.

"We should be very embracing of the new people who have been through fire to get here," Hawaii Sen. Mazie Hirono, who supports Schumer, said. "We have to make sure that all these voices are heard, and they've got a lot of talent to bring to the table."

Many incoming senators campaigned as political outsiders, promising to bring fresh perspectives to a Washington establishment they argued had failed ordinary Americans. Andrews, a 46-year-old pediatrician, became the first Democrat in nearly three decades to win a Senate race in South Carolina. She called for "generational change" and said she would not support Schumer. She has also rejected policies Van Hollen supports, such as "Medicare for All." On a press call Tuesday, however, Andrews said she had made "no decision" on the majority leader's race.

"The way I assess the situation as it is right now is that the generational change is represented by this incredibly dynamic incoming freshman class of senators," Andrews said.

Texas Sen.-elect James Talarico called for "new blood" in the Democratic Party, arguing that fresh perspectives were needed to change a political system that had failed. Talarico stopped short of rejecting Schumer, saying in a CBS interview two days after the Nov. 3 election that he would meet the leadership candidates and ask "tough questions."

Iowa Sen.-elect Josh Turek is taking a similar approach, saying his decision will depend on which candidate best reflects the priorities he campaigned on. During a news conference Monday, Turek said he had met with Schumer and Van Hollen and was waiting for "further updates" on his questions.

"Whoever provides those answers to me that most closely align with my common sense, prairie populist agenda that I've been running on for the last year and a half, that's who I'll support," Turek said.

Alaska Sen.-elect Mary Peltola, whose Senate race was officially called on Wednesday, told The Associated Press soon afterward that she had not yet thought about the leadership race. El-Sayed and Flanagan have been among the staunch supporters of Van Hollen. Another progressive, Maine Sen.-elect Troy Jackson, has said he would not support Schumer but has not said whether he would vote for Van Hollen.

Schumer backed alternative candidates during the primaries in all three races and supported all three once they were nominated. Senate leadership elections have traditionally been private affairs, decided in closed-door, secret-ballot votes shaped by the complicated inner workings of the Senate. The leader must hold together a caucus with competing political interests, a responsibility that does not always align with the demands of the party's most vocal activists.

Schumer's allies point to his success in the job, most recently during the midterms. He helped recruit candidates such as Brown, Peltola and North Carolina Sen.-elect Roy Cooper, while his allied super PAC was the largest Democratic spender in tightly contested races such as Michigan and Maine.

"When the manager takes you to the World Series, you give him another contract," Arizona Sen. Ruben Gallego said.

Democrats are also facing pressure from some in their base to confront Republican President Donald Trump more aggressively while delivering on ambitious policy promises. Large progressive organizations, including MoveOn and Indivisible, have endorsed Van Hollen, while many senators' offices have been flooded with calls urging them to reject Schumer.

"Our members are tired of politicians content with incrementalism — they want bold, creative leadership that will help Democrats usher in affordability and accountability without delay or compromise in the next Congress," MoveOn executive director Joel Payne said in a statement.

At a Schumer news conference Wednesday, several activists shouted from a distance, "Step down, Schumer! Step down, Schumer!"

Van Hollen supports progressive priorities including "Medicare for All" and restrictions on offensive weapons transfers to Israel. He has also promised a more confrontational approach to Trump, an ambitious economic agenda and changes to how the Democratic Party operates in primaries. Schumer, a fifth-term senator, has not said whether his term ending in 2028 will be his last.

The leadership fight has also drawn in more established Democrats, including senators whose political ambitions may include higher office. Georgia Sen. Jon Ossoff, fresh off a reelection victory in a presidential battleground state, is among the Democrats whose names have surfaced as potential White House contenders in 2028. Ossoff has served under Schumer's leadership for nearly six years.

"I haven't committed to any candidate, and I'll hear everybody out," Ossoff said this week.

Michigan Sen. Elissa Slotkin, elected in 2024 and now contemplating a presidential bid, said Thursday that she supports Van Hollen. A moderate who has called for change in Senate leadership, Slotkin has also criticized some of Van Hollen's more progressive policies, underscoring how the contest cuts across the party's ideological divisions.

"Because I've been clear that I wasn't supporting Schumer, I will be supporting Chris Van Hollen," Slotkin said.

The Associated Press account lists public commitments on both sides and multiple senators who have not decided, are waiting for more information or have not yet considered the race. The secret ballot is scheduled for Dec. 1.
