## OSPI's Oct. 31 caseload included 128 teachers, 19 for sexual misconduct

At a Nov. 19 meeting in SeaTac, a city between Seattle and Tacoma, the 12 governor-appointed members of Washington’s Professional Educator Standards Board discussed proposed changes to educator sexual-misconduct rules. The board establishes the rules that the Office of Superintendent of Public Instruction, or OSPI, is charged with implementing and enforcing. The proposals would expand who qualifies as a student under state law, broaden the definition of teacher sexual misconduct and give the state agency more authority to open investigations and temporarily limit licenses.

In July, Superintendent Chris Reykdal asked the board to update key definitions in state law, including who is considered a student and what constitutes sexual misconduct by a teacher. Reykdal also proposed giving OSPI authority to initiate an investigation if a district fails to report misconduct. Another proposal would require schools to report potential misconduct to the state within 30 days. If a district takes disciplinary action, such as placing a teacher on administrative leave, the proposal would require notification to OSPI within 10 days. A separate proposal would allow OSPI to place emergency limits on a teacher’s K-12 license while an investigation is pending.

Under current law, school district superintendents are supposed to report misconduct “within a reasonable period of time.” A teacher might remain on paid leave for months or years, leaving OSPI without the case while district officials negotiate a settlement or termination. Monique Malson, OSPI’s deputy legal adviser, described the consequence of that delay: “Then the district decides, ’OK, now we’re going to negotiate a settlement with this person, or we’re going to terminate them, and at that point, we get the case, and it is more difficult to investigate because the actual activity could have been three years before but it hung out in this gray area,” she said.

The Washington Education Association, the state’s largest K-12 teachers’ union, was the only organization to express reservations during the meeting. Nasue Nishida, a lobbyist with the union, told members of the standards board: “(The Washington Education Association) has met with OSPI, and we’ve done some back and forth feedback on their proposed rules, and we have some concerns,” she said. Nishida did not specify the union’s concerns. Julie Popper, a spokesperson for WEA, declined to answer specific questions from InvestigateWest over email but said the union questions OSPI’s ability to implement the proposals because of current resource constraints.

Kate Garvey, CEO of the King County Sexual Assault Resource Center, told the board that the proposed changes would help better protect students. The nonprofit encourages the state to move forward with the proposals “and to pair them with a meaningful investment in prevention education.”

OSPI asked the state Legislature in September for an additional $1.2 million to hire four more investigators, which would bring the agency’s total to seven. The request came as Washington grappled with a projected $4 billion budget shortfall for the coming budget cycle. Gov. Bob Ferguson also recently directed state agencies to develop proposals to decrease spending by up to 15%.

The proposals came in the wake of investigations by InvestigateWest and other media outlets that highlighted oversight gaps and systemic failures in how school leaders, law enforcement and OSPI handled reports of teacher sexual misconduct. The reporting said those shortcomings allowed accused teachers to quietly resign and move on to other jobs.

Michael Nguyen, the standards board’s director of policy, suggested that the board convene a work group to discuss OSPI’s proposals and solicit input from organizations such as WEA and the Association of Washington School Principals. The board did not discuss a timeline for the work group or its expected outcome. OSPI and the standards board convened a similar work group in 2019 at the request of lawmakers. That effort culminated in a final report submitted to the Legislature in March 2021, and some of the current recommendations were discussed during the earlier work group but never implemented.

Roshelle Cleland, director of public policy for the Washington State Coalition Against Domestic Violence and Sexual Assault, urged the board to take action in a statement provided to the board. “The overwhelming majority of educators work hard to keep students safe,” Cleland wrote. “When institutions fail to hold those who harm children accountable, the resulting loss of public trust affects educators as a whole.”

OSPI has declined to open 343 cases in the last six years, citing a lack of resources, though the complaints had been screened and deemed lower priority, Malson said. As of Oct. 31, OSPI was investigating 128 teachers for potential misconduct, including 19 for sexual misconduct.

An InvestigateWest analysis found that of the 349 teachers added to the state’s database over the last decade after their licenses were revoked, suspended or voluntarily surrendered, 160 had engaged in sexual misconduct with minors. The cases included sexual advances, intercourse and patterns of grooming behavior, representing approximately 46% of the total. InvestigateWest’s review of disciplinary files found that OSPI failed to categorize many of those situations as sexual misconduct because current law narrowly defines a student as someone under an educator’s direct supervision.

OSPI’s proposed definitions would cover more complaints, including cases in which a teacher leaves the state to have sex with a minor or pursues romantic relationships with students in neighboring districts or shortly after they graduate or change schools. “Those are not considered sexual misconduct under the current way the rules work because those are not considered students, so we open those as moral conduct cases,” Malson said.

Washington school districts have seen a surge in sexual-abuse claims in recent years, many of which have resulted in multimillion-dollar settlements. Recent high-profile cases include a $16 million settlement paid to a former Seattle Public Schools student in 2024, a $15 million settlement paid to two Federal Way Public School students and a $21 million settlement paid to six former students at University Place School District in Pierce County.

Adam Aguilera, the standards board’s chair and a middle school teacher in the Evergreen Public Schools, said he expects the policy proposals to go through “significant” changes after the board solicits stakeholder feedback. Aguilera hopes future proposals will focus on better training for educators regarding appropriate boundaries with students and sexual misconduct. “When you’re looking at the numbers here, it’s ridiculous that we have this many open investigations that are occurring within the profession,” Aguilera said. “I think part of it is because we are not, as a system, doing a good enough job at training our staff.”
