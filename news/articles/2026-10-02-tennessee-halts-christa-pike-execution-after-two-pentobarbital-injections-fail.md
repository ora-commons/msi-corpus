## Lawyers, doctors, and faith leaders call for US death penalty moratorium

Top lawyers, doctors and members of the faith community are calling for a moratorium on the US death penalty after Tennessee's failed attempt to execute Christa Pike left the prisoner alive but in critical condition and receiving life-saving medical care.

Tennessee Governor Bill Lee called off the procedure late on Wednesday after Pike had been injected with two full syringes of pentobarbital. She is the only person known to have survived being administered 5mg of the sedative, a dose that under normal circumstances is considered fatal. An hour after the first injection was given, Pike was overheard by eyewitnesses to be still breathing, choking, and struggling, and was found to have a heartbeat. Her lawyers said that her current condition, and the degree to which she may have been brain damaged through oxygen deprivation, were as yet unknown.

Sandra Babcock, a death penalty scholar at Cornell Law School who advises Pike's legal team, described the failed attempt as "unimaginably barbaric. They demand accountability." At a minimum, she added, Lee must now "commute Christa Pike's death sentence to life imprisonment."

Another of Pike's lawyers, Randy Spivey, who was present in the death chamber, called Pike's ordeal "cruel and torturous." He said he had personally counted seven needles stuck in Pike's left arm alone, "one of which appeared to be bent when it was pulled from her."

Pike, 50, was sentenced to death for the 1995 killing of her teenage classmate Colleen Slemmer. She was set to be the first woman executed in Tennessee in more than 200 years.

Leading voices on the death penalty in America told the Guardian that the failed execution was such a violation of the constitutional prohibition on "cruel and unusual punishment" that all judicial killings had to be halted. Bryan Stevenson, founder of the Equal Justice Initiative in Montgomery, Alabama, said that many warnings had been made in recent months that US executions were flawed and unreliable.

"Nearly a dozen botched executions in recent years should have suspended executions long before now," he said.

Stevenson said it was time to turn around the threshold question. Instead of asking whether a prisoner deserved to die for the crime committed, the question should be: "Do our governments which tolerate inequality, unreliability and bias deserve to kill?" He added: "Last night's tragedy makes clear we should stop the misguided policy of killing people to show that killing is wrong."

Wednesday night was the second time in five months that Tennessee had failed an execution. In May, Tony Carruthers survived the death chamber after the execution team were unable to set up an IV line. He has been given a new execution date for next May.

That followed Alabama, which also failed to execute two death row prisoners, Alan Miller and Kenneth Smith, after being unable to find a vein. The pattern of executions called off because of basic failures in IV procedures has sent death penalty states scrambling to find alternatives to lethal injections. Miller and Smith were both eventually executed by Alabama using an experimental procedure, nitrogen gas, that veterinarians have banned as inhumane for the euthanasia of cats and dogs. In June, a federal judge blocked Alabama's use of nitrogen in executions, ruling the procedure cruel and unusual punishment.

Other states have opted for the firing squad. But recent firing squad executions have been troubled by bullets missing the heart, to the extent that the suggestion has been raised that some of the mis-targeted shots might have been intentional.

Helen Prejean, a Catholic nun who is perhaps the best-known advocate for abolition of the death penalty in America after her book Dead Man Walking was turned into a film of the same name, told the Guardian that the events on Wednesday night were the definition of cruelty. "This is the essence of cruelty. Take a conscious, imaginative human being, give her a date for her death, have her anticipate it over and over again, and then take her out and try and kill her. She was rendered defenceless as they deliberately attempted to kill her — what greater mental torture for a human being is there?" Prejean added that she hoped the agony Pike had endured "would be redemptive for others, and help to wake up the nation."

Joel Zivot, a professor of anesthesiology in Atlanta, Georgia, who has reviewed hundreds of autopsies of executed people in the US, said that lethal injections would never deserve the label of medicine. "This is the impersonation of a medical act. It is the theft of a medical act. So when it goes awry, that is not at all surprising to me," Zivot said.

Zivot was invited to present as an expert witness at a hearing convened in August by a special master in the Pike case. He warned the senior judge that Pike had a blood condition in which her blood platelets were elevated that rendered her vulnerable to blood clots. Zivot told the hearing that Pike, a small woman who had been in solitary confinement on death row for almost 30 years, likely had veins that were of poor quality, small in caliber, and hard to find. "I told them. I warned them," he said. The special master rejected almost all the evidence presented to him.

Calls for a renewed nationwide pause are reaching fever pitch at a moment when pressure to carry out executions is also peaking. President Donald Trump has repeatedly exhorted death penalty states and the federal Department of Justice to speed up judicial killings, and his orders are having an effect. Last year saw the highest number of US executions in 16 years — 47 across 12 states.

Prejean pointed to a shift in public sentiment. In 1993, when her book came out, 80% of Americans supported the death penalty for convicted murderers; the figure has since fallen to 52%. "That's progress, though not enough. People have good hearts, but they are too removed from the process that is secret and hidden. We have got to bring this to the people, bring them into the execution chamber, so that they too become a witness," Prejean said.

The last time the US imposed a moratorium on executions was in 1972, when the Supreme Court ruled in Furman v Georgia that capital punishment was capricious and arbitrary in its rendition. The ban was lifted four years later.
