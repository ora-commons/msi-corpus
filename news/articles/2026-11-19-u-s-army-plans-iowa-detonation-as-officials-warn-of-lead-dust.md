## U.S. Army plans Iowa detonation as officials warn of lead dust

**Subtype:** fact

- An Army advisory said the detonation will create an air plume with some lead-containing particles that will disperse through the air and deposit on the ground.

## EPA approved the permit and required five days of public notice

The U.S. Army plans to detonate about 5 tons Friday at the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant in two blasts intended to dispose of degraded bomb-making chemicals that officials said have become unusable and might explode if moved. The material is lead azide, and officials said moving or relocating it would pose an unacceptable risk of accidental detonation. Residents in southeast Iowa worried that a cloud of lead dust could contaminate the environment and cause health problems.

An Army advisory said, “The detonation will result in an air plume with some lead-containing particles which will disperse in the air and deposit on the ground.” The advisory said lead is toxic to humans and animals. It said the advisory’s goal was to provide the public with actionable information, out of an abundance of caution, and minimize lead exposure. Army officials recommended that residents of a Middletown neighborhood begin vacating the area Friday morning; those unable to leave should stay inside with windows and doors closed.

Officials released maps showing the potential for lead dust to drift north toward other communities. Some schools in the path of the plume have canceled classes. Nearly 400 people live in Middletown, but thousands more are in the track of the plume.

The two blasts are expected to produce quick vibrations that could break old windows in town. Officials said the resulting dust should linger for no more than one hour, depending on wind speed. The Army recommends washing cars and cleaning windowsills and outdoor surfaces, while residents have used social media to question whether those measures will be enough.

Middletown residents learned in March that the Army needed to dispose of several dozen barrels after the ammunition plant paused operations for a review and degraded material was discovered. At the time, Lt. Col. Daniel Nosse, the plant commander, said the safety of residents and neighbors was non-negotiable while acknowledging the compound’s volatility. The Army shared more details during an October public meeting in Middletown, advising that a detonation was planned for an unspecified date in November.

The Environmental Protection Agency approved an emergency permit on Nov. 13, paving the way for Friday’s detonation. The permit required the Army to track potential air and dust dispersion and notify the public five days in advance. That requirement prompted Sunday’s notification to communities as far as 100 miles (160 kilometers) away.

Less than 20 miles (32 kilometers) northeast, Mediapolis city officials were approached late last month about allowing soil, water and air testing. They learned of their potential exposure only on Sunday, when a map placed the community at the center of the dust-exposure area.

The Mediapolis Community School District announced Tuesday that it would close school Friday and cancel events and sports practices because of “uncontrollable variables.” The public library was also set to close, and the local fire department planned to hose down playgrounds that could be covered in lead particles.

The EPA will have a command center in Mediapolis to sample air, soil and water during and after the detonation, according to city officials. Mediapolis also planned its own testing to try to assure concerned residents. “This is probably a first for me. I believe it’s a first for a lot of our citizens,” said Deputy City Clerk Amy Stucker. “The biggest issue we’re having is the lack of information.”

Environmental and public-health officials in Illinois urged the U.S. military to conduct comprehensive sampling in the state after the detonations. Even the office of Iowa Gov. Kim Reynolds was first briefed Monday on the public advisory, her office said.

The barrels of lead azide had been stored at the ammunition plant for more than 20 years in earthen-covered containers sitting in a bath of ethanol and water to keep the material stable, said Justine Barati, a public affairs specialist at the Army Sustainment Command. During a routine check earlier this year, some of the water evaporated, causing the lead azide to crystallize. That made the material more sensitive to friction and shock, “creating a highly unstable and dangerous situation,” according to the EPA permit. Officials said relocation posed an unacceptable risk, and Barati said the possibility of an accidental detonation increases in cold weather.

“We’re really concerned about the weather situation,” Barati said on moving forward with Friday’s detonation. “We’re really concerned that it will detonate on its own.” The Army assembled a panel of experts in munitions chemistry, engineering and environmental safety to develop the plan. The barrels are surrounded above, below and on all sides with concrete buried under soil, with sand barriers stacked two stories high around the soil. During the detonation, the soil will be sprayed with water and the air misted to try to keep the majority of the lead on Army property, Barati said.

Patrick O’Shaughnessy, a professor in the University of Iowa’s Department of Occupational and Environmental Health, said it was encouraging that the Army and EPA were taking steps to minimize the dust, but the amount reaching the atmosphere and nearby surfaces remained unknown. He said lead is a neurological toxicant that can decrease brain activity and development, especially in young children. O’Shaughnessy was particularly concerned about toddlers at the age of putting everything in their mouths.

EPA documents showed that officials considered alternatives such as neutralizing and decomposing the drum contents with other chemicals. Officials said any plan involving handling the drums would increase the probability of an accidental, fatal detonation. Friday’s blasts remained scheduled under the emergency permit, with Mediapolis officials preparing independent testing and the EPA command center scheduled to sample during and after the operation.
