## Years of implementation, technical hurdles and upfront costs challenge transit automation

Washington, D.C., and Los Angeles are pursuing fully automated mass transit as robotaxis become more prevalent in the United States. D.C. officials advanced a nearly $1 billion plan that could allow Metro's Red Line to run without a worker in the cab as early as 2033, while Los Angeles is reviewing a fully automated underground line through the Santa Monica Mountains. Both projects would take years to implement and still face financial and technical hurdles amid an active debate over safety.

Even as automated road vehicles from Waymo, Tesla and Zoox have taken the lead in U.S. automation in recent years, almost every mass transit system still requires a driver or operator on board. The Washington and Los Angeles proposals would change that operating model on the lines they cover. Supporters argue that the systems' substantial upfront expense could be justified by long-term savings in labor costs.

Supporters also call the push long overdue and say American cities lag far behind numerous places across the world that run unattended subways, metros or buses. They cite several cities in Europe and Asia, as well as major systems in Canada, Australia, South America and the Middle East. Baruch Feigenbaum, senior managing director of transportation policy at the Reason Foundation, a libertarian think tank that has advocated transit automation, said fixed-rail transit is easier to automate than on-road travel. “It should be much harder to automate something that’s on-road, that has all of these various obstacles coming at it, compared to a fixed-rail system,” Feigenbaum said. He added, “I don’t know a nice way to say this, but basically the transit world is where innovation goes to die.”

Wide-scale transit automation has developed slowly in the United States. Metro trains ran with some automated features when the subway system opened 50 years ago, though operators were always part of its operation. After the fatal 2009 crash, those features were turned off and then restored over the past two years. Washington officials have since renewed their push for automation on Metro's Red Line.

Nine people were killed and many others injured in the 2009 train collision near Fort Totten station on the Red Line. The National Transportation Safety Board blamed the crash on a maintenance problem in the automated signaling system that separates trains from one another. Randy Clarke, CEO of the Washington Metropolitan Area Transit Authority, said the Fort Totten issue had nothing to do with the cruise-control-like system restored on the Red Line in 2024 and the other Metro lines last year. He nevertheless acknowledged that the crash paralyzed Metro's plans for further automation for many years. “That was a big failure, there’s no question. We do not do anything like that now,” Clarke said, calling the current system “highly regulated, highly documented, very proficient.”

Under the nearly $1 billion plan Metro's Board of Directors backed in April, trains could conceivably run without a human worker inside the cab as early as 2033. The Red Line upgrade would include safety features such as protective barriers designed to prevent passengers from getting onto the tracks. Clarke said that even if workers would not be needed to physically run the trains, they would still be needed to maintain the tracks and respond to emergencies.

Unions have been critics of automation. John Samuelsen, a former worker in the New York subway system who now serves as international president of the Transport Workers Union, said unions have fought off automation efforts in New York and elsewhere, calling them “a catastrophe waiting to happen.” Samuelsen said, “Passengers will be left dealing with a self-evacuation, which is a disaster in a system which runs on a third rail.”

Honolulu opened its Skyline automated light metro system three years ago to ease major traffic bottlenecks for commuters and expanded it last year. The elevated driverless line is the most expansive automated transit system in the United States and will stretch 19 miles when completed. At that size, it would surpass the elevated driverless trains Detroit and Miami opened as pilot projects in the 1980s, the monorail connecting hotels on the Las Vegas Strip and smaller systems carrying passengers between airport terminals. The U.S. automated-transit record is also mixed: an automated monorail once ran in Memphis, Tennessee, but closed years ago, while the driverless Jacksonville Skyway will indefinitely halt operations soon because of budget cuts, despite initial plans for a larger automated system.

Skyline project director Vance Tsuda said there has not been a single crash or injury associated with automation. He argued that even hybrid systems are less safe than a completely automated one and that a human is far more likely to overlook something such as an open door and prematurely clear a train for departure. “If you have somebody making that decision, they could make a wrong decision,” Tsuda said.

David King, an associate professor of urban planning at Arizona State University who has researched automated transportation, said the success rate has to remain perfect to keep public trust. He said the Fort Totten crash and other accidents involving automation pushed the technology so far behind. “We allow a certain amount of failure with private industry that we don’t tolerate when the state does it,” King said, citing several rockets blown up by Elon Musk's SpaceX — a record he argues would cripple NASA's budget if it happened in the public sector.

Los Angeles officials voted to recommend a subway line beneath the Santa Monica Mountains, and the proposal is undergoing environmental review. The subway would be part of the city's heavy-rail system and cut through the rough terrain. The Associated Press wrote: “Perhaps the most ambitious transit automation effort being pursued in the U.S. is the subway line Los Angeles officials voted to recommend beneath the Santa Monica Mountains.” The mountains divide the Westside employment hub from the San Fernando Valley, and the area is a major traffic bottleneck. A subway already operates under the same mountain range near Hollywood, but it has a driver onboard. Cecily Way, who oversees planning for the Sepulveda Project for LA Metro, said the new proposal would use shorter, more frequent trains.

Like the Washington project, the new Los Angeles line would feature station doors to protect passengers on platforms from oncoming trains. Feigenbaum said automation of a major transit line in Los Angeles or Washington could open the floodgates for other cities to follow. “It sends a signal that this is technologically doable and this is something that maybe other systems are going to want to strive for,” he said.

The financial case for both proposals turns on whether substantial upfront expense is worth long-term savings in labor costs. Under the D.C. plan, trains could run without a cab worker, but Clarke said workers would still maintain tracks and respond to emergencies. The union response points to a separate risk: Samuelsen says passengers left to evacuate themselves could face disaster in a third-rail system.

The three cities illustrate different stages of transit automation. Washington has advanced a safety-focused Red Line plan, Los Angeles is reviewing an underground line, and Honolulu already operates and is expanding its automated system. The broader operating reality across nearly all U.S. mass transit remains unchanged, with a driver or operator still required on board.
