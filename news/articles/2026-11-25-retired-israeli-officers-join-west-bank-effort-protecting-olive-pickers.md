## U.N. documents nearly 400 settler attacks in October

Retired Israeli Brig. Gen. Yoni Shimshoni and other former officers are helping Palestinian families and their allies protect this year's olive harvest in the occupied West Bank as settler attacks intensify. PBS News reported that the United Nations described the harvest as the most violent on record and documented nearly 400 attacks from Oct. 1 to Oct. 26, a 100-fold increase from two decades earlier. PBS NewsHour reported that the olive harvest was concluding that week.

Nick Schifrin reported that olive trees have long linked Palestinian families to the land, with some families' roots dating back centuries and harvesting techniques unchanged over time. PBS reported that families sell the olives and olive oil but that this year the farmers need protection. Shimshoni said farmers would not dare come to their own grove to harvest for fear of a settler attack. He said, "They live under fear. They work under fear. They harvest under fear."

PBS News followed Israeli Security, a group of former Israeli generals who spent their careers defending Israel and now help defend Palestinians. The report focused on Duma, a Palestinian town surrounded by three Israeli settlements. PBS reported that settlers have long coveted the land and, at times, tried to push Palestinians out, though they had never acted this brazenly. The commanders saw the settlers first, and a man said, "They're filming us." Schifrin said one settler arrived with a smile, perhaps knowing he could act with impunity. The fight began quickly. At one harvest, a teenage settler tried to wrestle a bag of olives from Palestinian women. An activist wrestled it back and briefly grabbed the settler as both sides filmed. A man demanded, "What's your name? What's your name? Don't even think about it." Israelis on either side cursed and shouted over what they saw as the heart of the state.

Nick Schifrin reported that threats against Palestinians, Westerners and the media have become increasingly common. The United Nations said settler violence had spiked since the Oct. 7 attacks, which PBS described as the deadliest day in Israeli history. October recorded the highest number of attacks since the United Nations began documenting settler violence two decades ago.

Four days after PBS filmed in Duma, settlers badly injured Washington Post photographer Heidi Levine. The next day, a team of European diplomats visiting another harvest had its car window smashed by settlers. The United Nations documented nearly 400 attacks from Oct. 1 to Oct. 26, a 100-fold increase from the start of its two-decade monitoring. An unidentified man thanked the group, "Thank you that you came to free us from the bullies."

Former commanders accused the state of complicity. Schifrin reported that in Duma, army soldiers arrived first; as the occupying authority, they blocked everyone from leaving and called police, which has arrest authority. Soldiers asked settlers which commanders and activists were most provocative. Israeli army soldiers and police then detained an activist who tried to protect a Palestinian olive picker and retired Brig. Gen. Joel Feldschuh, a former Israeli fighter pilot, former chief of Israeli air force intelligence and former CEO of Israel's state airline and state champion El-Al.

Shimshoni questioned why the army was acting that way and said the military did not know how to stand up to settler pressure. He said, "Indignant. I mean, there's a travesty going on. There's a lot of violence against the locals going on." He added that former officers who had spent decades defending and fighting for the country wanted to help change its direction.

Producer Karl Bostic caught up with the commanders as they drove between Palestinian villages, including retired Maj. Gen. Eyal Ben-Rueven of the Israeli Defense Forces. Ben-Rueven said that when he commanded a division in the area, he sent police to work with civilians and said the military trained itself to fight militaries, terrorists in Lebanon, Hezbollah and Hamas in other places, not Jewish people. He said, "This is part of the problem."

Ben-Rueven also accused the larger settler movement, which he said had allies in the government, of pushing to annex the West Bank. He said, "Our government today support these people, no question about it." Netanyahu's allies rejected that assessment. Ophir Falk, Netanyahu's foreign policy adviser, said, "This government has been taking extraordinary steps to deal with that issue." Falk told Schifrin the previous month that Israel had investigated hundreds of settler violence cases, arrested dozens of settlers accused of violence and described those responsible as outliers in the West Bank, which he and many Israelis call Judea and Samaria.

Falk compared the crime rate in Judea and Samaria with Los Angeles, New York and London, saying it was lower. He said Palestinians faced what he called Palestinian terrorism daily, almost every day, and cited more than 1,530 Palestinian terrorist attack attempts over the past year.

PBS News traveled to three settlements outside Nablus in the northern West Bank to meet Israeli settler Meir Goldmintz. Born in Jerusalem, Goldmintz moved to the area 25 years ago and teaches biblical history. In Shavei Shomron, which the report described as a Jordanian base until Israel captured the West Bank in 1967, he said the area was specifically mentioned in the Old Testament. Goldmintz, speaking through an interpreter, argued, "The land of Israel belongs to the people of Israel. The entire land of Israel belongs to the people of Israel." Through an interpreter, he added: "The lord promised us the whole land, as we read in the weekly Torah portion. On that day, the lord made a covenant with Abraham, saying, to your offspring, I have given this land from the river of Egypt to the River Prat."

When Schifrin asked about Goldmintz's relationship with nearby Palestinian cities and villages, Goldmintz, speaking through an interpreter, compared it with asking whether someone who had been robbed had a connection with the robber. Through an interpreter, he added: "The land was not theirs. They came and took it from us while we were in exile." PBS described a biblical argument on display up a hill: that Jews had lived there two millennia ago, were evicted and that all the land belonged to Israel. Ruins of an ancient temple built after King Solomon's reign dotted the hill. Asked whether Palestinian olive trees were also Jewish property, Goldmintz replied through an interpreter, "If a person plants a tree in my backyard, who does it belong to?"

Earlier this year, Maj. Gen. Avi Bluth, the military's top commander in the West Bank, wrote that "A violent group of lawbreakers is operating in the territories of Judea and Samaria." He said the group violated the laws of the state, Jewish values and the principles on which Zionism was founded while harming a Palestinian population not involved in terrorism. Goldmintz, speaking through an interpreter, dismissed that characterization, saying he was not at all sure there had been even a rare case of a Jew harming an Arab and that comparing it with what he called countless cases of Arabs harming Jews was unfair.

Goldmintz lives in the nearby settlement of Sa-Nur, where no fence or wall is visible from his window, a feature of newly built settlements. Sa-Nur was first established in 1977 and dismantled 20 years ago as part of a separate Israeli agreement to disengage from Gaza, until this year when Smotrich and Israel's defense minister reopened it. PBS described Sa-Nur as one of more than 100 settlements approved in 2026. On the day of the visit, Hagit Ahlbershtat and her family stopped to inspect the neighborhood, hoping to move there from outside Tel Aviv. Through an interpreter, Ahlbershtat said their forefather Abraham walked there rather than in Tel Aviv, where there was sand. She said, "The true land of Israel is here."

The land dispute has divided Israel as well as the territory, PBS reported. Some people seek to expand the state in the name of religion, while others consider the violence a threat to Israeli democracy; both sides believe the future of the state is at stake. Schifrin framed the harvest confrontation as part of a battle within Israel over its own future. The PBS NewsHour report was the first of two stories from Schifrin and his team in the West Bank.
