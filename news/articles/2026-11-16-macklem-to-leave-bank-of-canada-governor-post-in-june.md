## Macklem to leave Bank of Canada governor post in June

**Subtype:** fact

- Bank of Canada Governor Tiff Macklem said Monday he will not stay on after his seven-year term expires on June 2.
- The central bank's board of directors has started a recruitment process to nominate a new governor and hired Egon Zehnder to manage the search.
- The Bank of Canada expects the search process to be completed by next spring, and the next governor will need approval from Mark Carney, Canada's prime minister and former governor of the central banks in Canada and England.
- Fixed-income traders have speculated that leading candidates include Jean Boivin, Carolyn Wilkins and Paul Beaudry.

## Central bank hires Egon Zehnder to run search for successor

Macklem, the 10th Bank of Canada governor, said in a statement that serving in the role was a career highlight. "My time as governor has seen Canada navigate a more fragmented, shock-prone world. The bank has faced these challenges by acting decisively," he said.

The formal decision marks the unofficial start of the race to be Canada's chief central banker, who would take over as the economy undergoes a transition to reduce its dependence on trade with the U.S. to drive growth. The candidate will ultimately need the approval of Mark Carney, Canada's prime minister and former governor of the central banks in Canada and England. No Bank of Canada governor has served beyond the seven-year term.

Fixed-income traders have speculated that among the leading candidates are Jean Boivin, a former senior central-bank official who now heads a BlackRock-affiliated think tank tasked with generating research for the asset manager; Carolyn Wilkins, the former No. 2 at the central bank whom Macklem beat out for the top job in 2020; and Paul Beaudry, another former senior Bank of Canada official and an academic. Boivin served as a deputy Bank of Canada governor when Carney was in charge.

As governor, Macklem oversaw an economy that recovered relatively quickly from the depths of the Covid-19 pandemic, although he was forced to aggressively raise rates to cool down historically high inflation. In late 2021, Macklem wound down the extraordinary stimulus provided to stabilize the economy and financial markets during the pandemic. The policy rate then climbed from a low of 0.25% to 5% to deter inflation. Inflation eventually settled back to 2%, but not before taking the steam out of a housing market that witnessed some of the biggest price gains among developed economies.

Since the start of President Trump's second term, Macklem's central bank cut rates further to provide a cushion for the Canadian economy reeling from hefty tariffs imposed by its largest trading partner. The Bank of Canada raised its policy rate in October, the first increase in about three years, to address the risk that more expensive fuel stemming from the U.S.-Iran conflict begins to lift prices for other goods and services. The level of interest rates also remains nowhere close to the rock-bottom levels that followed the 2008 global financial crisis and the height of the pandemic.

Macklem was appointed in 2020 in a surprise decision, as markets had expected Wilkins to be named governor. Macklem served as the senior deputy governor when Carney was the Bank of Canada governor, and later became a business-school dean at the University of Toronto after he was passed over for the top Bank of Canada job in 2013.

Following the pandemic, Macklem faced stiff criticism from Conservative Party lawmakers for doling out too much stimulus and helping fuel the historic rise in inflation. The current Conservative Party leader, Pierre Poilievre, said in 2022 he would fire Macklem should he become prime minister, although Poilievre toned down or didn't repeat the threat in the months and years that followed.

In speeches and remarks, Macklem said the pandemic offered myriad lessons for central banks — among them, to give as much weight to supply in the economy as demand. The inflation surge following the pandemic was due to producers' inability to provide enough goods and services to address built-up demand among households. Macklem also has argued the central bank needs to be clearer in its rationale for deploying extraordinary stimulus, like it did during the pandemic, and set out exit conditions more closely tied to inflation expectations.
