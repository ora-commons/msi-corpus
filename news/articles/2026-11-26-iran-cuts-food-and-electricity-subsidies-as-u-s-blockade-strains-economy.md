## Hard-liners suggest Tehran seek a nuclear weapon or escalate the conflict

President Masoud Pezeshkian told Iran's national television on Nov. 20: "We have no money." The president was describing what people in the streets already see as authorities cut food and electricity subsidies while workers protest unpaid salaries.

Speaking the next day at a conference in Tehran, Pezeshkian said: "We are engaged in a silent war that is far more difficult than one in which missiles are launched and destruction is caused," according to the state-run IRNA news agency. "It is an economic war…and it is no easy battle."

The remarks amounted to a public acknowledgment of the economic pain that a U.S. naval blockade is inflicting on Iran, with diplomacy over ending the war stalled. Washington has tightened its blockade, targeting flights and overland trade in addition to blocking oil exports by sea, most of which are bound for China. Inside Iran, tensions between relative moderates and hard-liners have burst into the open, with officials weighing whether to escalate the conflict over control of the Strait of Hormuz to break the blockade.

"Iran has been living on borrowed economic time for months," said Ali Vaez, an Iran expert at the International Crisis Group, a Brussels-based think tank. "What's remarkable is how long Tehran has managed to postpone the reckoning."

The strain is visible in government statistics, which show food and beverage price inflation over 110% year over year. Iran's rial currency has fallen to 2.9 million a dollar, from the 32,000 a dollar seen at the time of Tehran's 2015 nuclear deal with world powers.

The dire prognosis is pushing Iran to cut subsidies further in an oil-producing country where cheap energy and food staples are considered a birthright by many. Iran's state electricity company, Tavanir, has begun rolling blackouts to ration power, with outages lasting up to two hours a day in some locations. Tavanir said damage at Iran's natural gas fields during the war had led to fuel shortages. Reza Sepahvand, a spokesman for parliament's energy commission, said Iranians should brace for worsening outages in the winter. Power stations around Tehran burn mazut, a high-polluting, low-quality fuel common in former Soviet bloc countries, and authorities have also used methanol to stretch gasoline supplies. A decision in the summer to reduce gasoline subsidies triggered massive queues at the pump.

Food subsidies are being trimmed as well, though Iranian authorities denied the moves constitute rationing. Hossein Zafari, the spokesman of Iran's Flour and Bread Council, said the government cut allocations of cheap flour and bread in seven of the country's 31 provinces. Flour shortages after the cuts led to protests by bakers in Iran's western Zanjan province and other areas, Zafari told Nour News, a website affiliated with Iran's Supreme National Security Council. The government also more than doubled the price it charges consumers of industrial flour, the Pasta Manufacturers Employers' Association told Iranian state media.

The government had no choice but to resort to such cuts because it lacks the political mandate for broad subsidy reforms, said Esfandyar Batmanghelidj, the founder and chief executive of the London-based economic think tank Bourse & Bazaar Foundation. But its response is still falling short of a long-term solution, he said. "We are seeing a lot of half-measures that fail to address the root causes of economic mismanagement," Batmanghelidj said. "The best Iran can do is muddle through, possibly until a change in the U.S. administration."

Two years may be a long time for Iran's people, who have seen living conditions deteriorate further in recent years. The Wall Street Journal reported that economic pressures also led to nationwide protests in January, followed by a security force crackdown that killed thousands. There is no sign of widespread dissent now, with security officials maintaining tight control on the public since the war, but grumbling is audible across Tehran over the prices people see at markets and shops.

A Tehran resident in his 70s said he sees more people buying discounted potatoes and onions that have started to spoil. "I'm managing to get by," he said. He recently bought sangak, a traditional Persian flatbread, for the equivalent of 35 cents — seven times the subsidized price. The average pension in Iran is now worth $64 a month. "It was the first time in my life that I had paid that much for a single loaf of bread," he said.

Another resident expressed dismay as bread from a local bakery in Tehran's affluent northern suburbs grew progressively darker as it spoiled. "They mixed it with flour used for animals," he said.

Protests by retirees and labor unionists continue. After a crash killed 13 petrochemical plant workers in Abadan, the main city in Iran's western oil fields, a trade union held a demonstration in which protesters also used anti-regime slogans, tying the deaths to austerity measures and political repression. "Subsidized gasoline, subsidized bread, and now they are even trying to ration breathing, and if you protest, they cut it off," the union said on social media. Authorities later detained at least 12 of the protesters, said the Human Rights Activists News Agency, an advocacy group based in the U.S.

Iran's deputy interior minister, Ali Zeinivand, said officials allowed marches such as the one in Abadan as long as they focused on living conditions. Teachers and retired telecommunications workers have also demonstrated recently. "Inflation has caused their wages to be insufficient to cover their living expenses," Zeinivand told the semiofficial ILNA news agency. "It is natural that these groups have demands."

As the crisis deepens, Iran's hard-liners are emerging as some of the country's most strident critics of decisions by Pezeshkian's administration to cut subsidies. Such battles of influence between camps within Iran's theocracy are common, but they have escalated in recent weeks as hard-liners also suggest Tehran seek a nuclear weapon or escalate the war with the U.S. At nightly demonstrations, which began during the war, chants increasingly target Pezeshkian, Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi and parliament speaker Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf — all officials who have backed talks to try to end the war. Earlier this month, women dressed in white burial shrouds demonstrated in front of the headquarters of Iran's Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps, accusing the force of not doing enough to retaliate against the U.S.


In August, President Pezeshkian and parliament speaker Ghalibaf publicly pressed for an end to the war as Iran's economic crisis strained the country's finances, while U.S. officials settled on financial sanctions and a naval blockade of Iranian ports as the administration's strategy, as MSI previously reported in [Iran's top politicians urge end to war as economy crumbles](/articles/2026-08-22-iran-s-top-politicians-urge-end-to-war-as-economy-crumbles/) and [Trump returns to Iran economic pressure as war deadlock persists](/articles/2026-08-10-trump-returns-to-iran-economic-pressure-as-war-deadlock-persists/). Since then, Washington has tightened the blockade to cover flights and overland trade, Iran has cut food and electricity subsidies and imposed electricity rationing, and tensions between moderates and hard-liners have escalated, with hard-liners suggesting Tehran seek a nuclear weapon or escalate the conflict.
