## Department leadership grew frustrated with Quiñones's management of the probe

The Wall Street Journal reported that the conspiracy probe has been abandoned in favor of what officials say are a narrower but related set of cases run out of Washington. Officials said the cases were “politically charged.” The probe examined whether U.S. intelligence and law-enforcement officials engaged in a grand conspiracy against President Donald Trump, and was overseen by Jason Reding Quiñones, the U.S. attorney for the Southern District of Florida.

The Wall Street Journal previously reported that department leadership in recent months had grown frustrated with what they saw as Quiñones's management of the probe. Separately, The Wall Street Journal reported that senior officials had lost confidence in Quiñones's ability to run the Southern District of Florida office, which was known for prosecuting major international drug and gang cases.

Quiñones resigned Friday. Attorney General Todd Blanche announced his departure and separately that of John Eisenberg, head of the Justice Department's national-security division.

Quiñones often touted what he said were record violent-crime and immigration cases brought by his office. In a social media post, Quiñones said he was leaving with “immense pride in what we accomplished together.” As the son of a Cuban political refugee, he has said he was particularly proud of his office's indictment of Raúl Castro, Cuba's aging patriarch and former president, in May.

Eisenberg oversaw prosecutions of terrorism, espionage and leaks of classified information. As a White House legal adviser in Trump's first term, Eisenberg emerged as a key figure in the handling of a call with Ukraine's president that led to Trump's first impeachment.

A day earlier, Harmeet Dhillon, who oversaw civil-rights enforcement, said she, too, would be stepping down. People familiar with the matter said Dhillon had clashed with department leadership, including Stanley Woodward, about legal strategies surrounding the election.

Dhillon's monthslong effort to force states to relinquish sensitive voter-roll information was largely unsuccessful. Her signature was noticeably absent from court cases against Alaska and California filed just before the election, with Woodward and Blanche's deputy adding theirs instead.

Dhillon's record included suing elite universities over campus-speech and admissions practices, and fighting diversity initiatives while advocating the rights of gun owners and religious groups.

Quiñones's resignation is expected to be one of a series of Justice Department departures after the midterm elections. Other officials are expected to step aside in coming weeks, which isn't uncommon after a midterm election.

House Democrats are preparing congressional investigations targeting many of the Justice Department's actions during Trump's second term.
