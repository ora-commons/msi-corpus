## Hamer says funding for CDC wastewater surveillance is running out

Bipartisan lawmakers in Congress have introduced legislation to continue and expand federal funding for wastewater monitoring of measles and other diseases, according to News From The States. The outlet said the proposal comes days after the United States lost its measles elimination status and amid growing criticism of the Trump administration’s public health policy decisions. Reps. Troy Carter, D-New Orleans, Russely Fry, R-South Carolina, and Chrissy Houlahan, D-Pennsylvania, authored the PREDICT Act. A similar Senate measure has Democratic, Republican and independent co-authors.

The PREDICT Act would direct the CDC to increase its monitoring programs and laboratory testing capabilities. It also seeks a central database of disease-monitoring records for public health officials and would require the CDC to produce a national plan for strengthening wastewater monitoring nationwide and maintaining an early-warning system capable of detecting emerging health threats.

“With the expansion of this cost-effective technology, Congress and CDC can protect Americans’ health and stop the spread of deadly diseases like COVID-19, measles and more,” Carter said in a news release Friday.

The CDC coordinates the National Wastewater Surveillance System to monitor measles and other highly contagious viral diseases, including avian influenza and monkeypox. Relying on state data, the system also tracks common respiratory illnesses such as seasonal flu, COVID-19 and RSV. It gives health officials a snapshot of how concentrated a disease is in a population without requiring the logistics of human testing. The CDC’s data come from roughly 140 million people, representing less than half of the U.S. population.

“It can be a harbinger, or like a forewarning, of an outbreak,” said David Hamer, professor of global health at Boston University’s Center on Emerging Infectious Diseases. “It was during the COVID pandemic that we showed how useful it could be,” he added.

American Rescue Plan Emergency Fund money, related to tracking and managing COVID-19 in 2020, began the CDC monitoring program, which later proved useful for other diseases. “Basically, the money is running out,” Hamer said. “We need long term funding to keep this going.”

The legislation came just after the Pan American Health Organization rescinded the United States’ measles elimination status last week. The organization reports 58,213 confirmed cases globally and 82 deaths so far in 2026. As of Nov. 18, the CDC had logged 5,838 U.S. measles cases this year.

Pennsylvania is the current epicenter of the U.S. outbreak. Public health officials there have reported nearly 1,600 cases and eight deaths this year, more than any other state, and have clashed with the CDC. The CDC has attributed only six deaths nationwide directly to the measles.

Another policy change under Robert F. Kennedy Jr. ended the CDC’s longstanding practice of relying on state data to track the impact of measles spread. Hamer said wastewater monitoring is central to helping predict where a measles outbreak could arise, given widespread vaccine skepticism.

“Measles is a disease you really need to maintain, like, 93 to 95% coverage (in a population), ideally with two doses, and we’ve just fallen well below that in many places,” he said. “Wastewater has become a really good tool for tracking, early identification of outbreaks and then tracking the progress of outbreaks.”

Before the measles vaccine was created in 1963, major epidemics occurred about every two to three years and caused an estimated 2.6 million deaths each year, according to the World Health Organization. The organization reported 95,000 deaths in 2024, mostly among unvaccinated or under-vaccinated children younger than 5.

Increased calls for expanded public health measures are primed to take center stage once the next Congress, with a new Democratic majority in both chambers, convenes Jan. 3. Democratic lawmakers are expected to increase scrutiny of the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services and its secretary, Robert F. Kennedy Jr. The PREDICT Act also comes amid growing criticism of the Trump administration’s public health policy decisions.

News From The States reported that vaccine hesitancy, health misinformation and an overhaul of the CDC and much of the federal public health apparatus have marked Kennedy’s tenure. The outlet said a CDC advisory panel Kennedy selected had repeatedly gone against widely accepted medical practices and the science that supports them. Kennedy has said he recommends the measles vaccine. The panel’s proposed changes, which are being challenged in federal court, include ending the hepatitis B vaccine for newborns and removing the mercury-based preservative thimerasol from flu vaccines. News From The States said the proposed changes came despite widely debunked claims that thimerasol is harmful.

Last week, CDC scientists independently published a public health alert reporting that at least 112 pregnant women and 13 newborns in the United States had been diagnosed with measles. The reported complications included maternal pneumonia, preterm delivery and neonatal complications. The scientists found that 98% of the pregnant women with measles had not received the MMR vaccine or did not know their vaccine status.
