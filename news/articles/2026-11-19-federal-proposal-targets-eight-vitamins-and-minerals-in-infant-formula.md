## Manufacturers would get three years to comply with the final rule

Federal officials have proposed the first changes to federal infant formula nutrient requirements in nearly three decades, and the U.S. Food and Drug Administration said the review would proceed in phases, starting with eight required vitamins and minerals. The first phase would adjust acceptable levels for vitamin A, vitamin D, iodine and manganese and reaffirm specifications for chloride, potassium, selenium and sodium, with comments due by March 2, 2027. Experts said the changes could have the biggest impact in Mississippi, which has one of the country's lowest breastfeeding rates.

The initial proposal would lower, raise or maintain mandated levels for the eight vitamins and minerals already required in formula. If implemented as written, the White House's proposed changes would take place over multiple phases. The FDA said that “subsequent phases will assess the remaining required nutrients, potential new nutrient requirements and carbohydrate sources.” A second and third phase would propose changes to other ingredients and would also include comment periods.

All changes would be consolidated into a final rule. The final rule would take effect 60 days after publication, and manufacturers would then have three years from that effective date to comply. The FDA has not set a date for the rule to take effect, and the federal government has not announced when final changes would go into effect.

In 2022, four U.S. babies fell ill and two died after consuming powdered formula manufactured at an Abbott Nutrition plant in Michigan. Abbott voluntarily recalled 5 million units of powdered formula and closed operations for five months. Along with post-pandemic supply chain issues, the closure led to nationwide shortages and price surges.

During that time, Delta-based Brittany Reed-Isler worked as a designated breastfeeding expert for the Special Supplemental Nutrition Program for Women, Infants, and Children. Reed-Isler remembers dozens of families in her Bolivar County area having trouble finding formulas covered by WIC at their local grocer. The WIC program contracts primarily with two formula companies nationwide, Abbott Nutrition and Mead Johnson, making supply-chain hiccups more consequential for the low-income families that rely on the federal nutrition program. “That was a scary time,” Reed-Isler said. “Because you have to think about everything – it’s not just about the formula. It’s about the mom who may not have a car. She’s able to go down the street to go to her local grocery store. But what if she can’t find it there? How will she get a ride to another town?”

The National WIC Association was among dozens of groups that submitted comments to HHS. The association's letter states: “With the last comprehensive assessment of infant formula taking place in 1998, NWA appreciates the opportunity to consider new scientific data and information related to (commercial milk formula) nutrient requirements.” The association is largely in favor of the proposed changes.

Ali Hard, the association's director of public policy, said she hopes White House officials consider the “reality of the market.” Hard said a handful of companies control the infant formula market, making it vulnerable to collapse. She said changes to formula nutritional requirements may make it easier for international companies to enter the U.S. market. Hard identified a maximum iron level requirement, as other countries have, as one meaningful change that could help international companies enter.

Current U.S. standards outline a minimum iron level but no maximum. Hard said HHS asked stakeholders to submit input on an iron level maximum but had proposed no change at that point. Hard, a registered dietitian nutritionist, said: “Anemia is certainly a concern, but you also need to think about how iron also competes with calcium for absorption.” She added: “So we can’t just say ‘Iron, iron, iron.’”

Hard said different standards across countries complicate international companies' entry if the U.S. has a formula shortage, and she worried federal regulations could put international companies further out of reach. “The nutrition science is really important and that should be the number one thing,” Hard said. “But we also need to think about the reality of the market and make sure that we’re not setting ourselves up for a situation where we have even further consolidation and vulnerability with access to formula. Because it is a really unique food – there is no safe substitution besides breastmilk.”

Experts said the review was overdue and the changes proposed so far were positive, while expressing hope that new regulations would be created and implemented thoughtfully. They said increasing safety around infant formula could heighten nutritional standards and preserve families' access without delays, shortages or skyrocketing prices.

Dr. Cris Glick, a retired neonatologist and former director of Mississippi Lactation Services, a freestanding breastfeeding clinic in Jackson that closed in 2024, said regulators must ensure replacement products are on shelves before older formula is withdrawn. “If you’re going to ban the old formula, you have to make sure there’s a new formula in place right behind it, so that the shelves aren’t empty,” Glick said. She also said she hopes the federal government discusses feasible timelines for changing ingredients with formula companies.

Glick said she worries because she believes President Donald Trump's administration has a track record of moving “rashly and erratically.” The infant-formula effort is part of the broader Make America Healthy Again campaign. Glick also said she worries companies may not be able to reformulate their products in time, which could lead to shortages like the one families faced four years ago.

Dr. Patricia Tibbs, president of the Mississippi chapter of the American Academy of Pediatrics, said she has been surprised and impressed with the way the administration has handled the rollout of the proposed changes. Tibbs called the rollout a refreshing reminder of how leaders can work together to advance health policy, which she said she had not seen enough of under the administration, and said she has hopes for the future. “What has struck me is how correct the process has been for everything,” Tibbs said, applauding the agency for mobilizing on an important issue and for its concerted efforts to glean insights from experts and stakeholders.

Tibbs said the initiative represents the kind of organization and science-backed policy change she would like to see brought to the table on vaccine issues. “To not do that with the vaccines is horrifying,” she said, adding her disappointment that the United States had lost its measles elimination status “this week.”

Tibbs highlighted the change to iodine as an important update in the proposed ruling. The U.S. Department of Health and Human Services seeks to tighten the window of iodine levels from 5-75 micrograms to 15-50 micrograms. Tibbs said the change is good because iodine deficiency can be detrimental, while excess iodine can be toxic.
