## Sea Services says it paid vessel owners and fishers $34 million for work

Sea Services North America, a cooperative of about 200 fishers in New England, is funded through contracts with offshore wind developers and trains and equips fishers to support construction, providing a second income stream. It pays each fishing boat $4,500 per day plus fuel. To date, Sea Services says it has paid vessel owners and fishers $34 million for their work at several commercial-scale wind farms. Kevin Souza, initially skeptical of offshore wind, calls the opportunity a “godsend.”

On a rainy September morning, the rusty, 86-foot (26-metre) scallop boat Pamela Ann docked in New Bedford, Massachusetts. Capt. Kevin Souza and his three-man crew had spent the previous 12 days at sea without fishing. They recorded mammal sightings, monitored vessels to keep them out of construction zones and kept watch over workers at Revolution Wind, a 65-turbine offshore wind project just south of Rhode Island. Souza and the crew were fishers by trade, but this trip they worked for Sea Services, which had retrained them for offshore wind work. “God forbid, if anybody was to fall, we could be there to help them out,” Souza said. A bulldog tattoo on Souza's shin marks his time in the Navy. Photographs accompanying the reporting show fishing boats in New Bedford, two fishers unrolling a Jason’s Cradle on Souza’s fishing boat, Souza displaying an offshore wind farm on his iPad and a life raft aboard his boat; another image identifies Capt Jack Morris.

The arrangement put the participating fishers at odds with other fishers who feared offshore wind projects could harm their industry. Commercial fishers have sued wind companies, arguing the projects could displace them from traditional fishing zones and significantly harm marine ecosystems. U.S. fishers may fish in wind-lease zones except within 500-metre exclusion zones around construction sites, and construction can have positive and negative effects on marine life. In New England, Sea Services is challenging the idea that offshore wind and the fishing industry are natural opponents.

Since returning to office last year, Donald Trump has opposed offshore wind projects, saying his goal was to prevent any “windmills” from being built. In January 2025, he issued an executive order halting construction of five offshore wind projects on the East Coast. Courts rejected the order and allowed construction to resume. The administration then turned to a strategy of spending nearly $4 billion to buy back offshore wind leases from companies; the resulting economic risk has made developers hesitant to propose new projects. Revolution Wind installed its final turbine in September.

Gordon Videll, Sea Services' CEO, said work is drying up for the cooperative. “If you’re going to screw an industry, that’s how you do it – you put the chilling effect on the money,” he said. “The reality is, once these projects are done, even if administrations change, it’ll take years for construction to be back online.”

The slowdown is visible in Souza's schedule. In the past couple of years, he said, he worked 12 days on and three days off for Sea Services; now, he said, it is 10 days between shifts. “The work seems like it’s gonna get slower now because of Trump,” he said. Souza did not vote in the last election but preferred Trump to Harris, and said he had since soured on the president. “He uses executive orders to do whatever he wants,” Souza said.

Sea Services was founded in 2020 by another once-skeptical fisher, Gary Yerman, 76. Yerman had been fishing for more than four decades when he began hearing about proposed commercial-scale offshore wind projects. Yerman and other fishers feared the wind farms could harm their industry. He did not want wind farms in his own fishing zone, but believed they were too big to fail. “They’re gonna win,” he thought, deciding to cast his lot with the projects.

In 2019, Yerman and Gordon Videll, a friend and local lawyer, visited Northern Ireland, where they met fishers working with developers on offshore wind farms and other structures. “We saw how it was benefiting the fishermen there,” Yerman said. Videll noticed that the local fishers drove nice cars. “This provided a real annual income, which, if you’re raising a family, it means everything,” he said. After returning to the US, they sought to bring similar benefits to fellow fishers, which meant earning the trust of a skeptical industry. Trust was not easy. “There was a lot of fellas that were very angry because we joined in on building these windfarms,” Videll said. “Nobody trusted it.” Yerman and Videll first convinced one fisher to try the work; more participants joined over time.

The pandemic struck just as Sea Services was getting off the ground and may have helped Yerman and Videll's recruitment efforts. As restaurants closed, demand for seafood fell, and fishers in New Bedford and nearby fishing towns looked for extra work. “People had to make decisions they might not have made otherwise,” Videll said. The cooperative recruited local scallopers, whose boats are legally restricted from fishing more than 36 days per year to prevent overfishing. That restriction leaves the owners of highly specialized boats looking for extra sources of income during the off-season.

Capt Jack Morris, who owns a fleet of scallop boats including the Pamela Ann and joined Sea Services in 2021, said the arrangement helps boats keep operating and supports household expenses. “It’s a huge benefit Sea Services has brought,” he said. “With the [scallop] fishing season being so short, they are able to continue buying groceries, paying insurance, taking care of the house.” He also said: “These boats just tie up after their 36 days, but with the windfarm work, they keep going.” Morris said the additional work supports onshore mechanics, welders, carpenters, electricians and painters.

Stacy Alexander-Nevells, another fisher who works with Sea Services, runs a 19-boat commercial fishing operation with her father. Their boats mostly harvested scallops, but revenues were inconsistent and federal restrictions were always changing. “Working with Sea Services was another income stream,” she said. “We had to diversify. Now the boats aren’t just sitting at the dock, and the guys aren’t unemployed.”

At first, Souza was on the fence about offshore wind work because most fishers opposed it. Two decades of scalloping had taken a toll on him. Shucking scallops at a commercial pace eventually causes arthritis, carpal tunnel and rotator cuff tears. Souza lost the tip of a finger and said he probably has arthritis. “I saw the writing on the wall that scalloping is more of a young man’s game than us older guys,” he said.

Souza said he feared the economic impact of Trump's policies on offshore wind would be felt across New Bedford. “The bars and restaurants are going to lose,” he said. “The housing, everything else, it all funnels down.” New Bedford was once the global capital of whaling and remains a hub for seafood processing and the leading U.S. commercial port, generating $300 million in revenue. Its fortunes have ebbed and flowed with the ocean, overfishing and industry changes. Today, the city operates with a sizable deficit and higher unemployment than nearby cities.

Souza recently sold his New Bedford home and bought a house in Florida. “I’m gonna send out my resume, and I’ll probably be looking actually to work off the Gulf Coast in the oil industry,” he said. Videll considered how the Sea Services model could be adopted in places where new wind is being proposed, such as Nova Scotia or Brazil. The cooperative can also help with projects that do not involve wind; Sea Services recently assisted with the installation of an underwater cable bringing Canadian hydropower to New York City. With offshore wind in decline, hundreds of Sea Services fishers will soon lose an important income stream. “It’s sad because we really did build something that was great,” Videll said. “What’s ironic is, while Trump and all of his people talk about how wind is destroying fishing, I have 200 fishermen who have made money and shown that these industries can coexist.”
