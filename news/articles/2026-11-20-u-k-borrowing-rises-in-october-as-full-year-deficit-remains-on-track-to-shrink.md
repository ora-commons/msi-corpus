## Interest payments rise by 2.8 billion pounds amid inflation and bond yields

The U.K. government borrowed more in October than it did a year earlier, but its cumulative borrowing for the fiscal year remained lower. On November 20, the Office for National Statistics said government spending exceeded revenue by 17 billion pounds ($22.25 billion) in October, an increase of 800 million pounds from October 2025. Borrowing over the first seven months of the fiscal year, which ends in early April, totaled 111.3 billion pounds, down 7.3 billion pounds from the same period a year earlier.

The monthly increase partly reflected a rise in interest costs as inflation and bond yields picked up after the start of the war between the U.S. and Iran. The government paid 11.2 billion pounds in interest in October, an increase of 2.8 billion pounds from a year earlier.

“Much of the deficit is out of the government’s control, with the war in the Middle East continuing to put pressure on inflation and the economy,” said Kit Rees, an investment strategist at Quilter.

Despite the higher October figure, the year-to-date position left the government on course to reduce its budget deficit in the current fiscal year. The deficit for the fiscal year through October stood at 3.5% of gross domestic product, down from 3.8% a year earlier.

Total government debt stood at 3.1 trillion pounds, equal to 95.8% of GDP, up from 93.9% in October 2025. The debt ratio increased even as borrowing through October was lower than a year earlier.

The government’s October budget set out spending and taxation plans intended to reduce the deficit further in the current and subsequent fiscal years. For this fiscal year, however, borrowing is set to be higher than previously targeted.

The Office for Budget Responsibility estimates that the deficit will be 4.2% of GDP for this fiscal year, compared with 3.6% planned in March. The estimate is above the government’s previous target even though borrowing through October remains lower than a year earlier.

The overshoot partly reflects higher energy prices and increased costs for providing government services since the war began. Inflation is also raising the cost of interest payments because the U.K. has a higher share of bonds with payouts linked to inflation than its peers.
