## Government is appealing after a judge dismissed human-smuggling charges

WASHINGTON — Kilmar Abrego Garcia is self-deporting to Spain on Tuesday, according to a person familiar with the matter who spoke on the condition of anonymity because they were not authorized to publicly discuss the case. The reported move came as Abrego Garcia fought a second deportation to a series of African countries proposed by U.S. Department of Homeland Security officials. A judge told him to self-deport on Tuesday. His case became a focal point in the immigration debate after he was mistakenly deported to his home country in 2025.

Maryland Sen. Chris Van Hollen described Abrego Garcia as a “free man” and said he was traveling to a country of his choice. “I am relieved that he will be safe, in a country of his choosing, and free – including from this Administration’s constant persecution,” Van Hollen said in a statement.

Abrego Garcia’s attorneys, who represented him in Maryland immigration proceedings and his criminal case in Tennessee, did not immediately respond to messages seeking comment. A spokesperson for We Are CASA, an advocacy group that has supported Abrego Garcia and his family, declined to comment. “Our priority continues to be keeping the family safe,” said spokesperson Jossie Flor Sapunar.

Abrego Garcia, 31, is a Salvadoran national with an American wife and child. He has lived in Maryland for years and immigrated to the U.S. illegally as a teenager. In 2019, an immigration judge ruled that he could not be deported to El Salvador because he faced danger there from a gang that had threatened his family. The judge allowed him to live and work in the U.S. under Immigration and Customs Enforcement supervision, but he was not given residency status.

He was arrested in Baltimore in March 2025 and deported to a Salvadoran prison in what an official later called an “administrative error.” Facing public pressure and a court order, President Donald Trump’s administration brought him back in June 2025, but only after securing an indictment charging him with human smuggling in Tennessee.

A federal judge dismissed the case in May, finding that the Justice Department’s pursuit of criminal charges was designed to punish Abrego Garcia for challenging his deportation. The government is appealing that dismissal.

The administration vowed to deport Abrego Garcia to a third country, threatening to send him to Uganda, Eswatini, Ghana and, most recently, Liberia. Abrego Garcia had argued that if he were going to be deported, it should be to Costa Rica, which previously agreed to take him.

Todd Lyons, the former acting director of U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement, said in a March 2026 memo that deporting Abrego Garcia to Costa Rica would be “prejudicial to the United States.” Lyons wrote that Abrego Garcia should be sent to Liberia because the U.S. has spent governmental resources and political capital negotiating for Liberia to accept third country nationals.

The push for deportation to Liberia continued even after the U.S. signed an agreement with Costa Rica to accept deportees who cannot legally be returned to their home countries.

The case took an unusual turn on Oct. 6, 2026. Five minutes before a hearing expected to determine whether U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement could detain and deport Abrego Garcia to Liberia, attorneys for both sides left the courtroom along with Abrego Garcia. When they returned 15 minutes later, it was announced the hearing was canceled.

The two sides then gathered for another 90 minutes before leaving the courthouse without explanation. Abrego Garcia’s attorney said only that the court case was not ready to proceed and instructed reporters to watch the docket to learn the status.
