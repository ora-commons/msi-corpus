## PLA internal assessments list “five incapables” in command performance

A new Center for Strategic and International Studies report, titled “Peace Disease: Is the PLA Battle Ready?”, draws on interviews with more than 100 officials from the U.S., Australia, India, Japan, the Philippines and Taiwan who have direct experience dealing with Chinese forces in the air and on the sea. Its conclusion is qualified but clear: despite China's impressive arsenal and rapid modernization, the PLA could face significant operational weaknesses in an actual war. Lingling Wei wrote that the report should give both leaders pause.

The report describes the PLA's central problem as “peace disease” — 和平病, pronounced hé píng bìng — a term Chinese analysts and even Xi Jinping himself have used. The PLA has not fought a major war since its border conflict with Vietnam in 1979, and Lingling Wei wrote that it performed poorly in that conflict. It has studied other nations' wars, from the Gulf War to Russia's war in Ukraine. Wei said studying war and fighting one are very different things.

The report cites CSIS data showing that some 101 officers who served at the highest levels of China's military — members of the Central Military Commission, or CMC, theater commanders and their deputies — were purged or went missing between early 2022 and the beginning of 2026. Taylor Fravel, a leading PLA scholar at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, said in the report: “all CMC positions and theater command level positions have experienced a purge,” with roughly 38% of deputy-level positions also affected.

The report also says the purges uncovered corruption, including defective weapons, falsified training certifications, fabricated logistics records and poor quality control across China's defense industrial base.

The CSIS report says the PLA's own internal assessments are candid about its shortcomings. Its officers have cataloged what they call the “five incapables”: commanders who cannot accurately judge a situation, understand their superiors' intent, make competent operational decisions, deploy troops effectively or deal with the unexpected.

The PLA has also identified “two big gaps” between its current state and both its own national security needs and the capabilities of advanced militaries such as America's.

Wei wrote that Xi has concentrated more power than any Chinese leader since Mao Zedong, leaving subordinates with every incentive to tell him what he wants to hear. The CSIS report finds that as party oversight of the military has expanded, commanders face growing uncertainty over which mistakes will be treated as professional errors and which as political failings. That ambiguity, the report says, “naturally encourages consultation, centralized decision-making, and procedural compliance at the expense of rapid battlefield adaptation.”

Wei described the same fear-driven paralysis she said had frozen China's economic policymaking as extending to the military. As one Chinese military scholar cited in the report put it, there is “insufficient smell of gunpowder” in PLA training.

During the Asia-Pacific leaders' summit in Shenzhen last week, President Trump stepped off Air Force One and found Xi waiting on the tarmac, a rare gesture recalling Mao's reception of Soviet leader Nikita Khrushchev in 1959. Honor guards from the army, navy and air force stood at ramrod attention while jet fighters streaked overhead trailing colored smoke. Wei wrote that Trump loved the reception; he had previously called Chinese military displays “very, very impressive” and “incredible,” and after reviewing an honor guard during a lavish state welcome ceremony in Beijing six months earlier, called it “an honor like few have ever seen before.” Wei described the pageantry as a message from Xi that China was strong, the PLA fearsome and Xi in command of the force.

For Xi, the report's warning is specific. Wei wrote that a military that looks formidable on the tarmac yet struggles with the basics of joint operations in wartime poses a danger: “its commander in chief may not realize how brittle his own force actually is.”

For Trump, who publicly praised the Chinese military displays, the report shifts attention from parade-ground precision to battlefield readiness. Wei wrote that the gap Trump should watch was the distance between those conditions. The CSIS report documents weaknesses in weapons, training certifications, logistics records, quality control, command judgment and adaptation during joint operations.

Lingling Wei wrote that many analysts who study the PLA suspect political loyalists will fill the roles. The report leaves unresolved whether Xi is replacing the purged officers with combat-effective leaders or political loyalists.
