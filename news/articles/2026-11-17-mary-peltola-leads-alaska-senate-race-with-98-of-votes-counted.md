## Second-choice data weakens voter-confusion claims in the count

Mary Peltola remained ahead in Alaska’s U.S. Senate contest on Tuesday, with 98% of the vote counted nearly two weeks after the Nov. 3 election. BBC Verify found that Peltola’s lead would stand even if Republican incumbent Dan S. Sullivan and Daniel J. Sullivan Jr. received a combined vote. Alaska is America’s largest state, where the midterm vote count was still under way.

The Senate contest was the last Senate seat still without a winner after the midterm elections, in which Democrats swept to victory, and U.S. media had not forecast a winner. As of Tuesday, Peltola held 49.98% of the vote, while Daniel J. Sullivan Jr., who was listed in last place, had 1.65%. BBC News reported that Peltola said on Saturday she will “decisively be elected” as Alaska’s next senator.

Alaska uses a ranked-choice voting system in which voters select a first choice and can rank up to four other candidates in order of preference. If no candidate receives more than 50% of first-preference votes, officials eliminate the last-place candidate and reallocate that candidate’s ballots to voters’ next choices. The process repeats until two candidates remain and one clears the 50% threshold.

Dan S. Sullivan has represented Alaska in the Senate since 2015 and was seeking a third term. Daniel J. Sullivan Jr., a retired school teacher from a remote fishing village in Alaska, entered the race in May and ran a low-key campaign. BBC News reported that President Donald Trump joined Dan S. Sullivan at a campaign rally ahead of the vote.

Republicans and Dan S. Sullivan’s allies said the duplicate names could have confused voters, prompting an unsuccessful legal challenge. Dan S. Sullivan and his allies also accused Democrats of trying to confuse voters.

On Alaskans’ ballots, the two Sullivans were listed as:

- Sullivan, Daniel J. Jr.
- Sullivan, Dan S. (Registered Republican) Incumbent

There was a second registered Republican candidate, Gerald Heikes, who was also allegedly recruited into the race, according to The New York Times.

A New York Times story published the week before Election Day said Democratic operatives with links to the Peltola campaign had tried to recruit “spoiler” candidates, including Daniel J. Sullivan Jr. The report said it had seen text messages and interviewed 12 people who participated in or were told of the plan, or who were would-be candidates whom the team also tried to recruit. BBC News said it had not independently verified the reporting.

Peltola denied knowledge of the alleged “scheme,” and Daniel J. Sullivan Jr. denied links with the Peltola campaign. President Donald Trump posted multiple times on Truth Social since the election, claiming that the race had been “rigged.” Last week, he also said, “Anybody that voted for that second Dan Sullivan, the fake Dan Sullivan, was supposed to be a vote for the real Dan Sullivan.”

Experts said recruiting another candidate was not an illegal tactic if it was employed. Joshua Douglas, an election law expert at the University of Kentucky, said, “There is nothing to prevent a campaign from recruiting someone else to run, even if for political reasons.” Ruth Greenwood, director of the election law clinic at Harvard Law School, said, “There is no specific law preventing people with the same name running, but many states have laws or regulations about how names appear on the ballot. One of those regulations was used in Alaska.”

BBC Verify’s analysis of second choices found that 38% of the analyzed ballots ranking Daniel J. Sullivan Jr. first recorded no valid second choice. A further 29% ranked Peltola second, almost double the 15% that ranked Dan S. Sullivan second. Peltola was the next-highest second-choice preference among the analyzed voters, receiving nearly twice as many second-choice votes as the incumbent.

BBC Verify said it did not know how many voters who selected Daniel J. Sullivan Jr. and left the next ranking blank may have thought they had voted for Dan S. Sullivan. The analysis called into question claims that Republican voters may have been confused about which Sullivan they were voting for.

Richard Pildes, a professor of constitutional law at New York University, argued that “The likelihood that any confusion might play a significant role is diminished in Alaska” because any voter who was unsure which Dan Sullivan candidate they meant to vote for could rank them first and second.

At 98% counted, BBC Verify found that the two Sullivans’ combined vote could not overcome Peltola’s lead. The remaining count and ranked-choice transfers were still to be resolved, while BBC News had not independently verified the reported recruitment effort. Peltola denied knowledge of the alleged scheme, and Daniel J. Sullivan Jr. denied links with her campaign.
