## Boom and JetZero anchor an airport built on a $350 million land bet

About 15 years ago, Piedmont Triad International Airport in Greensboro, North Carolina, took what its executive director later called the "global domination plan": buying up neighboring land, investing in bridges, roadways and utility connections, and waiting for aerospace tenants to arrive. The gamble has delivered tenants, with Boom Supersonic finishing an aircraft plant on the airport grounds and JetZero starting construction on a $4.7 billion facility at the airport.

Kevin Baker, the airport's executive director, said the strategy was to assemble the pieces before the companies arrived, then pitch the airport at trade events such as the Paris Air Show. "We set out to make ourselves as ready as possible for companies that showed up," he said.

Boom Supersonic, a Centennial, Colorado-based company that aims to build a supersonic passenger jet, has completed construction of a $500 million aircraft manufacturing facility at the airport that is expected to create more than 1,750 jobs. This summer, Long Beach, California-based JetZero broke ground on a production facility at the airport to manufacture a manta-ray-like passenger aircraft; the plant will occupy more than 600 acres and is slated to generate about 14,500 jobs.

Those projects sit on top of a long decline. The Triad — anchored by Greensboro, Winston-Salem and High Point — was built on textile, tobacco and furniture-making industries, which were battered by globalization and offshoring in the 1990s and 2000s. Overseas competition badly hurt the domestic textile and furniture industries, and health concerns pummeled tobacco companies. The Triad lost tens of thousands of jobs as companies closed or moved away. Downtowns fell into disrepair. Decades ago, the Triad's economy was humming. Greensboro's textile industry made it the Denim Capital of the World, and Winston-Salem's tobacco manufacturing earned it the moniker Camel City, after the cigarette brand. High Point is still known as the Home Furnishings Capital of the World, though its furniture business is now centered on design rather than manufacturing. The Greensboro and High Point areas lost more than 34,000 manufacturing jobs between 1990 and 2010, a 40% decline, while the Winston-Salem area shed more than 31,000, a 52% drop, according to Bureau of Labor Statistics data. The poverty rate in Winston-Salem climbed.

The reversal shows in the census of the region's recent years. In the decade ending 2024, the Triad's population grew 7%, employment increased 16% and median household income jumped 48%, according to Carolina Demography, a unit of the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill. Within North Carolina, the region fell behind as Charlotte's financial hub and the Raleigh area's technology cluster helped those cities boom; it is now a center for aerospace, advanced manufacturing, life sciences and logistics. The renaissance has faced setbacks, as some plans have fallen through, but the projects keep coming.

By the time Baker's global domination plan was under way at the airport, it was one of the sites regional leaders were boosting. Baker thought the airport had momentum to build on, including recently added tenants such as a FedEx sorting facility and a Honda Aircraft plant that manufactures business jets. Getting there required public money spent before private tenants arrived. The airport graded purchased land, hooked up utilities and built access roads on spec. One obstacle was a planned interstate nearby that would divide newly acquired property from the runways. Baker proposed a $20 million bridge over the interstate so planes could taxi across it and pressed transportation and elected officials on the need for the connection. At the June groundbreaking for the JetZero plant, Chief Executive Tom O'Leary credited the airport's foresight in amassing land and building the connecting bridge. "This is a real growth-oriented mindset," he said. "It really set Greensboro apart when we were searching for a home."

Workforce supply was treated as part of the same bet, and securing a workforce was another of JetZero's considerations. The economic development team that wooed JetZero included Guilford Technical Community College, whose aviation program sits next to the airport. North Carolina's community college system was a pioneer in collaborating with companies to develop training tailor-made for their needs. At GTCC, composite technology is crucial to JetZero's lightweight, blended-wing aircraft, designed to use up to 50% less fuel than traditional commercial jets. The college is building a new training facility and securing funding for another, which together are projected to raise aviation capacity to more than 1,000 students a year from 500 currently, said GTCC President Anthony Clarke.

Graduates of the college make up a sizable part of the workforce at Honda Aircraft, which makes a six-passenger jet and is preparing to manufacture a new 10-passenger one. The company plans to add more than 175 workers to its existing staff of about 1,000, said senior vice president Rich Richardson. One of the college's former students, Jose Carrion, moved to Greensboro from Methuen, Massachusetts, in 2011 after hearing about job opportunities at Honda Aircraft. He worked his way up from technician to manufacturing supervisor and benefited from the area's lower cost of living. "We don't see ourselves living anywhere else," said Carrion, 36, who has five children.

Toyota opened a battery manufacturing plant in Liberty, a small town about 30 miles southeast of Greensboro, in November 2025, and the facility now employs about 2,700 workers, with a planned total investment of $13.9 billion and a workforce topping 5,000. A group including local governments, a railroad company and the Joseph M. Bryan Foundation, which focuses in part on economic development, began buying parcels of land in the 2010s to assemble the megasite. As automakers added manufacturing plants in the Southeast, the group hoped to lure one. Its 1,800-acre site was a finalist when a joint venture between Toyota and Mazda started looking for a location for an auto manufacturing plant, but the joint venture chose Huntsville, Alabama, instead. Triad officials requested a debriefing on why they came up short. Over a two-hour lunch, one project consultant detailed a list of issues the group needed to address. The group began tackling the problems one by one, including buying supplies to relocate a main power line that cut through the property and advancing plans to extend miles-long water lines from Greensboro, said Brent Christensen, chief executive of the Greensboro Chamber of Commerce. A few years later, Toyota chose the site.

Workers describe a different region than the one they entered. Tammy Smith, 52, a longtime beautician, landed a job on a Liberty production line in 2024. Years ago, the region "was going downhill, furniture companies were closing up, not a lot of jobs, people were struggling," she said. Now, she added, "I see a difference." A year before Toyota's November 2025 opening, Ziehl-Abegg, a German maker of ventilation systems in high demand by data centers, expanded into a highly automated plant in Winston-Salem and is projected to increase its workforce to about 1,000 in the next two years.

The region's research district grew out of the same bet on obsolete space. Developers and academic institutions turned former R.J. Reynolds Tobacco manufacturing plants in downtown Winston-Salem into a mixed-use district they call the Innovation Quarter. In 2016, Wake Forest University's medical school opened a new location in one of many former R.J. Reynolds Tobacco manufacturing facilities donated by the company, accelerating redevelopment in the area. The 330-acre district now includes lab spaces, tech companies, residences and restaurants, many in former tobacco plants. It employs about 4,000 people and plans to double its footprint in the coming years. At the Wake Forest Institute for Regenerative Medicine, hundreds of researchers work on endeavors such as engineering replacement tissues and organs to develop clinical therapies. The institute nurtures startups by providing lab space and equipment to research, test and manufacture products, said its director, Dr. Anthony Atala.

One tenant, BMI OrganBank, created a device that mimics the body's conditions to preserve kidneys for transplant recipients much longer than current technology allows, said co-founder Carrie DiMarzio. A clinical trial is anticipated to start next year, DiMarzio said. BMI OrganBank's vice president of R&D operations, Josh Copus, 32, grew up in Winston-Salem and moved to South Carolina for his undergraduate degree, thinking he wouldn't return. But he did, earning a doctorate in biomedical engineering through a dual program at Wake Forest and Virginia Tech. "Growing up, I don't think I ever went downtown," Copus said. "Now it's the place to be." In High Point, a former hosiery mill became Congdon Yards, with co-working spaces, business offices and an industrial-grade workshop for makers and designers.

Not every megasite has fared as well. Chip maker Wolfspeed's semiconductor plant in Siler City, opened in 2025, is largely dormant as the company seeks to rebound from a bankruptcy reorganization. Electric vehicle maker VinFast is locked in a legal battle with North Carolina as the state seeks to reclaim the megasite in Moncure after the company's project stalled.

Political and business leaders in the Triad say a high tolerance for risk helped. Economic-development groups rolled the dice with big public and private investments in land and infrastructure to try to lure new industries. City chambers of commerce in the Triad banded together to pitch the region collectively to companies rather than competing against one another. Toward the tail end of the deindustrialization era, Stan Kelly, who was on the board of the Piedmont Triad Partnership, a regional economic development group, recalled, "We were like, well damn, we've got a problem." He added, "And no one is going to fix our problem but us." Business leaders in the region joined forces to raise money for economic studies aimed at finding ways to counteract the damage, Kelly said. Those business leaders concentrated on several large sites, including closed cigarette factories in Winston-Salem, that could be developed or revitalized to attract new industries, and implored state officials to advocate for the area on trade missions.

When Christopher Chung became chief executive of the Economic Development Partnership of North Carolina in 2015, that message dominated his early conversations with Triad representatives. "Raleigh and Charlotte are getting all the wins," Chung said they told him. "We need you to think about this part of the state."

North Carolina's swing-state status — with a Democratic governor and a Republican-led state Senate — has pushed the parties to work together on economic development, some officials say. The Wall Street Journal described Roy Cooper as a moderate and noted that the former two-term Democratic governor, who won his U.S. Senate race earlier this month, has pitched the state as business-friendly. The Triad's resurgence is helping turn North Carolina into a major draw; in recent years the state has bypassed much bigger Texas and Florida as a destination for net domestic migration — people arriving from other states minus those leaving — according to the Census Bureau.

Affordability has helped, too. In the second quarter this year, the Triad's median home price was $304,000, compared with $409,000 in the Raleigh-area Research Triangle and $413,000 in the Charlotte area, said Tony Jarrett, a regional vice president at Howard Hanna Allen Tate Real Estate. The median home price nationwide was $408,000 in June, according to Redfin. Michael Walden, a professor emeritus of economics at North Carolina State University, said, "The leaders there have done a fabulous job of transforming that economy."
