## Reimbursements surged after the Labor Department eased PTSD rules

ProPublica reported on November 19, 2026, that U.S. taxpayers have paid nearly $5 billion over the past decade to insurance companies through a federal program for civilian government contractors injured or killed in Iraq and Afghanistan. The investigation found that some insurers delayed care for legitimate injuries and received reimbursements for fraudulent claims. ProPublica also reported that industry insiders described a wave of questionable claims by workers and suspect payments to insurers, while finding weak controls at the Labor Department, which said it was increasing oversight but that substantive reform would require an act of Congress.

The Defense Base Act and War Hazards Compensation Act place combat-related claim costs on the federal government while leaving insurers responsible for ordinary workplace injuries. Reimbursement includes medical care, lost wages, survivor benefits, legal and investigative costs and a handling fee of up to 15%, creating what former officials and worker advocates described as an incentive to settle some claims quickly, delay others and seek repayment after litigation. ProPublica said no one knows the true amount of misspent money.

ProPublica characterized the program as unusually vulnerable to graft because it paired strong incentives with few controls. Its analysis of Labor Department records found that American International Group received more than $1 billion since 2014, CNA Financial Corp. received more than $740 million, Starr received more than $730 million and Allied World received more than $350 million. The report said none of the four companies had been accused of wrongdoing.

Under the Defense Base Act, government contractors must purchase workers’ compensation policies for employees working overseas, and the government funds the insurance as part of the bid. Insurers cover medical care and lost wages and pay benefits to survivors when a worker dies. Under the War Hazards Compensation Act, the government reimburses an insurer when an injury or death results from hostilities and adds a handling fee of up to 15%. Insurers remain responsible for ordinary workplace accidents.

Insurers may challenge claims in administrative courts overseen by Labor Department judges, a process that can leave workers seeking benefits for months or years. Insurers say they contest 10% to 15% of claims. Before seeking war-hazards reimbursement, they must take “reasonable measures” to ensure a claim is not fraudulent, but the regulation does not define that standard. Applications require detailed receipts and submissions routinely exceed more than a thousand pages. A handful of specialized examiners decides whether and how much to reimburse.

The War Hazards Compensation Act also allows reimbursement for doctor bills, therapy, hospital stays, medication, prosthetic devices, lost income and funeral expenses. Insurers can recover legal expenses spent fighting claims, including lawyers, expert witnesses, private surveillance, translators, transcribers, transportation and hotel stays, as well as workers’ attorneys. Records showed that attorneys on both sides routinely charged more than $300 an hour. Labor officials then add a handling fee, up to 15% of the money the insurer spent on healthcare and lost-wage payments. Mark Reinhalter, a former Labor Department attorney who specialized in Defense Base Act law during three decades at the agency, called the handling fee “a free lunch.” Worker advocates and some attorneys said the fee could encourage insurers to prolong disputes.

The system traces its origins to the beginning of World War II. After Pearl Harbor, Japanese forces attacked Wake Island, where about 1,200 civilians and a vastly outnumbered contingent of U.S. Marines were defending a military base under a defense contract. The atoll fell two weeks later, and Japanese forces sent almost everyone to prisoner-of-war camps. About 100 civilians remained as forced labor, and all were executed when the U.S. Navy attacked the island in October 1943. In total, more than 150 contractors from Wake Island were killed or died in prison camps.

The Wake Island experience contributed to the Defense Base Act and its companion measure, the War Hazards Compensation Act. Insurers had resisted covering catastrophic battlefield risks, while Congress sought to preserve benefits for overseas workers without making premiums prohibitively expensive. The laws were intended to make the cost of insurance comparable to that for a construction site in New York and Wake Island. They eventually covered nearly everyone working abroad for a U.S. government contractor, whether American or foreign and whether or not the worker was in a war zone.

After World War II, the program remained small. The Vietnam War produced a surge in claims, but for decades only a few hundred were filed each year. The wars in Afghanistan and Iraq brought a much larger volume. In recent conflicts, the U.S. has supplemented its armed forces with hundreds of thousands of workers from across the world to provide security, maintain weapons systems and supply troops with food, housing and transportation. Without them, military officials said, it would be all but impossible for America to go to war. During the wars, civilian workers frequently made up half of the Defense Department’s presence on the ground. Many were injured or killed in roadside bombings, missile attacks and assaults by insurgents.

The Labor Department had been warned about the Defense Base Act system for years. A 2009 ProPublica investigation prompted congressional hearings, and the Pentagon and other agencies documented insurance-industry profiteering. A senior official at the time acknowledged “systemic problems.” Congress and the White House pursued proposals to replace private insurance with federal self-insurance, which industry officials said was generally cheaper. Legislation introduced by Democrats in 2013 died in committee, and a similar proposal from the Obama administration was not adopted. ProPublica said reform urgency faded as the wars approached their end, while claims continued to accumulate.

President Joe Biden appointed Chris Godfrey, a former head of Iowa’s state workers’ compensation system, to oversee the program in 2021. Godfrey discovered that reimbursements to insurers were jumping by hundreds of millions of dollars a year, far more than anyone had predicted. He said, “The war hazards reimbursements were a nightmare. They were ever-expanding,” adding, “We had a lot of concern this was going to blow up in our face.” He ordered a review in late 2022.

By December 2024, senior officials had drafted a 25-page memo that the Labor Department declined to release and ProPublica obtained. The memo described the program as “ripe for potential fraud or, at the very least, gamesmanship for profit.” It said an “abundance of actual fraud” was unlikely but acknowledged that the agency had little evidence supporting that assessment.

The memo and related records showed that examiners had no metrics for deciding whether an insurer’s expense was excessive. They generally checked whether an invoice documented payment. One official wrote, “There is not standard guidance regarding whether a particular expense or amount of expense is justified beyond whether payment was actually made.” The department’s computer system could not issue fines, and examiners recently relied on decades-old CD-ROM technology to process thousands of requests. Former officials said the agency lacked the expertise and employees needed to supervise reimbursements. One former senior manager said, “You can’t look under the covers. You just had to pay.” An attempt to hire experts to examine insurers’ records failed.

Godfrey estimated that as much as 10% to 15% of the money paid to insurers could result from graft, overbilling or other improper causes. ProPublica reported that estimate as $100 million to $150 million annually. Godfrey said reform efforts under the Biden administration encountered an entrenched bureaucracy that appeared captured by the insurance industry. He also said, “We just ran out of time. It was all slow-walked,” and added, “The agency should be doing their job to adequately police the reimbursement requests rather than just open the checkbook to insurance companies.”

Insurance-industry pressure helped produce the 2018 policy change, according to ProPublica. This spring, Labor Department officials, insurer executives, brokers and attorneys gathered at an annual conference at a four-star New Orleans hotel overlooking the Mississippi River. Many had known one another for decades and had worked at different times for the government, the insurance industry or legal firms on either side of disputes. The industry used the gatherings to resolve cases through mass arbitrations and to maintain relationships through charity golf tournaments and catered events. Keith Flicker, an attorney who had represented insurers in worker disputes for more than 40 years, called the field “a very small business.” Flicker and others worried that the Labor Department was taking too long to reimburse insurers and that examiners were too strict when approving combat-related claims. His firm also sought federal reimbursements and said on its website that it “leads the industry in effecting recoveries.” Some larger insurers were awaiting hundreds of millions of dollars in reimbursements for claims they had already paid.

The largest issue surrounded reimbursements for cumulative PTSD, an illness caused by exposure to a series of traumatic events rather than a single incident. Department examiners sought detailed evidence that insurers found difficult to produce for attacks that had occurred amid the chaos of a war zone years earlier. Flicker connected insurers with Labor Department officials and urged them to meet. Karen Colonna, then a senior vice president for Allied World, warned that failure to pay cumulative PTSD claims would lead insurers to cancel policies and imperil the government’s ability to hire workers willing to go abroad. In an email, Colonna predicted that premium rates “will materially increase and, with certainty, will likely lead to carriers limiting coverage in war zone areas.” Allied World and Colonna declined to comment on the email, which was obtained through the Freedom of Information Act.

In June 2018, the Labor Department issued Bulletin 18-03. The guidance removed the need for evidence tying a worker’s PTSD to a specific incident. Insurers only had to demonstrate that the employee had been present in an area attacked by insurgents, and a doctor’s affirmative statement could establish that the stressful environment caused the condition. The guidance also eliminated the need for a lengthy analysis of the cause. The department tripled to 18 the number of claims examiners.

Reimbursements more than tripled after the decision, reaching nearly $1 billion through September 2026. Annual payments rose from $242 million in 2018 to more than $915 million through September 2026. The department routinely reimbursed insurers more than 90% of the amounts requested, and that share had reached almost 97% by 2024. Psychological injuries accounted for 88% of reimbursements in 2024. Applications for repayment increased eightfold between 2018 and 2024, from about a thousand to more than 8,000.

Insurers received more than 21,000 reports of injuries in 2024, five times the 2018 total. PTSD claims rose from 21% of all cases in 2020 to 61% in 2024, many involving foreign workers. AIG said in its 2018 annual report filed with the Securities and Exchange Commission that the “expansion of the definition of reimbursable War Hazard claims by the US Government” had helped offset recent losses. In 2024, Starr sent the Labor Department a “privileged and confidential” notice saying that the “exceedingly slow reimbursement” of war-hazard claims had imposed a “heavy financial burden” on the company.

Industry insiders said the new policy changed insurers’ business calculations and prompted some to settle disputed cases quickly because the Labor Department was more likely to reimburse them. Labor Department records described settlement applications with minimal medical documentation, little investigation and negotiated amounts at what the memo called a “market rate,” which officials expected the department to accept. Other insurers spent years contesting claims and then reversed their position when they sought reimbursement for the same injuries.

Foreign workers were particularly affected because their settlements were smaller. A carrier could spend about $40,000 on lawyers and experts to litigate a claim with an uncertain chance of prevailing, or settle for a similar amount and receive reimbursement plus the 15% handling fee. Across thousands of cases, the costs to taxpayers mounted rapidly. ProPublica reported that many PTSD claims were valid, but it said the reimbursement structure reduced insurers’ incentive to investigate medical findings that industry sources characterized as questionable because the Labor Department was unlikely to detect them. A former insurer’s attorney said, “Some very doubtful claims were settled.”

Administrative judges in some cases accused workers’ attorneys of submitting fake documents. An insurance-industry official described the volume of claims as an “avalanche” and said carriers responded, “Eh, it’s recoverable, we’ll just settle and get money back.” For large claims, insurers had little financial downside if they went to court: winning meant they did not pay the worker, while losing meant the government would reimburse their costs and add the handling fee. Lawrence Postol, an attorney for AIG, wrote to an opposing lawyer, “If we win, great, and if we lose, War Hazard coverage is assured.” Postol declined to comment.

A former Labor Department attorney said officials worried carriers would engage in “rubber stamping” settlements on questionable claims because they were “playing with house money.” ProPublica said the policy also made reimbursement easier for insurers to obtain than benefits were for workers to secure. Insurers could deny and delay claims by requiring proof that a specific stressful incident caused PTSD, while seeking reimbursement by showing only that the worker had been on base during an attack.

Arbesa Zeqa’s case showed how an insurer could challenge a worker’s condition and later seek reimbursement after settling. Zeqa, a 23-year-old restaurant worker from Kosovo, worked as a cook at Bagram Airfield, a military base constantly attacked by insurgents, for 15 months beginning in February 2020. While contesting her claim, the carrier denied payment for her doctors’ care and lost earnings. She returned home in May 2021, told family she felt like a different person, and reported difficulty concentrating, poor sleep, nightmares and headaches. Doctors in Kosovo diagnosed her with PTSD.

Zeqa filed a claim with Starr. The insurer challenged it in a Labor Department administrative court and hired a neuropsychologist who wrote, “The current evaluation did not support a psychological condition.” After almost two years, Starr settled the claim for $210,000, covering anticipated PTSD treatment, other medical costs and earnings lost through her disability.

In a December 2023 reimbursement application, Starr said Zeqa was a victim of “violent attacks directed towards the United States and its allies.” The insurer wrote, “As a consequence of these insurgent attacks, Claimant developed post-traumatic stress disorder.” The application did not mention the contrary findings from Starr’s doctors, although the findings appeared in the 494 pages of accompanying documentation. Starr told the Labor Department that Zeqa’s “psychological injury arose from exposures to war-risk hazards.”

Five months later, the Labor Department paid Starr $269,287.15. The payment covered most of Zeqa’s settlement, more than $40,000 in fees for Starr’s attorneys, doctors and a private investigator who surveilled her in Kosovo, Zeqa’s attorney and a $24,857.78 handling fee. Labor Department regulations allow insurers to take contradictory positions on claims, and officials signed off on the reimbursement. Jon Robinson, an attorney representing Zeqa, said insurers were “manipulating the system for profit from the government. Period” and added, “They’re getting away with it because they can.”

In a statement, Starr declined to comment on any person’s claims, citing privacy concerns. The company said its responsibility to taxpayers “requires Starr to carefully evaluate each claim based on its individual facts, apply appropriate expertise, and ensure claims are handled reasonably and fairly before they are submitted to the U.S. Department of Labor for final approval and reimbursement.”

A worker from North Macedonia waited three years to receive $200,000 from Starr after working in Afghanistan for Fluor, a logistics company that won billions of dollars in contracts to house and feed U.S. troops. Insurgents repeatedly attacked the worker’s base, including one occasion when a missile slammed into the ground about 50 yards from the worker’s sleeping quarters. The worker was diagnosed with PTSD in 2019 and filed a claim with Starr that November but did not want to be identified because of the stigma surrounding the condition.

Starr rejected the claim because the worker had not identified a specific incident. A doctor wrote, “There is no objective evidence to support a diagnosis of PTSD.” Despite its expert’s doubts, Starr settled the claim in October 2022 for $200,000, reflecting wages the worker was expected to lose. Starr applied for reimbursement in March 2023 and said the PTSD resulted from constant exposure to insurgent attacks, noting that the insurer “need not provide details.” The Labor Department paid Starr $230,800 in October 2023. The worker waited three years for compensation; Starr received reimbursement in seven months. Flicker said the case involved a “viable dispute” and said, “We’re not hiding anything. It’s in the documents. The examiners can see what we’re submitting.” Fluor did not respond to multiple requests for comment.

Lloreta Sefedini, a 36-year-old waitress, also worked in Afghanistan. Her boss was killed in a suicide attack at the base, another friend was killed while traveling in a convoy and she spent countless nights in bunkers during rocket attacks. Sefedini returned home in 2019, saying she felt burnt out and exhausted, and a local doctor diagnosed her with PTSD. Her doctors and Starr’s doctors agreed that she had a psychological disorder, either anxiety, a sleep disorder or PTSD.

In August 2022, more than three years after Sefedini filed her claim, Starr agreed to pay $275,000 for lost wages and medical benefits. The insurer then received a reimbursement of $668,498.36. Almost $210,000 covered fees for Sefedini’s attorney, Starr’s attorneys, expert doctors, translators and private investigators. The reimbursement also included $24,345.54 in so-called “medical payments,” although records showed virtually all of that money paid Starr’s defense costs. Those expenses included $664.14 for a subcontractor to attend Sefedini’s forensic examination, $421.71 to hand-deliver records to Starr’s psychiatrist and $1,715.79 for deposing a paid expert. The Labor Department applied the 15% handling fee to the healthcare expenses — almost none of which paid for Sefedini’s treatment — and to other payments Starr made for Sefedini’s lost earnings, producing another $24,857.78 for Starr. Starr records indicated that department officials reviewed the charges and found them valid. The agency did not comment on why it paid the reimbursement.

Sefedini’s case also demonstrated how court battles could extend for years even when a worker’s injury appeared to meet the requirements for reimbursement. Starr’s lawyers had assessed the case as early as April 2020. They noted that Starr’s own doctors “unfortunately” agreed with Sefedini’s doctors that she had a psychological injury, weakening Starr’s ability to contest the claim. The lawyers also said Sefedini had “favorably” established a strong case for reimbursement by testifying about the constant violence she faced. They estimated a 75% chance that Starr would receive war-hazards reimbursement. The dispute continued for another two years over the settlement’s size and the degree of Sefedini’s disability, with additional delays caused by COVID-19, while legal fees continued to mount.

The program’s liabilities continued after the wars wound down. The United States ceased major operations in Iraq in 2011 and Afghanistan in 2014, but workers continued filing claims as severe injuries and occupational illnesses, including cancer from occupational exposure, appeared over time. Workers’ compensation has a “long tail,” with claims continuing for decades. As the market for new war-zone policies evaporated, insurers faced claims from workers whose employers no longer bought premiums.

Foreign workers were often unaware of the program. Worker attorneys advertised on airline seat backs, nonprofit organizations held seminars and workers used social media to spread information in places including Uganda and the Philippines, where government contractors had recruited labor. Both Starr and AIG said they had paid thousands of claims submitted by workers employed by companies that had long ceased paying premiums. Starr said it had handled more than 32,500 claims, including 24,500 arising after the Afghanistan withdrawal in 2021. AIG said it had received 5,000 new claims in 2026 under policies dating to 2015 or earlier.

Industry insiders said the business’s long tail had resulted in insurance companies losing money over the long term. With dramatically fewer premiums coming in, reimbursements became more financially important. Reinhalter said, “There’s way more to come, to be honest,” and predicted, “We’ll likely see continuous increases over the next 20 years or so.”

During Godfrey’s tenure, the Office of Workers’ Compensation Programs attempted to modernize its computer system at a projected cost of $60 million. The system never worked as designed and could not produce reports required by Congress. It was supposed to automatically penalize insurers and contractors that failed to follow reporting rules, but the agency suspended fines for companies that failed to report worker injuries or deaths promptly.

The internal review said the burden of proof for reimbursement, particularly for psychological claims, was low. Labor officials acknowledged that business-savvy insurers had stopped conducting thorough investigations because the department was paying claims supported by scant or unreliable evidence. One memo passage described settlement applications with minimal medical documentation, little investigation and negotiated amounts likely to be accepted. The memo said existing laws constrained the agency’s regulatory powers and insurers would fight proposed reforms. Officials considered applying “substantive scrutiny” to reimbursement applications but rejected the idea, writing that another review would extend a laborious process and likely produce “fervent objection from carriers.” Proposals to hire actuaries to examine insurers’ books and increase penalties on carriers also failed.

Godfrey left office shortly before President Donald Trump’s second term began, and the new administration did not answer questions about the memo. Trump appointed James Macy as director of the office in 2026. Macy previously worked at a law firm representing corporations in labor issues and subsequently left the position. Before leaving, the department issued an annual report whose only proposed war-hazards change was to speed payments to insurers.

Labor Department spokesperson David A. O’Brien said constraints imposed by decades-old laws and more recent court decisions limited the agency’s oversight. He said the sharp increase in reimbursements occurred under former President Joe Biden. “The Trump Administration, particularly at the Department of Labor, is taking steps to increase oversight of the program to address potential waste, fraud, and abuse,” O’Brien said. He also said “substantive reform” would require an act of Congress.

In August, a government fraud task force headed by Vice President JD Vance said the Trump administration had “stopped” $11.8 billion in fraudulent payments across the Labor Department. O’Brien did not respond to questions about whether that total included reimbursements to insurers or specify how the agency had increased oversight. The agency also did not comment on why it paid the Sefedini reimbursement, which included so-called “medical” expenses.

Insurance carriers contacted by ProPublica did not provide answers to detailed questions. Starr said it had “consistently endeavored to process all legitimate and proper claims in a timely manner and to administer these important programs in full compliance with all applicable laws and regulations in accordance with its fiduciary responsibility to American taxpayers.” The insurer noted that all its reimbursements were approved by the Labor Department. AIG said it “adheres to the Department of Labor’s requirements and guidelines.” Allied World and CNA declined to comment. CNA is ProPublica’s media insurance provider.

Insurers have also argued that they are losing money on the system. It sometimes takes years for the Labor Department to reimburse them, during which time insurers must pay healthcare and disability benefits from their own funds. The insurers describe this arrangement as an interest-free loan to the government. Insurers typically make money by investing premiums and other assets, and the department’s delays were “depriving carriers of investment income and imposing a significant economic burden on their business,” Starr said.

Starr said the department still owed it “hundreds of millions” of dollars. The company said Labor’s “delays have resulted in Starr floating an enormous, interest-free loan to the U.S. Government, and depriving Starr the opportunity to earn interest income on these funds.”

The military has relied on civilian contractors for decades, a policy ProPublica described as controversial because contractors cost less and free troops for combat roles. The official count of U.S. troops killed in Iraq and Afghanistan was just over 7,000. About 3,600 civilian workers died in the conflicts and were not included in that total. Civilian contractors were not members of the armed services, could not be ordered into combat and could quit their jobs; many were highly paid. ProPublica said an unreliable compensation system could make contractors less willing to accept overseas work or require higher compensation.

Doug Brooks, president emeritus of the International Stability Operations Association, which represents federal contractors working in conflict settings, said an insurance safety net “is essential for ensuring companies are able to operate in these dangerous environments cost effectively and with the risks to personnel properly addressed.” He added, “It’s important to have this in place for everybody.”

ProPublica based its investigation on interviews with scores of current and former federal employees, insurance-industry insiders, attorneys for workers and insurers, and civilian workers. The reporting also relied on internal memoranda, corporate documents and tens of thousands of pages obtained through court filings and the Freedom of Information Act. ProPublica described the spending as hidden costs of the U.S. wars on terror and said it was reporting them for the first time.

In December 2025, ProPublica requested a spreadsheet of all War Hazards Compensation Act payments. The Labor Department released a printed, 282-page PDF in July containing more than 18,000 rows of payment information but declined to provide the data electronically. Reporters used artificial intelligence to extract payment information from the printed spreadsheet. ProPublica said its extracted totals differed from the figures in the document by less than 0.5%, and a hand-checked random sample of 10% of the records had a similarly low error rate. T. Christian Miller reported the investigation, with contributions from Agnel Philip and Ryan Little. ProPublica said it was still reporting and that the full amount of misspent money remained unknown.
