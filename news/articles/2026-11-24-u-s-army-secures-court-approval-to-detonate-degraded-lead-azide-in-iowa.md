## Safeguards include barriers, weather limits, air modeling and public notice

The U.S. Army plans to detonate 68 barrels of degraded lead azide at the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant near Middletown on Wednesday morning, after a three-judge panel of the 8th U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals said the Environmental Protection Agency's emergency permit adequately incorporated safeguards against dust dispersal. The quantity was reported as 9,500 pounds. The site is near the Mississippi River, about 90 miles (140 kilometers) south of Cedar Rapids, and the Army said the operation would be coordinated with state, local and federal authorities.

The court said the permit conditions addressed environmental groups' concerns about dispersal. Those conditions include barriers around the storage area, weather restrictions, advance public notification, updated air modeling, and environmental monitoring and sampling.

The judges said the record showed the Army evaluated "a full range" of alternatives, including using other chemicals to neutralize the lead azide and thermal treatment. EPA documents said alternatives such as neutralizing and decomposing the drums' contents with other chemicals were also considered. Army officials said handling the drums would increase the probability of an accidental, fatal detonation, while the environmental groups argued in court that other alternatives had not been fully considered.

Environmental groups sued last week to stop the operation. The court temporarily halted the permit, prompting the Army to argue it did not need authorization at all because the situation was an emergency. An attorney for the groups said the legal challenge centers on the validity of the EPA permit, not the Army's decision to move forward under the emergency provision.

The detonation was initially planned for last Friday, but the Army changed the date three times following an outpouring of public concern about potential health and environmental effects. The Army said the weather would be most favorable on Wednesday.

Acting Army Secretary Adam Telle posted on social media that "Every expert agency agrees, and both Iowa's current and incoming Governors say the call belongs to the experts." He described the decision as a consensus. Current Gov. Kim Reynolds activated the state emergency management office Friday, and the state created a dedicated website for the operation.

President Donald Trump, commander in chief of the Army, said on social media earlier in the day that, beyond the court's go-ahead, he wanted Gov.-elect Rob Sand to approve the detonation. Sand, a Democrat who does not take office until January, said the decision was fully up to the Trump administration. In a Monday post, Sand said the administration should "make this decision based on the information they have from the people they brought in to do the job months ago and have been on the ground in Middletown for weeks." On Sunday, he wrote that because previous decisions had already been made, "No good options exist and the situation is more dangerous and unpredictable than it ever should have been."

Two people familiar with the travel arrangements, speaking on condition of anonymity because the details had not been made public, confirmed that a flight with Army officials bound for Iowa departed Joint Base Andrews to help support the Army's planned detonation before Trump's Truth Social post. Flight-tracking sites showed an Army business jet taking off shortly before 1 p.m., and Trump posted minutes later.

Earthjustice managing attorney Rachel Rintelmann said the lawsuit had alerted the public to the threat. In a Monday statement, she said the 8th Circuit had allowed the Army to proceed and that the group respected the decision. "But the Army created this emergency through neglect, and it owes the people of Iowa more information about the planned detonation and more safeguards for their health," Rintelmann said.

In March, staff at the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant found several dozen barrels of degraded lead azide, which is used in munitions production. Crystallization created an unstable and dangerous situation, prompting the Army to assemble experts in munitions chemistry, engineering and environmental safety to develop a plan.

In court filings over the weekend, Army Col. Aaron Teller, a munitions emergency response specialist, explained that less liquid inside the barrels meant the solution was more susceptible to freezing. That increased the sensitivity of crystals formed around the inside rim and underside of the lids. "Even a small amount of friction can break the crystal formation, causing an explosive chain reaction," Teller said in his declaration to the court.

Officials contend that the risk of an uncontrolled explosion is growing, particularly as temperatures drop in the coming days. John Goodpaster, a chemistry professor at Indiana University, Indianapolis, said lead azide is "very sensitive" and susceptible to heat, freezing, shock, friction and "all sorts of things that could cause an accidental detonation." Goodpaster said the material's age increases the risk because explosives become increasingly unstable as they age. He also said lead escaping into the atmosphere creates an environmental threat.

Goodpaster said, "I do not envy the Army." He also said, "They are in a situation where they've got a large amount of very dangerous material and not very many good options in terms of trying to dispose of it." Goodpaster said it is impossible to predict when the material could explode on its own, but "The longer we wait, the longer the risk accumulates that they might have an accidental explosion."

Some residents near Middletown have sealed windows and doors as a precaution against lead dust. Others living farther away raised concerns on social media about possible paths the plume could take depending on the wind. Officials acknowledge that lead is toxic and have recommended that people stay indoors whenever the detonation happens and clean outdoor surfaces with water afterward to limit exposure to lead particles.

Middletown Mayor Rodney Johnson said the detonation must take place "so we can move on with our lives." He added, "Improper storage and disposal is where things go wrong," in a social media post. Leaders in neighboring states also expressed concern. Missouri Gov. Mike Kehoe said state agencies were working to establish additional monitoring of air and water quality, public health and agriculture resources. "But I want to emphasize: this is a federal operation," Kehoe said. "The states of Iowa, Missouri and Illinois do not have a say on when this is conducted."

The Army's statement late Monday said it was proceeding with the controlled removal, and the operation remained scheduled for Wednesday morning. The appeals court has allowed the plan to move ahead, while Earthjustice continues to call for more information and safeguards.
