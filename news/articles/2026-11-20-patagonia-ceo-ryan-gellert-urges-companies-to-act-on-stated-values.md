## Reversing course on public commitments draws criticism from both sides, Gellert says

Patagonia CEO Ryan Gellert criticized companies that try to have it both ways on social issues, saying people quickly detect a lack of commitment. In an interview with The Wall Street Journal published on 2026-11-20, he said CEOs need not speak on every topic but should understand their organizations' values and provide leadership when appropriate. The interview, which addressed how companies navigate Washington and more, was edited for clarity.

## Consistency and corporate leadership

The Wall Street Journal described companies dialing back diversity efforts and retrenching on social issues, then asked Gellert what lesson he saw in their responses to pressures. Gellert said, “The companies getting it wrong are pretty consistently the companies that have tried to have it both ways.” He described companies that said they did not know what was important, then pretended an issue mattered until someone objected and pretended it no longer mattered. People “sniff that out in seconds,” he said, leaving the companies “in the crosshairs from all sides.”

Gellert singled out Costco as doing exceptional work because its position was rooted in consistency and clarity of values. He said he was not sure Costco thought of the stance as a big position, then described it: “These are our values, and with all due respect, don’t tell us how to run our f—ing business. We know how to do this, and so we will.” He said he respected that stance.

Gellert said he was frustrated by a total lack of commitment and rejected the idea that publicly traded companies could comfortably hide behind their corporate structure and do nothing else. His expectation was more limited: “My expectation is not that CEOs of every company speak out on every topic, but it sure as hell is that they understand the values of their organization, and they stand up and provide leadership when it’s appropriate.”

## Activism and political alignment

The Wall Street Journal wrote, “Patagonia has never worried too much about who it might bother with its corporate activism.” The outlet said the outdoor-apparel maker had fought for causes from the environment to better voting access and restricted its custom-branded vests to firms that “prioritize the planet” for a stretch. Patagonia later tweaked the policy.

Gellert, who became Patagonia's chief executive in 2020, said the company was not inherently partisan but did have an agenda. “We’re not an extension of the Democratic Party, and we’re not in opposition to the concept of the Republican Party,” he said. “We’re in full alignment with anybody that wants to save our home planet.”

The Wall Street Journal said that over the years Patagonia had honed a playbook for speaking out on topics it cared about and had mostly sidestepped the backlash encountered by other brands, citing Target and Bud Light. In September, Patagonia joined conservation groups in suing the Trump administration over plans to scale back protected national monuments in Utah.

Gellert rejected the suggestion that Patagonia could advocate differently merely because of its brand, saying, “Yeah, I think that’s bulls—.” He pointed to Patagonia's “incredibly unique founders” and said, “We put together and pioneered a very unique ownership and stewardship model, and we’ve got this history.”

## Product stewardship and customer selection

The Wall Street Journal characterized Patagonia as the “unofficial uniform of Wall Street and Silicon Valley” before the company restricted corporate logos on some clothing. Gellert confirmed the restriction and said it “took on a life of its own.” He said the branded-apparel business continued after a pause because the company sought to make products with as much quality as it knew how and wanted them to exist as long as possible.

Gellert said customers could return a product with a broken zipper for repair and, if it remained usable but they were finished with it, Patagonia would find someone else to use it. He said embroidered logos created a separate problem: If employees left the company, the product could become virtually unusable. Patagonia's challenge was to apply logos without that level of permanence.

Gellert said Patagonia was thoughtful about industries, customers, value alignment and whom it sold to, and he declined to provide a list of customers it did and did not serve. In a hypothetical case in which an AI company wanted to buy a large quantity of Patagonia products with its logo, he said he would be pulled into the conversation but the deal would be unlikely. “The determinant would not be a commercial one,” he said.

Asked whether climate change was an opportunity for the business, Gellert described the “funny dynamic” of making technical products for outdoor sports while operating under a mission to save the home planet. “Both can be true,” he said. He said climate change is driving different weather patterns and that the company asks what products people want in that environment, citing rain protection, insect repellency, UPF and sun repellency.

## Employment and layoffs

Asked about AI's potential impact on jobs, Gellert said Patagonia is continuing to hire and retains a bias toward promoting internally when that is the best decision for the company. Discussing Anthropic's IPO and its historically high valuation, he said: “It’s going to take tremendous efficiency, which is a nice way of saying reducing people and their cost from businesses. That really concerns me.”

The interviewer noted the “drip-drip-drip of layoff announcements across companies” and asked how AI changes employment decisions. Gellert said he had been responsible for one round of layoffs in his career, involving a small percentage of roles, “about 35 roles,” during a Patagonia reorganization a couple of years earlier. He said it was Patagonia and he did not want to hide from that. “It didn’t feel small to the 35 people who were impacted,” he said. “Not to a single person did that feel like a small thing.” He called the round a leadership failure: “It felt like a failure of leadership when we went through that, and I think that it is.”

## Institutional trust

Gellert placed trust at the center of the business-government relationship. “One of the biggest things we’ve got to address at the intersection of business and government is the lack of trust in institutions,” he said. He also addressed executives' credibility toward employees: “When CEOs say one thing and do another and are sort of cavalier or callous toward the impact of their decisions on employees, I don’t think they deserve trust.”
