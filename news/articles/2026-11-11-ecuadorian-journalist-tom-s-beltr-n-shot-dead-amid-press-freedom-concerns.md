## Almeida says six journalist killings in Ecuador last year remain unpunished

Ecuadorian journalist Tomás Beltrán was shot dead Sunday night while moving his car into a garage. Beltrán ran a Facebook page with news about Calceta, a small city in Manabí province. Police and the public prosecutor’s office had said little about the killing.

Calceta’s Pacific coastline has made it a key flashpoint in gang warfare over drug-trafficking routes. Ecuador has been transformed from one of Latin America’s safest countries into one of its most violent as the conflict over trafficking routes has spread.

Little was known about the killers except that they were armed and then fled the scene. It remained unclear whether the crime was linked to Beltrán’s work. He had previously reported allegations of corruption involving local authorities.

Many press-freedom groups cited Beltrán’s killing as the latest example of deteriorating conditions for journalists in Ecuador. Six journalists were killed in the country last year alone.

Artur Romeu, Reporters Without Borders’ Latin America director, said Beltrán had told the organisation months earlier “about his fears and the threats he was receiving.” “We took part in efforts to get him to safety,” he said. Beltrán had been placed in a protection programme run by the public prosecutor’s office, but it was not known whether the protection remained in place when he was killed.

“Reporters Without Borders calls on the public prosecutor’s office to investigate as a priority whether there is a link to his journalistic work, identify any possible masterminds and protect his family and colleagues,” Romeu said.

Fundación Periodistas Sin Cadenas reported that at least 19 journalists had gone into exile since gang violence exploded in 2023. Ecuador does not produce cocaine like neighboring Colombia and Peru but has become a major trafficking hub, particularly through the Pacific.

Journalists have received death threats with photographs of their homes and details of their routines and families. “Practising journalism in Ecuador is now an extremely high-risk job,” said María Fernanda Almeida, a project coordinator at FPSC, adding that all of last year’s killings of journalists “remain completely unpunished.”

“And one of the many terrible effects of this violence is self-censorship: at least in the coastal provinces, colleagues no longer want to report on violence,” Almeida said.

Almeida cited the stance of President Daniel Noboa’s government as another problem for journalists, saying the government had responded to the surge in crime with a controversial “iron-fist” approach to the gangs, repeatedly declaring states of emergency and expanding the armed forces’ role in measures criticized by human rights groups. Noboa is one of US President Donald Trump’s key allies in Latin America.

During coverage of military operations, reporters had their phones confiscated, a search warrant was executed at a reporter’s home, and a journalist was threatened by soldiers for filming them stop university students.

Several reports said Noboa had pressured media organizations to dismiss journalists critical of him. Columnist Roberto Aguilar said he was fired from the newspaper Diario Expreso after the government reportedly threatened private advertisers with audits if they continued advertising with the outlet. Noboa’s administration did not respond to requests for comment.

“I don’t think there is complete freedom of expression in Ecuador today,” Almeida said. “And that is very worrying because the more violence and silencing there is, the more journalism is needed.”
