## El-Sayed hopes for more Trump common ground when he takes office

Abdul El-Sayed confirmed that he has secured a meeting with Vice President JD Vance, according to The Guardian. The vice president’s chief of staff contacted El-Sayed after his Michigan election victory. El-Sayed did not disclose the meeting’s agenda.

El-Sayed said he wants Congress to change its posture on the United States’ relationship with Israel. His first step would be making sure the Democratic caucus is no longer funded by the American Israel Public Affairs Committee, or Aipac. The Guardian described Aipac as a pro-Israel lobby that spent heavily against El-Sayed in his election.

El-Sayed said he and President Donald Trump agree about ending foreign military aid to Israel and that he hopes to find more common ground with Trump when he takes office. “I agree with the president – I can’t believe I’m saying that – that we need to end foreign military aid that is unconditional to Israel, as well as Egypt and Saudi Arabia and Pakistan,” he said.

Trump had made a surprise election-day announcement that the United States would end military aid to Israel at the end of the year under an agreement reached with Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu. El-Sayed said basic international law should be applied to what Israel is and has been doing. “I think we need to be applying very basic international law to what Israel is and has been doing,” he said. “We should not continue to be running cover for them at the UN, at the ICC, at the ICJ.”

El-Sayed has sought ways to engage the Trump administration directly. In recent days, he created a shiny gold “TrumpCare” card and said Trump could call Medicare for All “TrumpCare” if it would help expand healthcare to more Americans. In an earlier Fox interview that week, Vance said that if El-Sayed wanted to work with the Trump administration “to make people’s lives better”, Vance would find “very much a willing partner”.

Vance regularly attacked El-Sayed on the campaign trail, calling the incoming senator “terrible evil” and “corrupted”. After El-Sayed won his race, he called Vance “habibi,” a term of endearment in Arabic, and joked that the vice president would have to preside over his swearing-in ceremony in January.

El-Sayed, who will become the first Muslim U.S. senator in January, overcame tens of millions of dollars spent against him, first in a closely watched primary and then in a swing-state general election.

The Guardian’s accompanying photographs showed El-Sayed speaking at the Michigan Democratic Party general-election kickoff rally in Detroit on Aug. 7, 2026, credited to Jeff Kowalsky/AFP/Getty Images. Other photographs showed him at a campaign event in Muskegon, Michigan, on 11 October 2026, credited to Chip Somodevilla/Getty Images; greeting supporters who were unable to attend a Traverse City campaign event that had reached maximum capacity on 10 October 2026, also credited to Chip Somodevilla/Getty Images; and arriving at a press conference in Detroit on 22 September 2026, credited to Sarah Rice/Getty Images.

El-Sayed said his three immediate goals once sworn in would be stopping the “nuclear bomb” of Medicaid cuts, ending the war in Iran and addressing the “stupid approach” to tariffs. He also said he plans to propose Medicare for All legislation, pursue voting-rights legislation after the U.S. Supreme Court weakened the Voting Rights Act, advance the Pro Act to expand union rights and overturn Citizens United. The Guardian described Citizens United as the Supreme Court decision that unleashed unfettered outside spending on campaigns.

El-Sayed is supporting Maryland Sen. Chris Van Hollen’s bid for the Senate leadership role. The Guardian described Van Hollen as an advocate for Palestinian rights. El-Sayed said Van Hollen would be an “amazing leader, and he’s a departure from a lot of the instincts of the past”. Van Hollen was an early supporter of El-Sayed in his primary election against U.S. Rep. Haley Stevens and campaigned for him.

As part of his campaign commitments, El-Sayed said he would not move his family to the U.S. capital and planned to spend considerable time in Michigan holding town halls and talking with voters. He has two young daughters.

El-Sayed was among a large group of incoming senators and representatives who skewed younger this cycle and also have young children. He said the “somewhat erratic” schedule of the job made it harder for people with children. Making the U.S. Capitol a “free and open place” for children would help, he said, because the Capitol should help “good senators and congresspeople also be good parents”.

El-Sayed argued that public service and family obligations need not conflict. “I think one of the challenges with service is that we forget that every public servant is a private servant too, and that’s to their families, in particular to their children,” he said. “And those two things shouldn’t be mutually exclusive.”

Throughout his campaign, El-Sayed said he faced Islamophobic rhetoric, including from Trump and Republican officials. Although his campaign was not about defending himself, he said it was important that Michiganders rejected those attacks when they voted him in. “There are a lot of kids who are watching,” he said. “And when the president of the United States is attacking somebody for their name or how they pray, it’s important that the people of the state of Michigan stood up and said, ‘No, this is not how we want to be.’ And I think they sent a message that was loud and clear, and I’m really grateful for that.”
