## Families of fallen say Trump's 250-foot arch would shadow Arlington

Cpl. Collart was killed in August 2023. A crew chief on a V-22 Osprey, an aircraft that functions as both a helicopter and an airplane, he had been deployed to Australia for several months when the aircraft went down during a training mission. Collart and the pilots, Capt. Eleanor LeBeau and aircraft commander Maj. Tobin Lewis, died in the crash. It was months later that the family learned he had survived the impact but went back into the burning aircraft to try to reach the pilots, who were trapped. He posthumously received the Navy and Marine Corps Medal, the highest honor the branches bestow for valor that is not combat-related.

Standing at his son's grave this week, the elder Collart spoke of the younger man's love of flying and of his pilots. He remembered how, on the day of the burial, a fleet of Ospreys had flown over the grave in a formation known as the missing man, meant to honor a fallen military aviator. Section 52, the corner of the cemetery where the corporal is buried, held just one visitor walking and reading the stones on a recent day, as a steady stream of funerals passed elsewhere on the grounds.

The arch itself is part of a series of renovations to Washington that Trump, a Republican, has set in motion to reimagine the city. When complete, the gold and granite structure would stretch 250 feet (76 meters) tall — taller than the Lincoln Memorial. It would be built on a traffic circle about half a mile (0.8 kilometers) from Arlington's main entrance.

Opponents say the arch would break the alignment between the Lincoln Memorial, the Memorial Bridge and Arlington House, the former home of Confederate Gen. Robert E. Lee, and with it the symbolism of a reunited North and South. They say it could cast a long shadow on the cemetery, with the potential to literally shade gravesites, disrupt solemn visits with the noise of construction and alter the carefully designed historic landscape of the capital.

"It's a billboard for an ego. It's a loud, boastful, gaudy piece of marketing dropped onto sacred ground," Bart Collart said of the project. He said his son would see it as "dirtying hallowed ground."

Behind the fences that line Memorial Drive, Arlington is an active cemetery. More than 430,000 people are interred there, and dozens more are added each week. They include retirees and their spouses, some dependents and a few eligible dignitaries. Two presidents, John F. Kennedy and William Howard Taft, are buried there, along with more than a dozen Supreme Court justices, more than 400 recipients of the Medal of Honor and the remains of military personnel from every war dating back to the Revolutionary War. Boxing legend Joe Louis and Audie Murphy, an actor and one of the most decorated U.S. soldiers of World War II, are also buried there, and the Tomb of the Unknown Soldier is one of its prominent features. An estimated 3 million to 4 million people visit each year.

On a recent visit, a small group of about 10 men came quietly from Section 60, where soldiers who died in Afghanistan and Iraq are buried. Their young ages, the "PH" for Purple Heart and the inscriptions reading Afghanistan or Operation Enduring Freedom mark premature loss. What carries across the grounds is silence and distant sound, apart from the passenger planes taking off from nearby Ronald Reagan Washington National Airport.

"I think you could, by any definition, consider it the most hallowed ground in the United States, but I think we're not even giving it credit for how hallowed, how representative, how much sacrifice and honor is there," said Micki McElya, a historian at the University of Connecticut and author of "The Politics of Mourning: Death and Honor in Arlington National Cemetery." She said the country's shifting values are represented there in all their complications, including slavery.

Supporters of the arch say it completes a plan mandated by Congress in 1925 to add a monument to the circle. "It was always supposed to be a gateway to connect D.C. to Arlington," said Jared Craig, general counsel and national vice president for Veterans for America First, which joined several veteran organizations in a brief in the lawsuit supporting the arch. Craig argued the arch honors veterans and fallen service members, and he said the project does not affect the cemetery itself and requires no changes there.

Jimi Shaughnessy, veterans program manager at the nonprofit National Parks Conservation Association, disagrees. A veteran, he visits his great-grandparents' resting place regularly. "The setting is open, it's quiet, it's peaceful, it is easy to navigate," he said, and there is space to make a transition "so I can properly acknowledge my great-grandfolks that are buried there." He said the sounds of major construction would intrude on that and on funerals, and that a tourist attraction at the arch would bring noise and distraction, along with a "looming dark shadow above me."

To the White House, the arch would serve "as a visual reminder of the noble sacrifices borne by so many American heroes throughout our 250 year history so we can enjoy our freedoms today," spokesman Davis Ingle said.

Federal judges in the District of Columbia and Virginia have temporarily blocked the project, and the court challenges are playing out. During a 10-hour hearing earlier this month at which a key planning commission approved the arch, Bart Collart spoke passionately of his son's sacrifice and asked commissioners to "do the right thing" and oppose Trump's desires. Many of the more than 100,000 public comments submitted about the project opposed it.

"Look across the road at those rows of clean, simple white headstones. Go take the time to walk amongst them," Collart told the commission during its hearing. "Show some real respect for the uniform my son died in."
