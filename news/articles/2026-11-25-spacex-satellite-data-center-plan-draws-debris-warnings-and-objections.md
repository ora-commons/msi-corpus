## FCC acknowledges petitions while dismissing environmental concerns as outside its authority

Adam Becker, an astrophysicist and author, called SpaceX’s plan to launch 1 million satellites to create data centers in space “a fantasy.” He said, “It’s not going to happen,” on Wednesday. His warnings came as the U.S. Federal Communications Commission authorized the proposed Starmind constellation; Becker said vacuum conditions limit how much heat satellites can radiate away, and he warned that possible in-orbit collisions could impact other essential satellites and trigger a cascading set of space junk collisions. The FCC acknowledged a “substantial number” of public comments, including petitions to deny SpaceX’s request, but said environmental concerns were outside its authority; the American Astronomical Society, Viasat and Amazon-owned Kuiper Systems had urged the FCC to deny the proposal.

The Trump administration gave SpaceX the regulatory go-ahead Wednesday through the FCC, which lauded its authorization as a historic first amid the artificial intelligence race. The FCC said SpaceX’s 1 million “orbital compute modules,” forming the Starmind constellation, would process massive amounts of data in orbit for use back on Earth and bring humans closer to harnessing “the full power of the sun.”

FCC Chairman Brendan Carr, whom Republican President Donald Trump appointed, said, “America is leading the world into the era of super intelligence.” He added that Wednesday’s move “opens a bold new frontier.” A lengthier FCC authorization order says SpaceX must deploy at least “one authorized space station” by 2033 and then gradually add more through 2040.

Elon Musk welcomed the decision over social media. The Associated Press described Musk as the world’s richest man and reported that he had long vowed to form vast, solar-powered data centers in space to help run artificial-intelligence tools on Earth. Scientists and industry experts have stressed enormous technical and financial obstacles, as well as environmental concerns.

Proponents of data centers in space argue that capturing the sun’s energy would ease pressure on power grids and cut demand for sprawling computing warehouses on Earth and the water needed to cool them. Space seems to offer a solution because it is cold, but it is also a vacuum that traps heat inside objects much like a thermos. Becker said this creates “basic physical limits” on otherwise radiating heat away.

Sending lots of smaller satellites could potentially carry the necessary material in combination, Becker said, but the approach would be expensive and difficult. He and others also pointed to atmospheric pollution through launches and reentries, as well as possible in-orbit collisions that could impact other essential satellites.

Becker said the project could end up with “a cascading set of space junk collisions,” with such crashes creating “a cloud of debris” in low Earth orbit. Many weather and telecommunication satellites are also found in that orbit, he noted.

In its ruling, the FCC maintained that the proposed Starmind constellation operates consistently with its orbital debris rules and that deploying the satellites “serves the public interest.” The regulator said it had received a “substantial number” of public comments leading up to its Wednesday authorization, including petitions to deny SpaceX’s request. It said it dismissed topics outside its authority, such as environmental concerns.

The American Astronomical Society, satellite communication company Viasat and Amazon-owned Kuiper Systems were among those urging the FCC to deny SpaceX’s proposed data centers in space. In a statement Wednesday, the AAS said it was “disheartened and dismayed” by the decision. The organization said placing data centers in low Earth orbit would undermine decades of federal investment in astronomical facilities and “the ability of astronomers and the public to enjoy and study the night sky.”

The AAS said the proposed constellation involves “larger (and therefore brighter) and more numerous objects,” which would make the satellites visible as “extremely bright objects for longer periods in the night sky.” Becker echoed that concern, calling the proposal “pollution of the night sky to the naked eye.”

The Associated Press reported that Viasat, Amazon, SpaceX and the FCC did not immediately respond to The Associated Press’ requests for further comment Wednesday.
