## Rama rejects resign demand, tells MEP she echoes Kremlin spokesperson

Dutch Greens group MEP Tineke Strik has accused Albanian Prime Minister Edi Rama of deploying "sneaky" practices to fast-track a €4 billion luxury resort linked to US President Donald Trump's son-in-law Jared Kushner. Her accusation came as student-led protests in Tirana entered a sixth consecutive day of violent clashes with riot police, with ministerial buildings, banks and businesses set ablaze, attacked and looted, and a police station set on fire.

Strik, who led a European Parliament fact-finding mission to Albania in June, said the Albanian government was rushing to "create facts on the ground" before being legally bound by EU environmental standards. Albania is a frontrunner in the EU enlargement process and hopes to wrap up accession talks by the end of 2027.

Rama pledged in a Sunday social-media address to "listen to the voices of young people" taking to the streets, but has refused demonstrators' demand that he resign. Asked about Strik's claims, Rama told the Guardian: "Please tell the honourable lady that the violence in Tirana is very real. Instead of imagining environmental plots that do not exist, she could help Albania by condemning the violence she appears to appreciate, in an unfortunate echo of the Kremlin's own lady, Madam Zakharova." Maria Zakharova is the spokesperson for the Russian foreign ministry.

On Monday, municipal workers could be seen removing paving stones as shopkeepers secured premises with metal sheeting following the sixth consecutive day of violent clashes in the capital. Riot police responded with rubber bullets and tear gas as they attempted to disperse the crowds. Students have vowed to continue demonstrating until the socialist leader resigns.

"If the Albanian government had taken the 'Flamingo Revolution' seriously we wouldn't be here," Strik said of the popular movement launched in response to the resort being built in ecologically sensitive areas of the Balkan state. "The students are voicing the frustration of people who feel that they haven't been listened to."

"This terrible resort idea was the match that lit this fire," said Greta, a student in Tirana, adding that the slogan chanted by young people attending demonstrations for months had been "Albania is not for sale." "Now there are other issues that are whipping up the flames. We actually really care about the state of our democracy, about living standards, about education."

The controversial project foresees construction of up to 10,000 villas in coastal areas that were environmentally protected until the government passed a contentious law allowing "strategic investors" to build in parts of the Vjosa-Narta landscape, which surrounds the last intact delta system in the Mediterranean.

In June, the European Parliament called for the legislation to be repealed by the EU candidate country, saying it posed a direct threat to environmental standards that are "non-negotiable conditions of accession." The Parliament's move came after construction workers broke ground in May, igniting protests that would spread nationwide after it was discovered that ancient dunes and forest had been destroyed to create access roads in the nature reserve.

Re-elected for a historic fourth term last year, Rama is on a mission to get the Balkan state — still one of Europe's poorest after the collapse of communism more than three decades ago — into the 27-member bloc by 2030. In his Sunday address he said: "Albanians need to think very clearly: do they want this, do they want that, or do they want the EU?"

Citing an environmental impact study whose "scientific results" Rama recently described as key to the €4bn project going ahead, Strik said: "The government has submitted an environmental package to the [EU] Commission but without repealing the law allowing for construction in the protected area … it is still trying to help realise the megalomaniacal luxury resort plans of Ivanka Trump and Jared Kushner. How? By conducting a fast-track impact assessment which, if [more comprehensively] done, could never lead to the conclusion that this construction is allowed."

Rama told a tourism forum in Brussels earlier this month that the milestone development would be a gamechanger for a nation that had been the "North Korea" of Europe before the fall of its Stalinist regime in the 1990s. "If construction goes ahead this will be decided not in the streets but by science," he told the forum. "The idea that nature will take care of itself, if we simply look at it, is preposterous and old. We are trying to come out with … a [high-end] destination where nature will be highlighted and where people will holiday, and where the Albanian people will get back what they deserve: a much stronger economy."

On 8 October, Kushner described the venture as having a single goal: "To build to the highest design and environmental standards and leave this extraordinary part of Albania greener, more biodiverse and environmentally stronger."

Private contractors, tasked with implementing the impact study, have been seen mapping, measuring and monitoring the area, prompting environmentalists, who had initially spearheaded the protests, to counter this week that "science had already spoken."

"It spoke when experts from the faculty of science at Tirana University elected to protect this incredible landscape in 2004," said Aleksander Trajce at the country's oldest conservation group, the Protection and Preservation of the Natural Environment in Albania (PPNEA). "Their decision was based on very sound scientific data. There's no way that science can tell us more. Reopening the discussion is not only disingenuous but clearly a way to justify construction that should not be happening at all." Insisting a proper environmental impact assessment would take "at least two years," he added: "You'd need several seasons, across several years, to really examine the species and biodiversity of the region."

With the violence showing no sign of abating, Albanians are angry at the EU's "lacklustre" stance, said Trajce. "If it had intervened earlier and told Rama to stop, the peaceful phase of the protest movement would not have escalated into what it is today."

Saying she had been surprised by the Albanian prime minister's enthusiasm for such a large-scale tourism complex in an area so fragile, Strik emphasised the importance of protecting unique shorelines on a continent that had learned from past mistakes. "I accuse Rama of serving his own interests and the interests of Kushner and not the interests of the environment and the Albanian people," she said. "The government clearly intends to finalise the procedures before it is legally bound by EU standards. Such sneaky and disloyal practices should be strongly criticised by the EU. If Albania seriously wants to be a reliable candidate member state, it should respect EU law and not try to circumvent it."
