## Teenager loses hand from police stun grenade amid school protests

Police fired tear gas to control crowds in Paris and other French cities on Tuesday as an estimated 256,000 people rallied nationwide in support of high school students demanding more education funding, the French Interior Ministry said. The demonstrations, which began at a Paris-region high school in mid-September and have grown into a nationwide movement, came as the latest of many protest waves that have marked President Emmanuel Macron's nearly decade-long tenure and increased pressure on the outgoing leader, who cannot run for a third consecutive term, The Associated Press reported.

The Interior Ministry estimated that 256,000 people took part in demonstrations across the country on Tuesday, including 56,000 in Paris, where small groups of protesters clashed with riot officers at a city square during a largely peaceful march through the city. Large crowds waving flags and placards gathered in Marseille, Strasbourg and other cities.

The widespread use of arrests, tear gas and riot-control weapons against teenagers is fueling defiance and angering some parents and teachers. More than two weeks of class boycotts and school blockades, and policing that has led to more than 6,100 arrests, mostly of minors, have become a flashpoint in the early days of the presidential election campaign. In the latest incident, a 15-year-old boy lost a hand Monday in an incident involving a police stun grenade. Nuñez said 715 officers had been injured.

Nuñez said the police response had been proportionate in "almost all cases" and that the protests had at times turned into riots, requiring police intervention. Prime Minister Lecornu, questioned in parliament about the protest movement on Tuesday, denounced "ultra violence" threatening schools. Responding to criticism of police tactics, he pledged that any "accidents" would be investigated and described allegations that the police are racist as a "lie." Lecornu said the government and school leaders must listen to students' concerns and find solutions.

Felix Barney, a university student, described the police response as disproportionate, saying it doesn't make "any sense to hit minors." "Riot police, batons, rubber bullets, tear gas," said Aurélien Le Coq, a lawmaker with France Unbowed, a party The Associated Press described as far-left. The party's presidential candidate is strongly backing the protests. Le Coq called the violence "absolutely unbearable." Léa Monimart, a demonstrator in the northern city of Lille, said: "We need to listen to them. They are 17 years old; they are not completely clueless, and they know what they are talking about. It's important to show that there are adults who support them."

Demonstrators have voiced a range of grievances, often centering on a lack of resources and opportunities for students in disadvantaged schools, despite the promise of France's national motto: "Liberty, Equality, Fraternity."

At the Hélène Boucher lycée, or high school, in Paris' 20th district, protesters tipped over trash cans and bicycles onto their sides to create a makeshift barricade. Like hundreds of others around the country, the school was closed. Authorities began shutting schools last week to take some steam out of the demonstrations after widespread damage to schools and mounting injuries among staff, students and police. Classes were totally or partially suspended on Tuesday at 891 schools, according to the Education Ministry.

Facing accusations that it was ignoring protesters, the ministry launched an online portal inviting high school students to anonymously describe the difficulties they face at school and the changes they want to see. "You say 'young people are the future,' so listen to them," read a handwritten placard attached to a metal barrier outside a Paris school. Students have drawn attention to rundown classrooms that lack basics such as desks and chairs, become sweltering in summer without air conditioning and, in some cases, are infested with rats. Other common complaints include staff shortages and exhausting workloads; French high school students' compulsory classroom hours are among the highest in Europe.

At Hélène Boucher, parents and other adults positioned themselves on Tuesday between protesters and police keeping watch. "They are our children. Teachers, parents, federations, we all share the same goal: the protection of children's rights, and it is a legitimate right to demonstrate and to make demands," said Emilia Sichili Suriam, who is a member of an association of parents.

The teenage protesters have struck a chord amid widespread discontent in France. Frustrations extend beyond immediate difficulties, such as fuel prices that have soared because of the Iran war, to broader concerns about constraints on public spending, rising poverty and wealth inequality under Macron, who can't run for a third consecutive term. Against that backdrop, pupils' complaints that education funding shortfalls are endangering their futures have found a sympathetic audience.

Suzanne Nijdam, president of the federation of student associations, vowed that demonstrations would continue until authorities address the complaints. "So, we really need to act now, and it's in the government's hands, to see these demands through to the end," she said. "It's a matter of political priority, a matter of prioritizing the youth and the future of our country."
