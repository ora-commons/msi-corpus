## Third-quarter growth revised up to 4.3%, the fastest in three years

The Commerce Department said Wednesday that consumer prices rose 3.8% in October from a year earlier, up from 3.6% in September. On a monthly basis, prices climbed 0.3% from September to October, down from a 0.6% increase the previous month. The annual rate rose as the monthly pace slowed, leaving elevated costs a continuing challenge for the Federal Reserve and for elected officials in Washington.

Excluding the volatile food and energy categories, core inflation accelerated to 3.3% in October from a year earlier, up from 3.1% in September. Core prices rose 0.3% from September to October for the third month in a row. The yearly core figure is now at its highest level in three years and above the Federal Reserve's 2% target, which economists said is likely to intensify concerns among Fed officials that inflation is getting worse and that further rate hikes are necessary.

Gasoline prices rose just 0.8% last month, according to the Commerce Department, after surging 9.2% in September. Food prices jumped 0.4% from September to October, the biggest monthly increase since April. The easing in energy prices did not extend to food, where monthly gains picked up.

Also on Wednesday, the government said the economy grew even faster in the July-September quarter than previously estimated, with growth revised upward to a 4.3% annual rate, the fastest in three years. Consumer spending jumped 4.2% in the third quarter, and business investment soared 16.1%, lifted by big gains in spending on equipment and intellectual property products such as software. The economy is expanding at a healthy pace, driven by strong consumer spending and surging business investment as large technology firms build out AI infrastructure.

Taken together, the October figures set up the Federal Reserve's next policy meeting on Dec. 8-9, where many officials have signaled that they support raising the Fed's short-term rate and economists expect an increase. Solid economic growth and still-high inflation suggest the Fed's key rate is not yet high enough to cool price increases.

The Fed fights inflation by lifting its key interest rate, which over time raises borrowing costs for cars, homes and credit cards. By discouraging borrowing and spending, the Fed seeks to cool the economy and inflation. Bradley Saunders, an economist at Capital Economics, a consulting firm, wrote that "Taken together, the data reinforce the Fed's latest assessment that growth in economic activity is 'strong,' meaning more must be done to bring inflation down."

The stock market fell sharply again Wednesday as worries about whether soaring AI investment will pay off continued to drag down technology stocks in particular. The broad S&P 500 index dropped 2.8%, and the tech-heavy Nasdaq tumbled 4%. [Federal Reserve Economic Data](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/SP500) recorded the S&P 500 index at 7,237.17 for Nov. 25, 2026.

A sustained drop in stock prices could cool the economy by prompting Americans who own stocks to spend less and technology companies to pull back on investment. That may temper the size of any benchmark rate hike from the Fed, whose other mandate — aside from price stability — is maximum employment.

In October, the government also reported that incomes jumped 0.7% before adjusting for inflation, a healthy gain after prices rose faster than incomes in the previous month. In another report, the number of people applying for unemployment benefits dropped 13,000 to 189,000, a very low figure by historical standards and evidence that layoffs are low and companies are mostly holding on to their workers.

Consumer spending has been resilient even as persistent inflation raises the costs of many necessities and has soured most Americans on the economy. Much of the spending is driven by upper-income households, and there are signs poorer Americans are relying more on credit to sustain their spending.
