## Climate scientists urge House members to reject Baaja permitting bill

## Environmental safeguards and project approvals remain in dispute before next week's vote

More than 120 climate scientists urged members of the House of Representatives to reject the Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act, commonly called Baaja. In an open letter, the Earth and social sciences researchers said they had “deep concern” that the bill would weaken environmental laws, lock in new fossil fuel projects without recourse and deliver only minor reductions in planet-heating emissions.

The intervention came as opposition to the sweeping permitting measure was growing. Baaja was co-written by two Democratic and two Republican senators and passed rapidly in the US Senate last week, bitterly dividing Democrats and environmentalists. The unusual 500-page measure advanced through the Senate without a hearing. Its pace increased its chances of securing needed Republican support, including Donald Trump’s, to pass, but such backing was not guaranteed if Democrats wrote a revised version from January.

The scientists wrote: “We understand the need for trade-offs and compromise when passing bipartisan legislation. But as scientists we feel strongly that the costs of this bill to the climate are too high.”

Michael Mann, a climate scientist who helped arrange the letter, called Baaja a “deeply problematic bill” that was “backed by a veritable who’s who of bad fossil fuel and dark money actors.” He said Democrats who take control of Congress in January should “scrap Baaja, which in the end only takes us further down the dangerous fossil fuel pathway, and put a sensible, popular datacenter bill on Trump’s desk and force him to veto it. And force Republicans to defend sustaining that veto.”

Timmons Roberts, a Brown University academic and letter co-signatory, said the bill was “very worrisome, it has felt very rushed and there is grave potential for damage that can’t be done any time soon.” Roberts acknowledged that the existing permitting system was broken and difficult to navigate, but said there had to be a better approach. “We are in a broken system, and it’s hard to build anything, but there has to be a better way to do this,” he said. “In some ways it feels like we are being held hostage. If you want more renewables you have to also see the lock-in of fossil fuels and the devastating gutting of core legislation that has allowed the US to improve its natural environment.”

Mann said Democratic Sen. Sheldon Whitehouse, a longtime climate-action champion and Baaja co-sponsor, had “long been one of my heroes, and nothing pains me to find him not only in support of the bill but in fact one of its principal backers.” Whitehouse responded: “I have great respect for these climate scientists, and I am sorry to disagree with them here. The important takeaways about this bill are the gigaton-scale emissions reductions, the freed-up clean power, the $10bn in annual consumer electricity savings, and the $35bn whack at datacenters.”

Baaja supporters include some Democrats, a minority of green groups and other climate scientists. They point to the laborious and sometimes terminal waits that clean-energy projects face before approval and operation, as well as a shortage of integrated power lines capable of carrying solar- or wind-generated electricity to where it is needed.

The bill would slash barriers to new transmission lines and shrink the role of statutes such as the Clean Water Act and Endangered Species Act when scrutinizing projects. Supporters say those changes would speed approvals and make projects virtually immune to being overturned by future administrations or courts. The measure is expected to unleash much of the clean energy waiting to connect to the grid, while opponents say it would also sideline legal challenges to new oil pipelines, data centers and other infrastructure, including ICE detention centers.

That prospect has raised alarm about increased fossil-fuel activity and data centers on public lands with little community or tribal input. The National Congress of American Indians said, “Congress must reject Baaja in its entirety,” adding, “Native sovereignty, sacred places, and cultural heritage are not negotiable.”

The varied coalition of opponents, including former vice president Al Gore, also argues that Trump will not faithfully execute the law. Trump, a major backer of the artificial intelligence industry, said Baaja would result in “tremendous amounts of new building” for data centers but said he would still not allow permits for new wind power, which he has called a “major loser.” In a widely viewed social media post, actor and environmental advocate Mark Ruffalo wrote: “Democrats, what is the matter with you? Don’t you know better than to trust Trump?”

Some House Democrats opposed to Baaja are trying to amend it rather than immediately kill it. They may succeed because Republicans’ already slender House hold has been largely erased, with many Republicans absent from Washington for the lame-duck session after losing in the midterm elections.

Jared Huffman, a California Democrat set to chair the House natural resources committee when Democrats take control, proposed five amendments. They would increase consultation with tribes, provide legal recourse for communities, require full reviews for data centers and end the “forever permit” provisions that allow projects to continue even if they are later shown to be destructive or unlawful.

Huffman said the amendments could draw broad bipartisan support, though he described the process as potentially a “take it or leave it” jam job. “Republicans will push for Democrats to abstain or not show up, that power play will come next week,” he said.

Huffman also said the White House, fossil fuel industry and big AI and data-center interests were thrilled with the bill and would continue seeking its provisions. “Look how thrilled the White House, the fossil fuel industry and big AI and datacenter interests are with this bill. They want what it gives and they aren’t going to stop wanting that six weeks from now,” he said.

Huffman said Democrats could negotiate better terms in the future but warned against delay. “We will be able to cut a deal on better terms then; we need to be better negotiators. This episode shows the dangers of not acting because you get something like Baaja, which is extremely destructive. If we go the next two to four years without legislating on a permitting deal, then shame on us.”

The scientists’ letter comes as the House prepares to vote on a measure that has already cleared the Senate. More than 120 Earth and social sciences researchers are on record against Baaja, while Whitehouse and other supporters defend its emissions and consumer-cost claims. Huffman’s amendments and unresolved disputes over environmental protections, infrastructure approvals, tribal consultation and future court challenges remain before lawmakers.
