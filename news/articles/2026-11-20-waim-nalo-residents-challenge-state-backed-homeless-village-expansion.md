## Waimānalo residents challenge state-backed homeless village expansion

**Subtype:** fact

- Honolulu's permitting department and the state's Office of Historical Preservation are reviewing developer plans and an archaeological monitor's report while work on Hui Mahiʻai Village has stopped.
- The state Office of Homelessness and Housing Solutions and nonprofit aio Foundation are developing 116 units for 188 people on a 14-acre Waimānalo state parcel.
- Blanche McMillan opened Hui Mahiʻai ʻĀina without official permission in 2020; the village now houses 106 formerly homeless people.
- McMillan and her supporters say the expansion would house more of the nearly 200 people identified as homeless from Kāneʻohe to Kailua to Waimānalo.
- Pono ʻOluʻolu has collected nearly 1,000 online signatures while seeking legal help to stop construction.
- Waimānalo opponents object to housing people from outside the community and to additional homeless facilities in an area that already has two emergency shelters.

## Permit and historic preservation reviews remain pending amid flooding and consultation disputes

Work on Hui Mahiʻai Village in Waimānalo has stopped in recent weeks while Honolulu's permitting department and the state's Office of Historical Preservation review plans submitted by the developers, including the report of the archaeologist required to monitor the site. Jun Yang, director of the state Office of Homelessness and Housing Solutions, called the pause a normal part of the timeline and said it was unlikely to lead to a lengthy delay. “We’re just waiting on these next steps as we work through the process,” Yang said.

The project is a joint undertaking by the state Office of Homelessness and Housing Solutions and the nonprofit aio Foundation. It would develop a 14-acre parcel owned by the state Department of Land and Natural Resources that borders Hawaiian homelands. Hui Mahiʻai ʻĀina would relocate to the Makapuʻu side of the property, where the planned village would house 188 people in 116 single, double and family units. The expansion would add water, sewer and electrical connections, a communal kitchen, a health clinic, classrooms and a garden for the larger community. McMillan would lead the expansion, and the current residents would be joined by dozens of additional homeless people.

The expansion is funded by public and philanthropic dollars. aio Foundation built a homeless village near Sand Island called Kahauiki in 2018, where about 700 people now live. The nonprofit's development arm, Mahiʻai Development, received a 65-year lease for the Waimānalo property in February. Opponents are concerned about the lease's duration and a condition in the governor's proclamation requiring the property to remain in homeless housing for at least 20 years, which they say could allow other development after year 20.

The project was fast-tracked under Gov. Josh Green's 2023 emergency proclamation on homelessness, allowing it to leapfrog certain initial zoning and permitting processes and environmental reviews. The village still had to meet health and other safety codes. “If the emergency proclamation was not in place here, the community would not have let this happen,” said Hiwahiwa Kaheaku-Rodenhurst, a Waimānalo resident and opposition leader.

Green signed the proclamation during his first State of the State speech in January 2023, launching the kauhale initiative. An annual count that month found 6,223 people homeless in Hawaiʻi, the great majority on Oʻahu. Green singled out McMillan as a homeless advocate who was “showing that it takes the embrace of a village to help people find their way to a permanent home.” He added, “Business as usual won’t work anymore,” and, “there is simply too much on the line to accept the status quo.”

By late 2024, nearly 20 kauhale had opened. The state homelessness office, then headed by Jun Yang's predecessor John Mizuno, proposed a larger village on the Waimānalo property with aio Foundation. The Department of Land and Natural Resources board unanimously approved the application in October 2024. Dawn Chang, then the department's director, said at the meeting, “I was very impressed with the model, but with this wahine as well. No one can stop her.” Special assistant Brandon Mitsuda told the board, “The governor is in full support of this project, full support of Aunty Blanche. She is the model we follow, and anything Aunty Blanche needs, we help her.”

Supporters describe the expansion as a win-win that would house more of the nearly 200 people identified as homeless from Kāneʻohe to Kailua to Waimānalo. They also say it would continue the work McMillan has won plaudits for by creating a close-knit community whose residents' lives were on the upswing as they recovered from hard times. “The goal really is to serve our community and get people off of the streets into a sustainable, healthy, stable home where they can jump off from into a new path,” Yang said.

Supporters maintain that the opposition is principally a reluctance to have a kauhale nearby. “The root cause of the opposition is they just don’t like homeless people,” said Kalaninui Akau, who grew up in Waimānalo and still lives there. Akau produced promotional videos about Hui Mahiʻai ʻĀina, most of them for free. More than half of Waimānalo's residents are Native Hawaiian. Supporters and opponents also disagree about basic facts concerning the project, along with housing people from outside Waimānalo, adding facilities in an area with two emergency shelters, flooding and other safety concerns, and protecting cultural resources and Hawaiian land rights.

At a table in the shade of a monkeypod tree, with the fluted black and green wall of the Koʻolau Range in the distance, McMillan gathered residents to talk about how they had found refuge, support and purpose in the village she created in 2020 — first for people living along the highway through town, and then for others from beyond the Windward Coast. Today, 106 formerly homeless people live in the kauhale McMillan opened without official permission and named Hui Mahiʻai ʻĀina, meaning the group farming the land.

McMillan said most residents are Native Hawaiian and said residents must be clean and sober to live there. They pay $250 a month for 8-by-10-foot, timber-framed single-room dwellings with two windows, while the newest arrivals live in tents. Mountain apple, ʻulu, avocado, ti, mangos and sweet potatoes grow in a community garden. Richard Muraoka, 64, said he turned to drugs after his son died and was living in a Kapolei park when McMillan took him in three years ago. “Without her, everybody would be lost, I’d probably be dead,” he said.

McMillan said, “What I know in my heart is to take care of the poor.” She added, “I love my people.” She said her people are Native Hawaiians, but that others would be welcome if room remained.

McMillan, 72, was one of 17 children, all raised in a small house next to the property. She said her parents cared for hungry people in town and others who were lost, lonely or in need. “One day I’m going to become what they were,” McMillan said. She also said, “Today that’s what I am. And when the pandemic happened, I knew there was time for me to walk the talk and do the work.” When not making rounds in the village, McMillan was often found sitting outside her childhood home with residents.

McMillan's voice carries without a microphone, she laughs quickly, and she frequently cites God and love as her guides. In conversation, she often starts sentences with: “You know what, let me tell you something.” There may be nobody in Waimānalo who does not know her, including those who now question her plans. She is universally referred to as Aunty Blanche.

The state land and resources department approved a community-garden project for McMillan and a local nonprofit on the property in 2015, but McMillan said that project sputtered. She switched tracks in 2020. As Covid advanced and more than 350 people were living alongside Kalanianaʻole Highway, she invited anyone who wanted to follow her to move onto the state land behind her childhood home. She asked them to help clear it of junk and brush and build homes for themselves.

The land was zoned for agricultural uses and was in a flood zone, so no building was allowed. Within a few months, more than 30 people took up McMillan's invitation. “The government, they said, ‘You have to stop,’” she said. “I said, ‘I ain’t stopping for you. I’m going to continue doing my work for the people. I don’t care what you say or what you do. I’m going to do it.’”

Opponents have formed a nonprofit, Pono ʻOluʻolu, to fight the project. It has collected nearly 1,000 online signatures and is seeking legal help to stop construction. Kaheaku-Rodenhurst and her husband, Onepookela Craig-Rodenhurst, both Hawaiian sovereignty activists, were asked by Pono ʻOluʻolu to help fight the project and are forming another nonprofit, Kupaʻa o Waimānalo, to fund and spearhead legal action. “A lot of people are being woken up,” Kaheaku-Rodenhurst said.

The dispute has spread among neighbors who have known one another since childhood. Residents who serve on McMillan's security team trailed opponents on ATVs and kept watch as opponents gathered by the roadside to plan a truck blockade. A human blockade of trucks headed to the work site has occurred. Police officers attended an October community meeting, and opponents have continued to raise objections at meetings, including their contention that rules underlying Green's proclamation were not followed.

Opponents point to a February 2024 archaeological inspection by a firm hired by Mahiʻai Development that found recent grading and clearing before the Department of Land and Natural Resources approved the project. They also cite work that began in earnest in November 2025, months before the lease was approved, to clear and backfill the property with trucked-in dirt that would elevate it above flood levels. Opponents also cite an August letter from the state homelessness office confirming certification under the proclamation as proof that the project had proceeded illegally for months. Yang said the letter was a paperwork step because the project had already been certified.

Cindy Parker was raised in the same house on ʻOluʻolu Street where she raised her family on Hawaiian homestead land bordering the project parcel. Parker said, “We grew up here, it was country.” She said monkeypod trees, koa haole, neighbors' cows and sheep and nocturnal owls once filled the view behind her home. Construction has replaced much of that landscape with a bed of earth that raised the land about 13 feet.

Parker said her property flooded during the March Kona low storms because a retaining wall prevented water from draining toward the mountains as it had. Portions of the wall have since been removed following discussions with the developer. Parker also said she had trouble breathing and blamed dust kicked up by backfill dirt. “This is not,” she said, “what we want.”

Gary Silva, who grew up in Waimānalo, said his uncles started a horse ranch about 65 years ago a quarter mile or so beyond ʻOluʻolu Street. He owns the ranch, and it also flooded during the March storms. Silva helped build many of the village's small homes when McMillan started the project but opposes its expansion. Comparing the site with a North Shore community extensively damaged during the storms, Silva said, “We’re very low here, everybody is low compared to up there. So all these kūpuna here — this is going to be an Otake Camp, you watch.”

As officialdom came to favor McMillan, Silva said she had come to enjoy her new stature among government officials too much. Silva said, “She’s trying to get all this land from the governor so she can build herself up.” He added, “It doesn’t make me feel well.” Opponents say their flooding concerns have not been fully addressed; the developer says they have. They also say the site's cultural importance is being brushed aside.

The dispute also reaches the history of land ownership in Waimānalo. After the Great Mahele of 1848, King Kamehameha III designated roughly 4,000 acres of the Waimānalo area's Crown Lands, including the planned village site behind what is now ʻOluʻolu Street. The lands were seized in the illegal 1893 overthrow of the Hawaiian Kingdom and eventually transferred to the U.S. government. When Hawaiʻi became a state in 1959, the land was transferred again to the state Department of Land and Natural Resources. Under the laws governing Hawaiʻi's admission as a state, public-trust lands are to be used for “the betterment of the conditions of Native Hawaiians,” among other conditions. That history has shaped many opponents' view that the project must account for Hawaiian homelands and cultural sites.

Silva said the expansion would disturb nearby cultural sites. He identified a cave called Ke Ana Luawai, just mauka of the project, as the most important. The cave is surrounded by lāʻau lapaʻau, or medicinal plants, and contains a freshwater spring that Silva said centuries ago sustained wounded Hawaiian warriors seeking respite from battle. “It's super sacred ground,” Kaheaku-Rodenhurst said. “You do not desecrate it. You do not disrespect it.”

Yang said he first learned about the cave at the October 6 community meeting. “I am interested if these are something of significance, we'd love to be able to see if we can find a way to preserve them,” he said.

A report from ASM Affiliates, a California-based cultural-resource management firm, said the firm had reviewed literature about the site and inspected three acres of its surface in February 2024. The report described the Waimānalo area as a historic landing site and occasional battleground for Hawaiians from neighboring islands but said the firm had “identified no historic properties within the project area.” Opponents say the review was inadequate. “It's just the surface. They're just kind of kicking rocks around,” said Mialisa Otis, who lives on Waimānalo's backroads and has led efforts to block trucks carrying backfill onto the property.

The August certification letter said the state historic-preservation department requested ongoing archaeological monitoring because it lacked enough information to determine what might be below the surface. Yang said an archaeologist monitor had been on site and had submitted a report that the state was reviewing.

Consultation remained a point of disagreement. At the October 2024 Land and Natural Resources board meeting, Kimeona Kane said the community had not been adequately involved and told the board, “As much as we love and really appreciate what Aunty Blanche is doing, there has been very little to no community involvement with this new expansion.” Kane said, “Aunty Blanche is a respected elder and has been for many years.” He said he told Mizuno that Waimānalo residents had not been adequately consulted and that Mizuno promised to remove the application from the agenda, but did not. After first agreeing to discuss the matter, Mizuno declined to talk with Civil Beat.

Kane said the expansion had departed from McMillan's original intent of helping people from Waimānalo and had been forced onto the community, causing “significant chaos.” He also said, “There’s been a lot of generational and intergenerational infighting,” adding that the conflict had been harmful because people were attacking personal beliefs.

Board member Wesley “Kaiwi” Yoon asked Kane whether the project might face complications: “Do you anticipate any hihia with this initiative going forward?” Kane said some concerns about the original village had not been addressed, so the expansion was likely to face opposition despite a local desire for “supporting the need and effort to address the issue of homelessness in our community.” He said: “I anticipate that there will be some concerns shared in and around the area. However … there is still a beautiful love that Aunty Blanche has brought to this particular topic and issue within our community. So it's hard to tell at this point what it's going to look like.”

Opponents acknowledge that only two of them attended a November 13, 2024 meeting at Hui Mahiʻai ʻĀina, a month after the application was approved. They say a second meeting, held on the property before the November 15, 2025 groundbreaking, came too late for meaningful input. Supporters cite the limited opposition at those meetings as evidence that significant opposition emerged only more recently.

Rep. Lisa Marten, whose district includes Waimānalo, described the 2025 gathering as “a large event.” She said: “It was in the news. Nothing. No comment. Nobody was upset. No one was saying, ‘Oh, why is this project here?’” Marten supports the project but did not initiate it and attended the groundbreaking. She said she had spent hours correcting information from both sides, including McMillan's statement to HNN that she wanted to house up to 1,000 people and opponents' social-media posts showing condominium towers on the site. Opponents name Marten among the officials who have limited their input.

At a March meeting on the property to discuss flooding, drainage and security, Otis said Marten told people like her who did not live on ʻOluʻolu Street that they could not participate. Marten remembered things differently. Otis had invited her to the March meeting at Parker's residence, along with other issues, Marten said. She then initiated another meeting the following month with immediate neighbors and the project's engineer and developer. Otis and others arrived uninvited at the second meeting but stayed and participated.

“I wanted to immediately figure out solutions to those solvable problems,” Marten said. “I didn't want it to be about other people's issues with the project that were not directly impacted by those drainage and security issues.” Marten said the second meeting led to security fences between the site and neighboring properties in response to worries that future kauhale residents would enter neighbors' yards uninvited and to the engineer's agreement to new drainage measures.

At another meeting later that month at Waimānalo Beach Park, Otis said McMillan tried to overwhelm opponents by bringing along a crowd of more than 60 Hui Mahiʻai ʻĀina residents. As chair of the House Committee on Human Services and Homelessness, Marten said the pushback grew only when the opposition broadened its complaints from flooding and security to encroaching on Hawaiian homelands and cultural and environmental concerns. “It became a social media thing with just a lot of propaganda and misinformation,” she said. “That's when a lot of people across the community who don't even live nearby, even in other communities who don't even live in Waimānalo, became very concerned about it because it was framed as taking away Hawaiian rights.”

Marten said residents had unrealistic expectations about their control over land-use decisions. “The community wishes they could choose what happens with every plot of land,” she said. “But they don't get to.”

The October 6 meeting took place at the Waimānalo Hawaiian Homestead Association hālau, a pitch-roofed pavilion open to the air on all sides where gatherings more often mark weddings, birthdays or graduations. Sponsored by Green's office, it had been promoted as a town hall to answer questions about the project. Yang and Duane Kurisu, a prominent businessman who is president of aio Foundation and leader of Mahiʻai Development, described the project to Parker, Otis, Kaheaku-Rodenhurst and other opponents. Police officers were present.

Kumu Hinaleimoana Wong, a longtime community leader asked by the governor's office to facilitate the meeting, opened it with a pule. As Wong finished, a group in the audience held signs reading “Betrayers,” “Shame” and “Judas,” and “Pilau,” “Kapulu” and “Haumia Nui,” or corrupt, desecrating and filthy. Onepookela Craig-Rodenhurst began chanting in a staccato kepakepa style to convey, he later said, his ancestors' argument against violating sacred land and to call for its preservation. “If more development happens, the more of our history gets lost and then everything is just in the books,” he said.

During the meeting, Wong suggested that Hawaiʻi's history of conquest and imperialism could leave Native Hawaiians susceptible to division. One opponent called out, “It's not about Hawaiians having a difference. It's about how it all began.” Wong, an artist, educator and political and cultural activist, then explained her role as a neutral facilitator and received more applause. She was a candidate at the time for Oʻahu resident trustee for the Office of Hawaiian Affairs. Someone asked, “Are you Hawaiian with us, or are you Hawaiian with them?” Wong responded, “Let's be clear. No matter how any kanaka feels. Support. No support. That's not who I'm being tonight. I came because even when you talk any kine to me, I'm still gonna tell you I love you.”

Kurisu told opponents, “You know what? I'm not kanaka. But we're building it for you.” His statement drew both derision and support.

Nearly three hours later, as officials tried to close the meeting, Waimānalo Neighborhood Board vice chair Kūʻikeokalani Kamakea-Ohelo asked about historic land rights. “The land … is, in fact, Crown lands and it's deemed Crown lands because of our kingdom,” Kamakea-Ohelo said. He asked whether the land could be transferred to the Department of Hawaiian Homelands in the future so “we can ensure that kānaka are taken care of in compliance with 1959 Admissions Act?” Scattered applause followed, but nobody answered.

As the meeting ended, Wong described experiences Native Hawaiians have shared and asked participants to aloha one another. “The divide can be made smaller by reminding ourselves we come from the same set of genealogies. We're the survivors. We're all the survivors after the collision with foreigners,” she said. “We're the ones that never succumb to the diseases. And we're still hanging on despite all of the injustices to the community. So I ask all of you, no matter where you stood tonight: Please aloha one another.” Wong later said she understood the opposition's anger. “Anything encroaching upon what little is left, anytime you have anything that's going to serve beyond Hawaiian interests in a community that has endured and survived historical and generational trauma,” she said, “this is the reaction that you're going to get.”

Before the meeting began, McMillan stood before a crowd spilling outside and said, “If I have done anything wrong, I apologize.” Many in the audience applauded. She later made clear that her apology was not because she had done anything wrong. “The Lord had told me forgive the people, what they’re doing to us, to all of us in Waimānalo,” she said. “So I needed to let the whole Waimānalo community know who I was. And I made sure that I forgive each and every one of them.”

Civil Beat reported that McMillan was tired of the controversy. “What the governor says, it goes. It doesn't matter. He's doing the right thing. He's taking care of the people.” She said opponents had judged the project without knowing what she and the village would do. “It makes me sick because we're Hawaiian. And it's unreal what they do. But you know what? I don't pay attention. I just do what I have to do,” she said. “You should put your two hands together and your mouth and your brains into this property and help me out.”

Kane said only legal action could slow or halt the project. “Not even being able to take a pause right on this and just help figure it out, that has been crazy to me,” he said. Opponents said they would continue researching the land's history, posting their case on social media and returning to the streets if necessary. “When my friends talk to me, what they tell me is, ‘You guys going lose. You're going up against millionaires and the state,’” Parker said, referring to Kurisu, who heads the development company. “What I always tell everybody that tells me that is, ‘Well, we're going to give it our best shot and we're going to do our damnedest to stop it.’” Work remains stopped while the permitting and historic-preservation reviews are pending.
