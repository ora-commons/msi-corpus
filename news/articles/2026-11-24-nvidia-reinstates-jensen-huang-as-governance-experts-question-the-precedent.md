## Workplace policy and succession planning remain unresolved after the reversal

Nvidia reinstated Jensen Huang as chief executive on Monday, restoring him to the company after he resigned following two extramarital affairs. One affair involved a subordinate; the other involved a customer whom U.S. officials believed was working with Chinese intelligence. Huang’s return followed advocacy from President Trump, pleas from Nvidia employees and a selloff that erased more than $2 trillion in the company’s market capitalization.

Nvidia employees celebrated Huang’s return, chanting “Jen-sen.” Surrounded by employees on Monday, Huang said, “I’m going to be a better CEO the second time around.” Business leaders who supported him also welcomed the decision. Bill Ackman, chief executive of Pershing Square, wrote on X, “The wisdom of the crowd prevails.” Elon Musk wrote, “The mistake has been corrected.” Sam Altman, whose own ouster as OpenAI’s chief executive three years ago ended in a return days later, posted an upbeat “Jensen!”

The broader governance question drew immediate attention from corporate-governance specialists. Former Baxter International CEO Harry Kraemer Jr., who now teaches management at Northwestern University’s business school, said he quickly began receiving texts and calls from former colleagues. He said he kept returning to one question: “What is the message that’s sent to every other CEO in the U.S.?”

Admitted missteps and allegations of misconduct have toppled executives at public companies and startups. McDonald’s Steve Easterbrook, BP’s Bernard Looney and Norfolk Southern’s Alan Shaw lost their jobs over relationships with colleagues. Astronomer CEO Andy Byron resigned after he was caught on the Coldplay kiss-cam with another company executive.

Margaret O’Mara, a historian of the technology industry at the University of Washington, said Silicon Valley has long had a tension between rules intended to protect workers and an entrenched idea of the “brilliant, disruptive leader.” “If there’s a tone set at the top of tolerating certain actions, managerial behavior, that’s the culture of the company,” she said. O’Mara said Huang’s decision to press his case, rather than withdraw, may set a precedent for other boards.

The case also turned on what leadership experts described as Huang’s irreplaceability. He had dozens of direct reports, no obvious successor and a deep relationship with President Trump. He led Nvidia, which acted as the cornerstone of the global artificial-intelligence transition. Leadership experts said his departure and return were a warning to boards not to let one executive become indispensable.

Charles Elson, a former board member at Sunbeam Corp., AutoZone and other companies and the founding director of the University of Delaware’s Weinberg Center for Corporate Governance, said the reinstatement makes a mockery of company policy. “You can’t have one person subject to the policy and someone else not—they’re all employees,” he said. Elson framed the episode as a question of sound management, saying someone will always argue that a “genius” needs to stay, then asking what happens if that person dies, falls ill or is no longer a genius. “It is really poor, in my view, business management,” he said. Nvidia did not immediately reply to a request for comment.

Board leaders and corporate advisers said a leader’s ability to hold onto a job reflects the distinctive circumstances of each case and urged caution against extrapolating a new normal in governance from Huang’s return. Irina Wolpert, who leads the Americas FinTech practice at Egon Zehnder, said employees watch what companies reward, tolerate and hold accountable. “When a uniquely consequential leader is treated differently, the board has to be particularly clear about the principles behind that decision and how those principles connect to the culture it wants to sustain,” she said.

Peter Gleason, chief executive of the National Association of Corporate Directors, said the situation harks back to 2008 with “too big to fail” parallels. Nvidia’s stock affects not only the U.S. stock market but the global economy, he said. Gleason said board directors need to maintain independence, founder-led chief executives are rare among public companies, and succession planning is one of a board’s top responsibilities. Not having a succession plan, he said, is itself a crisis. “I think this is going to be a case study for years to come,” he said.
