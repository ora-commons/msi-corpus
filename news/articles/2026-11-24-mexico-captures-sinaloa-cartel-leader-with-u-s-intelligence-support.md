## Three captured lieutenants could be as consequential as Meza Flores

Mexican special-operations troops, working with U.S. intelligence, captured Fausto Isidro Meza Flores, known as “El Chapo Isidro,” and 15 other members of his organization after a three-hour battle in the northern Sinaloan city of Guasave. The Wall Street Journal reported that the fighting terrorized residents. Four soldiers were killed, 10 were wounded and 16 alleged gunmen died.

Defense Minister Gen. Ricardo Trevilla said the operation also captured three of Meza Flores's top lieutenants, including the cartel group's second-in-command and its financial and logistics chiefs. Vanda Felbab-Brown, a security expert at the Brookings Institution who closely tracks Mexico, said the capture of those lieutenants could prove as consequential as Meza Flores's arrest. Her assessment depended on whether the three were responsible for running key parts of the criminal enterprise, including its finances and logistics, as is believed, or had served mainly as security personnel.

In parts of northern Mexico, U.S. and Mexican authorities have increasingly focused on dismantling the operational infrastructure of criminal groups, targeting not only local bosses but also financiers, money launderers, logistics operators and security chiefs. Felbab-Brown said that approach has the potential to cause more lasting damage.

Mexico's military had accelerated efforts to gather intelligence on Meza Flores and capture him since the beginning of the year, Trevilla said. The effort intensified three months ago, when the Mexican military began a significant intelligence exchange with the U.S. government, according to Trevilla.

Mexican military intelligence located Meza Flores in Guasave, Trevilla said. Mexico requested U.S. assistance in using drones to pinpoint his whereabouts a week before the operation, and his precise location was established Friday, he said.

The strike force assembled for the assault included about 371 soldiers and 14 aircraft, including helicopters, gunships and drones, Trevilla said. Some soldiers belonged to the elite special-operations army unit known as the Bats, while others were part of the National Guard special-operations unit known as the Wolves. About 22,000 Mexican troops were stationed in Sinaloa, allowing the military to assemble a large force on short notice, Trevilla said.

At about 8 p.m. Friday, a first detachment of special-operations soldiers entered the house where Meza Flores was staying. A video of the operation shows a small group of troops entering the large house and checking rooms. “Forward, forward,” a soldier shouts in the video. The troops made a man lie on the floor before bringing out Meza Flores, who was wearing a black T-shirt.

Meza Flores and his top lieutenants surrendered without a fight. Gunmen then rushed to try to rescue Meza Flores, firing .50-caliber machine guns and Barrett rifles at the helicopters circling overhead. The aircraft returned fire against the gunmen, some of whom were positioned in homemade armored vehicles reinforced with steel plates and known as monsters.

“This operation showed once again the strength of the Mexican state,” Trevilla said. He said the mission had weakened a criminal organization without harming civilians.

Meza Flores, 44, had led remnants of the Beltrán Leyva faction of the Sinaloa cartel since 2009, Trevilla said. Before his capture, he had extended his influence across 12 of Sinaloa's 20 municipalities and controlled two key drug-smuggling routes into the United States, one by land and one by sea, particularly through Arizona.

Meza Flores was on the Federal Bureau of Investigation's 10 Most Wanted list, which offered a $5 million reward for information leading to his arrest or conviction. Mexico's top security official, Omar García Harfuch, said Meza Flores was wanted in Mexico for participating in organized crime and drug trafficking. García Harfuch also said the United States wanted to extradite him for trafficking drugs, particularly fentanyl.

The Wall Street Journal described the Sinaloa cartel as Mexico's largest fentanyl producer and smuggler. For the past two years, the organization has been torn apart by a bloody war between its main factions, led by the sons of its founders, Ismael “El Mayo” Zambada and Joaquín “El Chapo” Guzmán. More than 7,000 people have been killed or gone missing in the conflict, which showed no signs of stopping.

Meza Flores had largely kept northern Sinaloa peaceful while expanding his territory and influence. John Creamer, a former senior U.S. diplomat who was stationed in Mexico until 2021, said Mexico was having more successes but that they were tactical successes. “You are having many more successes, but they are tactical successes,” Creamer said. “The Mexicans are moving much more quickly and at a much larger scale.”

The Wall Street Journal said the operation highlighted the limits of a strategy that had done little to dismantle the political and criminal networks that allow cartels to flourish. The report said the cartels' power rests on more than their armies of gunmen and trafficking networks.

The Journal cited analysts who said that in Sinaloa, the cartels' influence had penetrated government and other institutions, operating with the protection or acquiescence of public officials. The Sinaloa cartel was also woven into the fabric of daily life, employing thousands of people, sustaining parts of the local economy and, in some communities, being treated as a benefactor or hero.

The extent of that penetration became apparent in April, when the United States indicted Gov. Rubén Rocha Moya and nine other current and former senior Sinaloa officials on charges of accepting bribes from the Chapitos faction. The United States requested extradition of the accused officials, who remained free. President Claudia Sheinbaum said the evidence against them was not convincing.

At President Claudia Sheinbaum's news conference Monday, Mexico's top security official, Omar García Harfuch, said Meza Flores had acted with impunity because some authorities had allowed themselves to become corrupt while others had omitted needed action. “They acted with impunity because there were authorities, which let themselves become corrupt and others which simply, by omission, let them continue to do damage for many years,” García Harfuch said.

David Saucedo, a Mexican security consultant, said Meza Flores's removal could open a new front in the conflict as lieutenants fought for control of his organization and rival cartel leaders moved to seize his territory. “There will probably be a war between different factions of the Guasave cartel,” Saucedo said. Saucedo said Meza Flores probably had not made arrangements for a successor.

Meza Flores had largely allied himself with the Mayitos faction, led by Ismael “Mayito Flaco” Zambada, and had driven the rival Chapitos from much of northern Sinaloa, extending his influence to areas just outside the state capital of Culiacán. His capture could give the Chapitos — run by the sons of “El Chapo” Guzmán, who is imprisoned in the United States — an opportunity to reclaim territory or allow other criminal groups to move in. Saucedo said the Guasave organization still had the infrastructure to survive its leader's capture, including laboratories and networks of political contacts.

Across much of the rest of Mexico, security forces continue to rely heavily on capturing high-profile cartel leaders. Felbab-Brown said the strategy has often produced internal power struggles and new waves of violence without significantly disrupting criminal operations. “They just find a new boss, a more violent boss, a less competent boss, but one that can keep things ticking along,” she said.

Trevilla said the U.S. intelligence exchange helped pinpoint Meza Flores's location and complemented information already gathered by Mexican military intelligence. He said Mexico's cooperation with the United States followed principles of reciprocity and mutual trust while respecting Mexican sovereignty. “Coordination without subordination,” Trevilla said. “I appreciate the great support they gave us.”
