## IAEA chief says annex externally complete by late October, reactor likely active

Satellite imagery captured Nov. 6 shows a new extension attached to the main enrichment hall at North Korea's Yongbyon nuclear complex, according to The Wall Street Journal's analysis. Grossi, director general of the International Atomic Energy Agency, the United Nations' nuclear watchdog, said the annex had been under construction since mid-September and was externally complete by late October. The imagery and the IAEA's account leave the annex's function unresolved.

The Wall Street Journal characterized the expansion as “the latest sign of Pyongyang's continued expansion of a weapons program that has alarmed neighbors and drawn international condemnation.”

Sam Lair, a fellow at the Foreign Policy Research Institute who specializes in nuclear and missile programs and first identified the construction at Yongbyon, said the annex could provide more administrative space, accommodate new control equipment or support upgrades to existing centrifuges.

Alternatively, the annex could house new centrifuges, Lair said. If it does, its size suggests the centrifuges could produce more than 35 additional kilograms of highly enriched uranium a year. North Korea has had the capacity to produce up to 375 kilograms of highly enriched uranium annually, according to estimations from Vertic, a London-based nonprofit that helps governments implement and verify international arms-control agreements.

Grossi said the separate enrichment facility, the new construction at the original facility and ongoing enrichment work at Yongbyon and another complex just outside the capital were all “a cause for serious concern.” He also said Yongbyon's five-megawatt plutonium production reactor likely remains active. The Journal described that assessment as a sign Pyongyang was still producing both kinds of fuels for atomic bombs.

The annex's role remains undetermined. The imagery shows an attached extension and Grossi supplied a construction timeline, but the available evidence does not establish whether the space contains new centrifuges or instead provides administrative space, accommodates control equipment or supports upgrades to existing centrifuges.

The visible sequence this year runs from a new enrichment facility reported in June to an annex under construction since mid-September and externally complete by late October, with enrichment work continuing at Yongbyon and another complex just outside the capital. Vertic estimated the June facility could boost North Korea's annual production capacity by 160 kilograms — an increase of 75% at the time. Last spring, Kim inspected that plant, posed before rows of centrifuges and urging his scientists to increase production of weapons-grade material.

The Journal wrote, “Kim's expanding nuclear arsenal has given the 42-year-old dictator the confidence to ignore repeated overtures from President Trump to meet.” During a tour of Asia that week, Trump floated the possibility of a meeting with Kim, but no summit materialized.

Trump, Chinese leader Xi Jinping and Russian President Vladimir Putin said Thursday that they had agreed to open a dialogue on reducing nuclear risks and preventing nuclear proliferation. The statement offered no concrete next steps and made no mention of North Korea.
