## Allegations and security concerns accompanied Huang's return

President Donald Trump told The Times that he thought Nvidia founder Jensen Huang had been treated unfairly because the sexual nature of his scandal attracted attention. Trump called the sexual aspect “The Payton Place part,” which The Times explained as an evident reference to a 1960s soap opera featuring many sex scandals. “Without that, it would have been a lot easier,” Trump said, “because that got the attention. You know, sex was involved, and sex sells better than stocks.” Donegan said sex was one reason Trump had taken a particular interest in Huang’s case.

Trump separately posted his stated rationale on Truth Social: “The Country needs Jensen’s leadership at NVIDIA in order to ensure the Success and Prosperity of not only the Company but the American Economy.” Nvidia’s board reinstated Huang as CEO on Monday after a pressure campaign from Trump, Vice President JD Vance and other members of the Trump administration. Donegan described Huang as a technology founder and close ally of Trump. Huang had recently been ousted from Nvidia, the chip manufacturer he founded. His fall from power lasted less than two weeks.

According to early reporting cited by Donegan, Trump decided late the previous week to intervene because he thought restoring Huang as Nvidia’s chief could help rally a sluggish stock market. Donegan wrote that the reinstatement was reportedly orchestrated by Vance, whom she identified as the vice president and presumptive 2028 Republican nominee. Vance drew on his deep ties to friends, allies and benefactors in Silicon Valley to convey the administration’s strong desire for Huang to return. Donegan called the episode “a remarkable intervention of the executive branch into the personnel decisions of a private firm.”

Donegan described Nvidia, which makes the chips that enable AI, as a “load-bearing firm” propping up an AI sector that she said was increasingly understood to be in an overvaluation bubble that would inevitably pop. She said the US economy had become increasingly dependent on the success of AI companies.

Donegan said Huang had been accused of berating and mistreating employees, sleeping with subordinates and seeming to carry on what she called a staggering number of extramarital affairs. She wrote that US intelligence had identified one of Huang’s alleged extramarital partners as a likely Chinese spy who helped smuggle Nvidia’s product to China. Donegan said Huang’s alleged actions had risked national security.

Donegan said Huang’s allies and boosters repeatedly emphasized in public that he was personally essential to the company and, by extension, the economy, while minimizing national-security and chip-smuggling concerns. Their defense, she wrote, cast him as a singular and irreplaceable mind. In her account, that reasoning treated it as sensible if Huang could “spill secrets to China over pillow talk at the expense of your retirement fund.”

Donegan contrasted the belief in the irreplaceable brilliance of one AI CEO with what she called the ethos of an industry promising to automate away all human judgment and experience and make human beings in general — let alone any human being in particular — profoundly replaceable, if not completely superfluous. She suggested technology executives did not expect themselves to become disposable and interchangeable, a fate she reserved for what she called “the lowly, like you and me.”

Donegan wrote that the “shocking arrogance and display of elite impunity among the tech sector” has disgusted Americans and shocked their consciences. She said she thought the deep and emphatic rejection of datacenters expressed by voters in the recent midterm elections should be understood as a proxy for broader discontent with what she called the recklessness and greed of tech oligarchs who impose their whims on the country with life-altering consequences.

Donegan contrasted Huang’s outcome with what she wrote would probably happen if “you or I” acted as Huang had been accused of acting: “we probably would find ourselves out of work and out of favor for quite some time.” She used the contrast to suggest that rules differed for rich people, a pattern she called “elite impunity.”

Donegan asked why the Trump administration, which she said claimed that national security and the US economy rely on the returns of AI companies, would want one of the most important of those companies helmed by a man who may have been compromised by China. She described China as the nation’s primary adversary and main economic competitor.

Donegan cited the “flagrancy of the seeming corruption and incompetence” and the “evident rage of the American people.” She said she hoped the combination would spur Democrats to launch “serious and probing investigations” into Nvidia, Huang and their ties to the Trump administration when the party takes control of both houses of Congress in January.

Donegan called Trump’s response “a kind of sex-pest solidarity” and described the president as a “habitual adulterer.” She wrote that Trump had been accused of sexual misconduct by more than two dozen women and had paid an adult film star to keep quiet about an alleged affair; Trump denied the allegations. Donegan said Trump seemed to see something of himself in Huang, whom she described as “prolifically and allegedly perhaps treasonably adulterous.” She said “elite impunity” could gain additional appeal when it also provided male impunity.

Trump also used Truth Social to post an AI-generated image of Huang in a pose mimicking a famous photograph of Trump rising in the moments after an assassination attempt in Butler, Pennsylvania, in the summer of 2024. Donegan described the original photograph as showing Trump holding up a fist in triumph after surviving the gunman’s shots; the altered image posted on Monday morning showed Huang holding up a red bra. The Guardian credited the column’s accompanying photograph to Ann Wang/Reuters. Moira Donegan is a Guardian US columnist.
