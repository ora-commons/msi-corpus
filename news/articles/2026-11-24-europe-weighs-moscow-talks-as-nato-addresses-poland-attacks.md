## Tisdall urges joint European-US demands on Russian withdrawal

The Western pro-Ukraine coalition of the willing was ready for direct talks with the Kremlin after meeting in Berlin the previous week, Guardian foreign affairs commentator Simon Tisdall reported in a November 24 opinion commentary. The coalition was prepared to discuss Europe's wider post-Soviet security architecture, which Tisdall described as Vladimir Putin's longstanding and contentious demand. Andy Burnham said, “Europe has a clear and consistent and united negotiating position.”

The coalition condemned Russian aggression and pledged increased financial and material support for Kyiv, Tisdall wrote. He nevertheless argued that European backing could tolerate the war's continuation, intensification and widening, a pattern he called “collective western complacency.” European leaders, according to the commentary, were fully awake to the hazards. Tisdall said agreement that the war should end immediately did not amount to concrete steps to end it.

The European Union — principally represented in this instance by French President Emmanuel Macron, German Chancellor Friedrich Merz and the leaders of Italy and Poland — had struggled to agree with the European Commission over who should lead negotiations, Tisdall reported. He argued that Kaja Kallas, the European Union's foreign policy chief, should take the role, while noting Russia's claim that her appointment would collapse any future talks. Tisdall said Europe should act in close coordination with Kyiv and speak with one voice, and that the Kremlin could not be allowed a veto over who conducted the talks if a proposed delegation, including a UK representative, traveled to Moscow in the coming days.

Tisdall said lasting diplomatic success required a clear prior understanding between Europe and the Trump administration over red lines, what each side wanted and what could and could not be conceded. He called Trump “a liability” and wrote that the president “speaks without thinking” and “lacks strategic grasp.” Tisdall also wrote that Trump showed no loyalty to allies or to the values of liberty, democracy, law and justice.

During and after the November 19 trilateral summit at Kylin Villa in Shenzhen with China's President Xi Jinping and Putin, Trump floated what Tisdall called a “vague grand bargain” with Russia over Europe-wide security. The commentary said Trump failed to object to ongoing targeting of Ukrainian civilians, called Putin a “friend” whom he was proud to publicly embrace and had eased U.S. oil sanctions. Tisdall described Putin as an indicted war criminal.

For any talks, Tisdall said the minimum demands backed jointly by Europe and the United States should include a full Russian withdrawal from Ukraine, NATO-backed security guarantees, reparations and an independent international investigation into human rights abuses in the occupied Donbas. There could be no backsliding, he wrote. Tisdall said trying to win Trump over personally was pointless, pointing instead to other American politicians who understood U.S. interests better.

Tisdall said Democrats would soon control both chambers of Congress and oversee government funding, while leading Republicans, including Vice President JD Vance, were distancing themselves from Trump. He proposed a concerted diplomatic effort in Washington by Burnham, Macron, Merz and the European Commission, arguing that it could encourage a more realistic, less self-defeating U.S. policy toward Russia and revive the Western policy consensus. He also noted that capable diplomats remained in the State Department, although Secretary of State Marco Rubio was not among them.

NATO met in an emergency session Monday to discuss the attacks in Poland. Tisdall warned that retaliatory, proportionate NATO military actions against Russia would become “inescapable” if Western democracies jointly failed to coordinate their diplomacy, describing such actions as “offensive” and “intrinsically hazardous.” Tisdall argued that Putin was “his own worst enemy,” assessing that the Trump era represented a diplomatic opportunity for Putin that would not recur. He said Putin was squandering a chance to cut a face-saving deal and secure what he would see as his legacy. Tisdall wrote that Putin “seems doomed to go down fighting” and argued that NATO should ensure that outcome if Putin refused to compromise.

Tisdall invoked British Foreign Secretary Edward Grey's 1914 warning — “the lamps are going out all over Europe” — to compare the widening conflict with the approach of a broader European war.

The commentary placed the proposed talks against a series of attacks stretching from the Mediterranean to eastern Europe, the Baltic and the high north. Tisdall described the outcome of Ukraine's weekend drone attack on a new Russian warship docked in the Libyan port of Benghazi as the vessel's sinking. He placed the ship more than 1,300 miles (2,200 km) from Kyiv; the commentary said a satellite image from November 22 showed smoke rising from it, and Ukraine claimed responsibility for the strike the following day. Tisdall said the operation showed the conflict widening.

Tisdall linked what he called the vessel's sinking to a similar incident in the Baltic the previous week that crippled a Russian cargo ship, and to earlier Ukrainian attacks on oil tankers and Russian navy ships and bases in the Black Sea region. The commentary said Russia was blamed for a missile and drone strike on NATO member Poland, which Polish Prime Minister Donald Tusk said was intentional, and for deliberately targeting Western diplomatic compounds in Kyiv. It said Moscow's winter bombing of Ukrainian civilians and energy infrastructure continued while Russian sabotage, arson and cyberattacks were becoming daily occurrences across Europe.

After almost five years of continual fighting, Tisdall said tens of thousands of Ukrainian citizens and more than 400,000 Russian soldiers had died in the war Vladimir Putin launched in 2022. He described numerous U.S. efforts to negotiate a peaceful end as difficult and protracted and characterized many of the efforts as “half-hearted” and “half-baked.” Tisdall said ending the war had come to seem less urgent, and even less of a priority, to many Europeans and Americans, and called that development dangerous. He warned that continued escalation risked what he called “an existential conflict for all concerned” and argued that the human, economic and geopolitical damage already caused was smaller than what could follow if the conflict became global.
