## Studied centers' collective use equated to about 25.7 million metric tons of carbon dioxide

The hyperscale data centers studied by Harvard University researchers used almost as much electricity collectively over a year as the state of Michigan. The data centers studied consumed between 68 and 99 terawatt hours annually, according to the report.

The report said Michigan, Indiana and Tennessee, which it described as cold and energy intensive, each consumed about 100 terawatt hours. The Harvard study put the data centers' collective electricity use at 1.8% of total U.S. electricity consumption during the period the researchers reviewed. It equated that use with about 25.7 million metric tons of carbon dioxide released.

The Wall Street Journal's Business Resilience newsletter, renamed from WSJ Pro Sustainable Business, carried the report and said the report shows how the environmental impact of data centers is escalating. The newsletter said its mission remained unchanged and that it would continue covering business strategies for sustainability and resilience in the face of climate and other risks. It reported that increased attention to hyperscalers' energy demands was heightening pressure on the technology industry to be more transparent and that lawmakers and regulators were looking to monitor the industry's greenhouse gas emissions. Government officials and members of the public were calling on hyperscalers to reveal specifics about the environmental impact of the data centers that power artificial intelligence, along with their plans to limit greenhouse gas emissions.

Democrats who will soon be in the majority in Congress are poised to investigate data-center practices starting next year and are eyeing potential new restrictions, the newsletter reported.

In a September report, Pew Research Center found that Americans were growing more concerned about data centers and that 54% thought data centers mostly hurt the environment.

On the supply side of the power question, the newsletter reported a U.S. push to put nuclear reactors in ports and on cargo ships. Asante said officials had made themselves surprisingly available as the project moved forward. "They're texts or calls away from CEOs like myself," Asante said, speaking from his company's warehouselike hub at the California port. From his headquarters, he could see the nonfueled reactor, which the newsletter described as just a few feet wide and looking something like a rocket.

Support for Asante's project is part of a recently signed agreement between the Port of Long Beach and the U.S. Maritime Administration to help the port set up carbon-free offshore nuclear power. The newsletter described the agreement as one of many deals in a nationwide effort to boost nuclear power's role in shipping and as part of a broader push to advance nuclear power generally. The maritime drive aims to use offshore nuclear power to run ports across the country and fuel a fleet of privately owned cargo vessels.
