## Prosecutors' life request denied as Merchant plans to appeal

Asif Merchant, a 48-year-old Pakistani businessman, received a little more than 27 years in prison on Tuesday after his conviction earlier this year for murder for hire and attempted terrorism arising from an alleged plot to assassinate senior U.S. officials. U.S. District Judge Eric Komitee, a Trump appointee, denied prosecutors' request for life in prison. Trial testimony identified then-candidate and now-President Donald Trump, then-President Joe Biden and then-candidate and former U.N. Ambassador Nikki Haley as possible targets.

“Not many charges … are more serious than the contemplated, targeted killing of a U.S. president or other senior government official,” Komitee said.

Merchant shifted in his chair but otherwise showed no visible reaction, and he declined to speak at sentencing. One of his lawyers, Avraham Moskowitz, said afterward that Merchant planned to appeal and was grateful that the judge rejected prosecutors' bid for life. “I think what it shows is that our judiciary remains independent, thankfully,” Moskowitz said. Attorney General Todd Blanche said in a statement that Merchant was “justly sentenced.”

The case concerned an alleged Iran-driven plan to kill a prominent U.S. political figure during the 2024 campaign season. The Iranian government denied trying to kill U.S. officials.

At trial, Merchant told jurors that he researched Trump rally locations and spoke of carrying out an assassination at a political rally. He used objects on a hotel napkin to illustrate the scenario for an acquaintance, then lined up people he thought were hit men and paid them $5,000 in cash borrowed from a relative.

Merchant testified that he was just playing along with instructions from a Revolutionary Guard intelligence operative to protect relatives in Iran. He said he figured U.S. authorities would foil the plan, that he would cooperate and that he might receive immigration help as a reward.

According to his defense, Merchant met a Revolutionary Guard operative several years earlier, imagined the intelligence work would be exciting and profitable and feared for his relatives' safety if he defied the paramilitary. In March, Merchant testified: “My family was under threat, and I had to do this.” His handler named Biden, Haley and Trump as potential targets, Merchant said.

The people Merchant believed were hit men were undercover FBI agents. The acquaintance who saw the napkin demonstration tipped authorities, and law enforcement arrested Merchant in Texas on July 12, 2024, while he was packing for a flight back to Pakistan. The arrest came a day before an unrelated attempt on Trump's life in Butler, Pennsylvania.

Defense lawyer Christopher Neff said in court: “This plot was incompetent from the very beginning.” He added: “Mr. Merchant’s participation was half-hearted.”

Merchant worked for Pakistani banks for decades before entering businesses that included car sales and banana exports. He traveled periodically to the U.S. for work and to see relatives, and he also traveled to Iran for Shiite Muslim religious pilgrimages and visits with kin.

Prosecutors challenged Merchant's duress claim and cited his sharing of anti-American and anti-Trump social media posts after the U.S. killing of top Iranian military leader Qassem Soleimani in 2020 during Trump's first administration. The defense portrayed the posts as venting, and Neff said Merchant was not an extremist or inclined to turn his beliefs into violence. Assistant U.S. Attorney Sarah Winik countered that Merchant did “the most extreme thing you can do: Plot the assassination of a government official.”

In sentencing Merchant, Komitee said he believed Merchant had some concern for his family but was also “highly motivated to do harm to this country.”
