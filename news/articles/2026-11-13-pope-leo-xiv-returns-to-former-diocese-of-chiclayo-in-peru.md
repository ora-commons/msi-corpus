## Marinera dancers and flag-waving children greet Leo at airport

Pope Leo XIV returned Friday to his former diocese of Chiclayo in what the AP described as an emotional homecoming that underscored his love for his Peruvian flock and their pride that their shepherd now runs the Catholic Church. The pope touched down at Chiclayo's airport on the Pacific coast in a Latam charter plane emblazoned on the side with a message in Spanish that has followed Leo wherever he has gone in Peru: "Leo XIV flies home." The pontiff once known in the region as "Padre Roberto" affectionately greeted members of Chiclayo's clergy and Peru's air force at the airport, and walked alongside the current bishop of Chiclayo, Edinson Farfán, in the sweltering heat.

Dancers welcomed Leo with the traditional Peruvian marinera dance, and dozens of children waved Vatican and Peru flags as the pope passed by waving and smiling. Chiclayans lined the pope's motorcade route, some of them holding yellow and white balloons of the Holy See flag.

Chiclayo, population 700,000, is the commercial hub of Peru's northern Pacific coast. But much of the world heard about it on the night of Leo's election, May 8, 2025, when he gave Chiclayo a shoutout, in Spanish, from the loggia of St. Peter's Basilica. On Friday, Chiclayo was welcoming its former shepherd home.

"I'm so glad he's returning," said Rolando Antonio Mendoza, who was selling posters bearing the pope's image in front of the cathedral where Leo used to celebrate Mass. "Even though he's now Pope Leo XIV, he hasn't forgotten about his homeland."

Church organizers say as many as a million people could turn out for Leo's first big Mass in Peru, being held Friday morning on a massive field outside Chiclayo.

The former Robert Prevost spent two decades as a missionary priest and bishop in Peru and credits the place and its people with teaching him how to be a shepherd. Pope Francis sent the Chicago-born Augustinian to take over as bishop of Chiclayo in 2014 and he stayed until Francis summoned him to Rome in 2023 to run the Vatican's bishops' office. The AP reports that, in hindsight, the 2023 transfer to Rome signaled that Francis saw in Prevost a potential successor, since the job is one of the most important in the church and would give Prevost the Vatican experience that many consider necessary to be a viable contender for the papacy.

The AP reported that Prevost's heart remained in Chiclayo, where he was beloved for championing social justice issues and for his closeness to the people, especially poor people, during times of crisis: COVID-19, devastating floods, and a surge of Venezuelan migrants as the humanitarian situation worsened there in 2018.

"Chiclayo is a party," said Rocio Zena, who remembered how Prevost helped her when she lost her family home in the 2023 floods. "We're overjoyed to welcome the Holy Father with so much faith. He arrives, no longer as Prevost but as Leo XIV. And he's coming home."

In town, the El Trebol restaurant proudly calls itself the restaurant of the pope and his image is displayed in framed photos of Prevost riding a horse, wading through the mud in rubber boots and celebrating his birthday with a cake. A sign on his favorite table — No. 3 — with a view of the cathedral reads: "mesa bendita" — Spanish for blessed table. Like many other places around the city, the restaurant sells "Papaletas" or "Pop(e)sicles" with Leo's image in white chocolate. Just a few steps from the restaurant, a life-size cardboard cutout of the pope now welcomes visitors to his former residence at the diocese, where an exhibit displays the amaranth purple, scarlet and white zucchettos, or skullcaps, that he wore as bishop, cardinal and now pope.

"I feel so happy about my work because the pope is Chiclayano," said Cynthia Charrary, who was sweeping the streets near the city's main square ahead of Leo's arrival. "We want our city to be cleaner than ever."

Ever since Leo touched down on Peruvian soil on Wednesday, the AP has reported a very different pope. The AP described the normally reserved and placid 71-year-old as at turns positively giddy and overwhelmed with emotion to the point of tears. That was the case Thursday night, when some 60,000 young Peruvians sang for him in the Lima stadium, and it was also the case when he sang the national anthem at the presidential palace upon his arrival on Wednesday. Leo has also sung along with popular songs and hymns, tapping his fingers to the beat on an armchair. On other occasions, he has shown himself so relaxed and comfortable as to joke about his health: speaking to Peruvian bishops, he joked about how some "doubting Thomases" didn't think he'd make it because of his recent lung cancer surgery.

After the Mass, Leo's other main events of the day include a meeting with Chiclayo clergy and seminarians and a speech to the Catholic university where he served as chancellor during his years as bishop. The university is named for the 16th-century first archbishop of Lima, St. Turibius de Mogrovejo. Leo is particularly devoted to Mogrovejo and in many ways modeled his ministry on the saint's. Mogrovejo is revered for having traveled long distances on horseback to be with his flock and for having defended the rights of Indigenous and enslaved peoples. He studied Quechua and Aymara and oversaw the printing of church teaching in these Indigenous languages, because he believed the faith had to be transmitted in a language people could understand.

The Rev. John Lydon, an Augustinian who lived with Leo in Peru for over 10 years, says his commitment to Peruvians, human rights and involving the lay faithful in the running of church initiatives was born during his stints first as a young missionary priest in Chulucanas, then Trujillo and finally as bishop in Chiclayo. Lydon recalled that Leo first arrived in Peru in the mid-1980s and saw the period of the Shining Path terror campaign and the later crackdown by the authoritarian Fujimori regime. In the late 1990s, then-Rev. Prevost led a campaign to collect signatures to commemorate the 50th anniversary of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights. "These are the kind of things that just mark his identity," Lydon recalled.
