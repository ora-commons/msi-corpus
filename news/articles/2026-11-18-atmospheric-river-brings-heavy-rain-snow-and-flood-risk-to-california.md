## Bay Area flood advisories and burn-scar warnings identify localized hazards

An atmospheric river is moving across California and dumping precipitation, with heavy rain and snow forecast this week. The National Weather Service issued flood advisories for much of the Bay Area, while southern California may see coastal flooding, heavy surf, wave damage to coastal piers and parking lots, and beach sand erosion.

The National Weather Service said the heaviest rainfall was expected northeast of San Francisco, from Santa Rosa to Chico and Redding in the state's Central Valley. The southern Bay Area, along coastal mountain ranges from Santa Cruz to San Jose and Palo Alto, was also expected to receive some of the heaviest rain.

In southern California, the National Weather Service predicted 1 to 3 inches of rain in many areas and 3 to 5 inches in some mountain and foothill areas. The forecast included a 20% chance of thunderstorms on Wednesday and Thursday and as much as a foot of snow in higher-elevation areas above 6,000 feet.

The National Weather Service described a high risk of “significant road flooding” and a moderate risk of rockslides and mudslides in southern California. Areas still recovering from recent wildfires were also at risk of flash flooding, with specific warnings issued for “burn scar” areas including Big Sur and the Napa Valley.

Accuweather warned in an online forecast: “Travel delays, localized flooding and hazardous conditions are possible, especially along the coast and on mountain pass roads.”

The National Weather Service expected the risk of coastal wave damage to peak in southern California on Thursday, with additional risks the following week. Seasonal high tides were expected to overlap with storm risks during Thanksgiving week.

The National Weather Service flagged the risk of additional waterspouts in the ocean east of San Francisco on Wednesday. Waterspouts had been spotted in San Francisco Bay earlier that week.

Atmospheric rivers are massive currents of water vapor that can carry as much as or even more water than an actual river. The Guardian reported that the systems have been growing stronger and deadlier in recent years. In the relatively dry western US, they supply essential precipitation but also create flood risk.
