## Officials say China fell short of agricultural and rare-earth commitments

U.S. and foreign officials told The Wall Street Journal that, with no more elections to face, President Trump could become more willing to take risks or escalate conflicts abroad after Republican midterm losses. Weeks after those losses, Trump returned to the world stage, and his three-day Asia trip was being scrutinized by allies and rivals for signs of how he intended to wield U.S. power during the rest of his presidency. The Journal's analysis said that, like other second-term presidents, Trump had looked abroad to cement his legacy.

“The period between now and January is when President Trump can do a lot on the international stage,” said Ashok Malik, partner and chair of the India practice at the Asia Group. The Wall Street Journal said that in January, when Democrats take control of Congress, they will have greater power to probe Trump's foreign-policy decisions and challenge his trade agenda. Trump had dismissed the suggestion before leaving Washington that the midterm losses would hurt his influence abroad. The Journal reported that even when Democrats control both chambers, Trump will retain broad authority over foreign-policy decisions.

With Americans worried about the state of the U.S. economy, the White House has emphasized its desire to preserve a trade truce that Trump negotiated with Xi Jinping after what The Wall Street Journal called a brutal trade war early in his term. A senior U.S. official said the Trump-Xi meeting in Shenzhen would focus on implementing existing trade commitments. Administration officials said China had fallen short of its commitments to buy more American agricultural products and increase shipments of rare-earth minerals that are vital to U.S. manufacturers. The midterm results raised a question of whether Beijing could seek to extract greater concessions from Trump.

Craig Singleton, China Program Senior Director for the Foundation for Defense of Democracies, rejected that possibility. China would “be wrong to assume that makes Trump an easy mark,” Singleton said. “He still has very wide latitude on trade and foreign policy, and he has every reason to resist a deal that looks like Beijing took advantage of the election results,” Singleton said.

The trip itself was already under way. Trump landed Tuesday at Manila's airport, in the capital of one of the U.S.'s closest allies. His Philippine hosts rolled out the red carpet, a marching band played “YMCA,” the president's signature campaign song, and Trump waved American and Philippine flags before stepping onto Marine One. He was due to join a summit of Southeast Asian leaders in Manila and head to China on Wednesday for an unprecedented trilateral meeting in Shenzhen with Chinese leader Xi Jinping and Russian President Vladimir Putin. It was Trump's first trip abroad since the midterms.

Trump has said he expects to discuss vital issues such as nuclear arms control with Xi and Putin. He has also floated a possible meeting with North Korean leader Kim Jong Un during the China leg, although no such meeting is currently scheduled. White House officials have cautioned that they do not expect an agreement on nuclear arms control, one of Trump's priorities for the summit.

According to The Wall Street Journal, the first half of Trump's term has shaken America's relations with some of its closest partners. The report said Trump's barrage of tariffs forced allies from Asia to Europe to rush for deals. The report also said the war in Iran has sent energy prices higher and led the U.S. to shift some military assets away from the Indo-Pacific to the Middle East. The Journal said Trump has cast doubt on longstanding U.S. security commitments and cultivated ties with authoritarian leaders in China and Russia.

One sign of the administration's intent to reaffirm its commitment to its Asian allies came with the scheduling of a meeting of the so-called Quad during Trump's Manila leg. The meeting of the leaders of the United States, India, Japan and Australia was described by The Wall Street Journal as a signal that the four powers intended to act as a counterweight to increased aggression by Beijing in the Indo-Pacific. “These nations represent some of the most important partnerships in the entire world,” Trump told the other Quad leaders.

The prospect of a trilateral meeting with Xi and Trump has unnerved some smaller countries, particularly Ukraine, which worries that the bigger powers could make a deal without its participation. Democrats have criticized Trump's rapprochement with Putin and have promised to use their control of Congress to increase support for Kyiv.

The Wall Street Journal said Trump has given little indication that he intends to scale back his ambitions abroad. He has floated sending U.S. warships toward Cuba after the war in Iran ends and said he wants U.S. forces to prepare for their “next conquest.” He has also left open the possibility of renewed strikes on Iran and threatened to double tariffs on Mexico if Mexico did not stop migrants heading to the U.S. border.

The Wall Street Journal said Trump has struggled to deliver on some of his biggest foreign-policy objectives. His efforts to end the war in Ukraine have repeatedly stalled, while the conflict in Iran has dragged on for more than eight months. Analysts said the meeting with Putin and Xi is unlikely to produce a breakthrough in either conflict.

The Wall Street Journal reported that some allies and partners are cultivating other trade and security relationships as they wait to understand how Trump's foreign policy will evolve during the rest of his presidency. The Journal said some governments that had rushed to placate Trump were starting to look beyond his presidency and calculating whether they could afford to wait two more years. Hours before Trump landed in Manila, Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney met Xi in Shenzhen to expand trade ties. The Journal presented the meeting as a sign that countries were looking for ways to reduce their dependence on the United States.

The Trump administration's efforts to recruit Southeast Asian countries to join Pax Silica, the U.S. initiative to build technology-supply chains that are less dependent on China, could offer another test. Trump officials have pressed countries in the region to choose between competing U.S. and Chinese technology initiatives, and several countries have already signed on to Beijing's rival effort.
