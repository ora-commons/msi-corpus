## Republican lawmakers trust Duffy but question federal funding for upgrades

The Trump administration is asking Congress for $50 billion to modernize the nation's air traffic control system following last week's nationwide ground stop. In a PBS News segment, Geoff Bennett and congressional correspondent Lisa Desjardins discussed the proposal, which could face resistance in Congress as lawmakers question its cost and how the money would be spent.

Secretary Sean Duffy had been calling congressional leaders and had lunch with Senate Republicans to encourage Congress to provide the funding. PBS News said it was among the first outlets to obtain the two-page proposal.

The document assigns $20 billion for new terminals and upgrades at some airports around the country, $10 billion for air traffic control towers and computing needs, and $10 billion for five air route traffic centers that handle flights overhead at high altitudes. Some of the computing money is needed for a fiber-and-copper wire switch. Desjardins described the request as large and late because Congress was preparing to close its session.

Republicans coming out of the luncheon said universally that they trusted Duffy, and Desjardins said she did not sense any threat to that trust. They remained skeptical of the request. One senator said airlines should pay for all the work, while Senator Kennedy of Louisiana said the federal government should not pay to improve airports.

Duffy reiterated that a faulty Juniper Networks router was to blame for the ground stop, although he and the FAA administrator told the press they were not sure exactly why the router misfired. Based on the report, they said they had shut down all Juniper Networks routers in the system so far.

Duffy said the ground stop was critical for safety and defended the decision: "I get like it was hard for people. But I would rather have that story than the other story that we didn't do our job, and we let risk into the system, but we kept flying, because then all of you would say, why didn't you shut it down? Why didn't you make that decision?"

Congress is in the final weeks of a lame-duck session and is preparing to leave this week without returning until December. Lawmakers then have a few weeks to complete their remaining work, including keeping the government funded before a December 10 deadline. Desjardins said there were real warning signs of a potential shutdown.

Congress also must pass the next defense authorization bill, which Desjardins said is usually done each year and usually bipartisan, though she added, "We will see." Republicans then have a big wish list in another reconciliation bill, where Desjardins said they hope to get some agenda items in "before they're out of power." She did not see the serious discussions needed to get everything done, especially the reconciliation bill, in time.

The segment also included a midterm election snapshot. A new House race had been called that day, and Democrats held 257 seats after picking up a North Carolina seat. Seven races remained uncalled; three appeared to be Democratic flips, which would put Democrats at 260 or more.

On the Senate map, Alaska was the only uncalled race. Desjardins mentioned Alaska Public Media while noting that the state was having one of the longest election seasons in years. She said the race could end tomorrow. Desjardins said Mary Peltola "has been picking up votes" and is "poised to end this election tomorrow with 50 percent." Tomorrow's count would determine whether Alaska needed to go to ranked-choice voting or, if Peltola had 50 percent, she would be the winner.
