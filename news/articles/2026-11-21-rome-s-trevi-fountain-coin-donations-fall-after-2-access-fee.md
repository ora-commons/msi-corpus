## Caritas reports roughly €200,000 shortfall through September

Caritas Roma, the church-run charity that collects coins from Rome's Trevi fountain, has reported a shortfall of about €200,000 between January and September compared with the same period in 2025. The charity usually brings in more than €1.4m a year and uses the donations to fund food banks and other welfare projects.

The decline followed the introduction of a €2 fee in February for daytime access to the basin's edge. During the day, the sunken basin is accessible only to Rome's residents or to visitors who pay €2 (£1.64), and a low barrier separates the steps from the water. The initiative aims to curb overcrowding at the monument while raising funds for the upkeep of the fountain and other Rome monuments.

The disclosure of the shortfall is understood to have caused friction with Rome's council, which collected €435,194 from the access fee within the fee's first month. No further figures have been published, though the charge had been forecast to produce a tidy annual income for the council after 4.4 million people visited the Trevi in 2025.

“The closure of the direct access to the Trevi fountain might be a factor,” Giustino Trincia, director of Caritas Roma, told Il Messaggero. His comment identified the loss of direct access as one possible contributor, not a confirmed cause.

The shift toward contactless payments and mobile phone wallets is also believed to have eroded Caritas Roma's collections. A spokesperson for Caritas Rome said the charity was not making any further public statements about the dwindling coin collection. The spokesperson said the collection so far this year did not account for foreign currency coins, so the shortfall might not be as bad as it appeared.

Jeff Clinton, an American visiting Rome with his wife for the first time, found a US one-cent coin on a street near Termini train station and knew what he intended to do with it. “I’ll throw it into the Trevi fountain,” Clinton said, holding the copper coin in the palm of his hand. The distance to the sunken basin stopped him. He considered asking someone in the basin area to throw the coin on his behalf but finally slipped it back into his pocket.

Eric and Patty, a young couple from New York, said they might have thrown a coin but had no change. Neither wanted to pay €2 to get close to the basin. “It’s such a breathtaking monument, we’re happy just to take it in from here,” said Patty as they posed for selfies.

Alan and Ying, visiting from Australia, did pay the fee but still decided not to throw a coin. Having heard that throwing coins “is what people do,” they decided not to indulge because “nobody else was really doing it.” “I always carry cash with me because you never know when you might need it,” said Ying. “But we didn’t see anyone else doing it, so we didn’t either.”

A team of stewards staffs the ticketing system and monitors the flow of the crowd in the basin area. Nicolas Romero works shifts of seven or eight hours and said people often asked him for change so they could throw a coin. “But many don’t even seem to know about the tradition,” he said. “This might also explain the decline.”

The coin-throwing custom is fabled to bring a return visit to Rome or to make other dreams come true. Its origins are believed to lie in an ancient pagan ritual offering to the water gods, though the custom took off globally after the release of the 1954 romantic comedy film Three Coins in the Fountain.

The evidence published so far points to several possible pressures on the collection: restricted access, the move to cashless payment, and visitors who do not know the tradition. None of the published figures isolates the effect of any single factor. Caritas Roma said its count so far excluded foreign currency coins, leaving the full size of the decline unsettled by the available figures.
