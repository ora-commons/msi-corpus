## Female soldiers join Yahalom as Major G commands a company

Women in the Israel Defense Forces have expanded their combat roles amid Israel's war with Iran, according to a PBS NewsHour report published Nov. 26, 2026. Special correspondent Nurit Ben said the number of women in combat roles tripled over the last decade and now accounts for about 21 percent of the combat force. Lisa Desjardins introduced the report by saying Israel has been at war for the last three years, after Hamas attacked in 2023 and Israel later launched a war against Iran alongside the United States.

A three-month command course for a rescue brigade put 40 soldiers through a simulated missile strike in a central Israeli city. Ben said the brigade is sent to the most difficult crisis zones in war or natural disasters around the world. The drill put the soldiers through more than 46 hours with little sleep as they worked to save as many lives as possible and retrieve bodies intact.

Ben said the scenario reflected missile strikes seen across Israel during the past year of war with Iran and that, in Israel these days, the disasters are all manmade. PBS was at a Tel Aviv building after a direct missile hit in June, when the United States and Israel struck Iran's nuclear sites, Ben said. Just a few years ago, Ben said, a ballistic missile hitting a civilian center would have seemed far-fetched, but the war between Israel and Iran has made it a reality. She described the threat as a new normal for which the country and its military were on guard.

Staff Sergeant H, 20, was one of about 30 women in a mixed combat unit led by a female commander. IDF regulations required the report not to show her face or use her name. For her, a combat role was a clear choice. She described the drill's central hardship: “This specific drill, the hard thing about it is the amount of time. So it's more hard mentally, keeping your squad motivated without water, with the heat, without sleep.” She also said that fighting hard enough could lead to high ranks and access to many assignments.

Women made up about 75 percent of the soldiers in the drill. Staff Sergeant Y said: “I guess it's surprising because it's very physical, so you might guess there are more men, but, actually, they're more women. And we do exactly the same.” Ben said new pilot programs have opened a path to the most elite frontline units.

About 20 female soldiers marked the end of a year of training that tested physical and mental strength by officially joining Yahalom, also known as Diamond, part of the IDF combat engineering corps. A participant described the unit as “The most challenging and prestigious in the IDF.” Major G led the soldiers and was the unit's first female company commander.

Ben said Yahalom soldiers enter tunnels like those Hamas has used to launch attacks and hold hostages. Frontline roles may be new to women in significant numbers, but their military service is not. Women have served in uniform since the IDF's inception, when compulsory military service became part of Israeli cultural life.

Tova Dolev's career reached back to 1973. Instead of starting university at 18, a decision that shocked her parents and would change her life, Dolev was chosen for a top intelligence unit based in Sharm el-Sheikh in the Sinai Peninsula, which Ben described as highly strategic. Dolev said: “My job was to identify enemy ships, aircraft, missile batteries, anything that had a radar on it. If something posed a danger, we reported it.”

Dolev had finished a shift at midnight on a Friday and gone to sleep when the Yom Kippur War was about to break out. Egypt and Syria launched a coordinated surprise attack against Israel on the holiest day of the Jewish calendar. Dolev recalled someone suddenly shouting, “Egyptian MiGs had entered the base.” “Bombs are falling from the sky, and, like that, I ran toward the shelter that I barely even knew where it was. And everyone ran there,” she said. Communications with the base were cut off. Dolev, one of just two people trained to monitor radar, was asked to step in.

“And I picked up on two destroyers. The moment I saw the signals, I shouted out the code words, threw off my headphones, and reported immediately to the Red Sea maritime zone. I also reported to the air force. They told me to get out immediately,” Dolev said.

She and eight other women then began a long journey away from the base. Dolev said warnings came constantly that they were about to be bombed and that planes were overhead. “They tried to hide us, and everyone knew there were girls in the area,” she said. The women repeatedly hid in nearby sand dunes. Rumors said Egyptian commandos had landed close by. Eventually, they were flown to safety.

“We arrived at the Ben-Gurion terminal. There were masses of men who had been drafted. It was total silence. Everyone froze. They parted and let us pass through the middle. No one understood where we had come from without shoes, mud on our faces,” Dolev said.

Ben described the 1973 war as a national catastrophe that began with a shocking surprise, the kind the country had not seen in its young history. She said that kind of surprise did not recur until another early Saturday morning, exactly 50 years later, on October 7, 2023, when thousands of Hamas assailants carried out a coordinated attack, flooding Israeli communities near the Gaza Strip and a local music festival filled with hundreds of young concertgoers. The report said more than 1,200 people were massacred and 251 were taken hostage in Gaza.

Staff Sergeant S was studying for a master's degree in neuroscience in London when the attack unfolded. She recalled: “I just remember thinking, I can't sit in this room and read my books and go about London and have fun when my people are dying.” She also said: “The entire country's mindset shifted. There's always some threat. October 7 reminded us how big that threat still is.”

S is now a combat soldier in an air defense unit. “Without the air defense force, we wouldn't be here today, because every missile, every drone, every rocket, every UAV, it kills people,” she said. Ben called the air defense force a vital part of the country's security and said about 50 percent of all air defense personnel are women.

Ben placed the soldiers' experience alongside that of many 19- and 20-year-olds around the world, for whom this stage of life often means freedom and few responsibilities. For these women, military service was a time of being pushed to the limit and often finding purpose. Staff Sergeant Y said: “I think this is the best choice I could have made, because it's challenging, and because you want to maybe prove yourself, or prove, like, other people or men that you can do something that maybe they don't think you can do.”

Dolev described what she took from that experience: “I can tell you what I learned about myself, that I am genuinely strong and I have no fear. I'm not afraid of anything. If I want to do something, I do it. I go all the way.”
