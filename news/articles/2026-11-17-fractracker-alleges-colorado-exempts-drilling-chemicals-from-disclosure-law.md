## ECMC acknowledges differing views over whether drilling counts as production

A FracTracker Alliance analysis published November 17, 2026, alleges that the Colorado Energy and Carbon Management Commission has exempted drilling chemicals from a 2022 public-disclosure law by excluding drilling from the definition of oil and gas production. FracTracker says state records show fossil-fuel operators have used compounds that Colorado prohibits while fracking. ECMC said it is applying current law and rules while working with stakeholders to clarify the scope.

Colorado's 2022 legislation is called “Concerning enhanced oversight of the chemicals used in oil and gas production.” Described as the first in the nation to require such reporting, the law requires fossil-fuel operators and suppliers to make public all chemicals used in “downhole oil and gas production.” Under the law, operators and suppliers must submit all downhole chemical ingredients to ECMC within 150 days, after which the agency must post them on its website.

FracTracker says the drilling exemption contradicts the widely held definition of production, including the definition implied in Colorado regulatory literature. The report says legislators who authored the law, public-health groups and industry all use definitions that include drilling and that the law requires “disclosure of all chemicals used underground or ‘downhole’ in oil and gas wells.”

An ECMC spokesperson said the agency is “aware of differing views regarding the scope and implementation of the chemical disclosure requirements.” The spokesperson said: “We are applying the law and Commission rules under the current statutory and regulatory framework while continuing to work with stakeholders to clarify these issues and support consistent, appropriate implementation.” ECMC literature also implies that the regulator considers drilling part of oil and gas production, according to the analysis.

Halliburton, Chevron and Occidental Petroleum are among the state's top fossil-fuel producers. A Chevron spokesperson said the company “is committed to safe and responsible operations in Colorado.” The spokesperson added: “We take chemical-disclosure requirements seriously and remain focused on meeting our obligations and maintaining transparent, responsible operating practices.”

About 43,000 people live within a mile of the state's wells, and many live within a few hundred feet, according to the analysis. FracTracker's authors allege that regulators lack the information needed to assess the combined impact of chemicals on those communities. Shannon Smith, the nonprofit's executive director and a report co-author, said, “This is a public health crisis and it should be treated as such.” FracTracker investigates and monitors public-health threats tied to the fossil-fuel industry and advocates for protections; it began in Pennsylvania in 2010 and now organizes nationally.

To drill wells and extract minerals, fossil-fuel operators pump huge volumes of pressurized liquid into the earth to fracture, or “frack,” bedrock and release trapped oil and gas. Those fluids are mostly water and sand by volume, but they also contain industrial chemicals, including biocides, surfactants and lubricants. Industry records and literature from other regions show that drilling chemicals can include xylene, toluene, benzene, PFAS and other dangerous toxicants.

The report said hundreds of studies have pointed to health risks in fracking regions. It said toxic chemicals such as formaldehyde, PFAS and benzene have been widely detected or reported in fracking fluids. It said these and other chemicals used — or thought to be used — in production are linked to issues such as cancer, hormone disruption and neurological damage. The report said gas companies reported as many as 11 spills per week in 2018. People can be exposed through groundwater, above-ground spills, wastewater seepage into drinking-water supplies and air emissions, the report said.

State records show that wells covered by the law operated without drilling-chemical disclosure for at least a collective 843,369 days from the rules' implementation to May 16, when the analysis was conducted, according to FracTracker. The law also requires fossil-fuel companies to provide schools, first responders and other community institutions with lists of the chemicals they use, but advocates say that requirement has not been met.

The report follows a previous analysis reported by The Guardian that found Colorado fossil-fuel companies pumped at least 30m lbs of secret chemicals into the ground over an 18-month period without making legally required disclosures. Of more than 1,100 fracked sites in the state, companies did not disclose chemicals at 675, or about 60%. Advocates say the compliance rate since then is closer to 100%, except for drilling chemicals.

Dusty Horwitt, a senior research and policy consultant and report co-author, said the ECMC told FracTracker that it does not consider drilling to be part of fracking and production. Horwitt said, “That was news to us, and it is not consistent with The American Petroleum Institute definition of what oil and gas production is.” American Petroleum Institute literature says oil and gas production includes “drilling, extraction, and recovery of oil from underground.”

Colorado state representative Meg Froelich, who co-wrote the 2022 legislation, said she intended drilling chemicals to be disclosed and that production includes drilling. She accused the ECMC of “word-parsing” and said she was “mad about their stalling.” Froelich said: “The law covers everything that goes downhole because everything that goes downhole has the potential to affect neighbors.”

Froelich also said the ECMC until recent years had largely worked to promote the fossil-fuel industry. She said the agency had shifted to also protect public health only since Democrats took over the state government, and described that shift as coming in “fits and starts.”

FracTracker said at least five chemicals reported for use in fracking are prohibited by Colorado law, and it found that fossil-fuel companies reported using at least three highly toxic chemicals that raise serious safety questions. The report specifically listed 1-butanol, 2-butoxyethanol, 1,4-dioxane, N,N-dimethylformamide and BMPA as chemicals disclosed for use on some well pads despite state rules prohibiting their use in fracking. Some of those compounds are linked to cancer, liver disease and harm to developing children. ECMC said it has “issued notices of alleged violation when prohibited substances are identified, including cases involving 1-butanol, 2-butoxyethanol and 1,4-dioxane.”

The report also raises questions about which chemicals are being used for drilling. A recent Colorado law banned any use of PFAS at oil and gas sites, while industry literature and peer-reviewed research suggests that PFAS is a common drilling chemical. Horwitt said he fears the chemicals may be used in Colorado. FracTracker cited publications, including those issued by major oil and gas-producing nations Canada and Norway, that report PFAS being used for multiple purposes, including drilling and enhanced oil recovery, and said the lack of Colorado disclosure creates uncertainty about whether companies are complying with the PFAS ban.

Records from Ohio show frequent use of xylene, the report said, and the chemical can cause headaches, dizziness, confusion, loss of muscle coordination and, in high doses, death. Among potentially harmful chemicals permitted in Colorado are acrylamide, ethylene oxide and methanol, according to FracTracker. The group said those chemicals were among those disclosed as being used in a well in Galeton that sprayed a geyser of gas and at least 3.7m gallons of fluid for over four days in April 2025. The Guardian has reported that living near wells has well-established links to leukemia, birth abnormalities and other serious health problems. In July, residents told The Guardian they faced loud noise, acrid air emissions, headaches, nosebleeds and respiratory issues.
