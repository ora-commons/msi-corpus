## EPA expands field sampling as residents and lawmakers press for answers

The U.S. Army said Wednesday's controlled detonation of the nation's aging lead azide stockpile at the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant in Middletown succeeded, with monitors reporting no airborne lead in the region since the blast. The U.S. Environmental Protection Agency said Thursday that about 300 people were sampling schools, playgrounds, churches and homes across the region, with laboratory results expected in the coming days. Residents and elected officials continued to criticize how and when the federal government communicated its plans.

The EPA's Thursday field operation covered schools, playgrounds, churches and homes across the region. More than 1,500 samples of air, water, soil and dust had been collected before the detonation, and the agency took hundreds of samples afterward, including from homes, parks and drinking water, with results still expected in the coming days. So far, the EPA said, monitors had continued to detect “zero” airborne lead in the region since Wednesday's controlled detonation, and the Army and Trump administration officials called the operation a success, citing those monitor readings.

Detonation was the conclusion of an Army working group that spent months charting a path forward. Court documents and briefings by federal officials said much of the lead azide stockpile had been transferred to the Iowa plant in 2009, and that records then showed the material was old and already a concern for the military. The 68 barrels had been stored there nearly two decades. Contractors inspected them for years and replenished a water-ethanol solution that kept the lead azide stable, but court documents said crystals began to form inside the barrels and around the lids, which meant the containers could not be opened.

Army officials said the solution was last replenished in September 2025. The plant operator, American Ordnance, then gave notice that the lead azide would need to be neutralized; that contract was later withdrawn after the working group determined detonation was the safest option.

The dispute now centers on notification. A notice that plant managers planned to neutralize the stockpile was posted on a government website. The plant's closure in March was publicized, and an October public meeting warned that the chemicals would soon need to be detonated. Middletown residents knew of the plans, but few others did, and the warnings largely failed to register with elected officials and the broader public until days before the planned detonation.

Iowa Gov.-elect Rob Sand is among those calling for an investigation into why the lead azide was allowed to reach that point. “The lead-up to the detonation should never have been so chaotic and unclear,” he said after the detonation.

Middletown Mayor Rodney Johnson said residents were not blindsided. He said the plant commander briefed him when the plant closed in March and showed him photographs of the bunkers where the 68 barrels were stored. The Army working group was assembled in January, and about 200 residents attended the October meeting, where experts outlined the urgency of detonating the hazardous material before it could spark a chain reaction of unintended explosions. The Army said at the meeting that it was aiming for a date in November and that the weather would dictate the schedule.

Johnson has said he believes the Army did its job. “Unfortunately negative social media and unconfirmed theories have made this task more complicated for all parties involved,” he said in a social media post after a legal challenge delayed the original Nov. 20 date for the detonation. At the time, Johnson said Middletown residents were fearful, not knowing when the lead azide would be taken care of so they could get on with their lives.

The Army submitted an application to the EPA in late October and won approval from regulators within about two weeks. The lengthy document covered the alternatives considered, the environmental sampling that would follow and the community engagement that was required. “Because this action may raise public concerns related to noise, blast effects, and potential environmental contamination, this Community Engagement Plan establishes the framework for proactive, transparent communication with the EPA, local elected officials, the Middletown community, the media, and other interested stakeholders,” the application states.

Environmentalists and other critics argued that the Army should have cast a wider net, providing information sooner to more downwind communities. They said contamination from toxic lead dust can have long-lasting health and environmental impacts and that people needed time to prepare. “The failure of communication and transparency throughout the planning process is inexcusable and left many Iowans feeling confused, helpless, and scared,” said Josh Turek, a Democratic state lawmaker recently elected to Iowa's open U.S. Senate seat.

The EPA issued public advisories about the flurry of required permitting as early as May. In a letter to Iowa Gov. Kim Reynolds, the agency said the notices went to Iowa's congressional delegation, state lawmakers and local officials. Reynolds' office did not answer emails asking whether the governor knew about the plant's shutdown in March or the permit advisories. Some Democratic state lawmakers said they were blindsided, and Iowa House Majority Leader Bobby Kaufmann, a Republican, said he had concerns about how notification was handled.

U.S. Sen. Joni Ernst, a Republican and former lieutenant colonel in the Iowa Army National Guard, said the Army began discussing disposal with some communities months ago but that critical details about the plume and how far it could reach were not part of those conversations. “At a time when trust in our institutions is already low, the military owes the people it serves more transparency, not less,” Ernst said in a statement the day before the detonation.

U.S. Sen. Chuck Grassley, an Iowa Republican, said the situation had turned into political finger-pointing. His office had been in contact with federal officials since March, and the Oct. 15 town hall was well attended. “But it’s clear it wasn’t enough,” he said earlier this week, vowing to continue pressing for answers so people have a better understanding of the Army’s decision-making process.

The public outcry and the legal delay also gave the Army more time to add safeguards, including special tarps and foam designed to keep more contaminants from escaping, environmentalists said. The EPA used the additional time to bring in more staffers and contractors for air, water and soil sampling.
