## Hass: Xi may privately warn Trump that tighter controls risk tit-for-tat dynamic

President Trump and Xi Jinping meet in Shenzhen on Thursday at the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation summit, where The Wall Street Journal reported the case of ousted Nvidia chief executive Jensen Huang is unlikely to come up. An internal policy split within Trump's administration over export controls has burst into the open, and Trump set a conciliatory tone before the trip: “We’re heading to China, and I don’t think you want to insult China just before you get there,” he told reporters Monday.

Ryan Hass, a former senior national-security official in the Obama administration who recently returned from a trip to Beijing and now directs the Brookings Institution’s China Center, told Wei what he expects. The Wall Street Journal reported that the Jensen Huang affair has given fresh ammunition to forces in Washington pushing to close loopholes in current U.S. measures that have allowed Chinese companies to access chips subject to export controls through third countries, particularly in Southeast Asia. Beijing, according to Hass, is under no illusions: “Based on my recent discussions with Chinese counterparts, they view it as a question of when and how much, not whether, there will be additional export controls.” Xi may warn Trump privately that tightening controls further risks setting off a “tit-for-tat dynamic,” Hass said.

The case behind that pressure has drawn scrutiny of its own. Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang was ousted over an affair with Alice Huang, a suspected Chinese intelligence asset whose firm, Megaspeed, is under U.S. and Singaporean scrutiny for allegedly smuggling restricted Nvidia chips to China, The Wall Street Journal reported. The scandal has triggered a Justice Department probe and potential congressional probes, compounding concerns that Jensen Huang's lobbying to loosen chip export controls undermined U.S. national security. The outlet said the ouster following revelations of the affair “has rocked the world of politics and technology,” intensified calls in Congress for tighter controls on the sale of advanced semiconductors to China and “cast a harsh spotlight on the extent of Chinese intelligence-gathering within America’s most strategically important industry.” The outlet assessed: “These events underscore Beijing’s sweeping espionage and cyber campaign to acquire Western AI and semiconductor technology.”

Hass said the two leaders already accept that reality. “Both leaders are clear-eyed that each side conducts espionage against the other,” he said. “Neither leader could say anything to the other that would dispel or diminish this reality, and they both know it.”

Against that backdrop, The Wall Street Journal reported that Beijing has shown a willingness to escalate. After Trump sharply raised tariffs on Chinese goods last year, China “weaponized its dominance over rare-earth minerals, restricting exports critical to everything from electric vehicles to jet fighters,” the outlet wrote. Xi is unlikely to press the point in a way that would disrupt the fragile calm both sides have worked to maintain, the outlet said.

That calm is Beijing's overriding priority, especially as Xi prepares for a major leadership reshuffle at the Communist Party's 21st National Congress, set for late next year, where he is “expected to install a new generation of loyalists in top positions,” The Wall Street Journal reported. Hass said: “China is not pursuing any major breakthroughs or improvements in its relationship with the United States.” He added: “It is working to elongate the period of fragile stability in the relationship for as long as possible.” Extending stability buys China time to strengthen its position, including by developing its own versions of the technologies Washington is trying to keep out of its hands, the outlet reported.

Central to that effort is a concept Beijing and Washington have labeled “constructive strategic stability,” which Hass described as “a leader-level commitment to avoid confrontation and uncontrolled escalation.” He said the formulation remains more conceptual than concrete but is of great value to Beijing, and The Wall Street Journal reported that Xi will press Trump to reaffirm it.

Hass said expectations should be low for major deliverables from either the bilateral meeting or the much-discussed trilateral summit with Russian President Vladimir Putin. Xi is expected to use his time with Trump to signal that Beijing wants no surprises, and no sudden shifts, the outlet reported. Hass said the trilateral would push Xi to the outer edge of his comfort zone. Hass said: “Xi rarely enters diplomatic settings where the outcome is not predetermined.” He added: “His staff will do their utmost to minimize space for surprise or unexpected outcomes.”

With Trump and Putin across the table, The Wall Street Journal wrote, there is only so much choreography that can eliminate unscripted moments. Hass said Beijing will tightly control media access to ensure Xi is presented as in command and to limit any perception that events are going off-script.

The outlet assessed that Beijing “isn’t looking for a breakthrough in Shenzhen” and wants to stave off disruptions, even as the Jensen Huang case fuels pressure in Washington for tougher export controls. Trump and Xi may leave the summit with their fragile truce intact, The Wall Street Journal reported, while the tensions over technology and espionage will be waiting when Trump returns home. Wei framed the question as what happens when Trump comes home.
