## Directive limits ICE contact to jailable arrests and administrative warrants

Houston Mayor John Whitmire said Friday the city would limit cooperation with federal immigration authorities, heralding "a new model" of immigration enforcement after Democrats won power in Texas for the first time in decades.

The two-page order narrows when Houston police may reach out to U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement. Under the directive, officers should contact ICE only if the person stopped is arrested on a charge that is subject to jailing, or if the agency issues an administrative warrant — an order signed by a senior ICE official indicating that the person is wanted by immigration officials.

"The fact that a person is, or is suspected to be, subject to immigration enforcement shall not, by itself, be a basis to contact ICE, to prolong any detention, or to take any person into or hold any person in custody," the order states.

Whitmire said the city would go after immigrants "that pose a danger, not those that are going about their family business, work and education." A traffic incident, he said, "will not even [trigger immigration enforcement] … You write a citation or you warn them and they go about their way. No contact with [Immigration and Customs Enforcement] over nonviolent, non-jailable offenses. It reduces the fear and anxiety."

The move by the nation's fourth-largest city is one of the first steps to curb ICE's powers since Tuesday's midterm elections. Texas Governor Greg Abbott, who has closely aligned the state with the Trump administration on federal immigration enforcement, lost his bid for a fourth term to Democrat Gina Hinojosa.

Spokespersons for ICE did not immediately respond to an email seeking comment on the mayor's announcement.

The directive lands in a city still contending with the aftermath of a fatal federal operation. In July, ICE agents shot and killed Lorenzo Salgado Araujo during a traffic stop in Houston while reportedly looking for other individuals. A vigil for Salgado Araujo was held in Houston on 8 July 2026.

The shooting rattled residents of the Magnolia Park neighborhood, where it occurred. "We feel it on a level we can't even describe," a neighbor who declined to share his name for fear of retaliation told the Guardian in July. "Everybody knows that what happened is wrong. How will we not stand with our people, stand behind them, especially when something so vicious and ugly happened?"

The killing was also another blow to the housing construction industry in Texas, which has been losing skilled, experienced workers at a high volume and high rates.

In October, an ICE agent struck an individual with a vehicle during a pursuit of a different suspect whose family noted he died years ago. A grand jury indicted ICE officer Mario Alberto Morales on a charge of aggravated assault with a deadly weapon. Prosecutors say Morales intentionally rammed his car into Miguel Parra Hernandez, whose leg was broken; ICE has asserted that Parra Hernandez ran in front of the vehicle, an account that prosecutors say is contradicted by video.

Despite a one-time $75bn infusion from Congress, ICE remains heavily dependent on local police to find people subject to deportation and turn them over. It has done so partly by signing about 2,600 agreements — many in Texas — to deputize local police and other state and local agencies to enforce immigration laws. Texas has been a primary target for ICE arrests.

Fiel, an immigrant-rights group in Houston that has been working with the family of Parra Hernandez, planned to hold a press conference on Friday afternoon in light of the announcement. "Today Houston won. The Houston city council had already decided this months ago," said Cesar Espinosa, executive director of Fiel Houston, adding that it was time to follow through.
