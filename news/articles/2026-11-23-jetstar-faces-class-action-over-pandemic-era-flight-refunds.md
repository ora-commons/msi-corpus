## Jetstar faces class action over pandemic-era flight refunds

## Federal court orders notices to about 1.1 million potential participants

The federal court confirmed on Monday that it had ordered notices to customers explaining their eligibility to participate in or opt out of the class action against Jetstar. Echo Law says more than 1 million Jetstar customers could be eligible to join, and about 1.1 million people will receive the notices. Echo Law alleges the airline issued travel credits instead of cash refunds for flights it cancelled during the pandemic. Jetstar has denied the allegations.

The notices are due to be distributed by 4 December. Echo Law, which is leading the lawsuit, said it wanted to alert the public so recipients would understand the communication was not a scam.

The class action concerns flights cancelled between 2020 and late 2022, after Jetstar cancelled thousands of scheduled domestic and international flights. Echo Law alleges the airline issued customers flight credits instead of the cash refunds they were entitled to receive.

Echo Law alleges Jetstar breached its legal obligations to customers by failing to provide refunds and instead offering credit vouchers. The lawsuit also alleges that Jetstar misled customers and engaged in unconscionable conduct in breach of Australian consumer law. Echo Law further alleges that Jetstar unlawfully benefited by holding funds that should have been refunded.

Jetstar has denied the allegations and is defending the class action. “During the Covid-19 pandemic, Jetstar took a range of steps to respond to rapidly changing travel restrictions, while continuing to support customers affected by disruptions,” a Jetstar spokesperson said. The spokesperson said Jetstar provided customers with a range of options depending on the circumstances, including travel credits, fee-free flight changes and refunds.

The airline also said customers with a Jetstar Covid-19 voucher could use it whenever they were ready because Jetstar removed the voucher’s expiry date in 2023.

Andrew Paull, a partner at Echo Law, alleged Jetstar’s policy of pushing customers to accept vouchers was designed to preserve the airline’s cash position. Paull alleged that Jetstar held onto customers’ money while expecting them to accept credits or, in some cases, nothing, though they were entitled to cash refunds. He also alleged that many customers ended up paying Jetstar more than their original booking to use their credits on new fares, as they were led to believe they had little choice but to do so to avoid losing the value of their flights.

Echo Law also ran a separate class action against Qantas over its handling of Covid-19 travel credits. Qantas agreed in March to settle that case for $105m with no admission of liability.

The Jetstar lawsuit is scheduled for mediation in December and is expected to go to trial if the parties fail to reach an agreement. A potential trial date has been set for March 2027.

Echo Law said litigation funder Court House Capital is backing the class action. Court House Capital’s chief investment officer, Heather Collins, accused Jetstar of seeking to “delay resolution while continuing to hold onto money that belongs to Australian families.”
