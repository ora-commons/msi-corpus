## More than 3,500 airplanes airborne when FAA announced ground stop

Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy said a computer router installed as part of an upgrade from copper wire to fibre started sending out faulty signals. He said on Sunday that the router — a piece of equipment which directs traffic across the network — was installed in El Dorado, Arkansas, and "flooded our network". Investigators are still working to determine the root cause and who is at fault.

Duffy said the administration decided to ground flights after failures in a key system that allows air traffic controllers and pilots to communicate. The grounding of all aircraft scheduled to depart the US on Friday disrupted operations at airports across the country.

The backlog began on Friday, when the Federal Aviation Administration announced at 15:54 EST (20:54 GMT) that flights would be grounded until further notice. More than 3,500 airplanes were in the air at the time. Some were diverted to other airports and others flew on to their destinations, but passengers were left stuck inside planes on the tarmac, unable to take off or return to their gates, while others slept on the floor of airports and tried to reach their airlines. The US Homeland Security secretary said of the nationwide ground stop: "It's going to take some time." A video caption of US travellers impacted by the nationwide ground stop carried the line: "We'll get home one day."

In the aftermath of the Friday grounding, 8,500 flights were delayed and another 8,000 cancelled, according to FlightAware. The stoppage was lifted after about four and a half hours, but the effects continued through the weekend. On Saturday, airlines cancelled more than 5,900 flights and delayed more than 7,000 others. The disruption also had a knock-on effect on schedules around the world, with some flights out of the UK and Europe cancelled.

More than 8,800 flights were delayed and 2,400 cancelled on Sunday. Duffy told the BBC's US partner CBS that normal operations may not be restored until Monday. "We have airplanes and crews that are out of position, so it takes a little bit of time," he said.

Duffy said issues will continue to arise until the US makes major modernising changes to its air control system, which he said needs a complete overhaul. "Let's not lie to each other," he said on NBC's Meet The Press. "We have to make investments into a network that has been neglected for decades."

President Trump urged lawmakers on Sunday to approve the funding, writing in a post on Truth Social that "we can get it built quickly and efficiently". "It will be the BEST SYSTEM anywhere in the World. Much of our current air control system is 50 years old - RIDICULOUS! We have already started the work, but it should be done all at once, not in drips and drabs," he wrote.

Experts say the US uses antiquated technology for flight communications. Aviation analyst Miles O'Brien described the US system as "a house that's about to be condemned". He told the BBC: "I think most of us could look at this and go 'this is a system in the United States that we have allowed to decay'. It is worse than a fixer-upper."
