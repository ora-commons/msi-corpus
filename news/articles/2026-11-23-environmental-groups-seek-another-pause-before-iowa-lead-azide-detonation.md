## Environmental groups seek another pause before Iowa lead azide detonation

**Subtype:** fact

- The Iowa Environmental Council and Sierra Club asked a court on Sunday to pause proceedings so the groups can submit additional information before the planned Wednesday detonation of five tonnes of toxic explosives.
- Iowa's newly elected Democratic Senator Josh Turek called for a full probe into how the lead azide "was allowed to degrade into this critical state and why safer methods of disposal were not pursued."
- The Justice Department told a court that degraded lead azide posed an immediate risk of spontaneous explosion as temperatures were expected to drop below 30 degrees Fahrenheit.
- The Environmental Protection Agency granted an emergency permit to detonate 68 drums after finding the explosion risk "presented an imminent and substantial threat to human health and the environment."

## Senator Josh Turek calls for full probe into Army's disposal decisions

The Iowa Environmental Council and Sierra Club filed a new motion Sunday in response to the Army's filing. The groups accused the government of bypassing the judicial process and asked the court to pause proceedings so they could submit additional information before the scheduled detonation of five tonnes of toxic explosives at the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant in Middletown, Iowa.

Iowa's newly elected Democratic Senator Josh Turek called for a full investigation into how the lead azide "was allowed to degrade into this critical state and why safer methods of disposal were not pursued." The groups' lawsuit had argued that the Army failed to consider safer alternatives before selecting in-place detonation.

Earthjustice Attorney Thien Chau said, "Despite claiming public safety is its top priority and public communication is essential to trust, the Army seemed more concerned over the weekend with finding a way to sidestep the judicial process than on meeting the public's demands for greater transparency, notice to affected communities, and far stronger protection of people, property, and the environment from serious harm."

The plan has drawn multiple legal challenges and public concern about potential health risks to nearby communities. A court had ordered the operation paused after the Iowa Environmental Council and Sierra Club sued on Thursday. In a filing Saturday, the Justice Department told the court the Army intended to move forward because of an "immediate risk" that the material could "spontaneously explode." The filing described the circumstances as an "emergency situation," in part because cold weather was approaching.

The detonation was initially scheduled for Friday. The Justice Department's Saturday court filing said the Army would carry it out Tuesday, but in a social media post Sunday evening, the Army announced a further delay to Wednesday. The Army said the new date would allow for "optimal weather conditions and all mitigation measures to be emplaced."

The filing said, "As the outside temperatures drop the risk of an uncontrolled explosion increases because the formed crystals can break more easily in freezing temperatures." It also said, "There is an immediate risk that the lead azide at the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant will explode as temperatures are expected to drop below 30 degrees Fahrenheit (-1C) in the next several days." The government argued that a controlled detonation was necessary to protect the public from an unplanned explosion.

The government has argued that the Army can move forward without a permit for detonation because the event requires immediate action. The Environmental Protection Agency had also granted an emergency permit for 68 drums of the material, finding that the explosion risk "presented an imminent and substantial threat to human health and the environment."

The EPA says no level of lead exposure is considered safe. It says exposure through inhalation or ingestion can pose serious health risks to humans and animals, particularly children.

Lead azide is a highly volatile compound used as a detonator. Earlier this year, the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant found that the material had degraded over time to the point where it was too risky to move, according to Iowa Public Radio. Military and environmental officials determined that detonating it in place was the safest way to eliminate the explosive safety hazard.

The government has not released details for the rescheduled detonation. Earlier, the Army had warned residents to leave their homes for six hours on the originally scheduled day, during the explosion and until the dust settled. An advisory warned "all humans" to avoid being outside in the affected areas while the demolition detonation was set to take place.

On Friday, the Army said it had been preparing since March and was working with the Department of Health and Human Services and the EPA. The agencies said: "Agencies involved are prepared to take the most extreme mitigation measures available to ensure there is no risk to the public and any disruptions are minimized." In Sunday's post, the Army said, "The health and safety of all Americans has and always will be our first priority."
