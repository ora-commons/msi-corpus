## South Dakota Republicans split over federal permitting bill

**Subtype:** fact

- Fifty South Dakota lawmakers and lawmakers-elect — including 37 Republicans, 12 Democrats and one independent — urged Republican U.S. senators John Thune and Mike Rounds to oppose the Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act.
- The bill would streamline federal review and approval for power plants, transmission lines and pipelines.
- The bill would require data centers to cover their electrical infrastructure costs.

## Letter raises concerns over tribal sovereignty and state permitting

Fifty South Dakota lawmakers and lawmakers-elect, including 37 Republicans, 12 Democrats and one independent, asked Republican U.S. senators John Thune and Mike Rounds to oppose the Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act. The Monday letter targeted a proposal that would streamline federal review and approval for power plants, transmission lines and pipelines while requiring data centers to cover the cost of electrical infrastructure.

Supporters say faster approvals could reduce project delays and costs. Critics say the legislation could weaken environmental oversight, local control and public participation. The letter argued that economic development, affordable energy and infrastructure investment “must not come at the expense of environmental protections, agricultural interests, private property rights, tribal sovereignty, local control, or the health and well-being of our communities.”

Republican state Sen. Joy Hohn of Hartford and Democratic state Sen. Red Dawn Foster of Pine Ridge were the first two signers. Foster is a member of the Oglala Sioux Tribe. Hohn, who won reelection earlier this month, has opposed a proposed carbon-capture pipeline through eastern South Dakota. “This bill is an incremental erosion of our fundamental rights,” Hohn told South Dakota Searchlight. Other signers included a county commissioner and various Republican Party officials.

Hohn and Foster said in a news release that lawmakers cannot formally address the issue through a state legislative resolution because the annual session does not begin until January. “Recognizing the concerns being raised by constituents throughout South Dakota, participating legislators have come together through a joint letter to ensure those voices are heard at the federal level,” the release said.

Signatures alone did not give the letter the support needed for a resolution. It included 20 signatures from state House members who will be in office in January, below the 36 needed for a majority, and 12 signatures from state senators who will be in office in January, below the necessary 18. Some signers lost their reelection bids or did not run for another term.

Republican state Sen. Casey Crabtree of Madison, the majority leader-elect, did not sign. Neither did other members of the incoming Republican leadership teams in the House or Senate. Crabtree said he supports the federal permitting legislation and said lawmakers should commend U.S. Senate Majority Leader Thune for working in a bipartisan manner to “deliver affordable electricity, fix our roads and bridges, upgrade our telecommunications systems and maintain strong protections for South Dakotans.” He said, “The American people are united behind the issue of affordability.”

All members of the incoming Democratic legislative leadership teams signed the letter, including both Democratic state senators who will be in office in January and 10 of the 12 Democrats who will be House members in January. Democratic Senate Minority Leader-elect Erik Muckey of Sioux Falls said reducing red tape could be paired with less damaging effects on environmental law and state authority. “But the thing is, you can accomplish that without having to have very drastic, negative impacts on environmental law, the Endangered Species Act, the Clean Water Act, all the way to the utter disregard of tribal sovereignty, local control and even, frankly, the state Public Utilities Commission,” Muckey said.

Democratic House Minority Leader-elect Eric Emery of Rosebud said federal permitting needs work but the bill goes too far. “As a Native legislator from Rosebud, I can’t get past what this bill does to tribes,” Emery said. He said the bill narrows what states and tribes can review under the Clean Water Act and allows 150 days to challenge a federal permit in court, a period he said was too short. Emery also opposed what he described as a watering-down of tribal consultation requirements. “Consultation on sacred sites and cultural resources is part of what the United States owes tribal nations under the treaties. It shouldn’t be optional,” Emery said. “This is one issue where I agree with a lot of my Republican colleagues here at home. Local control and tribal sovereignty don’t belong to one party in South Dakota.” The Great Plains Tribal Chairmen’s Association and some other tribal organizations oppose the legislation.

South Dakota Public Utilities Commission Chair Chris Nelson, a Republican, said the commission was “adamantly opposed” to provisions that would let transmission-line developers seek federal approval if state regulators denied a permit or imposed conditions developers considered too burdensome. “What it really does is it takes away the South Dakota PUC’s ability to evaluate projects fairly under our statute and to work with landowners here, to work out their concerns, and make a final decision here,” Nelson said.

Nelson also raised concerns about federal eminent-domain authority and whether federal regulators would have to consider landowners’ objections. Eminent domain allows some developers to obtain access to land without the owner’s consent while paying just compensation. Over the last several years, the issue has been divisive in South Dakota because a proposed multi-state carbon-capture pipeline would have passed through the eastern part of the state. The Legislature and Republican Gov. Larry Rhoden banned eminent domain for carbon pipelines last year.

Rounds was the only Republican to vote against the legislation in a Senate procedural vote earlier this month. Afterward, he posted comments on social media expressing some of the same concerns Nelson raised and said he hoped to work with his colleagues to address them. The bill nevertheless passed the procedural vote 73-25 and awaits a final Senate vote.

Thune voted to advance the bill and said permitting reform is necessary to lower energy costs, create jobs and prevent projects from being delayed for years by federal bureaucracy. “Too often, projects are bogged down in bureaucratic delays for months, and often years, at a time,” Thune said during a Tuesday Senate speech. “Delays cause project budgets to balloon, which then leads to higher costs for American consumers.”

Thune said the bill would reduce permitting burdens for farmers and ranchers and provide technical assistance to communities considering a data center. “Whether that’s their operating and construction costs or the cost of grid improvements needed to accommodate them,” he said. “This legislation ensures those costs don’t show up on household utility bills.” Thune’s office said the legislation would not override South Dakota’s ban on eminent domain for carbon pipelines.

Last week, Republican U.S. Rep. Dusty Johnson of South Dakota endorsed the bill on social media, calling it “the most important affordability bill in decades.” Johnson said, “If we want lower taxes, lower energy bills, better roads and bridges, we have to right-size our regulatory approach,” adding that the bill “does that while protecting property rights and forcing data centers to pay their fair share.”
