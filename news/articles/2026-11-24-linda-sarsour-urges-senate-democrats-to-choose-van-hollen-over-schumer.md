## Column cites Senate deportation case and arms-sales restrictions

On 24 November 2026, The Guardian published a column by Linda Sarsour making the case for Chris Van Hollen to become the next US Senate majority leader, over the incumbent Senate Minority Leader Chuck Schumer. Sarsour, who describes herself as an award-winning racial justice activist, longtime community organizer and co-founder and executive director of MPower Change Action Fund, wrote that electing Van Hollen would show voters the Democratic party is "serious about change from root to branch" and would give the party a leader willing to fight for Medicare for All, restrictions on US military aid to Israel and more assertive oversight of AI.

The leadership contest she entered runs at a different pace from the House. Hakeem Jeffries, a Democratic congressman from New York, largely cruised to victory last week and will be the next speaker of the House, and Sarsour wrote that she does not expect Schumer to have the same fast track to majority leader — and that he should not have one.

Several senators have already taken positions. On Thursday, Senator Elissa Slotkin threw her support behind Van Hollen, citing the need for "a new generation of leadership". When asked on MS Now why she also supports Van Hollen, senator-elect Peggy Flanagan cited what Minnesota has been through in the past two years and Trump's appointment of Stephen Miller to the chief of staff role, concluding that the party needs a majority leader who will fight back. Over the weekend, Senator Cory Booker declined to endorse either Schumer or Van Hollen, while emphasizing the need for a new generation to lead in Congress.

Sarsour's case rests in part on her own campaign work. She wrote that she spent months crisscrossing the country to mobilize voters, particularly Muslim Americans and progressives, including time in Michigan with senator-elect Abdul El-Sayed and in Florida with Angie Nixon, and with candidates and voters in many states in between. She said she was proud to play a small role in the campaign behind Zohran Mamdani's historic win for mayor, strategizing and mobilizing donors and voters throughout New York City's five boroughs, and she wrote that the election cycle came right on the heels of that win.

She said she heard echoes of Mamdani's focus on affordability, his warm and inclusive political vision and his deep commitment to Palestinian freedom in other states, and she described candidates like Mamdani as leaders who "make voters feel seen and heard" against what she calls much stronger forces willing "to sacrifice us for more profits and more power". She also argued that Palestinian freedom is a winning issue, "on top of being morally just", and quoted Van Hollen's own assessment of the election results in an ABC News interview: "This was not a Washington-orchestrated election around the country. This was the American people rising up against a lawless president and demanding change."

The largest part of the column is a contrast between the two senators' records. Sarsour wrote that, "as ICE was terrorizing our neighbors", Van Hollen "did more than just issue a statement" — he "got on a plane to El Salvador to check on Kilmar Ábrego García, a constituent of his who was mistakenly deported in violation of a court order." She also credited him with joining early efforts in the Senate to restrict US arms sales to Benjamin Netanyahu's government "over the genocide in Gaza", and with insisting that candidates not take a dime from Aipac as awareness grew that such money was "morally and politically toxic", while Schumer, she wrote, "continued to cash in".

Sarsour also invoked her own long relationship with her senator, saying Schumer has been her senator since she was a teenager and that New Yorkers' urgency on the economy, corporate greed, healthcare and human rights had outpaced her senator's views and actions. On her own, she wrote that Schumer "bet on Haley Stevens", "bet on Janet Mills" and "bet on Alex Vindman", and that the Senate Majority Pac, which she said has close ties to Schumer, refused to invest in Angie Nixon even after Nixon won the Democratic primary. Nixon, Sarsour wrote, was "one of the midterm cycle's most energizing candidates who happens to be a Black woman"; she said Nixon lost Florida by 3 percentage points, with "$0 from the Senate majority pac", and said a few million dollars and "a little faith" could have done.

She faulted Schumer for breaking with longstanding tradition by refusing to endorse Mamdani after Mamdani cruised to victory in the Democratic primary. She also wrote that the 2024 election cycle was "so disastrous for Democrats" — a result she attributed in no small part to Biden's unwillingness to cut US complicity in Israel's genocide — and that Schumer pushed during that cycle to continue funneling aid to Israel. She wrote that Schumer "consistently vilified students protesting Israel's genocide", as generations of students before them had done against apartheid in South Africa, US involvement in Vietnam and racial segregation.

Near the end, the column cites support from outside the Senate. Democratic strategist David Axelrod, whose podcast she cited and with whom she said she does not agree on everything, said last week that Schumer looks like yesterday and sounds like yesterday.

Sarsour made her case plainly: "Yes, folks with names like Abdul El-Sayed and Zohran Mamdani can win elections" and "Yes, a Black woman named Angie Nixon can win Florida". She said winning takes leaders "clear-eyed about the future", independent funders willing to invest in grassroots organizing during and in between election cycles, and a strong ground game she has seen up close be decisive against big spending by what she calls bad actors; she added that it helps when leaders like Schumer are willing to "kick in" more.

She acknowledged it is possible that Schumer will retire in two years, but wrote that "we cannot afford to wait." She said voters handed Democrats a mandate two weeks ago for bold change — "more spending at home and less spending on the war machine; accountability for tech oligarchs, not collusion; peaceful streets where law-abiding residents can live free of fear." Writing that Democrats "cannot afford to celebrate for too long", she said "It is critical that Democrats show they have learned the right lessons" and maintain trust with the voters who elected them, ahead of what she called a "hugely consequential presidential election."

She closed by saying keeping to win requires leaders who reflect the mandate in both word and deed to represent voters on the national stage: "Chuck Schumer is not it. Chris Van Hollen is."

The column presents one organizer's argument for a candidate and does not set out how Senate Democrats will choose their majority leader or how much support either candidate holds. What it records is that the race already includes public backing from Slotkin and Flanagan, that Booker has chosen not to take sides, and that a prominent activist is pressing the party to read its own midterm results as a mandate for change.
