## Two national groups request an emergency framework for cattle vaccines

USDA data recorded first H5N1 diagnoses in dairy cattle in Oklahoma, Oregon and Indiana, bringing the number of states with detected infections to 22. Oklahoma's first case was reported last Saturday, Oregon's first diagnosis came last week and Indiana's first case was detected earlier this month. The detections came as state agriculture and animal-health officials asked the USDA to prioritize developing an emergency vaccination framework for cattle.

Highly pathogenic avian influenza H5N1, known commonly as bird flu, has affected poultry flocks for years. Wild birds spread the virus to cattle in 2024, when it began infecting milking cows. The virus is deadly to birds and milder in cows, but infected cows pose a risk to dairy herd workers.

Although cases peaked in 2024, states are still seeing new infections in cattle this year. Colorado, Idaho, Texas and Washington reported cases among cattle over the past month. Washington separately recorded roughly three dozen new cases during that period.

At least 146 dairy cattle had been infected this year, while more than 1,200 cattle cases had been recorded since the outbreak began, according to USDA data.

On Nov. 6, the National Association of State Departments of Agriculture and the National Assembly of State Animal Health Officials wrote to the USDA urging the agency to prioritize developing an emergency vaccination framework for cows. The National Association of State Departments of Agriculture represents agricultural officials across all 50 states. The groups said cattle can develop short-lived immunity to the virus but can become reinfected. Their letter called for the agency “to take additional action to control and ultimately eradicate” the virus in dairy cattle.

The associations wrote that bird flu in livestock “poses an urgent threat to public and animal health, national agricultural stability, international market access, and the economic viability of the dairy and poultry industries across the U.S.”

The letter warned: “Without a proactive, coordinated eradication strategy, H5N1 risks becoming an endemic disease in U.S. dairy cattle, requiring USDA, State Animal Health Officials and industry to manage the disease for years while continuing to respond to costly spillover events in poultry.”

The USDA said it supports the rapid development and timely approval of a vaccine for dairy cattle. Several vaccines are undergoing field trials or awaiting final approval for emergency use. Vaccine development and approval have a separate regulatory structure, which can slow testing and implementation.

The risk to the general public is described as low, but dairy workers who work closely with herds face the highest risk. One dairy worker in Washington died earlier this month, bringing the total number of deaths among people contracting the virus to three nationwide.

United Farm Workers said it believed officials were undercounting cases among farmworkers in Washington. The union also said immigration enforcement activity was hindering farmworkers' interactions with public health systems.

Last year, federal officials announced a $1 billion plan to fight the outbreak among poultry flocks in an effort to lower egg prices. Research into a vaccine for poultry was part of the plan.

Dr. Alex Hamberg, president of the National Assembly of State Animal Health Officials, said in a joint press release: “Veterinary and state government leaders need every available tool to protect the agricultural economy in their states and empower their farmers in combating this virus to protect their dairy herds and poultry flocks. We are asking USDA for their support in helping our farmers.”
