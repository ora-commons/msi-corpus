## Indictment details months of alleged planning and damage to the aircraft

A U.S. indictment unsealed Tuesday in Manhattan charges Daithi O’Corrain, 48, with destruction of U.S. government property for an April attack on a U.S. Air National Guard C-130 Hercules parked at Shannon Airport. According to the indictment, O’Corrain breached the airport’s perimeter at about 4:40 a.m. on April 11, disabled the aircraft over the next few hours and poured ball bearings and other objects into its fuel tanks before his arrest. Irish authorities arrested O’Corrain immediately after the attack, but he remains outside U.S. custody and his U.S. arraignment has not been scheduled.

The charge carries up to 10 years in prison if O’Corrain is convicted. Online court records do not list an attorney who might speak on his behalf. In Ireland, An Garda Síochána arrested O’Corrain and charged him with causing criminal damage and trespassing. Supporters describe him as an independent peace activist, and The Limerick Post reported that he is from Cork, about 60 miles (97 kilometers) southeast of Shannon.

The $60 million C-130 had been en route to a military assignment in another country when it was parked overnight at Shannon Airport. U.S. prosecutors said the plane was rendered inoperable and would not fly again. The FBI said the attack jeopardized critical military operations. The C-130 is a quad-engine turboprop mainly used to transport equipment and personnel.

The indictment describes how O’Corrain disabled the aircraft. O’Corrain entered it on a taxiway by destroying the locking mechanism on the rear crew door, covered the windows with the crew’s hammocks and left the cockpit windshield exposed. He then barricaded himself inside, secured the doors with straps, brackets and screws, and placed ball bearings mixed with epoxy in the door tracks to prevent the doors from being opened or closed, according to the indictment.

Over the next few hours, O’Corrain cut hydraulic lines, sliced wire bundles, crimped a liquid oxygen line, punctured air ducts and shattered every gauge in the cockpit, according to the indictment. The indictment also described O’Corrain pulling, snipping and otherwise disabling the plane’s key components. Had he cut the oxygen line instead, prosecutors said, he would have risked blowing up the plane.

The C-130 crew returned to the airport after resting elsewhere and spotted O’Corrain through the uncovered cockpit at around 9:40 a.m., then alerted security, the indictment said. Crew members saw him emerge through a roof hatch and swing an ax at the plane’s exterior, according to the indictment, which included images taken from a video recording that show a man standing on the roof of the plane. He poured substances and objects into the fuel tanks, including ball bearings, before being arrested.

U.S. prosecutors said O’Corrain plotted the attack for months. He searched online for information about the C-130’s construction and looked up coordinates for sneaking onto the airport’s grounds. O’Corrain subscribed to a cellphone app that provided live flight tracking, including for military aircraft, and ordered capsules of gallium, according to prosecutors. Gallium is a chemical that can corrode steel and break down aluminum, the primary material used to build the C-130, prosecutors said. O’Corrain also tried to contact someone involved in a prior breach at Shannon Airport.

Supporters attributed the attack to O’Corrain’s anger at U.S. involvement in Middle East conflicts. An online fundraiser organized by people describing themselves as O’Corrain’s friends and comrades said he disarmed the C-130 in protest of the U.S.’s war with Iran and support for Israel’s war in Gaza — a flashpoint in Ireland, where many people have expressed solidarity with Palestinians.

“An inspiring act of direct action was carried out at Shannon Airport to resist the unwanted presence of the US Military in Ireland in the face of their illegal war & support of Israel’s genocide,” organizer Tracey Ryan wrote.

Manhattan U.S. Attorney Jamie McDonald said the indictment sends “a clear and powerful message that attacks on U.S. military assets — and thus our national security — will not be tolerated.”

“This was not an act of spontaneous vandalism,” James Barnacle, the head of the FBI’s New York office, said in a statement.

Shannon Airport is an international gateway in western Ireland and a hub for American troops and military equipment. The airport, which was used by President Donald Trump and Air Force One during his visit to Ireland in September, has had several security breaches in the last 18 months. Those incidents included three people accused of spray-painting a U.S. military plane and three others who drove to a restricted area where a U.S. military jet was parked.
