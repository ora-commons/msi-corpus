## State seeks to end oversight with 19 of 24 measures unmet

Federal court monitors warned in a July report that rising abuse and neglect in Michigan foster care pose “an urgent safety threat to children.” Their findings came one month after Michigan urged a federal judge to end the long-running monitoring. The Whitmer administration argues that more than $2 million a year spent on federal monitors would “be far better spent on children and families in Michigan.” Michigan’s costs connected to the lawsuit and monitoring have exceeded $40 million, according to the state.

The overhaul began with a 2006 class-action lawsuit filed by Children’s Rights, which alleged that Michigan’s child welfare system “inflicts numerous harms” on the children it is responsible for protecting. The alleged harms included maltreatment and neglect, a lack of “basic” healthcare, excessive lengths of stay and frequent changes in primary caregivers after state intervention. Michigan settled the suit in 2008 rather than defend it, agreeing to give federal monitors extensive authority to oversee required improvements.

After two administrations and several state health directors had served, federal monitors said Michigan had failed 19 of 24 performance measures and was “moving in the wrong direction” in preventing abuse and neglect. About 9,200 children were in state care last year. The monitors’ July report cited 447 substantiated abuse or neglect incidents, 163 more than the minimum standard, and said a random sample of 150 state investigations showed that a third “were not undertaken adequately.”

Michigan is judged by the rate of child victimizations for every 100,000 days in foster care. Michigan’s rate of child victimization per day in foster care has exceeded the federal benchmark for abuse and neglect in each of the past three years. That rate topped 14 last year, up from 5.6 in 2021 and above the 9.07 federal benchmark.

Michigan’s legal position is that the oversight arrangement was designed for a different era. In a June filing, Assistant Attorney General Neil Giovanatti wrote that the consent decree was “initially beneficial” but “has become archaic” and is “premised in outdated theories of child welfare.” He said the decree now “imposes unnecessary and harmful burdens on both the state and children in foster care.”

Children’s Rights attorney Samantha Bartosz opposed the state’s request, arguing in a court filing that difficulty or inconvenience in meeting required metrics did not legally justify modifying the consent decree. Bartosz also said Michigan was seeking to “water down accountability” on kinship care after failing to meet performance benchmarks since 2020.

State Sen. Jim Runestad, a White Lake Republican who chairs the state Republican Party, organized a foster care reform rally at the Michigan Capitol last spring. Runestad said the request to end federal oversight should “absolutely not” be granted. “The state of Michigan never takes this seriously,” he said. “There’s always failures in every single audit report I’ve seen. Colossal failures.” Runestad said public safety should be the top priority for government and that protecting children was the “number one of the number one.” “And they fail at that,” Runestad said of the state. “And they never, ever will put forth the resources to fix the problem.”

A Clinton County trial detailed the consequences that can follow when a child-welfare system fails. Michigan Attorney General Dana Nessel said Tamal and Jerry Flore subjected their foster and adopted children to “prolonged, routine and systemic mental and physical abuse all for personal financial gain” over 14 years. Nessel said the charges brought in 2023 highlighted a moral and legal failure among those entrusted with children’s care and a system failure to ensure that children in custody were properly cared for.

The couple took in some foster children who were later adopted. At trial, testimony from police, medical experts and adopted children described beatings with a 35-inch boat oar, withheld meals, bedrooms that could only be locked from the outside, security cameras in children’s bedrooms and children being forced to live in a laundry room as punishment.

Authorities said the abuse began in 2009. The criminal investigation that led to charges started in 2021 when Clinton County Sheriff Sean Dush, who was a detective at the time, accompanied a CPS investigator to the Flore home to execute a search warrant. Dush testified: “I walked in the house and I went to Jerry and I asked, ‘Jerry, where’s the boat oar you beat your kids with?’” He continued: “… He walked right upstairs without saying a word and pointed it out in the closet in the master bedroom.”

From 2010 through September 2021, the Flores received about $1.7 million from Michigan in adoption subsidy payments, according to testimony. In late October, Tamal Flore, 59, was convicted on 11 felonies, including five first-degree child abuse counts that carry maximum sentences of life in prison. Jerry Flore, 61, was convicted on a second-degree child abuse charge and faces up to 10 years in prison at sentencing, scheduled for January. Jerry Flore plans to appeal his conviction, his attorney told Bridge Michigan. Tamal Flore’s attorney declined to comment.

The CPS investigator who accompanied Dush later testified that she was suspended and fired for failing to follow protocol. She interviewed some children who still lived in the Flore home inside a sheriff’s office patrol vehicle, contrary to forensic interview protocol. She had received a six-week training course on the protocol when hired less than two years earlier. She also made errors in her notes, including attributing some comments to the wrong child, and testified that limited time and resources caused the errors.

Under the 2008 consent decree, Michigan pays for the federal monitors. The state spent about $2.7 million on monitors in 2025. As it seeks to end the oversight, Michigan says the money could finance an estimated 18 additional CPS investigators, foster-care case managers or other staff. Staffing shortages were among the initial problems detailed in the 2006 litigation, and the consent decree required the state to add personnel.

National standards at the time called for one caseworker for every 10 to 15 children in foster care. Michigan caseworkers sometimes managed 25 to 40 children each, according to the 2006 lawsuit.

Despite the staffing increases, Michigan struggled with high turnover and inexperienced workers, said Sarah Font, who joined the state as a child-welfare worker in 2008 and is now a researcher at Washington University in St. Louis. During her 2½ years with the state, Font had three different supervisors, and new employees began shadowing her after only six months. Font said most of her eight-week training focused on the internal data system and applicable laws rather than the investigative process.

“Parents just don’t admit to whatever the allegations are,” Font said. “You need really strong investigation and observational skills to know what’s going on.” Font said the confrontational nature of child-protection work is present in well-run systems, requiring investigators to enter homes where they are not wanted and face hostility. It is not uncommon for reviews of child fatalities to show that an investigator was timid or had limited access to a child, she said. “And that’s where, I think, really serious risks or safety issues get missed or unaddressed.”

In January, the family of 5-year-old Ethan Belcher, who was killed by his mother and her boyfriend, sued Michigan in federal court. Ethan was killed less than two years after a CPS investigation was opened, and the lawsuit alleges that the state failed to investigate properly, the Detroit News reported.

Jill Berrick, a University of California, Berkeley expert, said child-welfare systems are “probably the most difficult public service to operate” and that CPS investigators must estimate a child’s safety under difficult circumstances. “It’s a system of scarcity,” Berrick said. “There’s scarcity of time. There’s a scarcity of money. There’s a scarcity of great community members stepping forward to be a part of kids’ lives.”

Joseph Ryan, a University of Michigan professor and director of U-M’s Child and Adolescent Data Lab, wrote in the state-hired assessment that children in foster care generally were safe despite the federal metrics. Michigan hired Ryan to assess permanency outcomes, the use of emergency shelters and incidents of abuse and neglect in state care.

Ryan noted that the 447 substantiated incidents occurred over 3.1 million collective days in foster care, translating to approximately one incident for every 7,000 days. From 2021 to 2024, about 1% of Michigan foster-care children were abused or neglected, compared with a 0.6% national average, placing Michigan among the 10 worst-performing states. Ryan cautioned that comparing rates between states is “not advised” because states have different policies, reporting systems, investigatory practices and data quality.

Ryan also found that the “largest share” of maltreatment incidents in Michigan foster care occurred in kinship care, where children are placed with relatives. More than half of Michigan foster children lived in kinship homes as of 2024, and Ryan’s analysis found that unsupervised and unapproved visits with biological parents were frequent factors in those incidents. Bartosz pointed to the analysis while arguing that Michigan was seeking to “water down accountability” after missing performance benchmarks since 2020.

Nationally, about 35% of foster children are in kinship care. Berrick said kinship placements have benefits but can also be difficult to integrate with a bureaucratic child-welfare system. Relatives are more likely to take sibling groups, increasing the likelihood that the children will remain together. Berrick said there is no definitive evidence that children in kinship homes are more likely to reunite with biological parents, although extended families are more likely to keep children longer. “You don’t always get great outcomes, but you get ‘better than’ outcomes,” Berrick said. “And there are compromises along the way.”

Federal monitors have identified an urgent safety threat and persistent performance failures, while a professor hired by Michigan says most children in foster care are safe. A federal judge must decide whether to end the consent decree that has governed the state’s child-welfare reforms since 2008.
