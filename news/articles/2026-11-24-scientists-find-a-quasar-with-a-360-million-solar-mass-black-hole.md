## The object formed within 630 million years of the Big Bang

Julien Wolf's German-led team reported the discovery on Tuesday, finding a quasar at a point when the universe was less than 5% of its current age. The object formed within 630 million years of the Big Bang, and observations from the Webb space telescope put the mass of its central black hole at about 360 million suns.

The quasar formed at a very early stage in the universe's 13.8-billion-year history. The team said the finding breaks the previous record for the most distant quasar, an advance that Wolf described as unusually large.

Wolf, who led the study and is with the European Southern Observatory, said in an email: “This time the leap in cosmic time is huge!” He added that “The previous two records each took us back by about 15 million years or less. This one takes us back by nearly 40 million years, the biggest advance in nearly a decade!”

Wolf and his team spotted the quasar while searching through millions of observations made by the European Space Agency's Euclid telescope. Launched in 2023, Euclid is surveying galaxies to shed light on the dark matter and dark energy that fill much of the universe and keep it expanding.

Finding any quasar this early is rare, Wolf said. What makes this example even rarer, he said, is the massive size of its central black hole despite the object's relatively slow consumption of everything around it. Scientists are viewing the quasar as it was 13.1 billion years ago, when the universe was less than 5% of its current age.

Wolf named the quasar Dun Aonghasa after the prehistoric stone fort in Ireland's Aran Islands. His wife and daughter are Irish, and he said he was awestruck when they visited the fort, a frontier that held “the promise of a vast horizon still to explore,” much like the quasar.

The team's findings were corroborated by the Hubble and Webb space telescopes as well as other observatories on and off Earth. Webb measurements showed that the black hole powering the quasar has a mass equivalent to 360 million of our suns. The final results appear in the journal Astronomy & Astrophysics.

“Dun Aonghasa gives us a new deadline” for the early universe, Wolf said. “About 630 million years after the Big Bang, its black hole had already grown to about 360 million times the mass of the sun. But how?”

Wolf said the team's search at the cosmic dawn continues. “Honestly, the record could fall again in a few months or it could stand for years,” he said.
