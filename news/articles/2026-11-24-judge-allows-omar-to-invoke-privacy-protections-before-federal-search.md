## Unsealed filings show the government agreed to a privilege protocol

A U.S. magistrate judge who authorized federal agents to search Rep. Ilhan Omar’s Washington, D.C. home ruled that she could assert the privacy protections afforded to sitting members of Congress at the outset of the search, according to court documents unsealed Monday. U.S. Magistrate Judge Matthew Sharbaugh had raised constitutional concerns before authorizing the Oct. 12 search of the Minnesota Democrat’s residence.

Before authorizing the search, Sharbaugh raised concerns about the “Speech or Debate Clause.” The filings describe the clause as protecting legislators from executive branch interference or intimidation and providing a privilege against compelled disclosures.

Sharbaugh rejected the government’s position that the privilege applied only to official congressional offices and certain electronic devices. “That logic does not hold,” Sharbaugh wrote.

He concluded that the government’s law-enforcement interests had to be balanced against Omar’s constitutional rights as a member of Congress. The government ultimately agreed to a search protocol allowing Omar to invoke the privilege over any Speech or Debate Clause material before agents carried out the search.

The heavily redacted documents do not appear to shed new light on the reason or outcome of the search or to disclose new details about the immigration-fraud investigation that Omar says is politically motivated. Federal authorities are investigating potential fraud in her family’s immigration paperwork and have issued grand jury subpoenas to some relatives, the AP reported last month, citing a person briefed on the investigation who was not authorized to discuss it.

Omar, a naturalized U.S. citizen born in Somalia, has not been charged with any crimes and has denied wrongdoing. The AP reported that President Donald Trump and many Republican allies have targeted Omar with anti-immigrant rhetoric.

Chief Judge James Boasberg ordered the partial unsealing after more than a dozen news organizations, including the AP, argued that the public has a First Amendment right of access to the records. The filings were released in response to a lawsuit filed by the media organizations.

Boasberg agreed with the government that it was premature to unseal a 67-page search warrant affidavit containing sensitive details of the investigation. In a filing last month, the government said unsealing the court records before a charging decision would jeopardize the investigation.

An attorney for Omar did not immediately respond to an email seeking comment Monday on the unsealed documents.

The search occurred less than a month before the Nov. 3 midterm elections, which Omar won in her reelection bid. The Justice Department has historically avoided public investigative actions involving a candidate within 60 days of an election, the AP reported.

Omar has said Department of Homeland Security agents seized 17 items during the search, including phones, notebooks, a Somali-language children’s book and a vintage camera her husband had received as a gift.
