## Malaysia does not legally recognize refugees, leaving Rohingya vulnerable to detention

Nur Sadek was once on track to become an Islamic scholar. His mother dreamed of raising a son who would serve as a faith leader in their village in western Myanmar. That path ended in 2017, when he fled alongside loved ones after soldiers opened fire on his Rohingya community and bullets struck the tin roof of his home. He ran with nothing but the clothes on his back, he said, and has never returned.

Sadek's family belongs to the Rohingya, a predominantly Muslim ethnic minority that shares an area of Myanmar's Rakhine State with Buddhists and a smaller Hindu population. Ethnic and religious divisions have long shaped the region's history. About 700,000 Rohingya escaped a military crackdown that year and crossed into neighboring Bangladesh, according to the U.N. refugee agency, known as UNHCR. Others have sought refuge across Asia, including in India, Indonesia and Thailand.

As of February 2026, about 126,000 Rohingya were registered with UNHCR in Malaysia, a Muslim-majority country where Sadek now studies law and advocates for Rohingya rights. The country does not legally recognize refugees, leaving them vulnerable to detention. Refugees and advocacy groups say the challenges extend beyond immigration status: many Rohingya struggle to practice their faith and find a sense of belonging.

CSW, a religious freedom advocacy group, said in a statement to The Associated Press that Rohingya refugees in Malaysia cannot officially register their own places of worship. Most pray at local mosques, but some face discrimination or avoid mosques for fear of immigration detention, the group said. "Religious solidarity is often overtaken by social and cultural differences between the two groups," CSW said.

Nurul Hakim was the first of 10 siblings to flee Myanmar, leaving in 2010 and reaching Kuala Lumpur after days of travel on foot, by car and by boat through Thailand. His family was not directly attacked, he said, but years of violence and discrimination had made life increasingly difficult for his community. Rohingya Muslims in Rakhine State faced restrictions on religious gatherings, access to mosques and Islamic education for years, and authorities have closed mosques and imposed limits on communal prayer, according to reports by the United Nations and Amnesty International.

Hakim said he can now pray freely and feels welcomed by local Muslim faith leaders, but he has heard from Rohingya elsewhere in Malaysia who have been barred from doing so. Rohingya Rights Response, an organization co-founded by Sadek that monitors issues affecting refugees in Malaysia, has also documented restrictions on religious practices. In some neighborhoods, banners declaring that Rohingya are unwelcome have been displayed outside mosques.

Even where Rohingya can pray freely, some aspects of their religious life remain difficult to recreate far from home. "Most of the Rohingya don't have their own imam leading the prayers because the mosques are run by Malay imams," Sadek said.

Hakim said he misses his days pulling a rickshaw, trading goods with his neighbors and learning about Islam alongside fellow Rohingya. "There is no difference in praying at a different mosque, but there are differences with who you are praying with," he said. "When I was back in Myanmar and I went to pray, I could pray with my neighbors, with my villagers, and I don't have that here."

For others, the barrier to worship is more immediate: fear of detention over their lack of legal status. Mohammed Rafique said he now prays mostly at home and rarely visits a mosque because he worries about encountering immigration authorities. Rafique fled Myanmar in 2009 with his wife and five children, who stayed in Bangladesh while he continued on to Malaysia. He has not seen them since.

Rafique said living alone in a foreign land has been tough. He earns 30 ringgit ($7.50) a day collecting scrap materials, barely enough to pay for needed medication. The hardest moment he has faced, he said, was learning that his wife had died and being unable to say goodbye.

Sadek knows that pain. He lost his mother in Bangladesh and has yet to visit her grave. "I always wanted to do a lot of stuff, but now I just want to visit her cemetery and that has become like a dream for me," he said.

Sadek's organization advocates for changes that would improve the lives of Rohingya in Malaysia. Its main focus is safety, he said: beyond the risk of immigration detention, his team has documented harassment by social media content creators who mock and demean Rohingya refugees. The group also pushes for fair wages, access to health care and education.

For Zahidah Begum, those challenges are part of everyday life. Her husband struggles to work because of health problems, and her school-age children cannot attend formal classes. "I don't know what the future holds for us," she said. Begum fled Myanmar in 2013, carrying her own memories of religious persecution.

Her father, an imam, was a respected religious leader in their village who taught Islamic studies every day from 8 a.m. to 4 p.m. "I love my father because of what he did," she said. "He is the one who raised me, who taught me everything, including the Islamic teachings." It was her father who introduced her to the Quran and taught her to read and understand its verses, and to distinguish between what her faith permitted and forbade, she said.

During a raid on her village, soldiers tried to arrest him, Begum said. He fell and broke his leg as he tried to escape and eventually fled to Bangladesh, where he was unable to receive medical treatment. The injury became infected, and he died. "I miss every moment of my father," Begum said.

Begum keeps part of him alive through the lessons she passes on to her two daughters, teaching them to pray, to recite verses from the Quran and to make duas, or personal supplications to God, just as her father taught her. "I still feel the same connection to my faith that I felt back home," she said. "Even here, I remain connected to my religion."
