## Global index funds hold more US technology exposure than many expect

Investors had shown signs in recent weeks of getting cold feet over the outlook for AI companies, The Guardian said. Global equity funds and S&P 500 index trackers both fell in recent days after their large exposure to technology companies met the sell-off, AJ Bell's Dan Coatsworth said. Investors sold technology shares last week, sending the New York Stock Exchange and other markets lower as uncertainty grew around AI valuations. The Guardian reported that global markets tumbled Friday and the US Nasdaq closed 5% below its mark on Jan. 1 after Anthropic made a record-breaking stock-market debut. Monday trading had so far been calmer.

FRED's raw index readings for November 23, 2026, showed the [NASDAQ Composite Index](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/NASDAQCOM) at 23,879.2 and the [S&P 500 Index](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/SP500) at 7,175.83.

The Guardian said investors appeared to be losing faith in AI itself, or more specifically in the valuations of companies involved in the industry. It cited, among events that led to this month's sell-offs, the surprise scale of the Democrats' win in the US midterms, swiftly followed by the exit of Nvidia chief executive Jensen Huang alongside a person alleged to be a Chinese spy. The report said the rapid offloading of technology stocks suggested jitteriness among investors.

Many people own shares that are falling in value through pensions or other investment accounts, according to The Guardian. The companies experiencing the largest falls have dominated major indexes, carrying that concentration into passive funds. Coatsworth, AJ Bell's head of markets, said global equity funds and funds tracking the S&P 500 US stock market index are two of the most popular passive investments and that both had fallen in recent days. “Both have exposure to the giants of tech in a large way,” he said.

Coatsworth said US shares represented half of the MSCI World index 15 years ago but now make up 73%. Technology officially accounts for 31.8% of the index. Alphabet and Meta are classified as communication services, but adding those companies to technology raises the figure to 37.5%. The Guardian noted that investors who viewed a global fund as diversified may therefore have been surprised by their exposure to US technology companies.

The investment platform ii reports its customers' top holdings every quarter. Its latest report found global tracker funds well represented in customers' portfolios, while Scottish Mortgage, an actively managed investment trust, held SpaceX, Nvidia and Anthropic among other holdings. The Guardian also reported that some tracker funds hold Anthropic shares.

A falling share price does not become a realized loss until the investor sells and crystallizes it, The Guardian said. Investors who bought at different times may have no loss on paper at all. The FTSE 100 remained higher than it was last year, and Scottish Mortgage's shares, though falling since mid-October, were still well up from this time last year.

Holly Mackay, chief executive of Boring Money, urged investors to resist panicking. “Humans are programmed to fight or take flight when something goes wrong. However this instinct to take action is often the worst response to falling markets when doing nothing is the most sensible response,” she said.

Dan Coatsworth said recent sell-offs “may seem unpleasant, but it isn’t out of the ordinary. History shows that markets go through cycles and what’s unfolding in front of our eyes is par for the course.” He pointed to rebounds after falls in April 2025 following Donald Trump's tariff announcement and to earlier drops driven by inflation and the Covid pandemic.

Camilla Esmund, ii's head of investor campaigns, called the sell-off a timely reminder to review investment strategy, the level of risk taken and whether a portfolio was sufficiently diversified. “It is wise to diversify your portfolio across sectors, regions, and asset classes, so no single shock derails your progress,” she said. Diversification spreads risk and helps prevent overexposure to one area of the market, Esmund said.

For investors approaching retirement, Coatsworth said the key was to review investments to ensure they remained aligned with how the investor planned to take income from the pot. He then presented two ways to adjust technology exposure.

One option was to switch to a global equity fund that excludes the US, such as the iShares MSCI World ex-US ETF, which Coatsworth said invests in companies around the world outside the US. Technology stocks outside the US account for 10.9% of the portfolio, while the main holdings include banks, healthcare companies, oil producers and miners. Coatsworth said that mix would provide more sector balance and less dependence on technology, but Nvidia and other key names that led the US market rally in recent years would no longer be in the driving seat. If those stocks recovered, an investor using the fund would miss potential gains.

Coatsworth offered the Invesco MSCI World Equal Weight ETF as a possible middle ground. At the end of September, the US represented 40.6% of that investment, less than in a traditional global equity fund. “The investor still has their toe in the pond for US tech exposure, but they wouldn’t be up to their neck in the water,” he said.

Some investors saw falling prices as an opportunity. Earlier in the month, Hargreaves Lansdown reported that its clients were buying overseas technology shares after the post-midterm falls. Holly Mackay said many technology companies make a lot of money and have huge growth potential. “I expect some pain but I’m sitting tight and will actually look to buy in to any quality companies which are unfairly punished as some rattled investors rush for any exit,” she said.
