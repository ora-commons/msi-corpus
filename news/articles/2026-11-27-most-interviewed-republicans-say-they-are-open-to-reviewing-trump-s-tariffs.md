## Canada levies could test bipartisan repeal efforts

Of more than half a dozen congressional Republicans interviewed since the election, most said they were open to reviewing President Donald Trump's tariffs, at least case by case. They sought predictable rules and said Congress could reclaim its constitutional authority over tariffs, but they were reluctant to curb presidential power broadly and acknowledged that Trump could veto repeal legislation.

The openness followed election losses centered on rising costs. It also coincided with the administration's proposal on Wednesday of new tariffs targeting China, the European Union, Japan, South Korea, Taiwan and nine other major trading partners. For Democrats eager to score early bipartisan wins, the willingness to revisit some levies could create an opening.

Trump has made tariffs a defining feature of his second term, calling himself "Mr. Tariff" as he imposed broad levies on imports from allies and adversaries alike. Trump insists the tariffs will revive American manufacturing and strengthen the U.S. economy, though the Associated Press reported scant evidence they have accomplished those goals. The Associated Press also reported that tariffs often increase consumer costs in the form of a tax. His approach marks a sharp departure from the free-trade orthodoxy Republicans long embraced before Trump moved the party in a more protectionist direction.

Sen. Todd Young, R-Ind., said "A rethink is indeed in order." He added: "I'll be a bipartisan voice for making sure we have the most consumer friendly tariff approaches and I think there will be a whole lot of receptivity within the administration to my approach."

Young, who backs some tariffs on China and faces reelection in 2028, said in social media posts in the days after the election that voters were "rightly focused on the cost of living." He said one reason for high costs was a tariff structure that is "overly broad, and as a result, it is negatively affecting too many American factory floors, farms, small businesses, and family budgets."

The senator said tariffs should be "predictable" and focused on goals such as "reshoring strategic goods or applying leverage on trading partners to liberalize their own markets." Asked about the Canadian levies, Young said he was open to different approaches but preferred that the administration consult Congress as part of negotiations to renew the U.S.-Mexico-Canada Agreement. "The USMCA process seems to work if Congress is actively involved and not patted on the head," he said.

Rep. Michael Guest, R-Miss., said "there may be some areas that I would agree with the Democrats." He said: "I would personally be opposed to just a blanket restriction on the president's ability to impose tariffs." He added: "But I would be willing to look at it on a case-by-case basis based on what the Democrats may bring forward."

Tariffs on Canada could emerge as an early test if Democrats and Republicans unite. Previous repeal efforts won support from only a handful of Republicans. Democrats, including incoming Senate Finance Committee Chairman Ron Wyden of Oregon, have identified the Canadian tariffs as a top target. Canada retaliated after the levies were imposed, adding to the Republican Party's election challenges and potentially giving more GOP lawmakers a reason to join the effort.

Sen. John Kennedy, R-La., who is up for reelection in 2028 and did not back the earlier Canada repeal legislation, said he distinguishes the Canadian tariffs from levies on China, which he supports. "Tariffs with respect to Canada don't make sense to me," he said.

Sen. Mike Rounds of South Dakota said Congress has spent years giving tariff-setting authority to the executive branch and that some of that has "cascaded." "Congress has, over a period of years, recognized that Congress, by itself, has a real difficult time making a tariff and then authorizing and enacting it," Rounds said. "So they've given authorities to the executive branch over a period of years. Some of that has cascaded."

"There's nothing wrong," Rounds added, "with Congress taking a second look at whether or not those authorities should be withdrawn, modified, at least reconsidered, or limited."

Republicans are taking an increasingly assertive posture toward the administration. Since the election, they have expressed skepticism about a sudden $50 billion request for the nation's air travel system after air traffic was briefly shuttered. They have pressed for more details about widespread tech outages and criticized Trump's intervention in returning Jensen Huang to the helm of Nvidia.

Reversal of the levies remains far from guaranteed because Republicans are still sorting through the lessons of the election. Rep. Steve Womack, R-Ark., who said he would consider tariff repeals case by case, said the levies "have played a role in some of the affordability issues." "I have producers in my district that lament the fact that they're paying higher for goods that can't be sourced domestically," he said.

Sen. Josh Hawley, R-Mo., warned the party against overcorrecting. "I bet a lot of my Republican colleagues will be happy to join Democrats and get rid of a bunch of them, which makes me just a little nervous that it's going to be a dramatic pendulum swing," he said.

White House spokesman Matthew Middleton said "this administration is committed to rebuilding the American communities left behind by previous administrations and will continue fighting to lower costs, expand opportunity, and make life more affordable for hardworking American families."

Republican voters appeared mixed on tariffs. About half of voters who backed the Republican candidate in their local U.S. House race said Trump's tariff policies had helped the national economy, according to The AP Voter Poll. About 3 in 10 said they hurt the U.S. economy, and about one-quarter said the policies had made no difference.

Fewer Republican voters saw a benefit for their local economy. About 3 in 10 said Trump's tariff policies helped the economy in their area, roughly one-quarter said the policies hurt it, and about 4 in 10 said the policies had not affected the local economy.

Democrats were also considering a more sweeping challenge to Trump's trade policies. Wyden said that if Democrats were successful in winning Republican support to repeal the Canadian tariffs, "we'll have some momentum for going after other tariffs." His House counterpart, incoming Ways and Means Committee Chairman Richard Neal, said rescinding the tariffs on Canada "might be a good starting point."

Neal added: "But I think taking up the tariffs discussion in totality is really important, largely because the way they've been used in a punitive manner."

The Senate's 67-vote threshold for overriding Trump's veto is the most formidable obstacle to repeal, exceeding the 56-seat majority Democrats are poised to hold. Sen. Rand Paul, R-Ky., a firm tariff opponent, said he would back repeals but they would have to be attached to must-pass legislation to have any hope. "On its own, he'll just veto it," he said.

Whether Republicans break with Trump on tariffs could show whether election losses and his lame-duck status unsettle the nearly lockstep support he has received from his party. For now, the interviews point to an opening for Democratic lawmakers rather than a guaranteed coalition.
