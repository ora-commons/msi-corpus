## Haitian arrivals in Springfield estimated at 20,000 over five years

Haitian migrants in Springfield in south-west Ohio face TPS terminations and ICE monitoring, while the Trump administration has said it is conducting law-enforcement operations in the city. The BBC reported that an estimated 20,000 Haitians arrived over five years and that the Supreme Court upheld the administration’s decision to end Temporary Protected Status (TPS) for some 350,000 Haitians in the US.

In June, the Supreme Court upheld the Trump administration’s decision to end TPS for some 350,000 Haitians in the US. The administration first announced in June 2025 that it would remove TPS for Haitians, and the ensuing legal battle reached the Supreme Court, which ruled in the administration’s favor in the summer. In total, more than 1 million immigrants from 13 countries have had TPS terminated under the administration, moving many people from legal to unauthorized status overnight. TPS had given vetted individuals the right to live and work in the US; it usually lasts 18 months but is routinely rolled over, often for years, if danger remains in the affected country.

At a nearby grocery store run by Paschur Castelly, who left Haiti 20 years ago, most shelves were empty except for religious candles and framed pictures of Catholic saints popular with the community. Castelly said, “I can’t purchase new merchandise because I don’t have people coming in.” He added: “So, our communities, we have doctors, we have engineers… we came here to get a better life, to help our family in Haiti, you know? Our country doesn’t offer us security.” After 20 minutes, the store’s first customer arrived. The man, who asked to remain anonymous, said he had worked in a warehouse for three years. The BBC reported that, like others who had lost TPS, he was likely to have lost the right to work. As he chatted with Castelly, he pulled up his right trouser leg and revealed a large ankle monitor. ICE said the monitors were part of its Alternatives to Detention program. The customer said: “I was given a choice, wear the ankle monitor and report in every two weeks, or sign my deportation order.”

Pierre Damas Bel, 20, arrived in Springfield in 2024 to join his father and stepmother after fleeing Haiti’s political instability and gang warfare. He entered the US with the government’s permission under a now-defunct Biden-era program for migrants waiting in Mexico. According to a family friend, Bel then received TPS. The friend said Bel aspired to build a life in America and described him as an honours student, talented athlete and cadet in a high school program training students to become officers in the US Armed Forces.

After the June ruling, Bel was told to report to an Immigration and Customs Enforcement field office, where officers fitted him with an ankle monitor used to track migrants facing deportation. He wrote on Instagram that he felt “shame and humiliation” at having to wear the tag. On 31 August, he parked his car on the side of a highway near Springfield, walked into oncoming traffic and was killed. His family blamed his death on his feelings about having to wear the monitor. The Department of Homeland Security did not respond to BBC questions about Bel’s case, and ICE had previously rejected “any attempt to blame ICE for the death.” A spokesperson told US media earlier this year when asked about the case: “Remaining in the country illegally, while fighting removal, is a choice. Leaving with a free flight home and $2,600 is also a choice.”

In the weeks before his death, Bel attended a Springfield support centre for Haitians facing deportation. Its co-founder, Viles Dorsainvil, told the BBC that Bel was in the process of filing a motion asking ICE to remove the tag. Dorsainvil said: “He went twice to the ICE facilities… begging them to remove the ankle monitor.” He added: “He did everything right to be here until they put the tag on him.”

After the ruling, Department of Homeland Security general counsel James Percival said: “The T in TPS stands for TEMPORARY, yet many of these designations became de facto amnesty.” He added, “This is a win for the rule of law and common sense.” In an August social media post, the Department of Homeland Security posted a video showing Haitians in chains and white uniforms being led onto a deportation flight. The video was accompanied by jaunty music and told Haitians whose TPS had expired to “LEAVE IMMEDIATELY.” The Trump administration has said it is conducting law-enforcement operations in Springfield to keep Americans safe and has argued that Haiti is safe enough for people to return. Haiti remained subject to the US State Department’s highest Level 4 “Do Not Travel” danger warning.

Signs of decades of economic decline remain visible in Springfield through shuttered factories and boarded-up homes. The BBC said the American Midwest was hit badly by last century’s de-industrialization, when workers laid off from once-prolific factories sought work on the coasts or migrated to warmer southern states. Within the last five years, a Haitian population estimated at around 20,000 arrived in the town. Many fled the devastating earthquake in 2010, while others left Haiti in 2021 during the violence and unrest that followed President Jovenel Moise’s assassination. Their arrival rapidly increased the city’s population, and many held TPS while working low-paid factory and distribution-warehouse jobs. The BBC cited accounts that saw Springfield as “a well-known cycle in the story of modern America”: the decline of traditional industry, followed by a spike in immigration fuelled by cheap housing and low-paid labour. It called the sequence “a familiar tale across the country.”

Rick Johnson, who served in the Marines, has a porch adorned with the Stars and Stripes, a US Navy banner and a Marine Corps banner. His wife served in the Navy while he was in the Marines at Camp Pendleton in California; outside his service, he has lived in Springfield his whole life. He made it clear that he is a strong supporter of ICE’s deportation drive. “You get where we’re coming from,” he said. “We love America, we love Americans - any kind of Americans, but you kind of have to be American.”

Johnson moved into the neighborhood a decade ago and said the loss of industry had left much of it empty. “They’ve torn down every house around me, I don’t really have any neighbours,” he said. “The only neighbours I have are Haitians right here and they don’t really bother me.” The Haitian neighbors had moved in around four years earlier, Johnson added, “but there’s a lot of them.” He referenced “the driving problems.” His concern was the numbers and speed of change: “Really it’s the amount of them,” he told the BBC. “If you come here you [should] come here legally. The Temporary Protected Status is done… it’s time to go.”

The BBC reported that Johnson’s perception that traffic accidents increased after the Haitian community arrived was shared by other Springfield residents. In 2023, 11-year-old Aiden Clark was killed when his school bus overturned in a crash with a minivan. The minivan driver, Herman Joseph, was originally from Haiti, did not have a valid US driver’s licence and was jailed. At a Springfield City Commission meeting, Aiden’s father, Nathan Clark, said politicians had used his son’s death as a “political tool” to fuel anti-immigrant sentiment and said his son was accidentally killed.

Trump made securing the border and the mass deportation of undocumented migrants core pledges in his 2024 election campaign. Polls suggest that border policy continues to have strong support among Republicans, but the nature of his immigration enforcement campaign has seen a drop in backing this year. Ohio has become a swing state in the coming midterm Congressional elections, and one of two Republican-held Senate seats is up for grabs. Trump has visited the state twice already in an attempt to rally turnout and counter a slide in his approval ratings. At a rally in Youngstown, Ohio, earlier this month, Trump returned to the topic of Springfield and told supporters: “You have a little town, beautiful little town in Ohio where they dropped about 35,000 Haitians. Well, they’re being taken out and they’re almost going to be gone and we’re giving their town back.”

Springfield in south-west Ohio was thrust into the national spotlight in 2024 when Trump repeated a false claim that Haitian immigrants were eating pets. The BBC described the small town as “a symbol of modern America” and said the symbol “changes dramatically depending on who is describing it.” It attributed to Trump the view that Springfield “crystallises America’s problems due to years of unchecked immigration.” He now says his mass deportation drive means Springfield’s people are “getting their town back.” The BBC account included additional reporting by Gringo Wotshela.

As the BBC reporter left, Castelly closed the door he now keeps locked. A neighbor who feared an ICE raid shouted over to ask whether Castelly was all right. The scene came years after Trump brought Springfield in south-west Ohio into the national spotlight in 2024, and weeks before voters were set to head to the polls to shape the remainder of his presidency.
