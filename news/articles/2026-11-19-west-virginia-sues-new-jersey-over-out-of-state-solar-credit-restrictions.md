## The lawsuit estimates missed credits could cost producers as much as $296 million between 2027 and 2031

West Virginia sued New Jersey and its energy regulators Wednesday in New Jersey federal court, alleging that the state's Solar Renewable Energy Credit programs discriminate against generators outside New Jersey. The complaint says geographic restrictions block participation on the 13-state grid run by PJM Interconnection, violate interstate commerce protections and raise electricity prices for ratepayers.

Under the programs, power producers earn renewable-energy credits for each megawatt of renewable energy they generate. The credits can be sold separately from the electricity and are used by gas and other fossil-fuel plants to meet renewable-energy requirements under the Regional Greenhouse Gas Initiative and similar programs. West Virginia argues that the rules favor New Jersey solar installations and force its own generators to sell power to ratepayers who voluntarily purchase more renewable energy than states require.

New Jersey permits generators in other states on PJM's grid to obtain credits through separate programs for wind, tidal, methane and other types of generation. Its rules for solar renewable energy credits exclude out-of-state generators by requiring their plants to be physically tied to New Jersey's energy infrastructure, according to the lawsuit. West Virginia says that exclusion makes the state's solar installations artificially profitable by comparison.

West Virginia framed the restrictions in the complaint as economic protectionism. “The geographic restrictions serve a purely economic purpose: channeling solar investment and jobs to New Jersey at the expense of out-of-state competitors,” the state said in its lawsuit. “That is precisely the kind of economic protectionism the Commerce Clause forbids.”

The financial estimates in the complaint depend on a wide gap between voluntary-market prices and New Jersey's solar-credit prices. Voluntary markets ranged from about $2 to $8 per megawatt hour, compared with $88.94 to $105.74 through New Jersey's solar credits. West Virginia says the exclusions have cost its producers between $20 million and $22 million in missed credits and could cost them as much as $296 million between 2027 and 2031.

West Virginia Attorney General JB McCuskey said in a statement: “We should all be working toward a more efficient and reliable system, but rules like these stand in the way of progress, and ultimately all households and businesses pay the price.” He said New Jersey's rules had raised electricity prices for West Virginia ratepayers because the state's utilities would have passed savings from the credits onto consumers.

The lawsuit also says New Jersey has failed to meet its own annual solar-procurement goals. The state targets 300 megawatts of new solar annually but met that goal only once between 2022 and 2025, according to the complaint.

Michael Symons, a spokesperson for New Jersey Attorney General Jennifer Davenport, said the state would defend the programs. “We will continue to work with Governor Sherrill and her administration to support the development of renewable energy and drive costs down for our state's residents,” Symons said. “We look forward to defending against this lawsuit in court.”

Spokespeople for Gov. Mikie Sherrill declined to comment on the pending litigation. Sherrill made energy affordability a key focus during her campaign and first 10 months in office.
