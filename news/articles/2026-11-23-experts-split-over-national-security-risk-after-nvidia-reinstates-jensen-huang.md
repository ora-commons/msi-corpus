## Alperovitch asks what Huang knew about chip smuggling and Commerce Department calls

Nvidia's board reinstated Jensen Huang as chief executive before an independent investigation into allegations involving him had concluded, and two experts who joined PBS News on Nov. 23 disagreed sharply about what the return meant for national security. Host Amna Nawaz asked Dmitri Alperovitch and James Lewis whether Huang's return created a national-security risk; Alperovitch called the decision "incomprehensible" and Lewis called the risk to national security marginal. Their disagreement centers on what Huang told Alice Huang, what he knew about chips that were not supposed to go to China, and what his general counsel said to the Department of Commerce.

Alperovitch co-founded CrowdStrike and was its chief technology officer; he is now co-founder and chairman of Silverado Policy Accelerator, a Washington, D.C., think tank. Lewis is a former diplomat with extensive intelligence experience who is now with the tech policy program at the Center for European Policy Analysis, another Washington think tank.

"It is incomprehensible, because, of course, there's still allegations here that are going to be investigated," Alperovitch said. Less than two weeks earlier, Nawaz had asked him what the board should do after revelations of Huang's affair, when he said the board had to act immediately. Alperovitch said Huang had lied to the board about the affair and had potentially lied about interference with the Commerce Department's investigation into the smuggling activity of the company Alice Huang led. Reinstating Huang before the independent investigation was concluded, Alperovitch said, left the board facing the prospect of firing him again if the investigation returned troubling information — "What are you going to do, fire him again?" He also acknowledged the difficulty of refusing the president of the United States: when the president calls into a board meeting, as has been alleged, and asks the board to reinstate Huang, "the most powerful man in the world telling you this, it's hard to say no," Alperovitch said.

Lewis compared Huang's position to Henry Ford's, saying "You can't fire this guy and not expect there to be great turmoil, both in the market and in America's AI effort." Lewis said Huang was at the center of the AI development program in the United States and was irreplaceable, and that he was surprised the board took Huang back — not because of the "bogus national security claims," he said, but because an affair with a subordinate usually ends a chief executive's tenure. "It must have been pretty persuasive, whatever Trump said," Lewis said.

Nawaz noted that stocks surged after the decision and that technology leaders had called for Huang's reinstatement. Alperovitch said "I don't think anyone's replaceable," citing the line attributed to Charles de Gaulle that "the cemetery is full of irreplaceable people." Nvidia's chips "were still the best chips in the world," he said, and the company "would have been fine" without Huang; at most, the next generation of chips might have been delayed or fallen short, he said, noting that Google and Amazon are among the American companies building great chips and that "all the AI companies are building their own chips." He asked whether the country has an exception for geniuses who can break the law and violate corporate policy, saying the episode "reaffirms that there are two classes in America" — one set of rules for the rich and powerful and for friends of the president, another for everyone else.

Lewis called the reinstatement "unusual" and said he would have waited for the investigation to be completed before Huang was fired. The board "panicked, it looks like, and now they have kind of panicked in the other direction," he said, adding that the sequence was not encouraging. Lewis said Huang was central to the financial deals that create AI capabilities and to the AI movement in the United States, though "irreplaceable is a kind of strange word."

On the security question, Nawaz said, as Liz reported, the Nvidia board said it had assurances of some kind from senior government officials, and the president also said he had given assurances, even as investigations continued into what kind of national-security risk the case poses. Asked whether anyone could be sure there is no national-security risk, Alperovitch said, "I don't think we know much yet."

Alperovitch pointed to a New York Times story published the night before the discussion, which alleged that Alice Huang had an ongoing relationship with the Chinese Ministry of State Security — "essentially the Chinese version of CIA," as he put it — and that her husband apparently had longstanding relationships with the agency as well. If that account is true, Alperovitch said, the ministry would have used Alice Huang as a "co-optee," telling her to ask questions and solicit information from Jensen Huang. As a result, he said, it is unknown what information Huang may have passed, either about Nvidia or maybe about U.S. government policy, and whether he was knowingly involved in the smuggling of chips that Alice Huang was involved in. Alperovitch said tens of billions of dollars of chips "were not supposed to go to China, illegal for them to go to China," and that she was involved in that activity, raising very troubling questions. The good news, he said, is that Nvidia's compliance culture "is going to be fixed because they brought in an outsider as a C.O."

Lewis said the implications were bogus because "we don't know what was transferred." "Unless you show me a picture of her — Jensen Huang handing her a thumb drive — I think it's minimal," he said, asking whether the same information could be obtained through "their subscription to The Financial Times." He said most of what Alice Huang heard and knew was publicly available, that Huang is not, in his view, a complete fool, and that he doubted Huang said anything useful to her. "It's just not a big contributor to national security risk," Lewis said.

Lewis, who said he worked with the MSS for about 15 years, called the ministry's recruitment "a trophy recruit" — "It sounds great. I got Jensen Huang's girlfriend. What did they get out of her? Nothing." Alperovitch responded that men do not always behave rationally when they are dealing with an affair with a pretty woman. Nawaz put the wider question to Lewis: with the president intervening in a private company to push for the reinstatement of a CEO whose degree of national-security risk is not yet known, and with investigations continuing around him, does any part of that concern him? Lewis said the only part that concerns him "is, this is a job for the divorce courts and for corporate law" — Huang probably should not have been reinstated after an affair with a subordinate, which is unusual. Congressional hearings might show the Chinese got "amazing degrees of secrets," he said, but he doubted it, and he concluded the risk to national security "is marginal." Lewis attributed the scrutiny to attitudes rather than evidence: "People don't like Trump, they don't like China, and they don't like Huang, a trifecta. And that's why they went after him. This is inside baseball in Washington. It's not real national security stuff."

Nawaz asked whether Huang will still appear on Capitol Hill for closed-door meetings and a public hearing, and what questions should be answered. Lewis said he hopes Huang "has a pretty thick hide, because that gotcha is the middle name for Congress," and that lawmakers will be "beating up on him, without a doubt." What he wants to hear, Lewis said, is what exactly Huang told Alice Huang: if he gave her technical information or information that was not public, there is concern, which might be commercial. That, he said, is standard MSS practice, and "it doesn't look like they got very much," even if it looked good in the ministry's annual report.

Alperovitch said he has two quick questions for Congress: what Huang told Alice Huang and, more importantly, what he facilitated in smuggling chips into China in violation of U.S. export controls and what he knew about it. He also asked what Huang knew about his general counsel calling the Department of Commerce to shut down an investigation into her company. "A lot of troubling questions here," Alperovitch said, adding that he hopes Huang "has got good legal representation," and noting that Huang "might even plead the Fifth."

The panel did not establish that sensitive information was transferred. Lewis called the risk to national security marginal and said the affair and the governance question belong in corporate law. Alperovitch said investigators still have to determine what information, if any, moved, whether Huang facilitated smuggling, and what his general counsel did, and he called the reinstatement incomprehensible while the independent investigation was still pending.
