## Shell, BP and other FTSE 100 multinationals draw Pratley's flight-risk warning

A Morgan Stanley analyst calculated that Compass Group traded at a valuation premium to its closest US peer Aramark and to Sodexo in Europe for most of the last decade. Pratley said the calculation undercut a standard rationale for changing a primary listing, under which a company says its shares trade at a discount to competitors. Compass instead describes its planned New York move as a “natural evolution,” while its board says the change would “strengthen its competitive position and support its next phase of growth.”

Pratley called Compass's stateside expansion over the past couple of decades remarkable but described the competitive case for the relocation as marginal at best. He noted that the group operates sub-brands in local markets and that owners of US sports stadiums did not appear concerned that the firm serving Budweisers in corporate boxes had a London-listed parent. The question he raised was whether Compass really needed to move its listing to strengthen its competitive position and support its next phase of growth, as the board says.

He contrasted that argument with an earlier era in which success in the US gave UK companies a reason to champion their roots, recalling a 1980s Hanson Trust television advertisement: “A company from over here that’s also doing rather well over there.”

Compass is now the world's biggest contract caterer and the 18th-largest company in the FTSE 100. Pratley described it as one of the few FTSE 100 companies that was the biggest in the world in its market, contract catering, which includes public-sector work and service for thousands of corporate clients. The group built part of its business on contracts to serve dinners in British schools and run catering operations in NHS hospitals. Its plan is to switch its primary stock-market listing to New York in 2028. Pratley called Compass a bad one for the London market to lose and framed the switch as a significant hit to the London Stock Exchange.

North America now generates two-thirds of Compass's revenues and three-quarters of its profits, the scale behind the company's “natural evolution” description. But Pratley called the dangerous part of the decision that it advances one step further the notion that, if a company makes its biggest chunk of money in the US, that is where its primary listing should be. Compass itself had not previously been viewed as a flight risk because it had downplayed the idea. Pratley said one could wonder whether megabucks pay at US companies had entered the thinking.

He said the shift reflects a growing mood in UK boardrooms. Flutter, the betting firm; Ashtead, the equipment-rental group; and CRH, the building-materials group have already played what he called the geography card. Pratley then named Shell, BP, British American Tobacco, Diageo, Relx, Experian, Smith & Nephew and LSEG, which owns the London Stock Exchange, as multinationals that could take a similar approach. He cautioned that they would not all move, but said a government concerned about the exodus needed to offer more than what he called ineffectual words. His question was what was being done to discourage others from delisting.

AstraZeneca has adopted an unconventional “harmonised” global listing, and Pratley said he suspected trading in its shares would gravitate toward New York. He said listing changes such as AstraZeneca's were set to take chunks out of the government's £3bn-a-year receipts. He also said Chancellor John Healey offered only yet another “under review” message in his budget.

London Stock Exchange chief executive Dame Julia Hoggett made what Pratley called fair points in a recent BBC interview, although it was traditional to criticize the exchange. Listing rules have been reformed to allow, among other things, companies such as Compass to have a dollar-denominated share price while remaining in the index; the exchange's research operation has undergone a shake-up; and corporate-governance requirements have been made more flexible.

Hoggett said the London Stock Exchange does not set tax incentives to encourage domestic investment by pension funds and cannot change stamp duty. The levy is 0.5% on share purchases; Pratley called it “hopelessly uncompetitive by international standards,” a point he said he had argued in the column many times, and said it mostly hits retail investors and UK pension funds. He described the survival of the levy as the most visible sign of what he called Treasury orthodoxy.

On capital export, Hoggett said: “We talk about growth in every postcode, but too much of our capital is currently funding growth in every ZIP code.” Pratley called the point fair but said the government, like recent predecessors, had done very little to encourage companies to stay listed in London when they had the option to move. He said the focus had been on directing pension money into private markets and infrastructure, with little attention to public markets. He also deplored bland corporate thinking.

Pratley concluded that events were moving fast and that Compass's unexpected departure was sending a worrying signal as corporate moves accelerated. His broader warning was that the listing move could encourage other large multinationals to place primary listings in the US when most of their revenue and profits come from there, while the government had offered what he called ineffectual words.
