## Jha says some children and adults may need an extra dose

The United States has lost its measles elimination status, according to Dr. Ashish Jha, who discussed the milestone with NPR on November 17, 2026. Jha, a senior fellow at Harvard's Belfer Center and a former White House COVID response coordinator, called the development "a sad day for public health in America" and said the country was backsliding while other countries moved forward.

Host Scott Detrow said public health officials had described the development as sad but expected. He asked how much responsibility the federal government bore, and Jha said the problem did not begin under the current administration but had gotten "dramatically worse" under it. Detrow also said NPR had previously reported that Secretary Kennedy is a vaccine skeptic, had downplayed the outbreak and questioned the deaths. Jha said there was "no question in my mind" that Kennedy had done "a completely inadequate job of addressing this problem."

Jha said the last two years had brought more measles cases than the previous 24 years combined. He cited disruptions during the pandemic that made it harder for people to reach doctors, and said COVID-19 vaccines had given the anti-vaccine movement a pretext to oppose vaccines more broadly. He also said Secretary Kennedy's becoming health secretary had only made the situation worse, describing Kennedy as "the biggest crusader against vaccines, globally."

Jha said most parents still get their children vaccinated, though he said enough parents were not vaccinating their children to leave the country in its current predicament. Asked what public health officials could have done differently, he said communication during the COVID-19 pandemic could have been better, including expressing certainty when officials did not have it, and that the public health community had not reflected enough on how the situation went awry.

Jha told parents that commitment to vaccination on schedule does not remove every risk. He said children lose immunity from their mothers at about three months and remain vulnerable until their first shot at 12 to 15 months. If measles is spreading without control, he said, children whose parents intend to vaccinate them can still be at risk.

Jha said he was hearing pediatricians argue that children may need an extra measles dose. He also said some adults who received a vaccine when only one dose was given probably need an extra dose because they did not receive two doses. He called for public health officials to engage parents who are skeptical or worried about vaccines, hear their concerns and help them make better choices for their children.

When a measles outbreak is nearby, Jha advised parents to be more careful and to consult a pediatrician early about vaccination. He said an earlier dose is safe and doable even though the preferred timing for children's immunity had been thought to be 12 to 15 months. He described measles as "the most contagious human virus that we know of" and said it would be difficult to avoid completely, requiring a community-wide effort to reduce cases.

Jha warned that lower measles vaccination is accompanied by avoidance of other childhood vaccines. He cited a huge outbreak of whooping cough and increasing rubella and mumps, and said he was worried about more cases of polio. He said the federal government needed to act and that Congress would have to hold the administration accountable.

Asked about the United States' standing after decades as a global public-health leader, Detrow said he wanted to know what the loss of measles-free status would mean for the country's reputation. Jha said its stature in the global community had taken "a huge hit" over the previous two years. He said colleagues and friends abroad were bewildered by what he characterized as America's decision to turn over its leadership role, though he hoped the setback would be temporary. He identified Germany, France and China as possible providers of greater global leadership, saying China had already filled gaps left by the United States. Jha said he hoped the country could return to doing and implementing great science in its own communities.

Jha closed by telling parents to speak with neighbors and friends even if they were fully committed to vaccination. Federal and state governments have a large role, he said, but protecting children would also require community-level, one-on-one conversations that encourage families to protect their children. He said reducing cases would require that broader community effort.
