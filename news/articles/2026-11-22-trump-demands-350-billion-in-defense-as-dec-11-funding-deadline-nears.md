## Two outgoing Senate Republicans block Trump nominees, including GAO pick

Congress is already on recess and will return to a Dec. 11 funding deadline, with only weeks to go before Democrats take control of both chambers. President Donald Trump is demanding $350 billion in new defense spending, and lawmakers will have limited room when they come back.

Eric McDaniel, NPR's congressional reporter, told host Ayesha Rascoe that a shutdown is "definitely possible," while doubting the defense figure would survive. "I don't think we'll see 350 billion in new Pentagon spending," he said.

McDaniel said lawmakers understand the stakes. The federal government is the nation's largest employer, he said, and the election just held was one in which the public rebuked a Congress that "dragged it through the longest shutdown in U.S. history."

He said some 90% of the public believes the government is corrupt and that lawmakers advocate for their own self-interests rather than the public's, a view he tied to the high cost of living.

Democrats are loath to fund the president's war in Iran, which McDaniel described as unpopular, but they do not hold full control until January. That makes the likely calculation short-term funding — keeping "their powder dry until the new year," he said.

Funding is not the only friction between the White House and the Senate. Two outgoing Senate Republicans have blocked Trump nominees. John Cornyn of Texas blocked an official from the Justice Department from a seat on a U.S. district court, and North Carolina's Thom Tillis blocked two nominees, one for a key U.S. cybersecurity agency and another to lead the Government Accountability Office.

McDaniel called the GAO vacancy a big deal. The office is the accountability branch that helps Congress with its oversight responsibilities, he said, a key constitutional obligation for lawmakers. Trump and allies were trying to install a figure that Democrats, and some Republicans, viewed as too partisan.

The other objection from lawmakers, McDaniel said, was that the nomination was another example of the White House and executive branch trying to yank control away from Congress, something he said has happened on everything from the power of the purse to the power to declare war. "That power grab just wasn't going to fly," he said.

Senate Republicans will still be laser-focused on confirming as many judges and other presidential appointees — ambassadors among them — as they can before they lose their gavels in January, McDaniel said.

The White House has also moved on what federal contractors tell Congress. On Friday, the president issued a new executive order barring federal contractors from sharing information with Congress without instructions from the White House.

Asked whether the order has teeth, McDaniel declined to assess its legal force. "I'm not a lawyer. I'm not that smart," he said, adding that the White House can do anything it wants to try to prevent people from complying with the legal investigatory powers of Congress.

But contractors and others outside government would be taking individual legal risk the administration cannot insulate them from, he said — legal bills, time, a potential contempt of Congress vote and reputational cost. "Getting dragged before Congress is no small thing," he said.

McDaniel pointed to Robert Garcia, the incoming House Oversight Committee chairman, who posted "lawyer up" when Democrats had won the majority. Garcia's party and its investigatory committees "aren't going to be timid at all at pursuing their legal avenues, no matter what executive orders the president issues," McDaniel said.

Trump is expected to head to Mar-a-Lago this week for Thanksgiving while Congress remains out of session.

The deadline those votes set is the one Congress now confronts, with the defense-spending demand and the shutdown question still unresolved.
