## Commerce proposes license rule for chip exports to five countries

**Subtype:** fact

- The Commerce Department proposed a rule requiring companies to obtain licenses before shipping Nvidia and other U.S. chips to Malaysia, Thailand, Indonesia, Singapore and Brazil.
- The Bureau of Industry and Security said the rule was "written by and at the direction of the Department of Treasury and the National Security Council."
- The rule targets companies like Megaspeed International that use Nvidia chips to provide computing power to Chinese tech giants through Southeast Asian data centers.
- Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said Treasury and other agencies had pushed for action on Megaspeed and chip exports for months while Commerce stood in the way.
- Chris McGuire of the Council on Foreign Relations called it unprecedented for a Commerce Department rule to be written by the National Security Council and the Treasury Department.

## Treasury and the National Security Council wrote the rule, agency says

The proposed rule would add Malaysia, Thailand, Indonesia, Singapore and Brazil to a list of countries subject to export restrictions. Companies would need licenses from the Commerce Department to ship to those countries, a step intended to prevent Chinese customers from accessing data-center capacity in those countries remotely. U.S. tech companies could get exceptions.

The stated goal is to crack down on companies like Megaspeed International, where Alice Huang, a top executive who was suspected of being a Chinese intelligence asset, was having an affair with Jensen Huang, who stepped down as Nvidia CEO last week. Companies like Megaspeed have used billions of dollars' worth of Nvidia chips to build data centers throughout Southeast Asia and provide computing power to Chinese tech giants seeking to catch up in the AI race.

"The rule is designed to ensure that existing restrictions on access to sensitive American technology cannot be circumvented through remote computing arrangements involving data centers in third countries," the Commerce Department's Bureau of Industry and Security said in a release.

The agency has been under pressure from Bessent and other officials who have accused it of moving too slowly to address export-control loopholes. In one sign of that strain, the release said the rule was "written by and at the direction of the Department of Treasury and the National Security Council." Export-control analysts said it was an unusual step for other agencies to take control of the rule-making process from BIS, which is normally in charge of chip export measures.

"It is unprecedented for a Commerce Department rule to be written by the National Security Council and the Treasury Department," said Chris McGuire, a senior fellow at the Council on Foreign Relations who previously worked on tech and security issues at the State Department. "The fact that the Commerce Department press release says this shows that Commerce resisted publishing this rule to the bitter end, and was forced to do so by the White House."

Bessent last week said that Treasury and other agencies had been pushing for action on Megaspeed and chip exports for months but Commerce stood in the way. The turmoil has increased pressure on Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick, who is receiving much of the blame inside the administration, The Wall Street Journal previously reported. The Commerce Department didn't respond to a request for comment on the dynamics between agencies.

Jensen Huang lobbied President Trump to allow Nvidia to send some chips directly to China and for a loose export-control regime, arguing that U.S. customers competing abroad would combat Chinese competitors.

BIS typically writes draft rules, then circulates them for feedback to the State and Treasury Departments and National Security Council. National security adviser Marco Rubio, who is also secretary of state, has pushed for tighter chip controls alongside Bessent.

The officials were among those frustrated when Megaspeed and other companies suspected of chip diversion were candidates to be added to a trade blacklist known as the Entity List but Commerce didn't move forward, the Journal previously reported. Administration officials have said the agency is wary of upsetting China during trade talks.

Lutnick is joining Trump on his trip to China this week, as is Bessent. Trump defended his commerce secretary on Monday, saying that he has done a good job and that delaying action on Megaspeed made sense despite the objections of advisers the Journal described as hawkish, like Bessent.

"He was doing it because if you didn't delay it, you could have a blowup with China," Trump said. "We're having a very good relationship with China right now."

The measure released by Commerce would also make it more difficult for data-center companies to provide Chinese companies remote access to AI chips anywhere in the world, and make it harder for Chinese chip designers to work with TSMC, the world's leading manufacturer.

Lawmakers are also pushing for stronger chip export rules, proposing several measures as part of this year's defense-spending package.
