## Alpöge said “Claude far outpaced me near the finish line”

Two independent groups announced that they had “full confidence” in Levent Alpöge’s claimed proof of the Riemann Hypothesis, according to The Wall Street Journal. Their statements came the next day, after mathematicians around the world began trying to verify all 17 million lines of the proof’s code. The groups wrote, “We congratulate him on this achievement.” The Journal said that if the proof withstood further scrutiny, the achievement would make the 34-year-old Anthropic employee one of the most prominent mathematicians in history.

Alpöge claimed that he and Anthropic’s Claude had discovered a proof of the Riemann Hypothesis. The Wall Street Journal described the problem as the single most famous in all of mathematics. The hypothesis concerns the distribution of prime numbers and was proposed by Bernhard Riemann in 1859. The problem had remained unsolved for 167 years, and Alpöge had pursued it since childhood.

Last week, Alpöge said he felt he and Claude were closing in on a solution. Days before Anthropic’s trillion-dollar IPO, he sent Anthropic chief executive Dario Amodei a message that began, “Hypothetically,” followed by, “if I were to have…” He held off publishing until the day after the company went public. That morning, he placed the proof’s code and the four-page `remark.pdf` on his personal website.

Alpöge wrote, “If I could have, I’d have finished this all much earlier.” He also wrote, “I just could not live,” followed by, “if I waited even longer.” The timing nevertheless placed the announcement immediately after Anthropic’s public listing, which the Journal described as the largest initial public offering in history.

The Wall Street Journal said the collaboration appeared to be the most dazzling example yet of humans and AI pushing the frontier of knowledge. The report said many in the mathematics world viewed Alpöge and Claude’s work as a preview of “centaur science,” in which humans work with machines to make discoveries that neither could have accomplished alone. The account portrayed Claude as a collaborator operating under Alpöge’s direction rather than an autonomous solver.

The Wall Street Journal said Claude was not working autonomously. Alpöge’s years of preparation and deep understanding of the Riemann Hypothesis allowed him to steer the model in the right direction. Because Alpöge has not revealed his prompts or compute budget, it remains unclear exactly who did what.

Alpöge wrote, “Claude and I,” followed by, “had a great time together.” He also wrote, “Claude far outpaced me near the finish line.” Describing the model’s final contributions, he wrote, “Claude flew, and danced, and sparkled near that finish line.” Alpöge acknowledged that he did not yet understand all the mathematics in his own proof and said he was trying to digest it with other humans. He said he pulled all-nighters and watched his collaborator in awe as the result came into view.

Alpöge also said Claude could not turn the result into readable prose. When he asked the model to write the proof in words, he said it produced 250,000 pages of what he called an “unreadable travesty of ‘slop.’”

Anthropic declined to comment and did not make Alpöge available to comment beyond his papers and social-media posts. Alpöge describes himself as an “old-school mathematician” who happens to work at the bleeding edge of the field. He speaks six languages and hopes to add Latin and ancient Greek.

The result came sooner than some forecasts had anticipated. The Wall Street Journal said some forecasters had predicted that the Riemann Hypothesis would stand until 2035. Terence Tao, widely considered the world’s finest mathematician, theorized last month that humans and AI might be able to conquer it by 2050. The report said Alpöge was ahead of schedule.

Alpöge’s background gave him years of preparation before he joined Anthropic. Born in New York to Turkish parents, his mother noticed him reading at age 3. He learned arithmetic from his sister in the back seat during long car rides. Alpöge says he has synesthesia, meaning he experiences letters and numbers in color. In school, he familiarized himself with tools he might need to tackle the Riemann Hypothesis and figured he had decades; as it turns out, he miscalculated.

As a high-school student on Long Island, Alpöge used Nvidia graphics processing units to analyze medical scans long before those chips would power the AI boom. He was selected as a finalist in the Intel Science Talent Search, whose reward included putting his name on the asteroid now known as 25898 Alpoge.

Alpöge graduated from Harvard in 2014 with a bachelor’s degree in math and a master’s degree in physics, finishing at the very top of his class with the highest GPA. He won the prize for America’s top young math researcher, and his résumé lists an International Mathematical Olympiad gold medal. He trained for marathons by waking at 5 a.m. He told CNN Türk, “My dream,” followed by, “is to solve the Riemann Hypothesis.”

The Wall Street Journal said few mathematicians were better positioned to actually solve the problem. At summer research programs in college, Alpöge gravitated to hard problems and stood out for his ability to simplify the complex into elegant work. Williams College professor Steven J. Miller wrote in a recommendation, “Of all the students I have known, he has the greatest potential to be a leader in mathematics.”

Alpöge studied number theory, probability and combinatorics before writing a prize-winning thesis with an epigraph from a different 19th-century German mathematician: “God made the integers; all else is the work of man.” His adviser, Jacob Tsimerman, who recently won the Fields Medal, math’s top honor, and pivoted to AI safety, said, “It’s always been clear that he’s very passionate about math.” Alpöge’s work earned him a scholarship to Cambridge, a place in Princeton’s Ph.D. program and an elite fellowship back at Harvard. He earned his Ph.D. in math from Princeton before taking a job at Anthropic.

Alpöge left academia for artificial intelligence when OpenAI released its GPT-4 model in 2023, the Journal said, as he saw the future of math. “To me,” he later wrote, “it was instantly the most interesting thing humanity had ever created.” In 2024, he started at Anthropic to work on AI—and with AI.

During the summer of 2026, The Wall Street Journal described Alpöge as a frenetic presence on X who had made a habit of dropping major results without the decorum his peers expected. On a Sunday night in July, he wrote, “hello there the jacobian conjecture is false,” announcing that Anthropic’s top model had found a counterexample to the 87-year-old riddle of algebraic geometry that day while billions of people watched the World Cup final. On Aug. 31, he posted the two words “Augustus Mirabilis.” OpenAI took the cryptic message to mean that something amazing had happened: Anthropic must have solved a Millennium Prize Problem.

In 2000, seven exceptionally difficult problems were designated as the Millennium Prize Problems, each carrying a $1 million reward; only one had been solved. After Alpöge’s post, amid rumors that Anthropic was on the verge of a solution, and perhaps two, OpenAI began work of its own. On Sept. 1, the company put a powerful model and millions of dollars in computing resources to work on a group of mathematical problems. A week later, OpenAI unveiled a claimed solution to the Navier-Stokes problem. For a year, Alpöge had been quietly working on the same problem with an NYU mathematician, and The Wall Street Journal said the resulting proof prompted a dispute over credit that left him in the middle.

In October, The Wall Street Journal reported that mathematicians were still dealing with the Millennium Prize dispute when OpenAI released hundreds of results simultaneously. Alpöge called the announcement “obviously the most significant moment in mathematical history.” The Journal described a resolution of the Riemann Hypothesis as the biggest prize for the time being; Alpöge had pursued the problem since childhood.

The latest confidence announcements add support without settling the claim. The Wall Street Journal said the proof remained conditional on further scrutiny, while Alpöge had not disclosed his prompts or compute budget and Anthropic declined to comment. The published code, the four-page explanation and the two groups’ statements form the current basis for evaluating the claim.

The two groups’ public confidence is the next development in that verification process, though the claimed proof still faces further scrutiny.
