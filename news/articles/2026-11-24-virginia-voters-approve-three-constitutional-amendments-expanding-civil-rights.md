## Virginia voters approve three constitutional amendments expanding civil rights

**Subtype:** fact

- Virginia voters approved three constitutional amendments on 3 November, protecting abortion and other reproductive care, restoring voting rights for people with past felony convictions who have served their time and establishing marriage equality.
- Virginia voters passed Question 1 with 62% of the vote, protecting abortion, contraception, childbirth, miscarriage management and fertility care, including IVF.
- Virginia voters passed Question 3, reversing a lifetime voting ban for people with past felony convictions who have served their time; the ban dates to the mid-1800s.
- Virginia voters passed the marriage-equality referendum with 57%, the same margin as the vote establishing the ban 20 years earlier.
- Virginia became the fifth state to repeal a constitutional ban on same-sex marriage, following Nevada, California, Colorado and Hawaii.
- Equality Virginia said it would advocate for HIV-prevention funding and gender-affirming care for trans youth and mobilize voters for the next legislative election cycle.

## Campaign organizers outline access, outreach and legislative next steps

Virginia voters approved three constitutional amendments on 3 November, establishing protections for abortion and other reproductive care, same-sex marriage and voting rights for people with past felony convictions who have served their time. The Guardian said the measures protected civil liberties that had eroded during Trump’s second term and described Virginia as no exception to historic gains for progressives across the South during the midterm elections. Women and Black organizers who led the campaigns said they wanted to capitalize on the momentum and continue advocating for policies that expand the protections.

In Suffolk on 3 November, veteran Sam Harris cast his first vote after recently having his voting rights restored, with his sister Pamela Copeland assisting him, in a photograph by Peter Casey for The Virginian-Pilot via Getty Images. Question 3 concerned voting rights for people with past felony convictions who have served their time.

Question 1 passed with 62% of the vote on 3 November. The amendment established protections not only for abortion but also contraception, childbirth, miscarriage management and fertility care, including IVF. The Guardian reported that, since the Supreme Court overturned Roe v Wade in 2022, abortion bans have swept the nation, particularly in the South, and that Virginia has become the only state without extreme restrictions on the procedure. A photograph by Jemal Countess for UPI via Shutterstock showed abortion-rights advocates protesting in Fairfax after Roe v Wade was overturned in June 2022.

The campaign for Question 1 was built by Virginians for Reproductive Freedom, a coalition of 30 local and national groups serving different constituencies, including advocacy and health-care organizations. Some groups handled Spanish-language canvassing, while others managed outreach to youth voters. Some 850 volunteers knocked on 45,000 doors across the commonwealth. Han Jones, the coalition’s campaign director, said its depth and diversity were pivotal to its success. Jones said much of the campaign focused on educating voters about Question 1 and the protections at stake, along with dispelling misinformation from anti-abortion groups about how the amendment would put women’s health at risk. Jones said the campaign could not assume that support for abortion and reproductive rights would translate into a majority at the ballot.

Abortion-rights advocates said constitutional protection did not necessarily make reproductive care available, particularly given the cost of abortion, contraceptives and IVF. “There’s a lot of work ahead to make sure this amendment is properly implemented,” Jones said. “We have to make sure Virginians don’t just have the right to reproductive care but also access to it.” At a briefing on 18 November, organizers described fundraising efforts for abortion funds, forthcoming lobbying sessions with legislators to ensure the law was properly implemented and plans to fight lawsuits from anti-abortion groups seeking to block the amendment.

Jamie Lockhart, executive director at Planned Parenthood Advocates of Virginia, said public understanding was essential to the new right. “A right you don’t know about is a right you cannot use,” she said. “We’re committed to making sure every Virginian knows what this amendment means for them.”

Question 3 enshrined a “fundamental right to vote” for all citizens, including convicted felons who have served their time. The referendum reversed a lifetime voting ban for felony offenders established in the mid-1800s, a system that had disproportionately targeted Black people. For decades, Virginia voting rights could be restored only on an individual basis through a governor’s executive authority. An October portrait by Maansi Srivastava for The Washington Post via Getty Images showed voting rights advocate Chris DeRosa in Arlington.

Sheba Williams, founder of the Right to Vote coalition that spearheaded the ballot campaign, said the campaign represented a reversal of a system rooted in racial exclusion. “We didn’t think Virginia, being the capital of the Confederacy, would ever give up this last remaining vestige of the Jim Crow era,” she said. “Our goal is to give dignity back to people who are imperfect.”

Williams said the ballot drive built on the work of Black female legislators Mamie Locke and the late Yvonne B Miller, who fought for decades to restore voting access for people with past convictions. She called the victory bittersweet because many advocates had died, including her mother, who was disenfranchised for 24 years.

Estimates from the Sentencing Project showed that roughly 4 million formerly incarcerated Americans were denied voting rights in 2024, including more than a quarter million Virginians. One out of every 10 Black Virginians, including Williams, had lost their rights because of a felony conviction. The Right to Vote coalition, which includes the ACLU of Virginia and the League of Women Voters of Virginia, is focused on notifying more than a quarter million formerly incarcerated Virginians that they can vote again.

Sheba Williams said the effort required a public education campaign in areas with the highest conviction rates. The coalition is asking the state Department of Corrections to potentially include voter registration information in re-entry packets. The coalition also wants to build on the amendment by pressing for reforms to Virginia’s barrier crime laws. “We still have to work on other bills that impact the lives of formerly incarcerated people,” Williams said. “We see people celebrating the changing of the tides, the younger generation coming in. But we know people who hold on to power will try to maintain it.”

The marriage-equality referendum passed with 57%, the same margin as the vote establishing the ban 20 years earlier. Virginia became the fifth state to repeal a constitutional ban on same-sex marriage, following Nevada, California, Colorado and Hawaii; in total, 37 states have protections for same-sex marriage. Same-sex marriage has been legal across the country since the landmark Supreme Court decision in 2015. Advocates said protections for LGBTQ+ people had felt increasingly tenuous under the Trump administration and a Supreme Court that had considered taking up challenges to the Obergefell ruling.

Reed Williams, communications director for the Virginians for Marriage Equality coalition, called the passage a “generational reversal” exactly 20 years after Virginia banned same-sex unions. Williams said organizers learned from other states’ marriage equality campaigns and emphasized storytelling. The coalition recruited more than 40 queer people across the state to share “what being gay was like in a pre-Obergefell world,” Williams said. “We had to show that the amendment had real stakes for people in the LGBTQ+ community,” Williams said.

A photograph by Mike Kropf for Richmond Times-Dispatch via Getty Images showed Governor Abigail Spanberger speaking at a rally in Richmond in support of the same-sex marriage amendment. Spanberger said, “This really shows how much Virginians have grown.” She also said, “The perspectives of certain justices on the supreme court have made it clear that state protections are more important than ever before.”

Equality Virginia wants to carry the momentum from the ballot victory into the upcoming legislative session, seeking state funding for HIV prevention and gender-affirming care for trans youth. The organization is also looking ahead to the next election cycle, when all seats in the state legislature are up for grabs. “This is an incredible opportunity to take the lessons we learned in the marriage equality campaign,” Williams said, “and mobilize voters to get more pro-equality lawmakers elected.”
