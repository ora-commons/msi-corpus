## ProPublica database tracks more than $28 billion in tariff refunds

**Subtype:** fact

- ProPublica published the Cash Back database on Nov. 19 with refund data from 343 public companies.
- ProPublica found more than $28 billion in tariff refunds recovered or expected across the 343 companies.

## At least nine firms disclosed using the money for bonuses

On Nov. 19, ProPublica published “Cash Back,” a database of tariff-refund figures disclosed by 343 public companies. ProPublica said the companies had recovered or expect more than $28 billion in total. The Trump administration has not publicly detailed which companies are receiving roughly $170 billion in refunds or how much they have requested. U.S. Customs and Border Protection denied ProPublica's requests for that information, and ProPublica said it was appealing.

The database covers refunds on tariffs paid under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act. Public companies must disclose financial events such as tariff payments and refunds on earnings calls and in regular reports filed with the Securities and Exchange Commission, giving ProPublica a record from which to identify refund figures. It also offers the public a company-by-company view of a process the administration has not detailed.

The Supreme Court ruled on Feb. 20, 2026, that the International Emergency Economic Powers Act does not authorize the president to impose tariffs. The U.S. Court of International Trade ordered U.S. Customs and Border Protection to refund the duties it had collected, and the government began processing billions of dollars in refunds to importers.

To compile the figures, ProPublica searched securities filings and earnings calls using key phrases to isolate companies that mentioned refunds. For each company, ProPublica assembled a document containing the extracted text around each mention and parsed Extensible Business Reporting Language when filings made it available.

An open-weight artificial intelligence model performed the first pass at identifying refund amounts. ProPublica reporters then reviewed every company by hand, checked the database's accuracy and contacted all companies shown to confirm the amounts. The information came from public sources collected through Oct. 7, 2026.

The filing review covered Forms 8-K, 10-Q, 10-K, 8-K/A, 10-Q/A, 10-K/A, 6-K, 6-K/A, 20-F, 20-F/A, 40-F and 40-F/A, along with associated exhibits. ProPublica grouped companies by industry using a combination of Morningstar designations.

To determine a company's recovered refund, ProPublica added amounts recorded as essentially certain or probable, sometimes described as a receivable, to amounts the company disclosed as already received. The government must pay interest on returned tariff amounts at rates determined by the Internal Revenue Service on a quarterly basis. Some companies reported the refund and interest separately, while others reported one combined amount. ProPublica added the interest when it was reported separately to capture the full amount the company would receive.

Some companies reported only their net refund, which could be what remained after the company reinvested the money in its business or passed part of it to customers. ProPublica reported the net amount in those cases. When a company reported a range, ProPublica used the lower end and noted the limitation on the company's page.

According to a ProPublica analysis published earlier in the week, at least nine companies disclosed that they were using the refunds for bonuses rather than paying back consumers who generally covered much of the tariff costs through higher prices. Businesses cited in that analysis included tool company Stanley Black & Decker, the owners of discount department stores TJ Maxx and Marshalls, and golf equipment brand Titleist. None of the companies that disclosed bonuses had said they planned to refund customers, and many other firms have said little about how they will use the money.

ProPublica's examination was limited to publicly traded companies, which are required to disclose detailed financial information. How private companies use their refunds remains unclear because those firms are not normally required to disclose such information.

Under the refund process described by ProPublica, only importers or the authorized custom brokers who filed the entries can request a refund. Even when a company passed tariff costs to customers through higher prices, the refund goes to the company, which determines how to use the money.

UPS and FedEx are itemizing how much customers paid in tariffs and reimbursing them. The companies set up online portals so customers can track their refunds. Although both firms are technically importers of record, they imported goods on behalf of businesses and individuals that paid them to ship products.

Other companies have said they will split at least some of their refund checks with customers, though those customers generally are other businesses. Trade experts said those business-to-business vendors likely have detailed contracts that itemize tariff costs. Some companies have described vague plans to pay the money forward, such as “returning tariff refunds to our members through pricing,” without saying whether that means price cuts or limiting future increases.

ProPublica said it would periodically update Cash Back with newer filings, corrections and additional information supplied by companies. The nonprofit newsroom also said readers could download the data. Robert Faturechi, Jeff Frankl, Al Shaw and Ruth Talbot contributed reporting.
