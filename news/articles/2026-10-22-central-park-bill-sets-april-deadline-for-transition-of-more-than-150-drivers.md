## Mamdani administration may consider compensation, but worker benchmarks remain unspecified

The legislation approved by the New York City Council requires Mayor Zohran Mamdani's office to develop a transition plan by next April to help more than 150 remaining drivers and the rest of the industry find other jobs. City Hall must include job referrals, career counseling and educational opportunities, and the administration may consider some form of compensation. The question of what happens to drivers whose jobs would be eliminated remains largely unanswered: the provisions are vague and do not set clear benchmarks for displaced workers. The Council approved the bill Thursday; it would end horse-drawn carriage rides in Central Park by June 2028.

Central Park carriage operations have lasted for more than 150 years, and animal-welfare activists have long opposed the industry. The latest push to end the rides began in June after 18-year-old Indian tourist Romanch Mahajan died after jumping from a runaway carriage during a ride. Mahajan's death appeared to be the first fatality involving a horse carriage in Central Park since the rides began. His family personally appealed to the city to end the rides and offered tearful testimony that they thought the rides were safe.

City Hall said the mayor's office had been working on a workforce transition program and had started discussions about potential compensation. A spokesperson said, “The mayor will sign this bill, and the Mamdani administration is working every day to ensure we can do so while providing the strongest possible protections for the owners and workers in this industry.”

For drivers, what comes next remained unclear. Alexus Lawson, 28, a carriage driver from Queens who grew up with horses and has worked as a riding instructor, worried about 7-year-old Starlight and her other horses. She said, “No one can come up with an appropriate answer because there is none,” and asked, “Without a job, how can I take care of my horses?”

Medet Fidan, 56, previously drove a cab before switching to carriage work after ride-hailing apps upended the taxi industry. He said the ban made him feel targeted. “I think the city is leaving us a big mess,” Fidan said. “We’re going to lose our businesses, we’re going to lose our jobs, we’re going to lose our livelihoods.”

Council Speaker Julie Menin “will remain engaged in the transition process,” a spokesperson said. The legislation seeks to address the longstanding question of what happens to out-of-work drivers by setting a firm deadline for City Hall to act. Menin, who helped spearhead the ban, has argued horses no longer have a place among Central Park's pedestrians, cyclists, dog walkers and gawking tourists. She said, “These workers deserve to be protected.”

The union representing carriage drivers had vowed to file a legal challenge “the minute the ink dries” on the legislation. Drivers have expressed sympathy for Mahajan and his family but argue they are being unfairly targeted because of a single accident. Christina Hansen, a longtime carriage driver and industry spokesperson, said, “It does nothing for alternate employment, for the continuation of our businesses,” and asked, “Who’s going to pay my rent while I’m looking for a job?”

Betsy Smith, head of the Central Park Conservancy, the nonprofit that operates the park, welcomed passage of the bill. “In the modern era, carriages pose a serious — and even fatal — safety risk to Park visitors, carriage drivers, and the horses themselves,” Smith said in a statement.

President Donald Trump opposed the ban; his namesake Trump Tower is a stone's throw from Central Park. He called carriage rides “a quintessential New York experience” and wrote on social media, “It was a Great Tradition. Another piece of History that’s gone. It will be missed!”

Two of Mamdani's mayoral predecessors had called to end the industry but neither succeeded. Former Mayor Bill de Blasio campaigned on ending the rides on day one of his administration, but the effort wilted amid backlash from unions and a big media blitz involving actor Liam Neeson. Eric Adams, the mayor immediately before Mamdani, floated a ban late in his term, but that proposal went nowhere.

At a rally Thursday, Mahajan's uncle, Gaurav Mahajan, stood beside local lawmakers and animal welfare activists and praised the proposal, saying “the hour of justice has finally arrived.” He said the family had not been allowed simply to grieve. “We’re turning our shattering loss into an unbreakable shield of kindness for the city. For months, our family hasn’t been allowed to just grieve,” he said. “We’ve had to fight. We’ve had to channel our devastating sorrow into a war for change.”
