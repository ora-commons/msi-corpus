## Few places to hide as a handful of firms dominate market value

The Nasdaq Composite finished the week down 10%, its worst performance since the COVID pandemic, as the technology shares that have carried the U.S. market for years sold off. Markets closed out one of the worst weeks Wall Street has seen in a while.

Martin, speaking to NPR's Rob Schmitz, said the decline had been building rather than arriving out of nowhere.

"It's been looking frothy for a while, right? There's been lots of signs of excess there," she said. "I'm not hugely surprised to see it unravel somewhat last week, but it was pretty brutal."

She compared the trading to the worst stretches of the pandemic sell-off. "You know, the sorts of trading activity you see in big crises like the COVID crisis a few years ago, it was pretty ugly," Martin said.

She said the market's reach is what makes the move consequential. "Markets matter to people. They don't exist in a parallel universe," she said. "You know, this is people's savings. It's endowments. It's pensions. It's all of that stuff. So it is pretty disturbing news, but it has been coming for a while, right?"

Martin dated the first tremors to the summer. "So we started to see some tremors right back in the summer when there were some really big shocks in Korean markets, and Korean stock markets are absolutely jam packed with semiconductors and memory chips," she said. "And so that was a bit of a warning that this was coming. And then there's just been one thing after another that has made it worse and worse."

She said trading in the coming week is likely to be thin and volatile because of the U.S. Thanksgiving holiday. "It's going to be a weird week because you guys have got Thanksgiving," Martin said. "There's generally not so much trading that happens in that week, so it could be quite jerky."

The index remains above its starting point for the year. "You know, markets are not down hugely on the year. You know, we're not quite back down to zero yet, but that still makes this a real stinker of a year for U.S. stocks so far," she said. FRED data put the Nasdaq Composite at 23,879.2 as of Nov. 21.

The investors stepping back are mainly large institutions, Martin said, and their problem is one of exposure rather than doubt about any single company. "It's the big institutions who are uncomfortable with the fact that tech has just become such a big part of their life," she said. "It's very difficult if you're a professional investor to avoid the tech trade because tech stocks account for something like half of the U.S. stock market."

That concentration narrows sharply at the top. "There's a tiny clutch of them that gobble up at least a quarter or a third of the entire market capitalization of the U.S. stock market. So if you don't like tech, it's very, very difficult to hide from it," she said.

The usual escape routes lead back into the same trade. "And investors have been talking for months about where are the little hiding places? How can I diversify? And the truth is, it's really hard," Martin said. "You know, if you try and diversify into emerging markets, well, guess what the emerging markets trade is now. It's all of those chip stocks in Asia."

She said investors are also focused on how the buildout of data centers is financed. "They're worried about that level of concentration in tech stocks. They're worried that a lot of these big data center projects that tech companies are coming up with, they're not financed out of money that these tech companies have just got kicking around, you know, behind the sofa. This is money that they're borrowing," Martin said. "And as soon as you start borrowing money to build stuff, then you're very sensitive to interest rates. You're very sensitive to the whims of people who buy debt from companies for a living."

In Martin's account, the final push came from Anthropic's market debut. "I guess investors are a bit superstitious. So when you have really big kind of fizzy investment themes, and they start to get a bit stretched and a bit too much, one of the ways you can often tell that these things are getting a bit too much is that you have massive stock market listings, and Anthropic's stock market listing was huge," she said. "And I think for a lot of people, that was just the point at which they said, OK, I'm out. This is just making me uncomfortable."

What happens next, she said, depends on whether buyers treat the drop as an opening. "It is a very worrying situation, and it will be really interesting to see next week whether people say, ooh, there's some bargains there, I'm going to pick up some stocks on the cheap, or whether they think this is just the beginning of something really ugly," Martin said.
