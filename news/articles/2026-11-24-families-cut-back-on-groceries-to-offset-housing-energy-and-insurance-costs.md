## Navy Federal data show pullbacks across every income level

Many U.S. families are reducing grocery spending as rent, gasoline and heat take up more of their budgets, according to a Navy Federal Credit Union study that tracked the spending habits of 6 million members across the country for two years. NPR's Scott Horsley reported that the analysis found cost-cutting at grocery stores increasingly common as families tried to offset higher housing, energy and insurance costs.

Navy Federal Chief Economist Heather Long said: "There's a lot of strain right now. It's a squeeze for many households." Her assessment was that consumers were dealing with two strains at once: prices were high, and people could no longer spend and shop as they had even two years ago.

Tax refunds were 10% bigger on average this year than last, which propped up spending a bit in the spring and summer. By September, Long said, that cushion was gone, and even upper-income families were dialing back on food. Long noted that a "pick-another-protein" ad resonated in Mississippi and said: "Every single income level has tried to scale back on their grocery spending."

Jody Levin recently received a high heating-oil bill. At $6.49 a gallon, staying warm this winter was expected to cost Levin and her husband more than $800 a month. Levin lives about an hour outside Seattle and works in sales; her husband works for a company that makes computer parts.

Levin said: "It's very, very stressful. It's hard on our marriage. It's hard on our budget." She said heating-oil costs had tripled since the couple lived there.

The couple cut back on concerts and restaurant meals and were watching their pennies at the supermarket. Levin said they bought less meat, more pasta than usual and dishes such as a baked potato with broccoli and cheddar. The family was also cutting back on bigger-ticket grocery items such as fish.

Robin McMillan, who recently retired to Glendale, Arizona, described a similar squeeze. Gas prices had come down from the wartime peak but remained about 18% higher than at the same time a year earlier. McMillan said: "Gasoline is outrageous. Automobile insurance is just about unaffordable. Groceries are through the proverbial roof."

To save money, McMillan hunted for markdowns on fresh food nearing its expiration date and more often chose cheaper store-brand products. She said: "I used to be a brand snob, but when I saw the reality of my monthly budget, I go, no, I can't do this. We just - we can't afford this."

Erin King of Starkville, Mississippi, reluctantly made a similar protein switch. "The price of beef is absolutely insane, so we just don't eat beef really anymore," she said. King said ground turkey was not the same.

King's husband works for a grocery store, while she works as a school counselor and earns extra money at her church. Even after moving to a smaller apartment, King said, the couple was still living paycheck to paycheck. "We're still living paycheck to paycheck. We're not really able to save for anything. Like our sofas - it's in rough shape. We can't afford to buy a house. We can't save for that," she said.

Retailers were expecting a healthy jump in holiday spending this year, but much of that increase was expected to come from upper-income households. For middle- and lower-income families, a holiday splurge was likely to require further belt-tightening in the new year.

NPR's reporting tied the cutbacks to heating oil for Levin, gasoline and insurance for McMillan, and beef prices amid tighter overall finances for King. Across the interviews, shopping changes included fewer restaurants and concerts, different proteins and brands, markdowns near expiration dates and closer attention to prices at the supermarket.
