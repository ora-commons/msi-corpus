## Midweek storm may bring 6 inches of rain and 50 mph winds to Bay Area

The National Weather Service has placed high surf and coastal flood advisories in effect across long stretches of the northern California coast after a weekend of heavy surf. The highest waves — reaching 50 to 60 feet (15.24 to 18.29 meters) — broke along Mavericks, the popular surf spot in San Mateo county south of San Francisco.

Officials from the NWS warned that waves along the coast are erratic and unpredictable and could quickly overpower anyone nearby. About 270 homes in Santa Cruz county were under evacuation warnings while the advisories were in effect.

On Monday, a local spotted two waterspouts emerging from the San Francisco Bay, startling residents. Officials said the spouts did not move offshore and caused no damage.

The forecast for the rest of the week points to more. Weather models predict additional storms arriving from a more westerly direction, which could expose slightly different parts of the coast to waves peaking around 20 feet (6.1 meters). The low-pressure system that brought rain across the Bay Area on Sunday was expected to be followed by large waves on Monday night. Rare tornado warnings were in effect for parts of Santa Cruz county through Monday afternoon.

Midweek, a powerful storm building along the Pacific is expected to bring between 1.5 and 6 inches (3.81cm to 15.24cm) of rain across the Bay Area as well as winds of up to 50 miles per hour (80.46 km/h).

Brian Garcia, an NWS meteorologist, summarized the outlook: "It's going to be a busy couple of weeks – be ready."

The sequence of storms carries implications well beyond the California shoreline. NWS said the two Pacific storms are expected to affect not only the coast but huge swathes of the eastern US, bringing gusty winds, rain and heavy mountain snow in the Sierra Nevadas.

Large waves of this kind usually accompany a strong El Niño, and this year's event is predicted to be the strongest ever recorded, with global implications. El Niños are a naturally recurring phenomenon in which the eastern Pacific temporarily heats up, causing global changes in weather patterns. Some research suggests that global heating is making El Niños more powerful.

Scientists have warned that the current, record-setting El Niño will be a "risk multiplier," warping global weather patterns that have fueled wildfires and crop failures in Indonesia, coastal inundation in the US, severe drought in Sudan, and severe flooding in several countries.

In California, officials are expecting more intense storms and erratic weather through the next several months.
