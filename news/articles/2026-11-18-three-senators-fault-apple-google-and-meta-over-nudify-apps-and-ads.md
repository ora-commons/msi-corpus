## Three senators fault Apple, Google and Meta over 'nudify' apps and ads

## Unicef reported at least 1.2 million children victimized by nonconsensual nude images across 11 countries early in 2026

Three US senators sent letters Wednesday to the chief executives of Apple, Google and Meta, saying the platforms' safeguards against so-called nudification apps are not sufficient and that Meta continues to profit from advertising for them. Ron Wyden of Oregon, Ed Markey and Ben Luján wrote that these apps are available on the Apple and Google app stores or are advertised on Meta platforms in spite of company policies that prohibit them. According to the studies, children continue to have easy access to the apps. The senators also said Meta was continuing to profit from ads for apps that “victimize women and girls worldwide.” The Guardian characterized the senators' complaint as Apple and Google letting teens access so-called nudification apps despite parental controls.

The letters follow multiple studies by the Tech Transparency Project (TTP), the latest of which was exclusively reported in the Guardian. In the most recent TTP study, carried out in October, researchers said they were able to access dozens of apps with features that enabled the creation of AI-generated nude and pornographic imagery despite indicating that the accounts they created were for 13-year-old boys. There were 135 apps available to those accounts across Apple and Google.

Apple and Google removed the apps that were mentioned in the studies in response to inquiries from TTP and the Guardian US. The removed apps, however, remained functional on phones where they had already been downloaded.

Keith Chu, the chief communications adviser and deputy policy director to Wyden, said clarifying policies is not the same thing as enforcing them. Chu said the apps continue to proliferate across the two app stores or be promoted in ads on Meta platforms despite the companies' reaffirmation of their prohibitions. Apple clarified its policies after TTP researchers reached out about nudification apps they found on the app store in October. Google previously told the Guardian that the company proactively works to detect and remove apps with harmful content, and Meta introduced new AI tools to detect and remove ads that contain CSAM.

Wyden said Congress will need to act if the companies “don’t do a better job of cracking down on apps expressly designed and marketed to create harm.”

In a statement to the Guardian US, Wyden said: “AI has supercharged the ability to create and share nonconsensual sexual images, creating tremendous harm to women and girls.” He said: “Instead of ramping up investment to meet this challenge, big tech companies are cutting back on the very teams responsible for protecting users from harm. I’m looking for answers about why Meta, Apple and Google are missing obvious violations of their own rules.”

In their letters, the senators said AI-generated nonconsensual nude imagery has seen an uptick in the last two years, an assertion that surveys bear out. Unicef reported at the start of 2026 that at least 1.2 million children across 11 countries reported being victimized by nonconsensual nude images. Law enforcement officers have also warned in various intelligence memos that officers should remain vigilant because AI may make it easier to circumvent CSAM (child sexual abuse material) detection methods, according to documents the Guardian previously reported on.

On Meta's social networks, many developers advertise the nudification functions of their apps explicitly. TTP and other research groups have discovered hundreds of paid ads on Instagram and Facebook that include AI-generated sexualized images of real children and other CSAM. In September 2026, TTP found 332 paid ads on Facebook and Instagram that included CSAM. This month, AIForensics, a European non-profit research organization, found that some apps that were hiding their AI nudification features from Apple and Google were explicitly promoting “sexual deepfake functionality” in ads on Meta platforms.

In their letter to Meta chief executive Mark Zuckerberg, the senators wrote: “Despite Meta’s stated prohibition on these ads, independent investigations have documented extensive ad campaigns on Facebook and Instagram directing customers to apps capable of digitally undressing people in photographs or placing their likeness into sexually explicit content without their consent.” The senators asked how ads containing apparent CSAM passed the company's review systems.

On 7 October, Meta said it had taken additional measures to combat ads with CSAM. Katie Paul, the director of TTP, said TTP researchers have reported dozens of ads containing CSAM to the National Center for Missing & Exploited Children since then. Screenshots TTP shared with the Guardian show an ad for an app to create “AI sex friends” highlighting images of young girls; those ads were active for several hours before being taken down but remain available in Meta's ad library. The organization has “yet to identify ads featuring videos of CSAM run by any other ad platforms”.

Paul said: “Meta has repeatedly boasted about its sophisticated AI systems for reviewing harms on platforms, but for more than a year those systems have repeatedly failed to flag ads featuring graphic sexual videos of children as young as toddlers.”

Paul also said the two app stores say they review every app on their platforms but do not disclose what that review entails. She said: “Vague descriptions of how each company defines ‘nonconsensual intimate imagery’ and lack of detail in how both companies actually go about app review allow them to have it both ways: claiming they have policies on the books, and applying those policies based on what best fits the narrative at any given time.”

Apple, for its part, said some developers pull a “bait-and-switch” and add features that enable users to create AI-generated nude or pornographic imagery after the iPhone maker approves an app. Wyden's office said the senators expect the company to review apps on an ongoing basis.

The senators' letter said the findings suggest Apple and Google are “not merely failing to remove applications that facilitate intimate image abuse, it may also be profiting from their distribution”.
