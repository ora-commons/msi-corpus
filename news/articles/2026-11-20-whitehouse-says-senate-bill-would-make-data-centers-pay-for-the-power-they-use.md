## Nationwide kilowatt-hour costs rose more than 6% in one year and 39% in five years

The Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act would speed the approval process for data centers and power plants. The Senate passed the bill 62-33 on Thursday as Americans struggled with rising energy costs and increased demand driven by artificial intelligence. Fourteen Democrats and one Independent joined most Republicans, and two Democrats and two Republicans authored the measure, which now heads to the House.

Supporters tied the permitting changes to affordability, development and energy policy. Senate Majority Leader John Thune said the bill would "cut through decades of red tape" and "save the average household hundreds of dollars each year, and Americans will see better roads, more American manufacturing, and better-paying jobs." West Virginia Republican Sen. Shelley Moore Capito, one of the bill's co-authors, said, "Tonight, the passage of permitting reform in the Senate finally became reality and we took a major step towards addressing the affordability concerns of the American people." In a recent social media post, President Trump urged Congress to approve the bill, calling it a "BIG WIN for U.S. ENERGY INDEPENDENCE and DOMINANCE."

Nationwide, the cost of a kilowatt-hour has risen faster than overall inflation: more than 6% in the last year and 39% in the last five years. Rhode Island Democratic Sen. Sheldon Whitehouse, a co-author, said the bill would make data centers pay for the power they use. He also said, "Americans will quickly begin getting some relief on their energy bills if the House follows suit." Data centers have drawn considerable public pushback in the U.S., cutting across party lines.

Some climate change advocates offered a different case for faster permitting. They said the U.S. needs to speed up building renewable energy projects and transmission lines and argued the bill would allow the country to build more solar, wind and battery projects.

The environmental opposition focused on accountability and pollution. Major environmental groups, including the Sierra Club and National Wildlife Federation, opposed the bill, arguing that it would weaken environmental accountability. Many Democrats also opposed it, saying it would worsen pollution by making it easier to greenlight oil and gas projects and energy-hungry data centers while weakening protections for clean air and water.

Tribal rights were a distinct concern. "Don't sacrifice the Clean Water Act, hunting or fishing grounds, historic or sacred sites, or protections of tribal sovereignty," said Washington Democratic Sen. Maria Cantwell on X. California Sen. Alex Padilla said, "I support cutting red tape and getting more energy projects built, but not at the expense of tribal sovereignty and California's environment."

Oregon Democratic Sen. Jeff Merkley called the bill "a bad deal." He said on X that it "opens the gates to a flood of natural gas for data centers while leaving expanded transmission of renewable energy subject to Trump's obstruction."

The Senate's 62-33 vote leaves the bill before the House. Supporters say the measure can reduce household costs, while opponents argue it weakens pollution protections, environmental accountability and tribal rights. Julia Simon contributed reporting to this story.
