## Vance says Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang needs no presidential pardon

- Vance said Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang does not need a presidential pardon.

## Huang's Dec. 1 closed-door interview precedes his Dec. 3 public hearing

Vice President JD Vance said Wednesday that Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang does not need a presidential pardon and is under no investigation. Speaking after a Thanksgiving Eve meal with troops at Wright-Patterson Air Force Base in Ohio, Vance said Justice Department officials and “multiple officials in our national security apparatus” confirmed Huang was “under no investigation.” The comments came days before Huang's scheduled appearances before Congress.

The Associated Press reported that Huang resigned earlier this month after acknowledging extramarital affairs, including one with Alice Huang, whom U.S. officials suspected of working with China's Ministry of State Security. Nvidia reinstated him 11 days later at the urging of President Donald Trump.

Vance addressed the possibility of charges: “I don’t think he needs a pardon at all,” he said. He said he had seen no evidence that Huang committed a crime and that relevant authorities should bring a prosecution if evidence existed.

Trump had told the New York Post on Tuesday that pardoning Huang was “always an option,” though he did not think it would be necessary. Trump also said he did not want Huang to invoke his Fifth Amendment rights amid potential criminal prosecution when he appears before lawmakers. Pleading the Fifth allows a person to refuse to answer questions or provide testimonial evidence if doing so could reasonably expose the person to criminal prosecution.

Huang is scheduled for a closed-door, transcribed interview with three congressional panels on Dec. 1, including the House Intelligence and Foreign Affairs committees and the Select Committee on China. He is expected to appear at a public hearing of the Senate Banking Committee on Dec. 3.

The board's reversal has prompted questions about its independence and how Trump's role bears on the boundary between business and politics. Nvidia and the White House also face scrutiny over Trump's relationship with Huang, a close adviser to the president on artificial intelligence issues. Trump has repeatedly welcomed Huang to the White House and earlier this year invited him aboard Air Force One for a state visit to China.

The New York Times has reported that Trump had been briefed on intelligence concerns about Huang's relationship with Alice Huang, an executive at the Singapore-based company Megaspeed. U.S. officials suspected that she was working with China's Ministry of State Security, and Beijing denied it.

Congressional staffers from the House and Senate Intelligence Committees and representatives of chamber leaders received a classified briefing on Tuesday, according to two people familiar with the matter who were not authorized to speak publicly and spoke on the condition of anonymity. One of the people said officials from the CIA, FBI and Office of the Director of National Intelligence were among those on hand.

Vance also said: “We don’t try people in the court of public opinion. We don’t try people based on anonymously sourced stories in the New York Times, we try people based on evidence.”

Trump, meanwhile, said he had pressed Nvidia's board to reinstate Huang and that his administration had assured company officials that Huang did not present a national security risk. Trump said he had staked the nation's economic growth on artificial intelligence and worried that Huang's ouster was tanking the stock market. He also said the ouster risked putting the United States behind Beijing in the race for artificial intelligence dominance if Huang, widely seen by backers and critics as a generational talent in the field, were poached by a Chinese firm.

Trump said earlier this week that Vance, Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick and Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent were among administration officials he enlisted to make the case to Nvidia's board. One person familiar with the matter who was not authorized to speak publicly and spoke on the condition of anonymity said White House officials viewed Vance, who worked in Silicon Valley before entering politics, as an effective emissary in helping Nvidia board members overcome their reservations.
