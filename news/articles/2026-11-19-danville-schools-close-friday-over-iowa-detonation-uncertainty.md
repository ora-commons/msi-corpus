## Updated map excludes Mediapolis as schools and library close Friday

Just a few miles northwest of Middletown, the Danville community school district will close Friday. Superintendent Pat Wallace announced the decision on Tuesday, saying his initial intention was to keep schools open but enough uncertainties made cancellation “the most responsible decision.” The closure comes ahead of the planned detonation of degraded lead azide at the Iowa army ammunition plant.

“I am not suggesting that the detonation will result in unsafe conditions,” Wallace wrote. “The reality is that we simply do not know exactly what conditions will look like Friday afternoon.”

Less than 20 miles (32km) northeast of the plant, Mediapolis city officials were approached late last month about allowing soil, water and air testing. The city learned of its potential exposure on Sunday, when an initial map placed it at the center of the dust-exposure area. The city of nearly 2,000 residents was no longer in the plume on an updated map, but Mediapolis schools and the public library will close Friday. The local fire department plans to hose down playgrounds that could be covered in lead particles. “This is probably a first for me. I believe it’s a first for a lot of our citizens,” deputy city clerk Amy Stucker said on Wednesday. “The biggest issue we’re having is the lack of information.”

The US Army plans to detonate about 5 tons of unusable bomb-making chemicals at the Iowa army ammunition plant in Middletown on Friday. The plan involves several dozen barrels containing degraded lead azide. The Environmental Protection Agency approved an emergency permit on 13 November, paving the way for the detonation. Officials recommend that some residents in nearby Middletown begin vacating the area Friday morning; those who cannot leave should stay inside with windows and doors closed. Residents in southeast Iowa were worried that a cloud of lead dust could cause environmental contamination and health problems.

The advisory states: “The detonation will result in an air plume with some lead-containing particles which will disperse in the air and deposit on the ground. Lead is toxic to humans and animals. The goal of this advisory is to provide the public with actionable information out of an abundance of caution to minimize lead exposure.”

The Army released maps showing the potential for lead dust to drift north. Updated maps showed the plume’s shift, and the Army said it would continue updating the map based on the weather. Some schools in the path of the plume have canceled classes. Nearly 400 people live in Middletown, but thousands more are in the potential plume’s path. Officials said the vibrations from two blasts would not last long but could break old windows in town, and the resulting dust was expected to linger for no more than one hour, depending on wind speed. The Army recommended cleaning cars, windowsills and outdoor surfaces after the detonation. Some residents raised concerns on social media about whether the Army’s cleanup guidance would be enough.

Middletown residents learned in March that the Army needed to dispose of several dozen barrels containing lead azide after the ammunition plant paused operations as part of a review and degraded material was discovered. Lt. Col. Daniel Nosse, the plant commander, said at the time, “The safety of our people and our neighbors is non‑negotiable,” and acknowledged the volatility of the chemical compound used in munitions production. The Army shared more details at an October public meeting in Middletown, advising that a detonation was planned for an unspecified date in November.

The barrels had been stored for more than 20 years in earthen containers, sitting in a bath of ethanol and water to keep the material stable, said Justine Barati, a public affairs specialist at the army sustainment command. During a routine check earlier this year, the Army found that some of the water had evaporated and the lead azide had crystallized. That made the chemical more sensitive to friction and shock, creating, according to the EPA’s emergency permit, “a highly unstable and dangerous situation.”

Officials said moving or relocating the degraded material posed an unacceptable risk of accidental detonation. Barati said that possibility increases in cold weather. “We’re really concerned about the weather situation,” Barati said about moving forward with Friday’s detonation. “We’re really concerned that it will detonate on its own.”

EPA documents showed that alternatives such as neutralizing and decomposing the drum contents with other chemicals were considered, but officials said handling the drums under any plan would increase the probability of an accidental, fatal detonation. The Army said it assembled a panel of experts in munitions chemistry, engineering and environmental safety to develop the detonation plan.

The barrels are surrounded above, below and on all sides by concrete, which is buried under soil. Sand barriers are stacked two storeys high around the soil. During the detonation, the soil will be sprayed with water and the air misted to try to keep the majority of the lead on Army property, Barati said.

The EPA’s permit requires the Army to track specific rates of potential air and dust dispersion and to notify the public five days in advance, prompting Sunday’s advisory to communities as far as 100 miles (160km) away. Patrick O’Shaughnessy, a professor in the University of Iowa’s department of occupational and environmental health, said it was encouraging to see that the Army and EPA were doing all they could to minimize dust. It remained unknown, he said, how much lead would end up in the atmosphere and on surfaces. O’Shaughnessy described lead as a neurological toxicant that can decrease brain activity and development, especially in young children, and said toddlers’ tendency to put objects in their mouths made them a particular concern.

News of the planned blasts also caught the attention of environmental and public health officials in Illinois. They urged the US military to conduct comprehensive sampling in Illinois after the detonations. The office of Iowa Gov. Kim Reynolds said it was first briefed about the public advisory on Monday.
