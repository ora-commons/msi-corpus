## Middletown mayor stays while nearby farmers question lead exposure and safety

The U.S. Army plans a controlled detonation of 68 barrels of lead azide at the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant in Middletown, Iowa, on Wednesday. The operation involves five tonnes of degraded explosives, and the Army said the blast is needed to protect the public from an unplanned explosion. Lead azide is a highly volatile compound used as a detonator.

The detonation is set to go ahead after an appeals court overturned an earlier decision to stop it over potential health and environmental hazards from lead exposure. The Army said handling the barrels could make an accidental detonation more likely as temperatures fall in the coming days.

Officials announced a five-mile optional evacuation zone and a 20-mile shelter-in-place advisory. Depending on weather and wind, they said airborne lead dust could reach as far as 100 miles. Residents who remained near the plant were told to stay indoors around the blast and expect "temporary noise, ground vibrations, and a dust plume that will contain some lead."

Reaction was mixed. In Middletown, where the population is around 360, many residents interviewed by the BBC appeared relatively unfazed by the planned blast. Their views differed from those in communities farther from the plant, where residents focused more heavily on possible lead exposure and the consequences for farms.

Middletown Mayor Rodney Johnson, who lives half a mile from the plant, planned to stay. He planned to monitor his mobile home by camera while working at his manufacturing job in a nearby town.

Johnson, who grew up 12 miles away, pointed to exposures to lead through fuel, fireworks and other common items. He said he survived lead poisoning as a child, although he cannot be certain how he got it. "Every two weeks, we have crop dusters flying over our homes," he said. "There's a lot more dangerous stuff being put into the air."

Johnson said he did not believe Middletown residents were as concerned as those living farther away. He believes many local residents "will be very angry" if the detonation is delayed again or cancelled.

Trisha Klaus, 49, took a different view: "I actually want it to happen, because I'd rather it be controlled than just blow up." She and her 99-year-old grandfather, Keith McNeil, who worked at the ammunition plant for half a century, planned to hunker down at the Army Reserve Center and bring their pets.

Andrew Wiese, a pastor at Harmony Bible Church, whose campus borders the plant, said "a lot of people are just kind of wishing it was over and done with." He also said uncertainty had created fear in the community. "There's just a lot of uncertainty, and that's just been a breeding ground for a lot of ideas… I'd say there's fear in the community," Wiese said.

Outside Middletown, residents focused more heavily on possible exposure and the effect on nearby farms. Many area residents came from families that had worked in farming, manufacturing or at the ammunition plant for generations.

Margo Sprenger, 71, a farmer and landowner in Donnelson, about 20 miles southwest of the factory, said her land was in the direct path of the projected plume. Sprenger said, "it's going to change my life forever." She added: "Our grandparents started on these farms, and now we don't even know if they can produce food that we can trust."

Sprenger also questioned who would determine whether the land was safe and how residents would pay for that assessment. "Who's going to tell us if it is safe or not? How much money is that going to cost for us to even find if it's safe? We certainly know the dangers of lead, but we don't know the dangers of what they're doing to us."

Her neighbor, Beth Howard, a pie-maker, planned to travel to Des Moines, about 150 miles away, for Thanksgiving while her partner, Doug, remained on their farm. His family has run the farm for more than 150 years.

"It's so upsetting because of the future," Howard said. "What does the future hold for us? Is this the end of times for our farm?" She added: "If you lived here, you'd be concerned too. That's a lot of ammunition, and we should have never been put in the situation in the first place."

Fort Madison, roughly 20 miles from the ammunition plant, learned about the operation late. City Manager Laura Leigois said the city was first notified on 17 November about the originally planned detonation three days later. "We were blindsided," she said.

Leigois said residents were frustrated by the lack of information and by how the operation had been changed. Locals took matters into their own hands "because we're going to take care of our people," she said, including covering the city's water tower vents, which let air move in and out.

Jeff Kurtz, 72, a retired locomotive engineer and former state representative in Fort Madison, worried about the aftermath. "There's no guarantee I'm still going to be alive after all this stuff happens," he said. "This could be apocalyptic."

After learning about the detonation, Kurtz worked with a team of scientists who assessed the situation on the ground in support of a legal challenge that caused the detonation to be paused. Kurtz said the team believed other means existed for removing the explosives and did not accept the Army's argument that the threat of a self-explosion was imminent.

Kurtz said he would "work up until the last minute" trying to get the government to reverse course. "I'm frantic trying to get this stopped," he said. "I don't see where anything good could come from this."

As the blast remained scheduled for Wednesday, the disagreement persisted. The Army said controlled removal of the material would protect the public from an unplanned explosion. Some residents favored proceeding, while others questioned the warnings and the Army's safety assessment.
