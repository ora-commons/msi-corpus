## Environmental groups withhold consent after judge says subpoenas likely unconstitutional

West Virginia Attorney General JB McCuskey has withdrawn document subpoenas issued to the West Virginia Rivers Coalition and 350.org and asked the groups to agree to dismiss their lawsuit. The environmental organizations indicated they will not agree after U.S. District Judge Joseph R. Goodwin said the subpoenas are likely unconstitutional.

The environmental groups filed the lawsuit in response to the subpoenas, calling them unconstitutional. Goodwin previously paused the subpoenas, agreeing that they probably violated the groups' First Amendment rights.

In a letter to attorneys representing Rivers and 350, McCuskey wrote that he closed the investigation into the organizations and withdrew his previous statement implying additional future subpoenas. He also adopted a formal written policy saying his office would not issue similar subpoenas again.

The Sept. 16 subpoenas demanded 10 years of documents and communications from the environmental groups. The Attorney General's office said it was investigating whether the groups relied on misleading information implying that regular use of fossil fuels is worse than it actually is in order to attract supporters. Both groups advocate for renewable energy. McCuskey argues that opposing fossil fuels negatively impacts West Virginia's economy.

McCuskey asked Rivers and 350 to agree by Nov. 25 to dismiss the case, which would mean neither party won or lost. If the groups do not agree, McCuskey will try to have the case dismissed. If that happens, the environmental groups said they will ask the judge to decide who is in the wrong.

If both parties agree to drop the case as moot because McCuskey stopped the investigation, the lawsuit would end without a final ruling on whether the subpoenas violated the groups' rights. That outcome would leave open the possibility for McCuskey to start similar investigations after the case is dismissed.

McCuskey also filed and withdrew investigatory subpoenas to at least two other advocacy groups: the Sierra Club and West Virginia Citizen Action Education Fund.

The subpoenas were filed under the West Virginia Consumer Credit and Protection Act. The Attorney General's office said it will continue to enforce the Act, but not in a way that violates the U.S. Constitution. “In a contest between even the most essential statutory protection and the Constitution, the Constitution must always prevail,” McCuskey wrote.

Jennie Smith, executive director of the West Virginia Rivers Coalition, said in a press release last month that the subpoena “was intended to stifle their voice and advocacy.”

Mountain State Spotlight's reporting said McCuskey has repeatedly used his office to protect the fossil fuel industry and has taken action against environmental groups and protections in the past in an attempt to protect extraction industries. A few weeks after filing the subpoenas, McCuskey described certain climate activists as “insane leftist greeno’s” on social media.

Goodwin noted in his opinion that McCuskey's subpoenas were part of a larger trend of state and federal governments investigating nonprofits' affairs. The immediate procedural question is whether Rivers and 350 agree to end the case without a constitutional ruling or ask Goodwin to resolve the dispute.
