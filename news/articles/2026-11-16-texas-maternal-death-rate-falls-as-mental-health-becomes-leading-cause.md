## Texas maternal death rate falls as mental health becomes leading cause

The Texas Maternal Mortality and Morbidity Review Committee's biennial report, released Friday, covers deaths that occurred in 2021 and in 2024 and skips individual case reviews for 2022 and 2023. It is the first comprehensive look at maternal mortality in the state since Texas banned nearly all abortions in 2021.

State officials were supposed to release the report Sept. 1 but delayed it until after the midterm elections. Women's health advocates complained the delay was politically motivated and gave lawmakers less runway to craft bills based on the report ahead of the upcoming legislative session, which begins in January. Bill filing began Monday.

The maternal mortality ratio — the number of maternal deaths per 100,000 births in Texas — was 20.5 in 2024, a decrease from 23.5 in 2021, which excludes COVID-related deaths, and slightly above where rates stood in 2019, according to the report.

More than three-quarters of deaths were found to be preventable in both 2021 and 2024, though that represented a slight decrease from 80% in the committee's previous report in 2024.

Pregnancy-related mortality rates among Black women were the highest of all racial and ethnic groups and 2.4 times higher than among non-Hispanic white women. Severe maternal morbidity — which causes short- and long-term health effects — increased from 80.4 to 83.5, and also affected women at drastically different rates based on race and ethnicity. Black women in 2024 experienced severe maternal morbidity at a rate of 146.2 per 100,000 births, more than double the rate of white women in the same time frame.

For deaths researchers determined were related to pregnancy, infection was the leading cause of death in 2021 and mental health in 2024. Contributing circumstances shifted significantly between the two years, including a quadrupling in the number of cases where violence like suicide or homicide was a factor, from 5% to 19%. Obesity as a contributor halved, from 63% to 29%, while every other major factor nearly doubled, including chronic health conditions, mental disorders and substance abuse.

More than half of the pregnancy-related deaths in both 2021 and 2024 were among women on Medicaid, though the share decreased slightly, from 57% to 53%. In both years, 22% of deaths were among women who paid out of pocket for medical care.

State Rep. Donna Howard, D-Austin, who has authored bills to strengthen the committee's data collection work over the years, said she was particularly concerned about how the findings show women who used Medicaid health insurance had high rates of maternal death. "I am glad the Department has finally decided to release the MMMRC's 2026 report, but it remains unacceptable that they delayed its release by more than 2 months," Howard stated via text message.

The committee's report omits 2022 and 2023 from individual case reviews, years that could have provided additional information on deaths occurring after the near-total abortion ban. The report makes no mention of abortion or the changes to state law.

The Texas Department of State Health Services, which oversees the Texas Maternal Mortality and Morbidity Review Committee, rejected the notion that the abortion ban was the reason those years were skipped. "The committee was completing 2021 reviews and decided to move next to 2024 (the most recent full year available at the time) so the data wouldn't continue to get farther and farther behind," said Chris Van Deusen, a DSHS spokesperson, on Friday. "The committee made the decision with no dissent at the commissioner's request and encouraged by the chair."

Agency officials also denied that the election was a factor in the report's delay this year and pointed to a state law passed in 2025 that gave agencies more leeway to submit reports to the governor.

As a result of a Texas power shift that puts Republicans in the minority party, more help may be coming to the maternal mortality committee. Democrats already filed legislation related to the committee on Monday. State Rep. Charlene Ward Johnson, D-Houston, filed a bill calling for a separate study on Black maternal health in the state, similar to a bill she proposed in the 2025 session. That bill passed the House but not the Senate.

Most of the report's 11 recommendations on improving maternal health outcomes in Texas were the same as in the 2024 report, but new recommendations include prioritizing access to "critical resources" including clinical trials and improving education and health care coordination for those with chronic or high-risk medical conditions. Recommendations carried over and updated from last year's report include engaging Black communities to address health care disparities and improving access to mental health services. For those services, the report added a note to increase education and behavioral health treatment for women who experience pregnancy loss and women with substance abuse disorders.
