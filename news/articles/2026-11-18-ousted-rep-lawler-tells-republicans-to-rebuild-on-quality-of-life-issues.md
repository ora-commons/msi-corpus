## Ousted Rep. Lawler tells Republicans to rebuild on quality-of-life issues

Republican Rep. Mike Lawler, who lost his seat in the 2026 midterms, told PBS NewsHour that House Republicans should rebuild after the party's losses by focusing on what he called the quality-of-life issues most directly affecting Americans: the economy, immigration, health care, housing and energy. In the Nov. 18 interview, Lawler said "we're headed for two more years of gridlock" and warned that Democrats' effort to use the Dec. 11 funding deadline to restrict funding for the Iran war could lead to a government shutdown. He said Democrats have the ability to cause one because of the Senate's 60-vote threshold, but that they need to work with Republicans in the final months. He also said he will not run for Congress again.

One of those incumbents was Congressman Mike Lawler, who represented New York's 17th Congressional District in the Lower Hudson Valley after flipping the seat for Republicans in 2023. PBS NewsHour's Amna Nawaz, who conducted the interview, noted that Lawler earned a reputation as a moderate willing to buck his own party and was rated one of the most bipartisan members of Congress during his time in office. Nawaz also set out the scale of the party's losses: more than 30 Republican incumbents in the House of Representatives lost their seats in the general election.

On his own future, Lawler said: "Four years is a lifetime in politics." He said he intends to focus on his family, including a 4-year-old and a 2-year-old, and that the children "know nothing other than me being in elected office." Asked by Nawaz about a possible run for New York governor in 2030, he said if the opportunity arises to serve again in another capacity "great," and if not, "that's fine too."

Lawler's sharpest criticism was directed at the Democratic majority that will control both chambers. Federal funding expires on December 11, and Nawaz said a growing number of Democrats now want to use that deadline to try to restrict funding for the Iran war. Lawler called that "a disastrous endeavor by the Democrats to once again play politics with funding when it comes to national security." He said the 118-day shutdown over the Department of Homeland Security was "outrageous" and should not be repeated, adding that Democrats' policy dispute with the president over Iran and the Middle East should be worked through legislatively.

"Shutting the government down, withholding funding for our military is an absolute disaster," Lawler said, and called it "the height of stupidity" for Chuck Schumer and Hakeem Jeffries "to embark on a government shutdown as their first priority." He said, "We are living in a dangerous time in a dangerous world," and cited AI's potential use by "nefarious bad actors and rogue states." He also identified the mechanism, saying Democrats "have the ability to do it because of the 60-vote threshold in the Senate" and that they need to work with Republicans in the final months. He called shutting down the government "a disastrous idea."

The economic conditions Lawler wants the party to address are documented separately from the interview. FRED's real gross domestic income series (GDI, raw values) stood at 32,752.528 as of Nov. 18, 2026, according to [FRED](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/GDI). Lawler cited no economic data during the interview; his argument rests on what he called the issues most acutely affecting Americans.

Lawler was gentler about the leadership his own party selected, and sharper about what lies ahead. House Republicans chose Jim Jordan to lead their conference the previous week. Nawaz noted that Lawler would not be in the House when Jordan took the role and that Lawler had voted against him during the 2023 speaker fight. Asked whether Jordan remained the wrong choice, Lawler said no and noted that the conference had spoken. Speaking with Nawaz, Lawler said Jordan "will do a great job" unifying the conference as it heads into the minority for the next two years.

House Democrats, Nawaz said, selected Hakeem Jeffries as speaker on the day of the interview. Lawler said it is "incumbent on Leader Jeffries to show that he can actually govern and not just do the bidding of the far left of his party." He added: "Obviously, they have very large margins, but that also is going to mean that their base expects them to deliver on things the base wants." With a Republican administration for the next two years, he framed the test ahead as whether "a House Democrat majority and Senate Democrat majority" can work with a Republican president. He then said: "My hunch is, knowing Leader Jeffries as I do, we're going to have two years of gridlock and investigation and very little results."

Lawler was also asked about a series of technology failures over the past week or so. National security officials testified on Capitol Hill, facing questions about outages that included email platforms, banking and a nationwide ground stop, and they maintained that they were separate service outages, not coordinated and not state-sponsored attacks. Asked whether he was satisfied with the administration's answers, Lawler said: "I'm not a conspiracy theorist, but obviously there's a lot of coincidences happening all at once." He said the administration should give Congress a full debrief in a classified setting to address all the questions and concerns. He added: "Obviously, we hope there was no nefarious actions taken or any rogue states seeking to cause disruptions or cyberattacks."

He tied those concerns to artificial intelligence as it becomes more prevalent, citing a GAO study that had just come out. Lawler said GAO "was able to have them create a terrorist manifesto," referring to the four major AI platforms, and called the result "deeply concerning from a security standpoint." He said the issues speak to "the seriousness of cybersecurity" and the need for "critical guardrails on AI." He said "cybersecurity is real" and warned of potential threats to "our economic security, our financial institutions, our airlines, our national security." He said addressing it "requires an all-of-government approach" and "full transparency between the administration and Congress."

Lawler's prescription for the party was to "rebuild, lick their wounds" and focus on "the quality-of-life issues." After Nawaz described the midterm results as "such a sweeping rebuke," Lawler said politics is cyclical, a pattern he said he had seen repeatedly. He cited his own reputation for bucking his party, working across the aisle and delivering bipartisan results. He argued that Republicans need to speak to a wider electorate, saying that, in the next presidential election, "the candidate that seeks to talk to the 60 percent of the country is going to do well," and that "the party that finally realizes in Congress and in statehouses across the country that bipartisan cooperation is important will do well." Lawler observed that the pendulum "keeps swinging harder and faster," and said two more years of gridlock "is not going to serve the American people well."

Three questions from the interview remain open as the new Congress organizes: whether the administration will give Congress the classified debrief Lawler requested, whether Democrats will use the December 11 deadline to restrict funding for the Iran war, and whether a House Democratic majority, a Senate Democratic majority and a Republican president can work together, or whether they will produce the two years of gridlock and investigation Lawler predicted. Lawler will not be among the members answering them, having said he is finished with the House.
