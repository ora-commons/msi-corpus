## UK reports of Com networks increased sixfold from 2022 to 2024

A UK judge jailed Beau-Jack Mensforth on Friday for his activities as part of the Com, short for “the Community.” Mensforth operated from a bedroom in northeast England, but investigators have described the broader Com as a sprawling, fluid global network of online communities. The FBI estimates that thousands of people identify as current or recent members, while warning that their levels of associated activity vary.

The National Crime Agency said UK reports of Com networks increased sixfold from 2022 to 2024. Crime agencies describe a typical member as a boy or young man aged 16 to 25, primarily a native English speaker. DCI Steve Brown of Northumbria Police, who led the Mensforth investigation, said the offenders operate on every platform. The offenses attributed to members may include data theft, crypto scams, bringing down IT systems and attacks that slow networks.

The FBI’s first major public alert, issued in July 2025 under the heading “Theft, extortion and violence are a rising threat to youth online,” called the Com a growing and evolving threat. The agency described it as “a primarily English-speaking, international, online ecosystem comprised of multiple interconnected networks whose members, many of whom are minors, engage in a variety of criminal violations.”

Young men such as Mensforth targeted vulnerable girls and encouraged them to harm themselves or sacrifice their pets. Police said members often coerce girls to take indecent images and share them, then use blackmail and threats of swatting and doxing. Swatting means reporting fictitious incidents to emergency services at a victim’s address, sometimes prompting responders to arrive in numbers and armed, while doxing means threatening to publish someone’s personal details online.

Brown said the offenders seek out people with known vulnerabilities who want to feel wanted or valued. “They gain that trust and then they turn that trust against them by asking them to perform horrendous acts,” he said. Brown also said some members wanted control and enjoyed directing people to do things they normally would not do.

The National Crime Agency described members as “usually young men who are motivated by status, power, control, misogyny, sexual gratification, or an obsession with extreme or violent material.” The FBI said members’ motivations vary but often fall into “Financial gain, retaliation, ideology, sexual gratification and notoriety.”

The Molly Rose Foundation, a leading UK online safety charity, issued a public warning and called for coordinated action by governments, regulators, law enforcement and tech companies. Chief executive Andy Burrows said, “The growing threat posed by Com networks is the most chilling and urgent threat to children online today and it requires a swift and comprehensive response.”

Courts around the world are increasingly dealing with criminals linked to the Com. Sentences vary, but the reporting said it was thought that none was longer than the 77-year jail term handed down in Tennessee in August to Kyle Spitze, 27. Spitze’s sentence was for abetting the distribution of animal-crushing videos and material involving child sexual exploitation. The US Department of Justice said his crimes occurred as part of his association with an online network known for its “sadistic extortion of young and vulnerable people around the world.”

The FBI urged people to exercise caution when posting personal information, photos or videos on social media, dating sites or other online platforms. Police also advised parents to monitor their children’s online activity and check their devices.

After working on Mensforth’s case, Brown said he began checking his own children’s devices. “Personally, since this investigation, I do that to my kids,” he said. His advice to families was direct: “It’s simply asking parents: talk, know and act. Talk to your children, know what they’re doing and act upon it.”
