## CDC data show at least 39 consecutive months of declining deaths

White House officials said earlier this week that U.S. drug overdose deaths have plunged to their lowest level in roughly a decade, a change the administration said was saving nearly 50,000 lives each year. Researchers told NPR the sustained decline began in 2023, before Trump returned to office, and disputed the administration's account of when and why deaths fell.

Sara Carter, director of the White House Office of National Drug Control Policy, said, “The historic decline in drug overdose deaths demonstrates that America is unequivocally winning the fight against illicit drugs.” Carter credited heightened border security under Trump and what she described as success dismantling “narco-terrorist” organizations.

The administration's statement said gains occurred primarily over the previous 18 months because of policies enacted under Trump's leadership. It cited an approximately 41.5% decrease in total overdose deaths compared with the peak under the previous administration.

CDC data showed a different chronology: Fatal overdoses began declining in 2023 and continued falling for at least 39 consecutive months, rather than 18. Most of the gains occurred before Trump returned to office. By the time Biden left office, deaths had already dropped by roughly one-third, an improvement addiction experts described at the time as unprecedented.

Vanda Felbab-Brown, a drug policy expert at the Brookings Institution, said, “Since the spring of 2023, we have seen a steady decline in overdose deaths,” and added that its sustained nature was very important. Nabarun Dasgupta, a street drug researcher at the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill, was one of the first scientists to detect the drop, NPR reported. Dasgupta said the trend began in some regions and among some demographic groups as early as 2021 before rapidly gaining nationwide momentum. Dasgupta said the greatest rate of decline occurred from early 2024 through spring 2025.

Deaths from fentanyl smuggled into the United States from Mexico began rising in 2013 during President Obama's administration and accelerated during Trump's first term, NPR reported. Fentanyl overdoses then spiked sharply during the first two years of Biden's presidency, during the COVID-19 pandemic. Despite the reduction in deaths, fentanyl remains the leading cause of drug deaths nationwide.

Dasgupta, Felbab-Brown and other addiction experts credited the Biden administration with wider access to health care, broader availability of the opioid overdose medication naloxone and a deal with China that disrupted the flow of industrial chemicals used to make fentanyl. They also credited the Biden administration with significant drug-interdiction wins against Mexican cartels that smuggle fentanyl. Felbab-Brown said, “We have certainly been seeing over many years, going back to the Biden administration, important efforts to crack down against them.”

Both researchers said the mortality improvements continued under Trump. They said an accurate timeline matters because researchers are still trying to explain why deaths involving fentanyl surged and why so many fewer people are now dying.

In an emailed statement responding to NPR's question about Biden-era progress in the fentanyl fight, Carter, whom NPR described as a former podcaster, journalist and Fox News contributor, blamed Biden for failing to secure the U.S.-Mexico border and for allowing drug deaths to surge in the first place.

Carter rejected the idea that the Biden administration deserved credit, saying, “While drug overdose deaths eventually began to decline following the peak, the Biden Administration doesn't get to take credit for overseeing the highest drug overdose numbers in history, then bringing them down from their own failure.” Responding to the CDC timeline, she said, “this could be misinterpreted as progress on a graph, but it was not progress.” Carter said the Trump administration was well-positioned to build on reductions made over the last three years, in part by maintaining border security and continuing to “relentlessly attack” drug gangs. She said, “We are dismantling and eliminating the violent cartels and drug trafficking organizations through coordinated operations.”

Felbab-Brown said she did not share the administration's confidence that significant progress had been made dismantling such organizations. “I cannot think of one narco-terrorist organization that has been dismantled,” she said. “They all continue operating and they are all very strong. They all continue smuggling drugs.” Military experts have also questioned the effectiveness and legality of Trump's ongoing strikes against civilian boats in the Caribbean and eastern Pacific, which the Pentagon described as smuggling vessels.

Dasgupta said he was concerned about whether the gains can be sustained next year as the Trump administration implements deep Medicaid cuts passed by the Republican Congress. The Congressional Budget Office estimated the cuts could mean as many as 10 million Americans losing access to health insurance coverage. Dasgupta said, “Medicaid is the largest funder of addiction services in the United States.” He warned, “Cutting Medicaid eligibility or making it harder to access forms of effective addiction treatment is guaranteed to leave people out in the cold.”

The broad trend was not in dispute: Fatal overdoses continued falling under Trump even as the researchers rejected the White House's account of when and why. Fentanyl remained the leading cause of drug deaths, while the researchers said they were still trying to explain why the fentanyl death toll had surged and why so many fewer people were now dying.
