## Columnist says Milburn review omits any look at 'bad work'

Guardian columnist John Harris argues in a column published on 22 November that Britain's Neet crisis — young people not in education, employment or training — will not be resolved by moving more of them into work, because the work itself has lost respect and decent pay.

Harris writes that he read all 1,005 numbered paragraphs of the Milburn review, the report on young people and employment assembled by the former New Labour high-up Alan Milburn and published this week. He was struck, he writes, by "the thoroughness of its research and the urgency of its tone" — but also by "one glaring absence": any meaningful examination of what might be shorthanded as "bad work," despite its centrality to just about everything Milburn has worked on.

The review, according to Harris, partly blames the current crisis on how the state delivers benefits, education and health. He credits Milburn with rightly pointing out a "long-term decline in entry-level employment, youth apprenticeships and employer investment in training," and with proposing "a new bargain between government and employers to create and sustain more opportunities for young people."

Harris reads that bargain as a retreat from holding employers to account. He points to "whispers currently circulating about the abandonment of some of Angela Rayner's new programme of improved workers' rights," and writes that Milburn comes close to proposing not to expect more from employers but less.

Harris quotes the review's own language on this point. "Employers have faced higher costs, a maze of schemes, fragmented support and growing uncertainty about the rules of the game," Milburn writes, and have been "treated as if they were the problem rather than a central part of the solution." Milburn says he has picked up concerns about Rayner's workplace changes centred on "the impact the new legislation may have on youth hiring, especially in hospitality and retail."

Harris answers with a recent TUC report which, he writes, highlighted the low pay and job insecurity seemingly endemic to those sectors and pointed out how they contribute to "economic inactivity generally and specifically the rise in mental distress and economic inactivity among young people." Letting those employers off the hook, Harris writes, may not ease the Neets crisis but worsen it.

He also cites a TUC report published in 2020 on working for Amazon, a company thanked in the Milburn report's acknowledgements. Its central complaint, Harris writes, was "a business model that functions on driving down workers' rights." In the UK, that report said, "pickers are expected to pick and pack around 300 items per hour," with working weeks averaging around 55 hours, "especially over the peak Christmas period."

The review does acknowledge that the quality of work available to young people has generally "worsened," Harris writes, but he says Milburn merely observes that "warehouse jobs require speed, precision and compliance with tightly managed performance systems." The 1980s decimation of traditional work that opened the way for businesses such as Amazon, Harris adds, is summed up in the report as "industrial restructuring" — a phrase he calls blankly, and hilariously, inadequate to the result. He notes that the company's annual employee turnover in the US was reckoned at 150% in 2022, and writes that "people recoil and retreat."

Harris also flags a proposed £3.6bn of cuts to so-called "welfare," which he says risk impoverishing vulnerable young people and pushing them further away from the labour market.

On the state of the workforce more broadly, Harris draws on the Resolution Foundation's analysis of new British Social Attitudes survey data. He reports that job satisfaction in the UK has dropped to the lowest level ever recorded, and that the proportion of workers willing to work harder than required to help their employer succeed has fallen to its lowest ever level over the past decade — 55%. The proportion saying they had an interesting job fell from 74% to 63%, and the share reporting opportunities for advancement went from 35% to 26%.

To set the contrast, Harris reaches back to an oral history of South Wales, Richard King's *Brittle With Relics*, and a quotation from Nicky Wire of the Manic Street Preachers, whose father was a miner before joining the army and later working as a carpenter and builder. "The pay levels in the 60s and the 70s were brilliant for working-class people," Wire says in the passage Harris reproduces. "Everyone in our valley was wearing suits and going to Tunisia on package holidays … My dad just worked his bollocks off; he was one of the great workers. When he passed away, we had 'Father, son, brother, worker' on his memorial stone, because that's what he did."

Harris places the loss of that arrangement in a longer political history, writing that a combination of trade union power and the collective social conscience built into the postwar consensus produced a labour market that now seems almost inconceivable, and that Labour's 18 years out of power and the defeat of organised labour in the miners' strike opened the way for the wholesale reinvention of work.

In the short term, Harris writes, what Milburn recommends — among other things, a new youth opportunities service and increased spending on subsidising young people into work — may well lessen the crisis the review says it wants to solve, but will leave the fundamental problems almost completely untouched. "The point is plain," he writes: "not least for the young, until work is comprehensively reinjected with respect, humanity and decent levels of pay, the Neets crisis – and our national malaise – will go on."

Harris closes by quoting Wes Streeting's line that "the only thing worse than no hope is false hope," and notes that the prime minister has spoken confidently about reindustrialisation and about somehow rolling back 40 years of neoliberalism. A 10-year plan is apparently due before the end of the year; until then, Harris writes, the country carries on waiting.
