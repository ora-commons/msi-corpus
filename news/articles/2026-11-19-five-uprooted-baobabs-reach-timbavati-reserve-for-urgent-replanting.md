## Trees weighing up to 29 tonnes were intercepted in Durban in July

Five centuries-old baobab trees arrived at a reserve in Timbavati, north-eastern South Africa, on Wednesday, where they are to be replanted and supported by cables to help them recover. South African authorities said the trees had been moved without the necessary permissions. Weighing between 19 and 29 tonnes, the trees had been intended for an unnamed buyer in Georgia before they were intercepted in July and stranded in a Durban warehouse.

An exporter dug up the trees and tried to ship them thousands of miles to Georgia. South Africa’s Department of Forestry, Fisheries and the Environment confirmed that the trees were destined for Georgia but did not name the buyer.

Sarah Venter, founder of the Baobab Foundation who has led efforts to replant the South African trees, said she believes they have a good chance of surviving if they are replanted quickly. Venter said the timing of the replanting is critical. South Africa’s winter months, from May to October, leave baobabs dormant and without leaves, making the period a good time to move them.

“As soon as spring starts [in November], the trees use their water to flush their new leaves, and that’s when we need to get them into the ground as soon as possible,” Venter said.

The rescue mission has faced delays because of the challenges of moving such large trees. The baobabs were transported on special trucks capable of carrying abnormal loads. The five trees weigh between 19 and 29 tonnes, have a diameter of up to 4.5 metres (15ft) and a height of up to 12.5 metres.

Baobabs, known as the tree of life, are protected under South African law, and a permit is required to cut or disturb them. Venter said the trees’ shallow roots mean they do not need to be planted very deeply, and that they regrow fairly quickly.

Once replanted, the trees will be stabilized with cables and protected by electric fences to prevent damage from elephants.

Wambui Ippolito, a landscape designer, lecturer and horticulturist from Kenya, said: “A baobab isn’t just part of an ecosystem, it is an ecosystem in itself.” “These trees are biodiversity anchors,” she said.

Animals ranging from bats and bush babies to grey-headed parrots and the southern ground hornbill shelter in the trees’ cavities, while elephants, monkeys and warthogs feast on their fruit.

The trees have huge cultural importance, Ippolito said. “It has always been a sacred tree in Africa. It doesn’t matter where you go: Senegal, Mali, Ghana – wherever you go, if there is a community around a baobab tree, that tree is sacred,” she said. “Somebody’s great-great-great-great-great-grandparents sat under that tree, and it is part of human history in that area.”

The case echoes an episode in Kenya four years ago, when eight baobabs were uprooted for export to Shekvetili Dendrological Park. The park is owned by Bidzina Ivanishvili, an influential Georgian billionaire and the country’s former prime minister. Ivanishvili has filled the park with ancient, giant trees from Georgia, transporting them by boat along the Black Sea.

The export of the Kenyan baobabs was initially blocked after an outcry by environmentalists, who condemned the export as biopiracy. The shipment was later allowed to go ahead.

David Newton, programme office director for east and southern Africa for Traffic, an NGO that monitors trade in wild animals and plants, said such exports are very rare. “It is very unusual to see baobabs being used as ornamental plants … mainly because they are exceptionally difficult to dig out of the ground and transport, and then to successfully re-establish,” he said.

A criminal case has been opened against the owner of a landscaping company allegedly responsible for the illegal harvesting and transportation of the trees.

The five baobabs are thought to be between 200 and 300 years old. “Baobabs easily reach 1,000 years old, so they’ve got a few hundred years ahead of them if they survive,” Venter said.
