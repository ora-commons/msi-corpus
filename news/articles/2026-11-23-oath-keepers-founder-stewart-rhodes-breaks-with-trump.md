## Rhodes cited selective pardons, Iran war and administration corruption

Oath Keepers founder Stewart Rhodes said in an Associated Press interview in Irving, Texas, that he is "absolutely done" with Donald Trump. He now considers the Republican president a traitor and "one of the very worst in our nation's history."

The interview took place at the Christian Warriors After-Party, held during the three-day "Christ is King: America After Trump" conference. People at the gathering had drinks, and some approached Rhodes to shake his hand, sign up for the Oath Keepers or register for training in community defense and hurricane relief.

Rhodes' history dates to 2009, when he founded the Oath Keepers, a right-wing militia with the goal of encouraging members of the military and law enforcement to resist what they viewed as unconstitutional orders, such as disarming citizens or carrying out warrantless searches. Rhodes graduated from Yale Law School and worked for former U.S. Rep. Ron Paul of Texas. He later allied himself with Trump's political movement, and the Oath Keepers supported Trump's effort to remain in office after the 2020 election. Rhodes said Trump lost that race to Democrat Joe Biden because of election fraud.

Fueled by voter-fraud conspiracies, prosecutors said Oath Keepers members gathered weapons, organized trainings and assembled in Washington, where some helped storm the Capitol on Jan. 6, 2021. Rhodes did not enter the building. He was sentenced to 18 years after a seditious-conspiracy conviction for his role, one of the longest sentences in any Jan. 6 case. Rhodes disputed the proceeding, saying he was a "political prisoner" who faced "illegality" in his trial.

During the hearing, U.S. District Judge Amit Mehta said, "You are smart, you are charismatic and compelling and frankly that's what makes you dangerous." Mehta also said, "The moment you are released, whenever that may be, you will be ready to take up arms against your government."

Rhodes was behind bars when Trump began his political comeback. He said he watched the assassination attempt in Butler, Pennsylvania, on a prison television with other inmates. He saw Trump pump his fist and yell "fight," and he figured that God's hand was on the man. While serving time, Rhodes got a tattoo of Trump's defiant face after the attempt. At the time, the tattoo reflected his devotion to Trump.

After Trump won the 2024 election, he pardoned hundreds of people convicted of crimes related to Jan. 6, including those who assaulted police officers. Rhodes received a commutation rather than a pardon, which did not wipe his record clean. He said, "That was the first inkling." He also recalled: "He let us out of prison. We were grateful. I didn't get a pardon though."

Rhodes said Trump's war with Iran violated Trump's promises to end foreign conflicts. He said he felt affronted when Trump dismissed people urging his administration to release files about the Jeffrey Epstein case, calling them "PAST supporters."

On domestic government, Rhodes said, "We elected him to end the corruption in Washington, D.C. 'Drain the swamp.' Unfortunately his administration has been one of the most corrupt administrations in U.S. history." He added, "The pardons are pay-for-play and everything is pay-for-play."

After historic Democratic victories in Texas this year, Rhodes said, "The lion's share is because Trump pissed off his own base."

The Justice Department vacated Rhodes' conviction earlier this year, lifting his felon status and restoring his rights. Rhodes said he was working to rebuild the Oath Keepers, which leaked lists previously placed at tens of thousands of members. With his rights restored, Rhodes said his first priority was to get some guns.

Rhodes said the Republican Party had taken supporters for granted and that he did not want to choose between "a half a dose of poison or a full dose." He had not registered to vote in time for the election but said he would not have cast a ballot anyway.

Rhodes also said he had plans to change the prison tattoo, though he did not specify them. In a text message, he wrote, "I will have this prison tattoo of Trump 'modified,' to reflect that reality. That treason."

The White House did not respond to a request for comment. Sam Jackson, an associate professor at the University at Albany with a focus on anti-government extremism, said Rhodes was not alone in his break with Trump but saw an ideological through line. "They went from seeing Donald Trump as their ally in this fight against the conspiracy, to actually being co-opted by the conspiracy," Jackson said. He said the shift "points to deeper consistency in that understanding of the world as driven by hidden, evil actors."
