## GAO nominee pledges independence after senators question his OMB role

WASHINGTON — Members of the U.S. Senate Homeland Security Committee questioned Michael LoSapio, President Donald Trump’s nominee to lead the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency, on Thursday about nationwide security disruptions that lawmakers said had left the American public on edge. Democrats also accused the Trump administration of trying to confirm LoSapio and other nominees quickly during the lame-duck session, before Democrats gain majority control of the Senate in January with 56 seats.

Oklahoma Republican James Lankford, who is running to serve as the committee’s top GOP senator in the next Congress, defended Trump’s decision to make the nominations now. “The White House seemed to have followed the letter of the law,” Lankford said. “I'm not completely sure they followed the entire spirit of the law, but they have actually gone through the process that needs to be done to be able to put the nominees forward, to be able to have the input.”

Trump nominated LoSapio to be CISA director. The agency, housed within the Department of Homeland Security, handles election security and oversees protection of federal infrastructure from cyber attacks. The panel also questioned Eric M. Ueland at length about his nomination as comptroller general of the U.S. Government Accountability Office, the top federal government watchdog, a nomination that has generated opposition among Democrats. Ueland would serve 15 years if confirmed, and he currently works at the Office of Management and Budget.

The hearing followed days of widespread technology outages across the United States. The disruptions included tens of thousands of canceled flights, transaction problems at major banks and shut-down email services at the National Security Agency.

Michigan Democratic Sen. Elissa Slotkin, a former national security federal government worker, pressed LoSapio about the outages. “American citizens are jittery,” she said. Slotkin raised concerns about the Trump administration’s decision to eliminate more than 1,000 positions at CISA and asked LoSapio whether he agreed with that decision and whether the agency needed more funding.

LoSapio said he could not comment “on the current state of CISA” because he was coming from the private sector. He is Palantir Technology Inc.’s chief information security officer. LoSapio said he would evaluate the agency and ask Congress for more funding if he concluded it needed additional resources. “I'm going to get involved and understand where the gaps are, and I will come back to this committee with the gap analysis,” he said.

Slotkin said she was not satisfied with that answer. “At the end of the day, every American knows that cyberattacks are going up, that the risks are going up,” she said. “You should be able to say clearly, ‘There's increasing threat, therefore, we need increasing resources to deal with it.’”

Connecticut Democratic Sen. Richard Blumenthal asked LoSapio who won the 2020 presidential election because CISA handles election security. Trump had falsely claimed the election was stolen from him, and supporters galvanized by Trump had stormed the U.S. Capitol to try to stop certification. LoSapio said, “I'm not going to answer the question about an election six years ago.” Blumenthal said LoSapio should not be allowed to lead CISA if he could not say former President Joe Biden won. “You are disqualified from the position that you've been nominated to fill,” Blumenthal said.

Blumenthal then asked whether the U.S. Capitol was attacked on Jan. 6, 2021. “I don't believe it was an attack,” LoSapio said. Blumenthal asked whether LoSapio was “alive on the planet” during the Jan. 6, 2021, events at the U.S. Capitol, and LoSapio said he was.

The committee’s questions for Ueland focused on the Office of Management and Budget’s impoundment practices. The Government Accountability Office investigates fraud or violations of federal law, and Michigan Democratic Sen. Gary Peters said GAO had found several times that OMB violated the Impoundment Control Act of 1974. The law applies when federal funds approved by Congress are withheld without congressional consent.

The Supreme Court last year allowed the Trump administration to move forward with withholding billions in foreign aid through a pocket rescission, a process in which the president makes a late request to cancel funds near the end of the fiscal year. The court stopped short of deciding the merits or whether the president may unilaterally impound congressional appropriations.

Peters asked Ueland whether he agreed with GAO’s conclusions, and Ueland declined to reach one. Ueland said he had not reviewed the reports and had chosen not to do so because he did not want to prejudge what GAO had advised Congress. “Instead, a clear commitment to continue to follow the process that GAO as comptroller general, is what I give to all of you,” he said.

Committee chair Kentucky Republican Sen. Rand Paul asked Ueland whether he would ignore a Trump administration request to stop an investigation. Ueland said he would. “We will fully follow through on charges from Congress, and in the event of obstacles, figure out ways to proceed to try to elicit information,” he said.

New Hampshire Democratic Sen. Maggie Hassan questioned Ueland about his nomination while he worked for OMB Director Russell Vought, who has publicly commented that GAO should not exist. Ueland said Vought had not said much about the nomination and that the most acknowledgment he received was a head nod.

Hassan appeared skeptical of Ueland’s account. “You've been nominated to serve as a watchdog over … an agency that your boss thinks shouldn't even exist, and now you are claiming to this committee that you only had a single passing conversation with your current boss about your nomination to lead that agency,” she said. “Is that correct?”

LoSapio did not say whether CISA needed more funding, while Ueland had not reviewed the impoundment reports about which he was questioned. Senators also took up other nominations during the hearing. Both men remained nominees awaiting Senate confirmation.
