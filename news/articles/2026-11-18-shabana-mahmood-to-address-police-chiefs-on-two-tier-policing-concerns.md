## Police impartiality standard under development would consolidate key guidance

UK Home Secretary Shabana Mahmood is scheduled to address the National Police Chiefs’ Council at 1pm on November 18. The Guardian’s live political blog said her speech would focus on so-called two-tier policing, a new impartiality standard and plans affecting recruitment and leadership. Advance extracts showed she planned to respond to the perception that two-tier policing existed rather than declare that it did.

The live blog opened by describing Mahmood as having taken on two of the government’s most difficult jobs. It said Labour faced pressure from anti-migrant populists in Reform UK, the Conservative Party and the media, placing Mahmood — who served as justice secretary and home secretary — on the frontline of debates between right and progressive politics. The blog called her strategy “bold, novel, and largely successful” and said she “disarms her critics by agreeing with them,” while noting that she remained a Labour cabinet minister and sometimes sounded “a bit like Chris Philp.” It said the speech would provide another example of that approach.

The Guardian described so-called two-tier policing as the theory that, under pressure to be politically correct, police had become biased toward leftwing causes and nervous about enforcing the law against minority ethnic people for fear of appearing racist. It said the existence of that version of two-tier policing had become an article of faith on the right, although actual policing was very different most of the time.

The live blog said Robert Hutton of the Critic had satirised the idea in a sketch two years ago. It described the sketch as dating from the 2024 summer riots, when racists were trying to burn down hotels housing asylum seekers. Hutton framed fairness around the treatment of different groups of marchers, pointing to rainbow flags on police cars at Pride and police taking the knee at Black Lives Matter. He asked whether police waved swastikas and when viewers had last seen video of an officer kicking a brown-skinned person in the head or trying to set fire to a building full of refugees.

Advance extracts of Mahmood’s remarks did not say that two-tier policing existed; they said it was important to respond to the perception that it did. The extracts say she will say: “We must tackle head-on the corrosive and growing perception of two-tier policing in this country.”

The extracts also say she will say: “Now let me be clear: I know the vast majority of officers police impartially and that policing without fear or favour is their creed.” The extracts further say: “But there have been examples of differential treatment and they have harmed policing, allowing people – often motivated actors – to whip up the idea that there is two-tier policing in this country.”

A Guardian article cited in the live blog said that, in some respects, policing was two-tier. It reported: “Black people remained more likely than any other ethnic group to be subjected to almost every form of force, from handcuffing and dog bites to firearms and Tasers, when compared with population or arrest figures.”

According to the Press Association’s preview, Mahmood will lament the low levels of black officers in police forces and warn against “misguided attempts at course correction” that lead to “differential treatment.” She will also warn officers against engaging with “activist groups” and direct them to “leave the politics to the politicians.”

Mahmood will also set out plans to fast-track talented recruits, including recruits from outside policing, into senior ranks, addressing what the live blog described as sometimes “inadequate” leadership.

She will tell chief constables and police and crime commissioners that a new “impartiality standard” is being developed to ensure all members of the public are treated equally. The standard will bring guidance on key impartiality principles into one place. It is intended to give police confidence in handling cases regardless of someone’s race, faith or other characteristics, and to give the public confidence that people will be treated fairly.

A More in Common poll conducted earlier in the year found that a third of Britons believe police treat people from ethnic minority backgrounds more favourably than white people; the poll said this marked a doubling in the last two years. Mahmood will tell the National Police Chiefs’ Council that it is “shameful,” 33 years after the murder of black teenager Stephen Lawrence, that the proportion of black officers remains virtually unchanged.

The planned remarks say Mahmood will argue that some approaches intended to promote equality, including the former Police Anti-Racism Commitment, have risked undermining confidence in impartial policing. Her planned warning was: “We must also be wary of misguided attempts at course correction, where the pursuit of equality leads instead to differential treatment.”

The live blog described the Police Anti-Racism Commitment as frequently criticised by the Conservatives and Reform UK. It quoted Mahmood’s planned remarks: “To the public, it was patently absurd to tell police officers that fair policing does not mean treating everyone the same.” The Police Anti-Racism Commitment says producing equality of policing outcomes “does not mean treating everyone ‘the same’”.

The Guardian’s political agenda listed: 9.30am, Chris Bryant, the Northern Ireland secretary, was due to give evidence to the Commons Northern Ireland committee; 11am, Yvette Cooper, the health secretary, was due to give a speech at the King’s Fund conference; noon, Andy Burnham was due to face Kemi Badenoch at PMQs; 1pm, Mahmood was due to give her policing speech; and after 12.30pm, MPs were due to begin a debate on a Tory opposition day motion opposing most key features of the prime minister’s proposed UK-EU reset deal due to be agreed on Friday.
