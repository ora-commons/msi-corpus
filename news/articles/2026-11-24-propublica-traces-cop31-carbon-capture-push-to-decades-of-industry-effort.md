## Final draft retained a nonbinding “gigatonne scale” goal for CCS

ProPublica and Drilled reported that oil- and gas-producing nations, led by Saudi Arabia, blocked fossil fuel phaseout language at the 31st annual United Nations climate summit in Antalya, Turkey, known as COP31. Oil- and gas-producing nations promoted expanded carbon capture as a way to reduce the pollution their fuels cause, while disputes over who would pay remained unresolved. The investigation traced the policy push to decades of effort by fossil fuel companies and by nations dependent on fossil fuels. The final draft retained a nonbinding “gigatonne scale” goal even as references to transitioning away from fossil fuels disappeared.

In an investigation conducted for more than a year, ProPublica and Drilled found that industry funding contributed to influential CCS research at universities, petrostates pressed the United Nations to validate the technology without supporting evidence, and climate modelers assumed it could operate at enormous scale despite its troubled real-world record. Amy Westervelt and Maddie Stone of Drilled contributed reporting.

Carbon capture and storage, or CCS, catches greenhouse gases from industry, or filters them from the air, and injects them underground. The technology theoretically permits continued use of fossil fuels while capturing some pollution. ProPublica and Drilled reported that the industry has struggled for three decades to meet even the lowest-end projections and currently buries only a sliver of the amount contemplated by the COP31 benchmarks, leading many climate experts to conclude that carbon capture will never be a practical solution to climate change. Most CCS is used to squeeze additional oil from aging fields. Critics say setting global goals for the technology would lead to further subsidy of oil production rather than limit or reduce the amount of carbon dioxide that reaches the air.

Michael Mann, a climate scientist at the University of Pennsylvania, wrote on Bluesky that “The effort by bad faith petrostate actors to block any agreement at #COP31 on fossil fuel phaseout in favor of (hypothetical future) carbon capture is the best evidence yet that moral hazard FUNDAMENTALLY compromises carbon capture as a climate solution.”

One draft released Nov. 16 included language encouraging a transition from fossil fuels and recommended scaling CCS so it would remove at least 500 million tons of carbon dioxide a year by 2035. By Thursday, drafters had removed all references to a transition away from fossil fuels while leaving the carbon capture benchmarks intact. In the final proposal submitted Sunday, two days after talks were scheduled to end, the carbon capture targets were less specific but remained as the nonbinding “gigatonne scale” goal.

Behind the scenes in Antalya, about 700 carbon capture lobbyists were registered for COP31, compared with 531 at the previous year’s summit in Brazil, according to an analysis of United Nations participant lists by the Center for International Environmental Law. That was more than the combined delegations of all 14 Pacific Small Island Developing States. The analysis found that 45 representatives were admitted to closed-door negotiating sessions; the center had not completed a comparable analysis of lobbyists for other technologies.

ProPublica described three recurring camps at the talks. Saudi Arabia, Russia and other oil-producing states — at times with tacit U.S. support — favored gradual changes to the energy system to preserve economic stability. Wealthy, industrialized nations pushed for an energy transition but resisted measures to pay for both the transition and damage caused by warming. Low- and middle-income nations would support fossil fuel phaseout only if powerful economies committed to supporting the cost of their transition.

A coalition of nations, including the European Union, rejected the proposal outright. Romain Ioualalen, the global policy lead for the nonprofit Oil Change International, said the proposed agreement and its emphasis on CCS “really had the fossil fuel industry’s fingerprints all over it.” Oil Change International is a research and advocacy group that pushes for a phaseout of fossil fuels.

ProPublica reported that scientists uniformly agree that phasing out fossil fuels is a prerequisite to addressing global warming. In the closing session, a delegate from Panama said the country could not support the language in the final COP31 text in part because it “wagers too much” on CCS “instead of calling for deep changes.” Vanuatu, a small island nation in the Pacific especially vulnerable to rising sea levels, warned that the text “risks legitimizing continued fossil fuel dependence” and said carbon capture must not become “an excuse to prolong the fossil fuel era.”

Saudi Arabia, speaking for the 22 countries in the League of Arab States, insisted CCS was an essential part of a “package of solutions” to address climate change. The Saudi delegate argued that removing concrete solutions from the table would make no sense if the climate challenge was as important as some delegates had said.

Carbon capture was one part of a larger debate that also covered other ways to clean up energy sources. Its weight in this year’s talks reflected long-standing efforts to tie climate action to the technology’s success and the failure to enact any other meaningful agreement that would slow the rate of carbon dioxide emissions, ProPublica and Drilled said. The final draft retained a carbon capture goal even as fossil fuel transition language disappeared.

ProPublica and Drilled found no signs that carbon capture could overcome its failures and setbacks at the pace the United Nations says would be needed to mitigate global warming. Saudi Arabia and other oil-producing countries still promoted CCS as necessary, while Panama and Vanuatu said the final language placed too much weight on the technology. The failed negotiations left the summit’s main political accord unresolved after two weeks of talks.
