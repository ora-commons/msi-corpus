## UBS estimates $50 billion or more could leave the AI startup market

The resignation of Nvidia Chief Executive Jensen Huang could be as consequential as any corporate shake-up in history, The Wall Street Journal's CIO Journal wrote Monday. Its implications extend far beyond the company itself, which has defined the cutting edge of AI systems around the world, the Journal wrote, and the effects are spreading through the semiconductor market, venture-backed startups and geopolitics. The workarounds that allowed China to continue to access Nvidia chips despite U.S. export controls "look like they could close for real," the Journal reported, and it remains to be seen how China will respond. The billions of dollars in Nvidia revenue that flowed from those workarounds could disappear, and Nvidia's role as a source of capital to smaller AI companies could be diminished.

The Journal examined how Huang turned Chinese demand for American chips into a multibillion-dollar business, building a network of partners across Asia that could serve Chinese customers. Those workarounds made up for much of what Huang couldn't win in Washington, where his push for direct sales of top chips to China mostly failed.

Markets were already pricing the shakeup. Nasdaq futures were down 0.9% on Monday morning, and chip stocks looked set to keep falling before the opening bell. The NASDAQ Composite index stood at 25,136.27 as of Nov. 16, according to the Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis FRED database (raw series value).

Nvidia's China exposure runs deeper than its reported totals. The company reported $12.4 billion in revenue from China in the six months ending July 26, about 7% of its total, but analysts say far more of its sales ultimately come from Chinese customers. Culper Research, the short seller that raised concerns about Nvidia's China exposure in May, estimates that China drove more than 20% of the company's revenue last fiscal year. Much of that business ran through Southeast Asia, where data centers can legally import Nvidia chips and rent the computing power to Chinese firms.

According to records reviewed by the Journal, Megaspeed — the company of Huang's onetime mistress, Alice Huang — arranged to ship nearly $12 billion in high-end Nvidia chips to Chinese companies between March 2024 and June 2025. Megaspeed told the Journal that it follows U.S. export controls and has hired outside lawyers to investigate. The records don't show how many of the chips were actually delivered.

## UBS estimates Nvidia's startup pullback could take $50 billion or more out of the market

Nvidia's reputation as an "open checkbook" for the AI economy was already tightening before Huang's exit, after he told cloud partners last month that those days were over and that they needed upfront customer payments to get GPUs, the Journal reported. Now, with Kress in charge, startups expect a deeper retreat, which UBS estimates could take $50 billion or more out of the market. Pending deals hanging in the balance include an offer to license technology from and hire staff away from Reflection AI, and a planned investment valuing Thinking Machines Lab at $40 billion. Both startups are, as the Journal put it, "burning huge amounts of capital each month to pay for the computing power to develop their AI models."

The political response is already in motion. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent on Sunday called on Nvidia's board to choose an outsider as CEO and questioned whether Nvidia knowingly sold powerful chips to China, in contravention of export restrictions. Bessent has tried to distance himself from the Commerce Department, led by Howard Lutnick, which attempted to stall restrictions on chip exports. Amid the investigations, the Trump administration is pressing ahead with plans to spread U.S. AI abroad, naming more than two dozen companies, including Nvidia, as AI "national champions" — a designation that gives them faster export-license approvals, federal financing and government support overseas.

In Silicon Valley, a number of figures have rallied behind a "Stand With Jensen" petition that has passed 175,000 names. Some people, including prominent Nvidia employees, told the Journal that their names appear on the petition even though they never signed. The Financial Times, citing several people familiar with the matter, reported that Huang has privately talked with people close to him about coming back.

The Journal's assessment is that Huang's resignation does not signal the end of Nvidia or the global adoption of AI. Corporate demand for AI predates the launch of ChatGPT in late 2022 and will continue, the Journal wrote, and the innovation powering the technological and economic "flywheel" that Huang liked to talk about will keep spinning at Big Tech companies and early-stage startups alike. That flywheel may turn a bit more slowly, and a generally more cautious industry could end up favoring current market leaders. The most lasting implications may be geopolitical, depending on how far China doubles down on development of its own national AI supply chain and what that means over time for the ongoing contest between the U.S. and China for global technological dominance.

The week ahead will test the fallout. Nvidia delivers earnings Tuesday, and investors are looking to Anthropic to price its initial public offering. China's Alibaba has added a new flagship model of its own.
