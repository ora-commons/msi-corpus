## Western Australia secures permanent carve-out under domestic supply rules

A federal gas reservation scheme requiring Australia’s largest exporters to reserve up to 20% of their product for domestic use will go ahead with Coalition support. The opposition voted with the government in a late Senate sitting on Thursday night, giving Labor the numbers it needed. The scheme is scheduled to begin in January 2028.

The agreement followed weeks of hard negotiations between the two major parties. Speaking in the upper house on Thursday, Shadow Resources Minister Susan McDonald said the Coalition would not oppose the proposal. “The Coalition will act in the national interest,” she said. She also said, “We will allow the government’s proposal to move forward because we do believe in a domestic gas reservation and more gas being made available to Australia.”

On Friday, Resources Minister Madeleine King said the scheme would mean cheaper gas prices for Australians but said she could not say by how much. King said, “We don’t want to set a figure at this time because the experience with price caps when you name a figure is that also becomes the floor, the lower price,” and added, “And we want to make sure the market’s able to operate to drive prices down as far as they can.”

Labor granted Western Australia a permanent carve-out after lobbying from the federal Coalition and the state premier, Roger Cook. King said: “WA is really the inspiration for this national scheme because they’ve had a scheme since 2008, which has been really important for gas prices and supply in WA.” Western Australia already has a scheme requiring exporters to reserve 15% of LNG for the domestic market over the life of a development.

The existing Western Australian target had not been met. Cook said in August that the proportion of LNG delivered to the domestic market was 8.7% in 2021 and rose to 12.5% by 2025.

The Guardian reported that business groups and gas companies pressured the federal Coalition to reach a deal with Labor and keep the Greens out of negotiations. Industry Minister Tim Ayres said the changes would deliver “fundamental economic reform that will make Australia stronger.” He also said, “Australia’s gas is a national asset, and that national asset should be managed in the national interest.”

Ayres said the policy was not an export ban or cap. “This is not an export ban, nor is it an export cap. It is a predictable and proportional contribution to domestic supply,” he said. Ayres also said Australia would remain a reliable supplier of gas for its key partners.

The key sticking point between the parties was the government’s requirement for gas companies to oversupply the market by 10%, a provision designed to drive down prices for households and industry. That requirement remains in the agreement. The Coalition had originally argued the requirement would lower prices to a level that would dry up future gas investment, and the Coalition secured a statutory review to assess the policy’s impact on investment after five years.

The Greens demanded a 25% tax on gas exports in return for their support and voted against the scheme in its current form. Greens leader David Shoebridge called the scheme a “stitch-up” against the Australian public. “This week, parliament ends with Labor and the Coalition cutting their deal for the gas industry, delivering for the 1% of their corporate donors,” he said before the vote in the Senate.

The Guardian described the Senate vote as advancing the government’s final major piece of legislation for 2026, with the Greens’ bid for a 25% export tax denied. The lower house was set to sit for a short period on Friday morning, a day later than expected, to pass legislation amended in the Senate.
