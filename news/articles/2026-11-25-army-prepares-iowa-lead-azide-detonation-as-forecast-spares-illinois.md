## Army prepares Iowa lead azide detonation as forecast spares Illinois

**Subtype:** fact

- The U.S. Army plans to detonate nearly 10,000 pounds of lead azide at 9 a.m. Wednesday at the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant near Burlington, Iowa.
- Army models forecast that northeast winds would carry any lead fallout toward Iowa and northeast Missouri rather than Illinois.
- Illinois health officials say blood testing is the only way to evaluate lead exposure and advise residents concerned about exposure to contact a doctor to decide whether they should be tested.
- The Federal Emergency Management Agency will help relocate residents within a 5-mile evacuation zone southeast of the Iowa plant.
- The Army urges people in the affected area to stay inside or evacuate until the Army issues an all-clear.

## Illinois health officials advise concerned residents to consult a doctor about whether to be tested

Illinois state agencies are preparing for possible fallout from a controlled U.S. Army detonation near Burlington, Iowa, as an updated Army forecast says northeast winds should keep the lead away from Illinois. Army officials said Tuesday that nearly 10,000 pounds of lead azide would be detonated at 9 a.m. Wednesday at the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant. The plant lies about 10 miles across the Mississippi River from Illinois.

New forecasts released at an Army news conference at the plant showed conditions expected to spare Illinois from any lead fallout. Winds are predicted to come from the northeast, which Army modeling says would carry any lead toward communities in Iowa and northeast Missouri. The forecast differs from previous versions in which the wind could have carried fallout into Illinois.

“We’re taking advantage of tomorrow, Wednesday at 9 a.m., because the forecasted weather patterns are ideal to protect health and safety across the region,” Acting Army Secretary Adam Telle said.

The plan has met backlash from members of both political parties and members of the public, who complained that the Army had failed to communicate its plans and the risk of lead exposure adequately. Telle said, “We don’t believe that the public should be in a panic.” He said the Army was calm about the operation and wanted to provide as much information as possible.

Although Army models show no effects in Illinois, U.S. Rep. Eric Sorensen, a Democrat from the Quad Cities, said residents immediately along the river in Hancock County should remain cautious. Sorensen is a certified meteorologist who worked for television stations in Rockford and the Quad Cities for decades before his election to Congress in 2022.

“When we look at a possible detonation on Wednesday, we still have that wind out of the north so that is going to put areas like Fort Madison (in Iowa) and far western Illinois into that zone,” Sorensen said in a video on Tuesday on his social media. He urged residents to follow precautions and rely on trusted local media, warning of misinformation online.

The Illinois Environmental Protection Agency released a webpage with resources and recommendations for Illinois residents. As many people travel for Thanksgiving, state health officials recommend avoiding affected areas if possible and using air recirculation in cars when driving through the area. Health officials say blood testing is the only way to evaluate exposure to lead and advise people concerned about being exposed to contact their doctor to decide whether they should be tested. No amount of lead is safe for humans.

Children under 6 and pregnant women are the most vulnerable to lead, according to health officials. The Illinois Department of Public Health said it would provide additional testing guidance if necessary after the detonation. Illinois also posted information for residents seeking claims against the Army if livestock suffer health effects or property is damaged.

U.S. Rep. Mary Miller, a Republican who represents potentially affected parts of Illinois, said in a Monday-afternoon statement that residents deserved clear answers and accountability. “My constituents in Illinois, and indeed all Americans, deserve clear answers and accountability,” Miller said. “The lack of transparency and communication since this problem was first uncovered has been deeply disappointing. My colleagues and I will get to the bottom of what went wrong.”

U.S. Sen. Dick Durbin, an Illinois Democrat, said in a statement that the federal government should do more to prevent public-health impacts. “If we can spend billions to wage war in Iran, (the Department of Defense) can afford to do everything it can to protect families in Iowa and Illinois from the explosion at the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant,” Durbin said. “I’m calling on DOD to position a public health emergency team ASAP to assess the risks of the Army’s detonation tomorrow.”

The detonation was originally planned for the preceding Friday but was postponed after public backlash and a lawsuit by Iowa environmental groups seeking to stop it. A judge ruled Tuesday afternoon that the Army could proceed. Iowa Republican Gov. Kim Reynolds said she trusted the Army’s plan was the best choice.

Army officials had held town halls in recent months in Middletown, the Iowa community of a little more than 300 residents where the plant is located. Nevertheless, many people in the region who could be exposed to lead learned of the planned detonation only days before the original Friday date.

Army and other federal officials said they explored all options earlier in the year to neutralize the deteriorating lead but determined a controlled detonation was the only safe option. They said the risk of an uncontrolled explosion grew as winter approached because the stockpile became more unstable in cold weather. “The controlled detonation is the only way to go and further inaction leads to potentially greatly increased results,” said Maj. Gen. Eric Shirly, the commanding general of the Army Sustainment Command.

Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, Firearms and Explosives Director Rob Cekada said the government stopped using lead azide, an explosive material used to make military ordnance, 15 years ago because it was sensitive and difficult to maintain. The stockpile was moved to Iowa in 2009, when some of the material already was more than 60 years old and deteriorating.

When the Army reviewed the stored material in March, Cekada said, it found crystallization outside the water- and ethanol-filled barrels used to keep the lead azide stable. The crystals made it impossible to remove the barrel lids and treat the material, he said.

Cekada said an uncontrolled detonation was “just a matter of time” because the crystals were highly sensitive to shocks and friction. He said movement or significant temperature changes, especially freezing temperatures, could cause the lead azide to explode without warning.

The Army has insulated the bunkers to prevent spontaneous detonation and began developing disposal plans in April. The stockpile is stored in two earth-covered concrete bunkers. Two-story sand barriers and a mixture of water, foam and jell will be used to absorb as many particles as possible.

The Army will combine C4 explosives, diesel and other charges to set off two detonations at the plant. The operation will produce loud noise and ground vibrations that the Army has warned could shatter windows in Middletown. “Our goal is to keep as much of it as we can here on our Iowa Army Ammunition Plant to prevent it from traveling,” Telle said.

Col. Aaron Teller of the 71st Ordnance Group in Fort Carson, Colorado, oversees the operation. Teller said the last similar detonation occurred in Colorado in 1977 without mitigation, but 75% of the lead was contained at the blast site.

The Army forecast shows lead could settle on the ground across a narrow stretch of Iowa and northeast Missouri. It could remain airborne farther into Missouri through Kirksville toward U.S. Route 36 about eight hours after the explosion. About 350 farms are in the path, according to the U.S. Department of Agriculture. The Army recommends that people remain inside or evacuate until it issues an all-clear, while officials said they hope the forecast represents a worst-case scenario.

Regional temperatures approached 70 degrees over the weekend, but snow was forecast later Wednesday. The U.S. Environmental Protection Agency has deployed teams to the area, including in Illinois, to conduct sampling before, during and after the explosion, with results to be shared publicly. FEMA will help relocate residents within the 5-mile evacuation zone southeast of the plant beginning Tuesday and sent cell phone alerts Tuesday morning. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention said it was prepared to assist the Iowa Department of Health.
