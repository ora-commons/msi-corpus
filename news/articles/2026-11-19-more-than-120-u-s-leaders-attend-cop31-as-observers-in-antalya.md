## Midterm gains shift state and local climate politics, Quebec envoy says

More than 120 state, local and business leaders from the United States are attending U.N. climate talks in the Turkish resort town of Antalya, where they attend meetings, offer advice and spread goodwill without holding a chair at the negotiating table. The U.S. government pulled the United States out of the entire process, leaving those officials to make the case that the federal position does not represent the country. The American delegation includes 18 Democratic lawmakers, city mayors, former Vice President Al Gore, former Secretary of State and chief climate envoy John Kerry, and California Gov. Gavin Newsom, a potential U.S. presidential candidate in 2028.

Gina McCarthy, a former U.S. Environmental Protection Agency chief who leads the climate advocacy network America's All In, said the delegation can show that American climate work continues without federal participation. “Hopefully, we’ll be able to explain to people that while our federal government is not standing up the way that certainly we think they should... we can move forward even in the absence of poor leadership,” McCarthy said. Her organization is one of the groups coordinating the delegation.

U.S. Rep. Mike Levin, a California Democrat, said he and his 17 colleagues have attended between 60 and 70 meetings at COP31 so far. “In every single meeting, I think, we’re making that good faith attempt to signal that we are all in,” Levin said in an interview with The Associated Press. He added: “Over time, we’ll restore trust and confidence, as we’ve attempted to do in these last few days.”

President Donald Trump withdrew the United States from the 2015 Paris climate pact and from global negotiations on curbing climate change after he returned to office in 2025, and he rejects the established science showing that global warming is caused by the burning of coal, oil and natural gas. Fossil fuels, which Trump wants to increase, produce heat-trapping carbon dioxide that stays in the atmosphere for at least a century and leads to global temperature rise and extreme weather events. China is currently the world’s biggest annual carbon polluter, while the United States has emitted more carbon dioxide in the past century than any other nation.

Democratic Rep. Jared Huffman of California said at COP31 on Monday that the administration’s approach does not speak for the country. “The wrecking ball of Donald Trump and this out-of-touch administration, and the temporarily empty chair of the United States at this COP31, does not represent what the American people and businesses want or need,” Huffman said. White House spokeswoman Taylor Rogers said “The United States is focused on common sense, not climate nonsense” and that “The Trump Administration will continue to work with our allies around the world to unleash affordable, reliable, secure energy sources.”

In the U.S. midterm elections earlier this month, Democrats took back both the Senate and the House of Representatives with large margins, and many people at COP31 said the result changed the atmosphere. Jean Lemire, a climate change envoy for the Canadian province of Quebec, said “the midterms changed completely the way that subnationals are thinking in the U.S.” Referring to the more than 80 state and local governments at the talks, Lemire said some had previously been afraid of retribution from the U.S. government if they acted heavily on climate change. “Now, it’s completely changed. And you will see that more and more U.S. states will be part of the solution,” she said.

Spanish Environment Minister Sara Aagesen said the midterm results were “also giving us a lot of hope,” and said Spain is working on climate change agreements with California and Washington.

Experts said other nations and leaders must fill the void left by the U.S. federal government. Mohamed Adow, director of Power Shift Africa, a climate advocacy group, said the U.S. absence was disappointing but that the gathering of American leaders showed Trump does not speak for the whole country. “The absence of the U.S. government at COP31 is deeply disappointing, but the presence of so many American leaders shows that Trump doesn’t speak for the whole country,” Adow said. “Climate change won’t wait for the next American election and neither can we.”

Danish climate and energy minister Samira Nawa said the U.S. withdrawal leaves other governments responsible for carrying the work forward. “I’m very sad that the United States of America turned their back on climate action, but that means that the rest of us, we have to do our part,” Nawa said.

Ilana Seid, chair of the Alliance of Small Island States and a U.N. ambassador from Palau, said the United States remains “incredibly persuasive when they negotiate” and that, “Just by virtue of their size and their power, they influence the world.” Without the country in the room, she said, “it feels a little bit different because there’s no... big country that’s really trying to influence decisions.”

Final agreements require near-unanimous approval, which leaves China, the European Union and India — the largest emitters among non-American countries — with substantial influence. Small island nations often act as the moral voice for countries harmed by climate change, while a coalition of poorer nations presses for more financial help for developing countries. Oil-rich nations in the Middle East frequently put a brake on stronger climate action.

Jasper Inventor of Greenpeace International said other countries focus less on America’s political swings than they used to and are learning to stop waiting for Washington. “The world hasn’t stopped caring about American climate action, but it is learning to stop waiting for Washington,” Inventor said. The lack of U.S. leadership also makes it easier for other countries to opt against strong actions to reduce emissions, said Jose Reyeros, an activist with the Make Polluters Pay campaign, who called it “a lot of backtracking as a ripple effect from other nations.”

The delegation holds no chair and no vote, and Levin said restoring trust and confidence would take time: “It won’t happen overnight, but I think there is a general acknowledgment that the United States is needed in the room.”
