## Experts weigh recusal compliance against an institutional gap

The U.S. Supreme Court was unable to hear a music copyright case this week after four of its nine justices recused themselves, leaving the court without a quorum. Justices Sonia Sotomayor, Neil Gorsuch, Amy Coney Barrett and Ketanji Brown Jackson all stepped aside; the court did not publicly explain why.

The case concerns who owns foreign copyrights. Major publishing companies and lobbying groups for movies and the recording industry all lined up this past summer to ask the Supreme Court to accept it. Paul Clement and Lisa Blatt, two of the most prominent lawyers who practice before the high court, had contributed briefs.

The court never reached the substance, apparently because of extracurricular work by nearly half of the justices, NPR reported. "All this work was done, probably hundreds of lawyers participated in this matter, they all strategically said, 'Let's bring this case to the court now,' and then what happened?" asked Josh Blackman, a constitutional law professor at the South Texas College of Law in Houston. "There's no quorum."

With four of the court's nine justices recused, the court was left without enough justices to proceed. The watchdog group Fix the Court advanced one explanation: all four recusant justices have book deals with Penguin Random House, which is part of a German conglomerate that is a party in the case.

"When you have a financial stake in a party or your finances could somehow be impacted by the outcome of a case, under federal law and the Supreme Court code of conduct, the justices are supposed to recuse," said Gabe Roth, who leads Fix the Court.

Roth and other experts called the situation good news, bad news because it was a good thing the justices followed the recusal rules. Renee Knake Jefferson, a legal ethics expert at the University of Houston, said the recusals in this case point to a deeper problem. "When four justices need to step aside, the Supreme Court can no longer decide the case, and that becomes an institutional problem, not just an individual ethics issue," she said.

Knake Jefferson said the problem was acute at the nation's highest court because "there's no other justices who can step in and substitute for them." Legal observers said the situation highlights a broader institutional issue with the court's recusal policy, which they described as largely self-enforced.

Tim Kappel, a lawyer for the songwriter in the case the court declined to hear, said in a written statement that the recusal speaks to the broad impact of copyright law and the expansive definition of what it means to be an artist. "This fight was and will continue to be on behalf of all artists, even those who happen to sit on the Supreme Court," Kappel said.

Three years ago, the Supreme Court published a code of conduct, which NPR identified as the court's first-ever code of ethics. NPR's account said the justices largely police themselves. Knake Jefferson said justices who want to write books could put money from their advances and royalties in separate accounts they could not touch until retirement. The court had no comment on that proposal.

Blackman said Congress should pass legislation to address the book royalties issue. "We need to have the justices focused on their day job more," Blackman said. "Grant more cases, decide more opinions, write more. The books are fun, but that's not why they're there."
