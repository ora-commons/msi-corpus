## New procedures aim to streamline immigration habeas petitions

The U.S. Attorney’s Office for the Western District of Washington will again assign attorneys to represent the federal government in every case in which a person challenges detention by immigration authorities. The change followed a period when the office lacked enough staff and district judges raised concerns about orders releasing detainees.

Acting U.S. Attorney Charles Neil Floyd wrote to Chief U.S. District Judge David Estudillo late last month that the office had been swamped by immigration detention challenges. A former immigration judge, Floyd said the office intended to stop assigning attorneys except in cases it considered a priority.

The challenges are filed as habeas corpus petitions in federal court and typically prompt numerous hearings, motions and responses. Judges concerned about Floyd’s proposal worried there might be no federal government attorneys present to ensure orders releasing detainees were communicated to the Department of Homeland Security and carried out.

On Tuesday, Floyd issued a statement saying the office would once again assign staff to appear in court on behalf of the federal government in all immigration habeas cases.

“After collaborative meetings with the judges of the Western District of Washington, we have agreed on new procedures which will result in a more streamlined process for immigration Habeas petitions,” he said.

Floyd did not detail what the new procedures require.

In one immigration habeas case Wednesday, Floyd’s office filed what it called an “abbreviated return.” The filing acknowledged the detainee’s petition and said the federal government did not intend to contest it. Instead, the office asked the court to rule on the detainee’s petition and any related filings.

U.S. District Court Clerk Joshua Lewis said the change would restore government attorneys to the proceedings and facilitate communication between the court and the parties.

“The Court is pleased that the U.S. Attorney’s Office will be appearing in every immigration habeas case and facilitating communications with the parties it represents,” Lewis said in an emailed statement.

The policy reversal comes as federal courts confront a large volume of immigration detention cases. Judges across the country have frequently ruled against the Trump administration in cases filed by tens of thousands of detainees who argue they are being unlawfully held by U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement. U.S. border czar Tom Homan recently told Fox News that courts were weighed down by about 85,000 immigration habeas cases.

In western Washington, judges have ruled hundreds of times against the administration in such cases, according to Matt Adams, legal director of the Northwest Immigrant Rights Project. Those rulings found it unlawful for ICE to re-arrest immigrants who had been detained and then released pending deportation or other proceedings.

Adams described the consequences for detainees and their families.

“These are people who are in the middle of immigration proceedings, who showed up for their appointment and then were arrested at their appointment,” Adams said Wednesday. “It’s just caused a tremendous amount of hardship — families are being torn apart, people are losing jobs, people don’t have money to pay the rent. The bottom line is the government is digging in its heels and continuing to unlawfully arrest our clients, and the courts are doing what they can to provide relief.”

Adams said the procedural change could affect release timing. Clients whose cases are not contested by the U.S. Attorney’s Office could be released more quickly than they otherwise would be, he said. It typically takes a month or more for detainees to be released after filing habeas petitions.

A separate legal question remains before the Supreme Court: whether detainees who have been living in the United States are entitled to a hearing challenging their detention in immigration court while they contest deportation.

Most appeals courts to have considered the issue have found that it violates federal law to detain immigrants, including those living in the United States illegally, without a bond hearing. Those decisions conflict with rulings by the 5th and 8th circuits, which held that immigrants accused of illegally entering and remaining in the United States must be detained while their deportation cases are pending.
