## The next chief inherits tariffs, fierce competition and an uneven EV shift

General Motors' board has hired a search firm to prospect for outside candidates to succeed Mary Barra, GM's longtime leader, according to The Wall Street Journal. Three company insiders — the chief product officer, the chief financial officer and President Reuss — are among the potential contenders named in the assessment.

The successor would take over an automaker facing tariff headwinds, fierce global competition and an uneven shift to electric and self-driving vehicles, the Journal said. Those conditions frame the case for each internal candidate: one is assessed on the technology agenda, one on financial management, and one on command of GM's operations.

GM's chief product officer has held the title for 1.5 years. He co-founded the autonomous trucking firm Aurora Innovation Inc. and led autonomous-driving efforts at Tesla, and The Wall Street Journal described him as recruited from Silicon Valley.

The report said he carries responsibility for delivering self-driving cars, AI-powered software and other big bets on the future, and that he has set ambitious targets for autonomous technology and expanded GM's digital offerings. The same assessment flagged his limitations: he is young and relatively untested when it comes to running a big industrial company.

GM's chief financial officer has been in the job for six years and is a former CFO of Delta Air Lines. The Journal credited him with putting GM on its strongest financial footing in decades and with returning billions to shareholders while helping the automaker navigate supply-chain chaos, trade wars and a rocky transition to electric vehicles.

He is well regarded on Wall Street, according to the report. The Journal also relayed the principal objection to his candidacy: skeptics say even a good finance chief lacks the ability to run an industrial giant like GM.

GM President Reuss has spent 43 years at the company and serves as Barra's No. 2. He has headed GM's product development, its North American operations and global engineering, among other jobs. His father was GM's president in the 1990s.

An engineer by trade, Reuss oversees GM's manufacturing and supply-chain operations, marketing and product development. The Journal described him as a seasoned operator with deep command of GM's sprawling operations. The report also noted that, like Barra, Reuss is nearing retirement age.

The three profiles differ in what they bring to the role: autonomous-driving and software experience for the product chief, a record on GM's finances and shareholder returns for the finance chief, and decades of GM operations for Reuss. Each profile in the assessment carries a stated objection: relative inexperience at industrial scale, doubts about a finance chief running an industrial giant, and proximity to retirement for Reuss.

The board has not confined the process to GM's own ranks: hiring the search firm opens the field to outside contenders, according to the Journal. For any successor, the conditions described in the assessment are the same — tariff headwinds, fierce global competition and an uneven transition to electric and self-driving vehicles.

Sharon Terlep, who wrote the assessment, is an enterprise reporter covering the global automotive industry from The Wall Street Journal's New York offices. She joined the Journal in 2008 in Detroit, where she covered the bankruptcy and restructuring of General Motors. In New York, she wrote about Boeing and the aerospace industry, consumer products giants such as P&G and the retail pharmacy industry. Before that she covered deals on Wall Street and did sports investigations for the Journal, and she started her career at the Lansing State Journal in Michigan and also worked at the Detroit News.
