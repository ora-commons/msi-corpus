## Gasoline fell from $4.41 to $3.61 while Fed officials remained concerned

U.S. consumer prices rose at a 3.8% annualized rate in October, according to data released Wednesday by the Bureau of Economic Analysis, marking the highest reading since May. The Fed's preferred Personal Consumption Expenditure index increased 0.3% from September to October ahead of the Federal Reserve's final interest-rate-setting meeting of the year.

The monthly gain came amid soaring energy prices. The Bureau of Labor Statistics separately reported that prices climbed 0.4% from September to October, above what economists had anticipated.

By mid-November, gasoline prices had cooled. AAA data showed gasoline falling from $4.41 a gallon in early October to $3.61 by mid-November, following a surge in energy costs during October.

The Federal Reserve has set a 2% inflation target, and inflation had not been at that level since February 2021. Officials held rates steady at their late-October meeting, but the minutes showed that some were concerned about inflation's stickiness.

The minutes recorded concern among some officials about the possibility of waiting too long. “A few of them warned that further delay risked allowing high inflation to become entrenched or necessitating a larger and more disruptive tightening later,” the minutes said.

Officials were widely expected to raise interest rates for the second time this year at the Federal Reserve's early-December meeting, the last rate-setting session of the year. A higher rate would make borrowing more expensive, slow price increases and bring up the costs of loans such as mortgages and student debt.

A Conference Board survey released Tuesday showed that consumer confidence had ticked up in November to its highest level since January 2025. The change was likely driven by the shake-up from the midterm elections.

U.S. stocks fluctuated amid uncertainty over datacenter regulations and what The Guardian described as continuing scandals coming out of Nvidia. Investor concern also centered on what The Guardian described as “the seemingly limitless amount of money” being spent on building AI and the infrastructure to support it.
