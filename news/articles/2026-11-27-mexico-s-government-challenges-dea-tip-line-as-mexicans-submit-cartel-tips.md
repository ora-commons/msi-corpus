## Supporters say the WhatsApp channel can expose criminal activity and official corruption

MEXICO CITY — Mexican President Claudia Sheinbaum filed a formal complaint with the U.S. Embassy on Friday over the U.S. Drug Enforcement Administration's anonymous WhatsApp tip line, accusing the effort of interfering in Mexico's internal affairs. DEA chief Terry Cole had called for anonymous reports on X the day before Sinaloa Journalists Association president Juan Manuel Partida Valdez wrote to the line Thursday. Partida Valdez was among the first to write to the line, saying he received a quick response.

The announcement opened another dispute between the U.S. and Mexican governments over which country has the authority to act against cartels in Mexico. Sheinbaum's government characterized the channel as foreign interference, while a Sinaloa journalist used it to report alleged political ties to criminal groups and an opposition senator urged Mexico not to obstruct the effort.

At her morning press briefing Friday, Sheinbaum said, “We have made it very clear in our relationship with the United States that the relationship is one of coordination, not subordination.” Her complaint formally escalated the government's opposition to the tip line.

Sheinbaum once again urged Washington to address drug use and combat drug traffickers within U.S. territory. She affirmed that Mexico would continue confronting cartels and preventing drugs from reaching the United States while maintaining that drug enforcement was a matter of Mexican sovereignty.

The dispute follows a period of significant operational cooperation between the two governments. As U.S. President Donald Trump has pressured Sheinbaum to crack down on criminal groups, Sheinbaum has underscored Mexican sovereignty at the same time her government has cracked down on cartels more forcefully than her predecessors. Homicide rates have fallen sharply, and the government has captured a number of top cartel bosses, often with U.S. intelligence.

The latest operation came over the weekend, when Mexican forces captured an important ally of a faction of the Sinaloa Cartel. The government requested that a U.S. surveillance drone fly over Mexican territory to help pinpoint the capo's location.

That cooperation has been marred by disputes over U.S. activity in Mexico and allegations that American officials have operated without Mexican knowledge. In April, two CIA agents died following a drug lab bust with local authorities in northern Chihuahua. Sheinbaum said her government had not been informed that the two U.S. intelligence agents were carrying out an operation in Mexican territory, paving the way for a potential treason trial.

Weeks later, Sinaloa Gov. Rubén Rocha Moya was forced to step down after a U.S. indictment on drug-trafficking charges, dealing the Sheinbaum government another political blow. Rocha was a powerful figure in Sheinbaum's party.

Partida Valdez said the Rocha case was a key reason he contacted the DEA line. He sent information about alleged ties between Sinaloa politicians and criminal groups because authorities have failed to put an end to violence and corruption that has bled his northern Mexican region dry in recent years.

“I talk to so many people — in the streets, on social media, on WhatsApp and in chats — and you realize what people think: We're all fed up,” the 67-year-old told The Associated Press on Friday. “We're fed up with narco-governments.”

Partida Valdez called the case against Rocha “a very clear example of what we're suffering because of these governments that do absolutely nothing but protect criminals.” He also said, “This whole song and dance about sovereignty is just collusion with the politicians tied to drug trafficking who are being protected in our country.”

The reaction to the tip line was not uniform. Some observers warned of dangers in the DEA strategy, while other academics and politicians celebrated the line as a potential tool or said they trusted the intelligence agency more than their own government.

Lilly Téllez, a senator for Mexico's National Action Party, welcomed the channel. The Associated Press described the party as a weakened opposition party that has also been plagued by corruption scandals. Téllez, a politician from the border state of Sonora, wrote on X on Friday that the tip line was “a good way to cooperate against the common enemy of both countries” and would strengthen efforts to combat cartels.

“On behalf of millions of Mexicans oppressed by the cartels, I ask President (Sheinbaum) not to obstruct the opportunity to gather more information to locate criminals and identify their activities,” she wrote.

Sheinbaum's complaint and Téllez's appeal left the competing positions in direct opposition on Friday: the Mexican president characterized the DEA channel as interference in domestic affairs, while the senator urged her to allow more information to be gathered about criminal activity.
