## Mary Peltola wins Alaska's U.S. Senate race on first-choice majority

**Subtype:** fact

- Mary Peltola has been called the winner of Alaska's U.S. Senate race with 50.1% of first-choice votes in preliminary results counted through Tuesday evening.
- The Associated Press called the race after Alaska Division of Elections officials confirmed Peltola had more than half the vote.
- Peltola will replace Republican Sen. Dan Sullivan and become the Senate's 56th Democrat when the chamber convenes in January.

## Certification remains pending while appeal over Justice Department lawsuit proceeds

Mary Peltola has been called the winner of Alaska's U.S. Senate race, with preliminary results showing 50.1% of first-choice votes through Tuesday evening. The Associated Press called the race shortly after Alaska Division of Elections officials confirmed that Peltola had more than half the vote. She will replace Republican Sen. Dan Sullivan in one of Alaska's two U.S. Senate seats and, when the Senate convenes in January, become its 56th Democrat.

The result places Peltola in a chamber that will convene without the Republican governing majority that preceded it, following a series of election results. Her return also brings back the first Alaska Native woman to serve in Congress: Peltola was elected to the U.S. House in 2022 and will be among the first Indigenous people to serve in the U.S. Senate.

Peltola held a decisive advantage in the count released Tuesday. With 209,003 ballots counted through Tuesday evening, she had 50.1% of first-choice votes, enough to avoid a ranked-choice runoff. Republican Sen. Dan Sullivan had 45.8%, Republican challenger Gerald Heikes had 2.1%, and independent challenger Dan J. Sullivan had 1.7%.

Alaska's ballot system allows a candidate with more than half of the first-choice votes to win outright. All candidates for an office compete together in an August primary, and the top four vote-getters advance to the November general election; when four or fewer candidates run, all of them advance. Voters then rank the four candidates in order of preference. If no candidate reaches 50% on the first ballot, the candidate with the fewest first-choice votes is eliminated and those ballots are redistributed to the voters' second choices. Eliminations and redistributions continue until two candidates remain, and the candidate with the most votes wins.

The count remains provisional. Final preliminary results are expected Wednesday, and the Alaska Division of Elections plans to publish a certified result on Nov. 25. In a statement issued Monday morning, Republican Sen. Dan Sullivan's campaign spokesman Nate Adams said that “Alaskans deserve a complete and accurate count, and we will let that process run its course until every legal vote is counted and certified. The Division of Elections has work left to do, and we respect the Alaskans doing it. We will not be commenting on individual ballot updates or outside projections in the meantime.”

Peltola wrote on Facebook on Tuesday evening: “As a lifelong Alaskan who has dedicated my career to serving our state, there is no greater honor than having Alaskans’ support in our fight to take on corporate greed, lower costs, and put Alaska first.” She added: “Thank you, Alaska. Let’s get to work.”

Peltola's path to the Senate was set before the general election. National Democrats tried to recruit her as far back as 2025, with Senate Minority Leader Chuck Schumer playing a key role. After she announced her run in January, she campaigned under the slogan “Fish, Family, Freedom,” promoting an anticorruption platform, limits on commercial trawl fisheries, and a politically moderate stance that includes support for development and oil and gas drilling on federal land in Alaska.

One of 16 candidates in the August primary, Peltola immediately became Sullivan's leading challenger. David Leslie, a progressive Democrat who finished fourth in the primary, withdrew after the final result. Leslie's withdrawal promoted Heikes into the general election and concentrated the Democratic vote around Peltola.

Republican and Democratic campaigners collectively spent more than $100 million to promote Peltola and Sullivan, making the contest the most expensive election campaign in state history. Final tallies will not be available until early next year.

Dan J. Sullivan of Petersburg immediately drew public attention because he shares the first and last name of the incumbent senator. The Alaska Division of Elections ruled Dan J. Sullivan ineligible to run, citing irregularities in his candidacy that indicated he was running only to confuse voters. He appealed, and the Alaska Supreme Court ruled in his favor. Dan J. Sullivan went on to finish third in the primary.

Shortly before the primary, Peltola fired her campaign manager, Elisa Rios, and made other major staffing moves. Rios subsequently told The New York Times that Peltola was physically and verbally abusive toward staff and was a key source in a Times article that said Democratic campaigners worked to recruit independent Dan J. Sullivan as a spoiler candidate to help Peltola defeat Sen. Sullivan.

Peltola has denied knowing about the recruitment. Republican officials, including President Donald Trump, have called for legal action against Peltola and her campaign.

The FBI and the Alaska Department of Public Safety are investigating whether Democratic campaigners committed a legal violation. No charges have been filed.

The U.S. Department of Justice filed a civil lawsuit against the Alaska Division of Elections seeking to prevent it from certifying Peltola as the winner. A federal judge in Alaska dismissed the case, and the judge's dismissal is now on appeal to the 9th U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals. Part of the federal case rested on the idea that confused voters might have chosen Dan J. Sullivan instead of Sen. Sullivan; Peltola now has more votes than both Sullivans combined.

Peltola's victory is called but not certified, and the legal challenges to certification remain unresolved. The Alaska Division of Elections expects to publish final preliminary results Wednesday and a certified result on Nov. 25.
