## FDA leaves sample size, selection and enforcement details unspecified

The Food and Drug Administration announced last week that it will test prescription drugs more often and identify poorly performing products for further study. Under the expanded program, the agency said it will buy medications from retail pharmacies and wholesale distributors.

The agency will test the medications for impurities and other quality lapses. Drug-safety experts described the expansion as a critical step toward better protecting America’s drug supply.

ProPublica reported that generic drugs account for about 90% of prescriptions in the United States. Many are manufactured thousands of miles away in countries that include India and China. Until the announcement, the FDA had only periodically tested a sampling of those drugs to determine whether they were safe and worked as effectively as the brand-name medications they are meant to replicate.

Drug-safety experts said the expansion addresses long-standing concerns that some suppliers sacrifice quality to keep costs low and that the FDA has for years looked the other way.

A ProPublica investigation last year found that the FDA routinely relied on drug companies to do their own testing even as doctors, researchers and the Department of Defense warned that essential medications were contaminated or ineffective. On its website, the FDA said it has relied on a “more targeted, risk-based” approach since 2018, choosing samples of drugs with safety, effectiveness or quality concerns. The agency publishes results and says a majority of the tested drugs meet its standards.

ProPublica’s investigation also found that the FDA had for years failed to regularly test both the generics that worried outside experts and drugs coming from factories that accumulated so many serious quality and safety violations they were ultimately banned from the United States market. ProPublica later used FDA data to create Rx Inspector, described as a first-of-its-kind tool for consumers and doctors to look up inspection records for factories where more than 40,000 generic drugs are made.

Researchers have repeatedly urged the FDA to test more often. In fiscal year 2025, the FDA reported testing 46 different drugs, and for 29 of those drugs, the agency tested only one manufacturer. All but one passed every test.

Dr. Kevin Schulman, a professor of medicine and health policy at Stanford University, called the FDA’s new testing regime “a huge validation of the research that we’ve been doing raising concerns about the quality of generic drugs.” He added, “This is a great effort on their part.” In February, senators also introduced a bill targeting transparency in the drug supply chain process, in part because of similar public concerns.

The FDA spokesperson did not answer questions about how the agency would select drugs for testing or how it would hold manufacturers accountable for inferior products, beyond posting the results on its website. The spokesperson said the initiative will “help the FDA identify potential drug quality issues faster and will provide more information the agency can use to decide if further action is needed.”

“All drugs, regardless of where they are manufactured, must meet the same FDA standards for safety, effectiveness and quality,” the FDA spokesperson told ProPublica.

Experts said they ultimately hope the FDA program will work alongside independent testers to shift the generic drug market to competition on quality rather than price. Retired Col. Vic Suarez, a former medical supply-chain commander, said the agency can focus on weeding out the worst actors, while independent laboratories can test manufacturers against one another to find the most effective products. A similar system is already used at the Department of Defense.

Suarez said, “It’s an important thing because they will probably catch extreme violators of regulatory standards, so that’s a good thing,” and added, “But you also need to have a complementary system where you’re actually raising the bar.”

ProPublica previously engaged the independent lab Valisure to test several generic versions of three of the most prescribed drugs in the United States. In two cases, the testing identified irregularities that experts said could compromise effectiveness. David Light, Valisure’s co-founder, said, “I think, high level, the idea that we should be doing more testing is fantastic,” adding, “I think there’s so much growing energy behind that.”
