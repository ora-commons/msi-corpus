## Army reports 68 barrels destroyed without secondary explosions

The U.S. Environmental Protection Agency's air monitors detected no lead around the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant in Middletown on November 25, after two controlled blasts destroyed a degraded stockpile of lead azide. A cellphone alert sent late Wednesday afternoon told residents that normal activities could resume, and the Army said FEMA issued an all clear around the plant.

The operation disposed of what the Army described as the nation's entire stockpile of lead azide — about 5 tons (4,500 kg) of a chemical used to make explosives. The Army cited the degrading material and colder weather as reasons for detonating it immediately, saying the stockpile might explode uncontrollably at any time as temperatures fell. What the blast left behind is not yet known: agencies and independent researchers plan extensive lead testing on water sources, farms, homes and playgrounds in the weeks ahead, and experts said early air readings cannot yet determine whether people face health risks.

An Associated Press reporter filming from less than a mile away and upwind from the site recorded two loud booms around 9 a.m. local time. Two white plumes rolled high into the air and dissipated quickly, and the Army attributed the smoke to foam sprayed on the bunkers to tamp down dust. Maj. Gen. Eric Shirley, who led the operation, said the two controlled blasts destroyed all 68 barrels and set off no secondary explosions.

Earthen barriers were erected before the operation, along with blast barriers of concrete and sand that stood two stories high. Ballistic gels, foams and water were added to absorb dust, and officials said those measures appeared to have absorbed much of the blast.

The Army scheduled the detonation for Wednesday because winds were blowing to the south-west and away from populated areas, including Burlington, a city of just under 25,000 people along the Mississippi River. Government officials had urged residents closest to the site and in range of the plume to evacuate and others to stay indoors. Schools not already closed for the upcoming Thanksgiving holiday canceled classes, many businesses closed, and some residents sealed windows and doors as a precaution.

Government agencies and independent researchers said they would test water sources, farms, homes and playgrounds in the weeks ahead. Colin Wellenkamp, executive director of the Mississippi River Cities and Towns Initiative, said water treatment facilities along the Mississippi and Missouri rivers would test periodically to check for detectable spikes.

Andrew Whelton, an environmental engineering professor at Purdue University who has helped communities recover from chemical disasters, called the EPA's air quality data promising. He said samples must be tested in a laboratory to determine whether people face health risks. “This should take days to weeks,” Whelton said. “But what we may see is different guidance issued to different communities based on how far they are from the detonation area and whether or not they were upwind or downwind.”

Shirley said the risk was “reduced as close to zero as it’s absolutely humanly possible.” Acting Army secretary Adam Telle and the Republican governors of Iowa and Missouri said the explosion appeared successful and well contained. Donald Trump posted on social media that the Army did a “great job.”

The military had announced the detonation and then delayed it over the preceding two weeks. A court ruling this week put the operation, originally planned for the previous Friday, back on track, and the Army changed the date amid public backlash and to allow more time for safety measures and favorable wind. Environmental groups that sued to halt the detonation said Wednesday that legal action and public pressure had forced the Army to take more protective measures. Had the blasts happened the previous week as planned, many safeguards, clearer evacuation plans and expansive monitoring would not have been in place, the groups said. “Iowa and neighboring states deserve a full accounting of why the situation was allowed to get to the point of crisis,” said Sarah Green, executive director of the Iowa Environmental Council.

The stockpile was brought to Iowa from bases around the country in 2009, according to Robert Cekada, director of the Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, Firearms and Explosives. The lead azide was stored in case the military needed it again. During an inspection in March, plant staff found the chemicals had partially crystallized, making them sensitive to friction and cold temperatures and creating the risk of spontaneous detonation.

Some residents said the timing could have been better, given that the detonation came the day before Thanksgiving, but they acknowledged it needed to be dealt with then. Burlington resident Tim Brandt, who parked on a roadside to watch, said the blast reminded him of noisemakers that crack when thrown on the ground. “It was nothing,” he told the AP. “It seemed like government put fear in a lot of people over nothing.”

Doug Seyb, a livestock farmer from Donnellson, lives far enough away that he did not hear or feel the explosions, but said he remained concerned about the accuracy of EPA air monitors. “All of these cows are pregnant,” he said, “and we just cross our fingers that we don’t have deformed calves and stillborns.”

Researchers said the final results are not in yet, and some experts cautioned that it was too early to know whether communities were in the clear. Whelton said the laboratory work should take days to weeks.

Lucy Giehl, who lives in nearby West Burlington, said: “I don’t think lead danger goes away any time soon.” She added: “It seems someone might have thought about all of this a little sooner before it came to this.”
