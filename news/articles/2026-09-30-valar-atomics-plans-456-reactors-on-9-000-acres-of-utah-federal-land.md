## Valar Atomics plans 456 reactors on 9,000 acres of Utah federal land

**Subtype:** fact

- Valar Atomics has proposed placing 456 small nuclear reactors and data centers on over 9,000 acres of Bureau of Land Management land near Price, Utah, under a plan the company calls "Project Beehive."
- Valar's proposal says the facility would generate approximately 9.6 gigawatts of electricity for the data centers plus additional thermal heat for industrial applications — more than double Utah's current average grid output of about 4 gigawatts.
- Project documents reviewed by NPR place nonnuclear construction as early as the end of 2026, the first reactors online in 2028, and full site completion by 2032.
- The Bureau of Land Management confirmed receipt of Valar's application and said the project will undergo National Environmental Policy Act review; Utah environmental groups called the proposed use of public lands for nuclear power and waste storage unprecedented.

## Project would generate nearly 10 gigawatts, more than double Utah's average output

Valar Atomics, a startup whose founder has vowed to "make nuclear great again," is proposing to build 456 small nuclear reactors and data centers on more than 9,000 acres of Bureau of Land Management land near Price, Utah, according to documents reviewed by NPR. The proposal, which the company calls "Project Beehive," also includes a facility to produce nuclear fuel and others for storing nuclear waste on the same site.

In aggregate, the reactors would produce around 9.6 gigawatts of power for the data centers and additional thermal heat for other industrial applications, according to a proposal from the company to federal regulators. The entire state of Utah currently produces only around 4 gigawatts of electricity on average, though its peak summer capacity can reach about 10 gigawatts.

The project's timeline calls for nonnuclear construction to begin as soon as the end of this year, with the first reactors coming online sometime in 2028, according to company documents reviewed by NPR. The full site could be completed by 2032.

Valar has also sought the use of around 650 acres of state land as part of the same project, according to state records. The Salt Lake Tribune first reported on the project's use of state land last week, but not the much larger proposed use of federal lands.

The project has raised alarm among local environmental groups already facing a wave of data center proposals in the state. "It's concerning to see that the Trump administration, with the support of the state of Utah, is pushing this forward on significant acreage of public lands," said Neal Clark, wildlands director at the Southern Utah Wilderness Alliance, a Utah-based nonprofit that seeks to protect public lands.

"The coalition of people who are concerned about these things completely transcends political lines," said Lexi Tuddenham, the executive director of the Healthy Environment Alliance of Utah, which has tracked nuclear-related industries in the state. Tuddenham said her primary current goal is awareness. "The biggest thing we can do right now is to let people know about it," she said.

In a written statement, the Bureau of Land Management's Utah office confirmed it had received an application from the company. "We are currently reviewing the application for completeness," the office told NPR. As the application moves through the required National Environmental Policy Act process, the office added, "we will make sure the public has meaningful opportunities to provide input."

Valar did not immediately respond to NPR's request for comment on Project Beehive. In a July interview with NPR about a smaller test reactor the company was operating in a neighboring county, Valar CEO Isaiah Taylor said the company was committed to safety and community engagement. "We're doing it responsibly and accountably to this community," he said at the time.

Project Beehive reveals the scale of the ambitions held by a new generation of companies planning a nuclear-power comeback. The firms are backed by the Trump administration, which has enacted a series of regulatory reforms over the past year and a half to make "small modular reactors" easier to test and build.

Valar has been closely involved with those reforms and a beneficiary of them. Taylor was in the room when President Trump signed a series of executive orders last year designed to accelerate the development of nuclear power, and his company was accepted into the Department of Energy's Reactor Pilot Program in August 2025. In February, the U.S. military flew Valar's test reactor from California to Utah aboard a C-17 transport aircraft.

The reactor, known as Ward 250, received rapid approval this year after the Department of Energy conducted what NPR described as a quiet overhaul of its internal safety and security regulations for nuclear reactors. It achieved criticality by reaching a self-sustaining nuclear reaction in June, in a tentlike structure in the Utah desert about 30 miles from the site of Project Beehive.

At a press event on July 1, the company marked another milestone when it used Ward 250 to produce electricity. Taylor powered up the test reactor and used a simple thermoelectric system to run a single Nvidia microchip. "Ladies and gentlemen, Valar Atomics just became the first startup in history to make nuclear electricity right here," he told the crowd.

The scale of Project Beehive vastly outstrips that demonstration. Since the turn of the century, the U.S. has switched on just three new nuclear power plants, producing a total of around 3.4 gigawatts of electricity, according to NPR. Those reactors are far larger than the small modular reactors Valar plans to use, and each cost many billions of dollars to build.

Valar and other companies are hoping to upend the nuclear business model by mass-producing small reactors and placing them in series, like a set of nuclear batteries. Valar's 25-megawatt electric units could be installed one at a time, allowing Project Beehive to be built in phases and reducing both cost and construction time.

Tuddenham said she considers the use of federal lands for such a project to be without precedent. "In this case, I think it's bigger than the local community," she said. "If we have facilities like this on public land for waste storage, for massive nuclear development, for data centers, that's going to have tremendous implications for how public lands are used."
