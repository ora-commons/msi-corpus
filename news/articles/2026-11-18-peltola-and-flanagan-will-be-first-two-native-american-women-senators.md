## A nine-seat midterm gain gives Democrats 56 seats next year

Democrats gained nine Senate seats in the 2026 midterms and will control 56 seats beginning next year. The Guardian described the outcome as a sweeping victory that would put the party decisively back in the majority and said Democrats were expected to embark on an aggressive campaign of lawmaking and investigations next year. The midterms also saw Donald Trump’s party lose ground across the country.

Alaska was the last Senate race remaining to be called. Mary Peltola defeated Republican incumbent Dan Sullivan in a result called two weeks after the midterm elections, making her the first Democrat the state has elected to the U.S. Senate since 2008. Her victory came just two years after Alaska voters unseated her while supporting Trump by a double-digit margin.

Peltola, who is Yup’ik, will join Minnesota senator-elect Peggy Flanagan, a member of the White Earth band of Ojibwe, as the first two Native American women to serve in the U.S. Senate.

Late in her campaign, Peltola posted on Instagram a photograph of herself from 1995, wearing the winner’s sash from the Miss National Congress of American Indians pageant. She wrote, “Then and now, Alaska is at the heart of who I am,” in the post.

Peltola was a former state legislator and U.S. House lawmaker who grew up in small communities along the Kuskokwim River in Alaska’s interior. She rose to prominence in 2022, winning a special election to replace longtime Republican at-large congressman Don Young following his sudden death and becoming the first Alaska Native elected to Congress. Peltola won a full term later in 2022 but lost her re-election to Republican Nick Begich in 2024, then entered the race against Sullivan.

Sullivan was a two-term senator first elected in 2014 after defeating a Democrat. Though Alaska is a Republican stronghold in presidential years, The Guardian described Peltola as a uniquely potent challenger to Sullivan. Alaska is nonetheless a right-leaning state heavily reliant on its fishing and oil and gas industries.

The race was shadowed by significant controversy after The New York Times reported that Peltola’s staffers were involved in a scheme to recruit another Republican challenger, also named Dan Sullivan, to appear on the ballot. Peltola’s campaign denied any involvement in encouraging the second Dan Sullivan’s run. State election officials tried to remove the second Dan Sullivan, but Alaska’s supreme court ruled in June that he could appear on the ballot.

Donald Trump called for Peltola’s prosecution over what The Guardian described as an apparent ruse. Alaska’s top federal prosecutor announced that criminal and civil inquiries were under way, while Alaska’s governor separately announced plans to open a criminal investigation into the alleged scheme.

The Guardian reported that Peltola’s fortunes appeared to rise as Alaskans grew frustrated with price increases brought on by Donald Trump’s tariffs and his decision to attack Iran earlier that year. Alaska is the largest state in the union and is dotted with cities and villages disconnected from the road system. The Guardian said the state was particularly hard-hit by the oil-price increases the war caused.

The Guardian reported that gas prices in some interior Alaska communities jumped into the double digits per gallon. The rise in diesel prices affected everything from groceries to alcohol because many communities rely on shipments by sea.

On the campaign trail, Peltola used the slogan “fish, family, freedom” to highlight how she would stand up for Alaska’s unique interests in Washington DC. She cast herself as an advocate for expanding oil production in the state and addressed bycatch, the accidental fishing of unwanted species, which some Alaskans believe is to blame for declining salmon runs.

Sullivan stayed with Trump even as the president’s popularity declined nationwide and, according to some surveys, especially in Alaska. He did not join other vulnerable Republicans in urging an end to the war with Iran.
