## 99.7% of the fake accounts LinkedIn removes are shut down before a report

Rebecca Keithley, who retired in July from her role as an assistant section chief in the FBI's Financial Crimes Section, says she was impersonated twice online this year, on Instagram and Facebook. Though the Facebook account used her name and photos, Keithley said Meta repeatedly determined the account did not violate its community standards; it was removed only after FBI leadership contacted the company. Around a week later, the same fraudulent account was restored. Meta again initially declined to remove it, she said, and it took another intervention to get it taken down. "I'm at a loss for what to do," Keithley said. "I have no confidence in Meta's security's ability to do anything." Meta told The Associated Press that its systems initially detected and disabled the impostor account but it was reinstated in error.

Meta, which owns Facebook, Instagram and WhatsApp, is the largest social media company in the world. In a statement to the AP, Meta said that in the first half of 2026 it removed over 179 million accounts, pages and groups for misrepresenting identity — the vast majority before anyone reported them — and that new AI tests cut reports of the most impersonated celebrities by over 80%. "We fight impersonation on our platforms because it's not good for us or the people and businesses that rely on our services," a Meta spokesperson said. LinkedIn — another platform used by Bigham's impersonators — told the AP that 99.7% of the fake accounts it removes are shut down before a report is made.

As the losses deepened, of money and of hope, Bigham grew desperate to shut it all down. His own requests to internet platforms have been largely rejected. He reported fake profiles to Facebook, he said, but the platform repeatedly told him they didn't violate community standards and removed only a handful. Meta told the AP it removed four accounts Bigham recently reported and is investigating his case. He tried watermarking his photos; the scammers stripped them off. He asked victims for the scammers' phone numbers and tried tracking them down. After Bigham posed as a potential victim, one scammer, "Kyle," promised to pay off his debt if Bigham sent him credit card details; when Bigham baited Kyle by asking, "What do you look like?", Kyle responded with a series of photos of Bigham. Enraged, Bigham filed a report with the FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center and called the local FBI office, but apart from advising him to share the agency's contact details with victims, he says, an FBI staffer told him there was little they could do. The FBI told the AP it couldn't comment on individual complaints.

Bigham has some compassion for the scammers themselves; he recognizes that many are trafficking victims forced to work in prisonlike compounds. But he has no patience for internet platforms like Meta, which he says could prevent fraudulent profiles by requiring people to provide identification. Meta told the AP it aims to limit the amount of sensitive information it collects. "I hold the most responsibility for the platforms," Bigham said. "They could stop this today."

The scale of what he was up against became visible on a sidewalk in Palm Springs, California, where Bigham was waiting with his husband to cross the street when a screaming woman bore down on him, shouting, "I TOLD her you weren't real!" It was then that Bigham knew he was the ghost, the cause of the chaos — and he knew why. The woman's story was grimly familiar: Her friend had fallen in love with a stranger online who was using Bigham's photos as his own, and the woman had been trying to persuade her friend that the romance was not real. Bigham assured the woman that he didn't know her friend, that he was a happily married gay man, and that he, too, was a victim of romance scammers — not as their financial target, but as their unwilling bait.

For more than eight years, romance scammers have stolen Bigham's photos to create hundreds of fake online profiles used to trick lovesick men and women out of their money. When many victims realize they have been duped, they dig, eventually finding Bigham. The losses he has tallied just from victims who have contacted him have reached roughly $1.4 million. Some victims, wrongly believing Bigham is the scammer, threaten to hurt him, his family and themselves, injecting a thread of dread into his daily life. One victim furiously cursed at Bigham, said he knew where Bigham lived and vowed to destroy his son's career. At its peak, he says, up to 15 victims a day contacted him, from the United States, Spain, Venezuela and Hong Kong; they have been cancer survivors, a retired cop, a CEO. The scammers have claimed he is a widowed petroleum geologist in Houston, a Spanish secret agent named John and, frequently and conveniently, an oil rig worker stuck at sea while the landlocked lovelorn pine for him — or rather, for the person they think he is. Nor is Bigham's experience unique, said Bryan Denny, a retired U.S. Army colonel whose pictures romance scammers have used for the past decade and whose life has been upended by victims who harassed him and his family. "I've got to do something to make it stop for the kids because now it's starting to really affect them," he says. "They don't like it and I don't blame them." The only thing that will stop it, Bigham says, is if the internet platforms change their policies — but both he and Johnny admit that's unlikely. "I don't know what else to do because I'm a dude in Mississippi with no power," Bigham says. He lets out a rueful laugh. "I think we're faced with this for a very, very long time," he says. "Probably forever," Johnny replies. "Probably forever," Bigham agrees. Then he picked his phone back up, his thumb returning to its relentless swiping, waiting with dread for the arrival of another victim's pain.
