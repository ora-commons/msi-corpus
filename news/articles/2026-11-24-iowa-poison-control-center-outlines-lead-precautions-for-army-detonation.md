## McCabe says Army or EPA sampling will guide lead testing

In an NPR interview, Dr. Dan McCabe, medical director of the Iowa Poison Control Center, said residents near Middletown, Iowa, should close windows and turn off heating and cooling systems, or switch those systems to recirculation, during the detonation and until the U.S. Army issues an all-clear. The guidance addresses a dust cloud expected to follow the explosion.

McCabe, whose hometown is Burlington, Iowa, right down the street, described Middletown as a very typical smaller Iowa town with hardworking, blue-collar residents who mostly want to do their jobs, take care of their families and have an overall good life.

McCabe said residents had expressed unease and some fear as the operation approached. Their main questions, he said, were what would happen next, what information was available and how to protect themselves. McCabe said his information came from public sources and did not go beyond the public domain.

McCabe said federal colleagues had done all the mitigation efforts they possibly could, and that he was hopeful much less lead would enter the environment than had been originally feared. His main concern was lead that remained in the environment over time. He said the likelihood of longer-term exposure was unclear at that point.

NPR host Juana Summers noted that McCabe had said a brief exposure was not likely to cause a medical emergency, while concern remained about the effects of long-term exposure.

“The clear answer is there is no safe level for lead in our body,” McCabe said. He also said people have no use for lead and that once lead enters the body, it remains there for quite a while, if not forever.

McCabe identified children as his biggest priority because they touch everything and put objects in their mouths. He said he and public-health colleagues get very concerned about even a little lead in the environment because a child needs parents or caregivers to be extra diligent in helping with that. For children, he said lead can limit or affect brain development in relatively vague ways and reduce their ability to reach the highest levels in life.

He also identified pregnant patients and malnourished patients as higher-risk groups of particular concern. For adults, McCabe said concerns tend to be more related to other health effects than to brain development.

Based on modeling provided by the Army or EPA, McCabe said the environmental impact could be relatively minimal. He said the Army or EPA told him they would do extensive sampling in the environment to help determine which areas are affected and where people might need lead testing. He said the sampling results could allow providers to know that patients they see were not at risk and to provide reassurance, but time would tell with surveillance and monitoring after the fact.

After the detonation and while monitoring data are pending, McCabe recommended washing hands and children's hands, wiping down outdoor playground equipment, leaving shoes outside and rinsing off a car that was outside during the detonation before bringing it into a garage. He said those simple measures should help minimize short-term risk over the coming weeks.
