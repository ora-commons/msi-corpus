## The recent nationwide ground stop was the first since 9/11

The busy 2026 holiday travel week effectively begins Friday, and some travelers are approaching it with more than the usual concerns. Crowded airports, long lines and jam-packed flights come with the recurring worry about delays, cancellations and extra expenses. A nationwide ground stop last week — the first since 9/11 — and a separate airline outage a few days later have added concern about possible disruption in the air-travel system.

Tracey Evers, an energy-industry executive in Raleigh, North Carolina, whose work requires frequent travel, is unsure whether to continue plans to fly to the Caribbean this weekend to see her mother for the upcoming week. She has things to take care of at home and said getting stuck away from it would be a hassle. Evers said she believed she was not the only traveler reacting that way.

She described the concern as fueled by the past 10 days of technology failures across industries and government, including airlines and other sectors. “For a lot of people, especially as they’re paying attention and because it’s in the news where they’re tying together all of the IT-based infrastructure challenges we’re having ... I think more and more people are now getting anxious,” Evers said. She said travelers were tying those separate problems together through news coverage. For people who must fly for work and to live their lives, that combination adds uncertainty beyond the familiar possibility of a delay.

For some travelers, cost may be as much of an obstacle as the potential for delays. Hopper estimates round-trip domestic tickets for Thanksgiving and Christmas travel are up 31% and 23%, respectively, from the same time a year earlier, meaning travelers are dealing with the highest holiday airfares in a decade. The Associated Press report linked some increases in fares and fees to higher jet fuel costs tied to the war in Iran. The Consumer Price Index showed airfares up 22% in September from a year earlier.

For broader context, [FRED's CPIAUCSL series](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/CPIAUCSL) put the all-items Consumer Price Index for All Urban Consumers about 3.7% above its year-earlier level on a year-over-year basis as of Nov. 20, 2026. The two readings cover different scopes: the 22% increase was for airfares, while CPIAUCSL tracks consumer prices overall.

The complaints are familiar. The Associated Press report placed them in a decades-long history of Americans complaining in different ways as air travel grew into a staple of American life, and complaints continued as the industry resurged after the COVID pandemic. The report likened the response to bad weather, which irritates many Americans while dissuading comparatively few. University of Nevada, Las Vegas professor Dan Bubb, who teaches commercial aviation history, said, “For the most part, air travel still remains strong,” adding that people “might be unhappy. They might grumble, but they are still going to do it.”

Bob van der Linden, acting chair of the aeronautics department at the National Air and Space Museum, said the country's aviation system could be seen as a victim of its own success. He described human beings willingly flying in a metal machine as an unfathomable miracle, then a romantic adventure, and now, for many, a high-altitude commute. “Air travel now is indispensable for a modern economy ... and frankly, it is taken for granted,” van der Linden said. More people flying produces more congestion and inconvenience, he said. “You’ve got all these airlines out there. People want to fly. They want to go to new places,” he said. “It’s a commodity. There’s nothing romantic about it anymore. But in a way, that’s a clear sign of its success — that you’re in a bus.”

Miami resident Michelle Jolene, 50, is accustomed to delays, cancellations and rerouting. She said she was not thrilled at the idea of adding infrastructure issues to the list of potential problems, though she was not rattled by it. “I literally haven’t had a normal flight in so long and I fly a lot. So I’m like, just, okay, here’s another thing,” she said.

Jolene expected some reluctance among “the people who fly maybe once a year for their holiday,” who might drive instead to “Disney or the beaches in North Carolina or something,” she said. For people who must fly for work and to live their lives, the choice is narrower, she said: “But those of us who have to fly to work and to live our lives, there’s just not much choice. What are you going to do?”

Jolene found a way to bring her daughter home for Thanksgiving from Washington, D.C., where the daughter attends college. She saw a promotion on American Airlines' online shopping portal earlier this year that awarded 10 miles for every dollar spent. She bought 10 boxes of 48 rolls, earning about 8,500 miles — enough to cover the entire flight to Miami. She said the upfront purchase made sense because she would have bought the product anyway and it would not go bad. She is keeping the rolls in her garage. “The funny part,” she said, “is we have to buy toilet paper anyway.”

Holiday travelers face two visible pressures: possible service disruption and the highest holiday airfares in a decade. Bubb said many people would continue flying despite complaints, while Jolene described a practical way to cover a family trip. Evers had not resolved whether to make the Caribbean journey, and the ground stop and airline outage left the immediate stability of holiday travel uncertain.

AP Airlines and Travel Writer Rio Yamat in Las Vegas contributed to this story.
