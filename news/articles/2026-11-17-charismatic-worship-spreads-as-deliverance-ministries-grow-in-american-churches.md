## Lorenzo Sewell called the Democratic platform demonic at a May rally

Deliverance ministries that combine emphatic worship, claims of healing and prayers to expel demons have gained visibility in American evangelicalism, while a 2023 Public Religion Research Institute study found that 56% of Christian respondents attended services that included charismatic practices. In a Columbia, Tennessee, parking lot, Freedom Collective Ministries leader James Thayer led a late-summer 2026 event where participants prayed over illness and what they described as demonic influence. The Guardian's reporting traces how practices once discouraged in many conservative evangelical settings now appear in churches, political rallies and short-form online religious media.

At the gathering, a young woman pressed her forehead against the warm pavement while two women in white ministry shirts prayed over her. Her whimpers escalated into moans and guttural cries: “No! No! No!” One woman poured water on the prone woman's feet in a gesture compared with Christ washing his disciples' feet during the Last Supper. About 35 people stood nearby, some talking and laughing while children chased one another and a band played contemplative music under a white tent. Thirteen people formed a line before Thayer, who said he would gently touch their fingers and ask the Holy Spirit to clothe them with power, fill them and possibly free them from whatever was troubling them.

Thayer, 36, leads Freedom Collective Ministries, which he describes as uniting 70 believers from 14 churches in evangelism, healing and deliverance. During a five-month “gospel crusade” in 2026, the ministry traveled through 16 Tennessee cities, holding events in parks and parking lots. Its stated aims included delivering people from physical ailment, mental anguish and “false doctrine,” while framing the work as a battle against demons to win souls for Christ.

The Guardian's reporter first encountered the ministry through a college friend who posted on Instagram about miraculous healings to her own body and to others, along with warnings about spiritual warfare and ominous forces. The reporter said the violent allusions she encountered on social media, in Christian churches, at political rallies and in the Trump White House seemed to signal a new chapter in white American evangelicalism, combining beliefs she had grown up with with charismatic practices that had been forbidden in the evangelical circles she knew.

At the Columbia event, Thayer held the first woman's hand and prophesied that she might be a painter and a dancer for the Lord, with the Lord pleased. After three more women — and those in line were mostly women — Thayer instructed those watching to shine their phone flashlights down at their own hands to see sparkles of gold dust, a manifestation of God's glory. Most complied; some saw something, others were puzzled.

Thayer later encountered a woman in a dress who had become increasingly emotional. He gripped her wrist, looked into her eyes and commanded, “Up, out!” and “All of it, go out of her belly!” He said a demon had been affecting the woman's health and organs but would plague her no longer. When she coughed, he told her it was fine to throw up because volunteers had a bag. He chanted, “Break, break, break, break, she belongs to Jesus, she belongs to Jesus, she belongs to Jesus.” The woman shuddered and coughed more.

Thayer then told the woman, “People have controlled you and manipulated you,” adding, “It’s witchcraft. But no longer.” He declared, “I break every spell on your life, every attack of the enemy, every bit of witchcraft,” and commanded the demon to “Go!” He stomped his foot and said, “You loose her and you go now!” before calling, “Release the daughter of God!” The woman began to cry, then slumped forward, weeping, as a volunteer handed her a tissue and began to talk to her quietly.

These rites belong to a form of Christianity commonly described as charismatic, which emphasizes emphatic worship, miraculous healing and the existence of demons. The Guardian reported that deliverance ministries such as Freedom Collective and the New Apostolic Reformation movement were growing in popularity, introducing more Christians to charismatic worship and beliefs about demons. The report said religious practices and ideologies can morph in times of political and cultural upheaval.

Leah Payne, a professor of American religious history at Portland Seminary, said the terms overlap: most charismatics would identify as evangelical, while most evangelicals did not identify as charismatic. She used Billy Graham, who was known for zealous preaching but generally avoided large emotional displays, as an example of mainstream evangelicalism. Payne said Graham wielded significant political power across the Eisenhower, Johnson and Nixon administrations, spoke passionately about his faith and wanted nonbelievers to enter a personal relationship with Christ, but never sobbed or screamed and was unlikely to lift his hands in rapturous praise. “If you look at him, Mr Evangelical, the guy who helped popularize that as a category in the 20th century, you will not see charismatic practices at all,” Payne said. “Like you won’t see people making big emotive displays.”

The reach of deliverance ministries is difficult to measure, partly because they lack a central governing body such as the Southern Baptist Convention or the Catholic church. Maps created by adherents have produced sharply different totals, with one showing nearly 600 ministries in the United States and another showing 174. The 2023 Public Religion Research Institute study co-authored by Payne found that 56% of Christian respondents attended religious services that included charismatic practices, which Payne characterized as “rapid growth.” Pew Research data had found that 23% of American Christians were charismatics or Pentecostals in 2006.

Payne said more of these ministries were becoming multiracial, as Freedom Collective was, but they typically retained what she called the “markers of white evangelical groups,” including white male pastors and what she called surface-level depoliticization. She also said a growing number of charismatics were millennial and Generation Z churchgoers who were more likely to encounter the movement through screens. The report said the romantic, high-stakes drama of spiritual warfare and miraculous intervention suited short-form video. “If you look up TikTok exorcisms, they’re just everywhere,” Payne said.

Thayer's online output follows that format. A pinned Instagram post instructed his followers to “watch Jesus heal or deliver 9 different people in 3 minutes,” a supercut from footage recorded when he set up a booth at a paranormal expo and promised healings to people who, according to him, were likely under the influence of witchcraft.

The Guardian situated the movement's growth against a decade marked by a global pandemic, economic inequality and war. It said a similar phenomenon took place after the September 11 terrorist attacks, when warnings about the end times reached a fever pitch. “I think those supernatural readings of the world are always what people turn to in times of uncertainty,” Payne said. “Americans like apocalypticism culturally, and they like interpreting the world spiritually.”

The Guardian said such bellicose declarations were not new within the religious right, but they had increased in volume and fervor. It contrasted an earlier intersection of American Christianity and conservatism defined by more reserved faith leaders such as Billy Graham and Jerry Falwell with an Overton window shifting toward the religious figures those leaders regarded as charlatans for their theatrics, including television shows featuring people speaking in tongues and convulsing on the floor. The report said that on the ground in places such as rural Tennessee, the practices were often less political and more personal.

Paul Djupe, a Denison University political scientist affiliated with the Public Religion Research Institute, said some deliverance ministries combine supernatural beliefs with a siege mentality that is “primed for political weaponization.” He tied that belief to what he described as a militant view among some American evangelicals: “like we’re under attack, we’re going to be discriminated against, and we need to upend the order and go to war.” Djupe said, “I think that’s really tightly connected with these beliefs that territories are controlled by demons, and sides and parties are controlled by demons.”

That connection was visible at Rededicate 250, a May 17, 2026 faith rally on the National Mall in Washington DC that partnered with the White House. Lorenzo Sewell, senior pastor of the non-denominational charismatic congregation 180 church in Detroit, said, “we do not believe that every Democrat is a demon, but we do believe that the Democratic platform is demonic.” House speaker Mike Johnson prayed for God to save people from “the forces of evil, oppression, injustice and tyranny,” both within the United States and abroad. A video message from Pete Hegseth also played at the rally.

The reporting found similar spiritual-warfare language in other political settings. Paula White-Cain, the president's spiritual adviser, spoke about “demonic networks” that threaten Trump; Southern Baptist minister and theologian Russell Moore once called her a heretic. Under Pete Hegseth's leadership, the Department of Defense repeatedly quoted scripture to present the US military as a divinely sanctioned force in a cosmic battle against evil. Ahead of the midterms, Johnson told Christian voters they faced a “spiritual battle” against socialists; three out of four Republicans or Republican-leaning Americans identified as Christian. A Republican candidate for governor in Kansas accused his rival of witchcraft and called the election “a spiritual battle that I’ve never seen before” before losing the race by a narrow margin.

The Guardian also said Christians have long believed they were under global siege for their faith, citing 2 Timothy 3:12: “all who desire to live a godly life in Christ Jesus will be persecuted.” It said apparent confirmations of that persecution now appear with a few taps on a phone. Fear-laden memes circulated in evangelical feeds, including claims that prayer in school was being banned, that Muslims intended to murder Christians and that Democrats were demons in disguise, lying in wait to prey on children physically or through ideology.

Freedom Collective also partnered with Patriot Church for a two-day stop in Knoxville. The Guardian described Patriot Church as a Christian nationalist church whose pastor, Ken Peters, regularly preaches against abortion, drag performances and Muslim people. The report said demons are heavily cited in perceived threats to a Christian way of life. A woman affiliated with Freedom Collective said some of the ministry's advertisements were banned by Facebook. “We know that’s the enemy intervening,” she said.

Not every deliverance ministry has the same political focus. Thayer told The Guardian that he no longer preaches about politics. He said his earlier conservatism had produced such a deep hatred of California that he refused to travel there for years. After he eventually visited the state following a friend's death and preached at the funeral, he said 30 people were saved. Thayer described his earlier stance by saying he “was such an idolater of politics.” Freedom Collective now focuses, in his account, on what he described as Christians' everyday struggle against Satan and demons, with Satan and his demons working in opposition to the ministry's mission of winning souls for the kingdom of heaven.

The reporting also described the personal stakes behind those public rituals. The Guardian's reporter spoke with a college friend who said demons had overwhelmed her with anxiety to the point that she contemplated suicide. She described immense relief after the Holy Spirit was called down to drive the demons away and repeatedly said, “I know I sound crazy.”

The reporter said that about a year earlier she began seeing more supernatural posts in her feed, largely from evangelicals she knew; she had once counted herself among them. She grew up in a tiny country Methodist church in western Tennessee that was essentially Baptist, with less talk about hell and shorter services. More recently, the church split from the United Methodists over LGBTQ+ affirmation and inclusion and now calls itself a “community church.” In college in the late aughts, she joined Cru, formerly Campus Crusade for Christ, an interdenominational campus ministry. She said one of the quiet rules in the evangelical spaces she knew was to limit charismatic displays of worship, including crying out, convulsing or acting out, because they could draw attention away from God and toward oneself. Talk of demons and witchcraft was discouraged as fantastical at best or a path toward evil at worst. Even raising her hands while swaying felt like a small rebellion. She contrasted the quiet prayer, polite greetings, simple hymns and brass offering plate of her childhood Sundays with the louder megachurch culture she later encountered.

Night was deepening as the Columbia event continued. Ethan, a leader at the nondenominational Citipointe church in nearby Franklin, warned the crowd, “When I walk through the city, there is a very heavy air of witchcraft.” He added, “You may not notice it when you live here. But there is a darkness that inhabits this atmosphere,” and said the only relief was the light of Jesus. The crowd murmured to each other, to themselves and to God.

Thayer then called forward people experiencing physical pain. Four people came forward, including a bespectacled, goateed man in a plaid shirt and cargo shorts who looked to be in his 60s. Thayer rested a hand on the man's shoulder, abdomen and head, snapped his fingers beside his ear and shouted, “Go! Go now!” and “Out! Out! Out!” The man coughed as the commands continued for nearly 10 minutes. He then smiled, briefly gripped Thayer's biceps, nodded and walked away. Thayer told the crowd, “I believe the Lord just freed a man of cancer.” Across the lot, the young woman remained prostrate on the ground with two ministry volunteers beside her as she continued battling her demons.
