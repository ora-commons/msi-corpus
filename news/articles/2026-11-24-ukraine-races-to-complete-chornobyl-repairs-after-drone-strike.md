## Temporary patches precede the permanent work needed by 2030

The Wall Street Journal reported that Ukraine is racing to complete permanent repairs at the Chornobyl Nuclear Power Plant after a drone struck the site last year, with experts warning that the work must be finished by 2030 to prevent radiation from spreading. The structure has been temporarily patched, but a video by video journalist Louisa Naks features foreign correspondent Matthew Luxmoore inside the plant as he examines how the war is making the site harder to secure.

A Feb. 14, 2025, drone strike damaged the outer layer of the New Safe Confinement, the arch built to cover Reactor No. 4 and limit radiation. Oleh Solonenko, head of a radiation-safety shift at Chornobyl, said the drone did not fully penetrate the structure and that monitoring found no rise in radiation outside the arch. At the time of the strike, Ukrainian officials and plant monitors reported no injuries.

Completed in 2019 as a $2.1 billion project, the New Safe Confinement was designed to enclose the original concrete sarcophagus and prevent radioactive debris from the damaged reactor from leaking. The International Atomic Energy Agency warned after the strike that the damage could significantly shorten the structure's 100-year lifespan and impair its core safety functions, including containing radioactive material and enabling safe dismantling of the reactor remains.

The repair schedule is therefore tied directly to radiation containment. Experts warned in the current report that the temporarily patched structure must be fully fixed by 2030 to prevent radiation from spreading.

At the time of the earlier damage report, workers said dismantling work had been placed on hold because the structure was no longer fully sealed. They also said the strike ignited a fire on the outer membrane that took more than two weeks to extinguish.

The current report shows that security is now the other side of the repair problem. The war is making the site harder to secure, even as Ukraine works to finish repairs on a facility already hit by a drone. Matthew Luxmoore went inside the plant for The Wall Street Journal to see how those wartime conditions are affecting the site's security.

Earlier MSI reporting described how the conflict has already altered work at the plant. Engineer Liudmyla Kozak said she was on duty when Russian troops seized the facility in February 2022. Klavdiia Omelchenko, who has worked with more than 2,200 engineers, scientists and others at the defunct plant, said the February 2025 strike revived memories of 1986 and that her current fear centers on what the war means for the site's safety rather than contamination risks.

The issue is long-term as well as immediate. The International Atomic Energy Agency's warning focused on the New Safe Confinement's 100-year lifespan and its core safety functions, including containing radioactive material and enabling safe dismantling of the reactor remains. The current report pairs that warning with a 2030 repair target.
