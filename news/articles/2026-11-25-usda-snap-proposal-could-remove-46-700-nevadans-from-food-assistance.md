## USDA SNAP proposal could remove 46,700 Nevadans from food assistance

**Subtype:** fact

- The U.S. Department of Agriculture proposed ending broad-based categorical eligibility for SNAP.
- The Nevada Division of Social Services estimated that 46,700 Nevadans would no longer qualify for SNAP, more than 13% of the state's 350,600 current recipients.

## State officials report no federal notice or implementation guidance

Earlier this month, the U.S. Department of Agriculture proposed a rule that could remove an estimated 46,700 Nevadans from SNAP, according to the Nevada Division of Social Services. That is more than 13% of the 350,600 people who currently receive food assistance in the state. If implemented as proposed, the change would end Nevada's use of broad-based categorical eligibility, a policy that has expanded access to the nation's largest food assistance program.

The proposed rule is scheduled to take effect in 2029. Households that currently qualify would have to meet new income and asset requirements when they renew their benefits to remain eligible. Nevada has used broad-based categorical eligibility since 2009, allowing more low-income working families, older adults and people with disabilities who otherwise would not qualify to receive SNAP.

Under the policy, states can raise income eligibility limits and waive asset limits. The policy also automatically makes a household eligible for SNAP if it meets requirements for Temporary Assistance for Needy Families. Nevada is one of 39 states that raised income limits and waived asset limits under the policy, and its current program has allowed households with more than $3,000 in assets to qualify for food assistance, which was not previously allowed. Advocates say asset limits penalize low-income working families trying to build a financial cushion.

Nevada has one of the highest monthly income thresholds for SNAP, at 200% of the federal poverty level, making more working people eligible for benefits. As of October, about 347,000 people in Nevada received SNAP assistance through broad-based categorical eligibility, nearly 99% of all beneficiaries in the state.

Nevada has not received the details needed to prepare for the change. According to a Nevada Division of Social Services spokesperson, the state did not receive formal notification from the Food and Nutrition Administration about the proposed rule or instructions on how to implement it. The division said the rule would increase Nevada's administrative costs, though it could not estimate the amount until, when and if, the requirements were finalized.

Advocates argued the proposed rule would let states streamline SNAP applications for households already deemed eligible for other assistance programs, reducing administrative costs and payment error rates. The Nevada division said the change would increase administrative costs for the state, though officials would not know the amount until, when and if, the requirements were finalized.

Dottie Rosenbaum, a senior policy analyst at the Center on Budget and Policy Priorities, said the policy supports people whose savings are modest but whose incomes remain low enough to qualify. “All people who have modest savings but whose incomes are low enough to qualify for SNAP benefits are helped by (broad-based categorical eligibility), and this rule would take it away,” Rosenbaum said. “The people who would lose SNAP would primarily be working families with children, older adults, and people with disabilities.”

The Trump administration has long targeted broad-based categorical eligibility, and the current proposal follows an earlier attempt. The USDA proposed a similar rule in 2019 during the president's first term but never finalized it. The Biden administration withdrew that proposal in 2021.

Nevada is already dealing with a separate SNAP contraction under the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, which Republicans passed in 2025 and which enacted stricter work requirements. Since the law took effect, Nevada has seen a 23% reduction in SNAP participants, or more than 100,000 people. The Nevada Division of Social Services estimated administrative costs associated with implementing the bill's new federal requirements at $19 million for the 2025–26 biennium and approximately $50 million for each future biennium.

Rosenbaum connected the proposed rule to voters' focus on affordability. “On the heels of an election where the public has indicated that they want their elected officials to focus on making life more affordable, here the administration is talking about taking away large amounts of food assistance benefits,” Rosenbaum said.

The proposal remains subject to finalization, and Nevada has not received the federal instructions needed to prepare for the change. If the rule takes effect as scheduled in 2029, households would face the new income and asset tests at renewal, and the division said the state could not estimate its added administrative burden before the requirements were final.


Earlier [MSI reporting](/articles/2026-11-24-usda-snap-proposal-could-end-eligibility-for-3-7-million-households/) cited a Beyond the Cliff Coalition estimate that 3.7 million households could lose eligibility under the same proposal. The Nevada Division of Social Services' estimate of 46,700 people provides a state-level figure, while the division said it had received no implementation guidance.
