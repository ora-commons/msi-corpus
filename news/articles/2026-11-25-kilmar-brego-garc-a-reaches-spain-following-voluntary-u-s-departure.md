## Judge dismisses immigration case, leaving his return to the United States uncertain

Kilmar Ábrego García arrived in Spain on Wednesday after voluntarily departing the United States, in what his attorney Simon Sandoval-Moshenberg called "a temporary departure." The Salvadoran national hopes to return to his family's home in Maryland after President Donald Trump leaves office, and U.S. District Judge Paula Xinis dismissed his Maryland immigration case after receiving confirmation that he had left the country. His immigration status in Spain was not immediately clear.

Ábrego García, 31, has an American wife and child. He was arrested in Baltimore in March 2025 and deported to a Salvadoran prison in what an official called an "administrative error." A judge later ordered his return to the United States, where he fought the Trump administration's effort to deport him again, this time to a series of African countries proposed by Department of Homeland Security officials.

After receiving officials' permission to leave, Ábrego García voluntarily departed Tuesday from Washington Dulles International Airport with his wife. In a virtual news conference Wednesday after reaching Spain, he said, "The government does not decide where my story continues." He added, "My family and I will decide our future."

Simon Sandoval-Moshenberg, a Virginia-based attorney representing Ábrego García, called the move "a temporary departure" but declined to describe his client's immediate plans, including where he would live. He said Ábrego García had many options to return as the husband of a U.S. citizen spouse and the father of a U.S. citizen child. Sandoval-Moshenberg said he did not expect a return under the current administration, which he said would never give Ábrego García a fair shake, but expected the next administration to prioritize reuniting the family and bringing him back to Maryland.

David Leopold, an Ohio immigration attorney and past president of the American Immigration Lawyers Association, said a return would require government permission and a Department of Homeland Security waiver. He said Ábrego García would have to show that his continued absence would cause his wife extreme hardship, including financial and psychological harm. "My guess is he can do it because he has a family he was supporting," Leopold said.

Ábrego García migrated to the United States illegally as a teenager. In 2019, an immigration judge ruled that he could not be deported to El Salvador because he faced danger there from a gang that had threatened his family. The judge allowed him to live and work in the United States under Immigration and Customs Enforcement supervision, but did not grant residency status. Sandoval-Moshenberg said Ábrego García would decide where to settle for the next two years while waiting out the remainder of the Trump administration.

The court also unsealed an order that Xinis signed Friday, setting Tuesday as his departure deadline, requiring removal of his GPS ankle monitor before he boarded the flight and spelling out the terms of his "unimpeded" departure. In the order, Xinis said the government had informed her that Ábrego García was free to go "to any country that is willing to accept him." The order did not explain why the government had changed its position on his leaving for a country of his choice.

The Department of Homeland Security said Tuesday night that Ábrego García left for Spain as part of a removal order, received no incentives and would not be allowed to return. Sandoval-Moshenberg declined to describe how the agreement with the government was reached, citing confidential settlement negotiations. Spokespeople for the Department of Homeland Security and the Justice Department did not immediately respond Wednesday to requests for more information about the resolution of Ábrego García's immigration proceedings and the agreement leading to his departure.

Maryland Sen. Chris Van Hollen, who met with Ábrego García in El Salvador last year in a visit that put the national spotlight on his predicament, celebrated Ábrego García's decision to leave the United States on his own terms. Speaking of the Trump administration's decision to send him to the CECOT mega-prison, Van Hollen said, "They wanted to leave him to rot there, to rot there for the rest of his life." The Democrat said he was grateful Ábrego García would be a free man "living in dignity."

It remained unclear how Ábrego García's departure would affect prosecutors' effort to revive a criminal case against him in Tennessee. After a judge ordered officials to arrange his return from El Salvador, prosecutors charged him with human smuggling in a case stemming from a 2022 traffic stop. Prosecutors alleged that he had been paid to transport people who were in the United States illegally. Ábrego García pleaded not guilty, and his attorneys called the prosecution politically motivated.

A federal judge dismissed the smuggling case in May, calling it an abuse of prosecutorial power and noting the time between the traffic stop and the charges. Prosecutors appealed, and a three-judge panel of the 6th Circuit Court of Appeals is scheduled to hear the case in February. Sandoval-Moshenberg said a successful appeal would leave prosecutors with a choice: drop the charges they sought to reinstate or seek Ábrego García's return to the United States for trial. "That will be up to them," he said. "I don't think it will happen, but if it does, it will be interesting to see which of those two options they choose."

U.S. Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin said in a post on X that he was "delighted" about Ábrego García's departure. Mullin listed allegations supporting his deportation, including that Ábrego García was a gang member; Ábrego García has repeatedly denied the accusation.

Ábrego García's early-2025 deportation occurred as Trump, a Republican, was ramping up immigration enforcement and promising 1 million deportations annually. Congress later approved billions of dollars for an administration that grew its cadre of immigration officers, expanded detention capabilities and pushed to deport an increasing number of people. His immediate legal situation involved a dismissal in Maryland, an appellate hearing scheduled for February in the Tennessee smuggling case and unclear immigration status in Spain.
