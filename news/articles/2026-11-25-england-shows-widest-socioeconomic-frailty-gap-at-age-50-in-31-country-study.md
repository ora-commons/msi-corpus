## The divide was larger at age 80 than at 50 in every country studied

A study led by researchers at King's College London and published in BMC Medicine has found that England had the widest gap in frailty between people in the lowest and highest socioeconomic groups at age 50 among 31 countries. The analysis covered 71,703 people and found the divide widening with age in every country studied, reaching about 12 points by age 80.

Frailty was defined in the study as struggling with multiple daily activities and being unable to recover easily from minor illnesses. Researchers measured each person on a scale from 0 to 100, based on the proportion of health problems and functional limitations they experienced, with higher scores indicating greater frailty.

The dataset covered England, the US, China, France, Italy, the Netherlands, Sweden, Greece, Cyprus, Latvia and Lithuania. Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland were not included.

People in the lowest socioeconomic group had frailty scores nearly seven points higher than those in the highest socioeconomic group at age 50 in all countries included. By age 80, that difference had widened to about 12 points. England's gap at age 50 was the widest in the comparison.

First author Dongfeng Tang, from King's College London's department of primary care and data science, said the findings showed that developed nations such as England could "still have wide socioeconomic disparities in health compared to other countries." Tang said the cross-national data showed England with a lower-than-average number of physicians per population and more people with unmet healthcare needs. "These relative differences in healthcare capacity and access may disproportionately affect people from lower socioeconomic groups," Tang said.

Tang said the results also showed the "dramatic effect of socioeconomic disparities on healthy ageing. People from lower socioeconomic groups tend to experience poorer health across the life-course and accumulate health problems at earlier ages." He said: "What our research adds is a clearer picture of how these inequalities in frailty develop across mid to later life and how substantially they differ between countries."

The researchers identified social and physical inactivity as the two main drivers of the higher levels of frailty among people with lower socioeconomic status, suggesting strong links between frailty and social habits. Tang said: "We also found that lifestyle factors, particularly physical activity and social engagement, accounted for part of these inequalities, although much of the gap remained unexplained."

Matthew O'Connell, senior lecturer in population health sciences at King's College London and the study's senior author, said: "It is highly concerning that people from lower socioeconomic groups had higher levels of frailty across all countries studied, with the greatest inequalities in England."

O'Connell also pointed to the increasing role of lifestyle factors at older ages. "More encouragingly, the increasing role of lifestyle factors at older ages suggests increasing physical and social activities in mid to later life might help to reduce frailty at older ages," he said. He said such activity could be encouraged for everyone as they age and targeted at groups potentially at higher risk.

The study did not examine Scotland, Wales or Northern Ireland, and the researchers said much of the socioeconomic gap in frailty remained unexplained even after they identified physical inactivity and social inactivity as contributing factors.
