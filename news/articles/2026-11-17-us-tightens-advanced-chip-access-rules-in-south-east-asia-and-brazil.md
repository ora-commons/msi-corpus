## US tightens advanced-chip access rules in South East Asia and Brazil

## Bhattacharya says China loses a practical route to frontier computing

President Donald Trump was due to meet Xi Jinping in Shenzhen this week when his administration announced interim rules on Monday to curb Beijing's access to advanced semiconductors. The two leaders met in Beijing in May, according to the BBC. The US Commerce Department said data-centre operators in Singapore, Malaysia, Thailand, Indonesia and Brazil would face greater scrutiny.

The rules do more than restrict the export of advanced chips. Chinese firms currently rent overseas computing power to train AI models, and operators buying advanced chips from Nvidia or other American chipmakers will need a US government licence. The requirement allows Washington to monitor whether chips serve Chinese companies or are being smuggled into China. Licences will be issued on a "case-by-case basis," the Bureau of Industry and Security said.

The bureau said Brazil and the four South East Asian nations "present a higher-than-normal risk of diversion" of chips Washington wants to keep out of China's hands. The controls are intended to ensure sensitive US technology "cannot be circumvented through remote computing arrangements involving data centers in third countries," it said.

China's Foreign Ministry called the rules "economic coercion" and said Beijing "will take necessary measures to firmly safeguard its own legitimate rights and interests." The BBC described the move as a fresh challenge for Beijing, which has become deeply dependent on computing from abroad after US export restrictions. Beijing has made self-sufficiency in technology a national priority, but it needs the best chips to build powerful AI models. Experts cited by the BBC said China needs advanced US chips to keep its best AI models competitive.

Chinese firms DeepSeek and MoonShot have come close to US rivals Anthropic and OpenAI. The Institute for Progress, a US-based think-tank, estimates that top US AI models lead the best Chinese platforms by only about six and half months. "This is despite US AI companies having significantly better compute access and financing," it said. The institute also projects that at least 30% of China's advanced AI computing power will come from renting overseas or smuggling in 2027.

Reema Bhattacharya of Verisk Maplecroft said the restrictions had taken away "one of the more practical routes Chinese AI developers had to accessing frontier computing power." The BBC's reporting noted that tighter restrictions could also push Chinese firms toward domestic semiconductors and further drive advancement at home.

Naoise McDonagh, an international trade expert from Australia's Edith Cowan University, described a longstanding dilemma for Washington: whether to keep selling chips to China to retain its reliance on the US, or restrict access and risk further spurring Chinese innovation.

South East Asian countries have invested heavily in data centres to power their AI ambitions, and large numbers of data centres have sprung up recently across the region. Chinese firms seeking computing power overseas have been a key market. McDonagh said countries like Malaysia and Thailand, whose data-centre rollout has been heavily driven by Chinese buyers, could be hit hardest. The BBC reported that the region could now find it harder to obtain advanced chips.

Singapore was an early adopter of data centres, but limits on new construction sent investment toward Malaysia and Thailand. Johor in Malaysia and Batam in Indonesia have approved billions of dollars worth of data centres to catch spillover demand. Batam in Indonesia is "emerging as a major regional data centre hub," the BBC reported. Countries have offered incentives including cheaper land to attract foreign investment, including Chinese technology giants Alibaba and Tencent.

Bhattacharya said the rules were unlikely to derail the data-centre boom as a whole. Investment backed by major technology firms and clearly identifiable customers should "remain relatively resilient," she said.

Megaspeed is a Singaporean data-centre firm that the BBC described as the biggest buyer of Nvidia chips in South East Asia. It has been accused of illegally smuggling chips to Chinese companies after purchasing them for data centres in Malaysia, Indonesia and Thailand, as some operators have been accused of helping China evade US export controls. The BBC reported that Megaspeed's former director Alice Huang is suspected of being a Chinese spy. It said calls for tighter export controls grew after Nvidia's Jensen Huang admitted to an affair with her and resigned as CEO.

Megaspeed denied the smuggling allegations, described its business as "legitimate and lawful" and said it had "retained external legal counsel to conduct a thorough internal investigation." The company said Alice Huang stepped down as a director in late 2023 and left the firm last year.

Singapore said it was "engaging relevant US authorities" to understand the rules and was "exploring how to facilitate trusted access" for local firms. A spokesperson for Brazil's Ministry of Development, Industry, Trade and Services said the ministry was aware of the announcement and "closely monitoring any potential developments regarding this measure." Malaysia previously said it welcomed data-centre investment but "will not allow itself to be used to get around export controls." The BBC said it had contacted Indonesia, Malaysia and Thailand for comment.
