## Army canceled the Georgia-based firm's lead-neutralization contract

In an opinion column, environmental lawyer Steven Donziger said the US Army detonated 68 barrels of lead azide over Iowa on Wednesday after canceling a contract for a chemical treatment intended to neutralize deteriorating lead. He said a New York Times investigation found the treatment had been validated by the National Academy of Sciences and the US Environmental Protection Agency and had been used successfully on multiple large cleanup projects. Donziger said Trump officials issued an “all-clear” within hours, but no air or soil sample from the explosion had been tested in an independent laboratory with a known result.

The timeline Donziger described began with the Army storing lead underground at a munitions facility for decades. He said the government knew by 2009 that the material was deteriorating and could blow up on its own, yet delayed action. In March, the Army hired a Georgia-based company to apply a chemical spray that would neutralize the lead and produce a non-hazardous byproduct that could be disposed of in landfills. Donziger said the Army canceled the contract two months later, ignored public outcry and chose detonation instead.

Donziger described the Army’s action as the deliberate detonation of a “poison bomb” of lead azide when a safe alternative was available. He wrote that the Wednesday detonation produced a plume of cancer-causing toxic dust that spread an estimated 100 miles (160km) southwest. He said the plume covered farmland, food crops, forests, rivers, houses, hospitals and schools, along with the people and animals underneath.

“This is as stupid as trying to blow up an entire office tower because asbestos was used in the construction,” Donziger wrote.

Donziger cited the EPA’s finding that lead is particularly harmful to children and women and that no level of exposure can be considered safe. He said the agency links lead to impaired fetal brain development, damage to reproductive systems, and lung, stomach and kidney cancers.

Donziger wrote that Trump officials said it was safe for evacuated residents to return home and for normal life to resume. He argued that the recommendation lacked a scientific basis because no air or soil sample had been tested in an independent laboratory with a known result. He also said point samples assess only one location at one moment, do not test for every possible harmful substance and cannot assess damage throughout all affected areas. Donziger said tests could also be manipulated and predicted that political pressure would lead officials to downplay the danger to reassure the public.

His first comparison was East Palestine, Ohio, where a train carrying vast amounts of the cancer-causing chemical vinyl chloride derailed in February 2023. Donziger wrote that after the derailment, “the fateful and hasty decision was made to burn off the remaining spilled chemical,” which produced what he called “a terrifying mushroom cloud of poison that hovered over the area for days.” Donziger said residents reported headaches, dizziness, nausea and respiratory challenges, and that residents continued to report the same ailments. He wrote that cancers often take decades to manifest after exposure but that damage to cellular structure starts immediately.

A former hazmat official told a local news outlet at the time that the response had “basically nuked a town with chemicals so we could get a railroad open.” Donziger said the EPA under the Biden administration told local residents the air was safe and sent the company a letter asking it to pay for a cleanup. He said the company eventually agreed to a $22m settlement with the town and a $600m class-action settlement for eligible residents and businesses.

Donziger said he had spent decades battling Chevron over what he called its “Amazon Chernobyl” disaster in Ecuador’s rainforest, which he said had devastated Indigenous communities. He said the US response to the Iowa blast followed a corporate playbook that managed the situation as a public-relations and political challenge rather than the ecological and humanitarian disaster it was.

Donziger also cited the 2016 water crisis in Flint, where Barack Obama traveled to the city and drank a glass of tap water, declaring it safe before a single sample had returned from a laboratory. He said studies found high quantities of lead in the water and continuing health ailments. He also cited East Palestine, where Ohio governor Mike DeWine and EPA leader Michael Regan drank tap water two weeks after the derailment and declared it safe. Donziger said studies and anecdotal reports from residents and doctors told a different story.

His 9/11 comparison focused on EPA chief Christine Todd Whitman. Donziger said Whitman declared the air in lower Manhattan “safe” only seven days after the attack that felled the twin towers and released asbestos and toxins, before an empirically valid study had been completed. He said thousands of lower Manhattan residents returned home based on that statement. Donziger cited a 2003 EPA inspector-general report that found the safety declaration lacked a scientific basis and appeared to have been made largely for political reasons.

Donziger also cited a Northwell Health study reporting that 57,000 people who had been in lower Manhattan had been diagnosed with 9/11-related cancer and that cases had risen sharply in the previous four years. He wrote that cancer has a long latency period and that it was impossible to gauge how many cases might have been avoided had the truth been told during the first days after the attack.

Donziger said the agency charged with protecting the public had misled people about their exposure to danger. He wrote that public officials consistently give overly optimistic assessments when toxic disasters strike because they want to reassure a nervous public, and he said the initial “all clear” in Iowa was probably no different.

He placed the Iowa blast within a broader criticism of military pollution. Donziger said the Pentagon is the world’s largest emitter of fossil fuels, the United States operates roughly 750 military bases worldwide and the country has been on a permanent war footing for more than a quarter-century, citing Iraq, Afghanistan, Iran, Israel and Ukraine. He said the United States accounts for more than 40% of global military spending.

Donziger cited the film Earth’s Greatest Enemy, by Abby Martin and Mike Prysner, for its argument that the Pentagon does not have a pollution problem but is itself the pollution problem. He said government lobbying keeps Pentagon emissions out of domestic carbon budgets during negotiations over the global transition to clean energy.

Donziger said the regulatory regime under Donald Trump had been largely captured by corporations. He said the administration gutted the EPA’s professional staff and hired several former lobbyists for the chemical industry. He described EPA leader Lee Zeldin as a “lackey” for Trump who appeared more interested in pleasing his boss than documenting pollution threats, and said he did not trust the agency under Zeldin’s leadership.

Donziger said Zeldin’s EPA was conducting most of the air and soil monitoring in Iowa. He also said Zeldin had revoked the EPA’s landmark finding that greenhouse gases endanger public health that year.

In his climate context, Donziger said the failure of global climate talks in Turkey at Cop31 meant the planet would warm by 2.8C (5F) by the end of the century, almost double what had been agreed to in Paris in 2015. He said oil-industry profits were higher than ever, drilling was producing more fossil fuels than ever and the United States, the world’s leading carbon emitter, was no longer participating in global climate talks.

Donziger accused major media outlets of repeating Trump’s assurances that there was no harm in Iowa and normalizing what he called deception. He said entities that pollute, whether rail companies or the Army, know that media outlets generally report their experts’ assertions without checking them. He said longitudinal studies documenting harm take years, their results are rarely publicized and it is virtually impossible for the truth to catch up once initial deception has circulated globally.

Donziger said an industrial accident was bad enough, but argued that the Iowa blast was worse because a safe alternative had been available. He described that choice as planned madness and said the Army had essentially decided to “nuke the American people with poison.” He concluded: “When the government that’s supposed to protect us from harm becomes the cause of the harm, you know we are in serious trouble.”

Donziger called the Iowa situation a metaphor for much of what he said was wrong with the United States. He is a US-based human rights and environmental lawyer and the author of Donziger On Justice on Substack.
