## Missouri tax deduction aims to lower entry costs

Over nearly 70 years, Katie and Gerald “Buster” Newberry have raised or grown everything from sheep and cattle to corn and beans on their farm in Parnell, Missouri. Now, their fields and tools sit mostly at rest. Katie’s once-popular ceramics studio and Buster’s best machines are gone, and none of their children or grandchildren has chosen to continue the legacy.

The couple were born into farming families and learned the business from their parents. Tough profit margins and lofty land values have lessened the appeal of carrying on the work. Katlyn Miller, 27, said of her 87-year-old grandfather: “The day Buster doesn’t go out and do what he can on the farm, we’ll know something happened to him.”

In 1965, two years into their marriage, Buster and Katie bought their first 200 acres (81 hectares) for about $250 an acre. They later bought more land, eventually reaching 1,000 acres (405 hectares). Katie, 81, recalled, “We just had a few milk cows — seven or nine — and four sows,” and said, “We bought a milker for $200, and the milk carrier would make pickups each week.”

Persistent drought across the Midwest made dairy production more costly, so the Newberrys transitioned to raising beef and grain crops. They relied on only one hired laborer, even as their herd reached 400 head and their pastures rolled into the neighboring ZIP code. The farm remained primarily a family-run operation.

Missouri has the third-highest farm count in the country, but it lost approximately 10,000 farms between 2015 and 2024, according to the Missouri Agricultural Outlook. The Missouri Agricultural Outlook projects total farm income in the state to fall 17% in 2026.

Small and mid-sized farms, such as Katie’s and Buster’s, have historically represented the bulk of American agricultural production, but as their owners “age out,” fewer households are buying in.

Missouri Farm Bureau president Garrett Hawkins said: “A tremendous amount of wealth and productive farmland will change hands as this generation ages. The question for us is how we create opportunities for the next generation of farmers to compete for that land.”

The effects extend beyond the fields. “When you lose a farmer, you potentially lose a customer for the local equipment dealer, feed store, bank and veterinarian, as well as a taxpayer, community leader and volunteer. Farm succession is really a rural-community issue,” Hawkins said.

The trends are also visible nationwide. According to the U.S. Department of Agriculture, the United States is losing 2 million acres (809,371 hectares) of land in farms each year as urban development and other infrastructure demands encroach on the countryside. The economic concerns go beyond the future use of that land.

The demographic pattern is clearer in USDA’s most recent Census of Agriculture, conducted in 2022. The average age of Missouri producers was nearly 58, while the number of mid-career farmers ages 35 to 64 fell 9.2% since 2017. The average farm size continues to climb, and land becoming available is selling to increasingly larger operations.

University of Missouri’s Rural and Farm Finance Policy Analysis Center director Alejandro Plastina said crop production has been less profitable every year since 2023. Large farm owners can typically sustain lower earnings or even losses when they own their land without debt. “But that’s not the case for beginning farmers or even younger farmers,” Plastina said, putting many smallholders or medium-scale growers at risk of having to exit.

The Newberrys lease approximately 850 acres (344 hectares) of crop land and 150 acres (61 hectares) of pasture to local corn and soybean farmers. For farmers who must reserve a big chunk of their earnings to pay land rent, “it’s really hard,” Plastina said. That pressure is leading to a steep rise in farmers who report having primary off-farm jobs, especially among farmers aged 35 and under, who typically carry greater debt while trying to get their businesses off the ground.

Missouri’s Beginning Farmer Tax Deduction seeks to alleviate the high costs of entry by encouraging established farmers to enter rental, sale or crop-share agreements with starting farmers who have less than 10 years of experience. The number of new and beginning producers in Missouri rose from 41,416 in 2017 to 46,312 in 2022.

“But this is bigger than any one program,” Hawkins said. He emphasized increasing access to affordable credit and risk-management tools and addressing cumbersome regulations.

Buster remembers how he got started while he was still a teenager, working alongside his father on the farm as an only child. “We just had sows, but Dad let me have a little livestock,” he said. Today, a little livestock is all that’s left. After breakfast, Buster fires up an ATV that helps him take feed to a few dozen cows and move between barns where the remaining tractors collect cobwebs.

Katie invites the mail carrier in for a visit between organizing and boxing up their many belongings. “You spend all your life building this and then what do you do with it?” she said. The Newberrys will pass the farm on to their two daughters, but Katie said the thought of it becoming something else “worries us.” “They’ll just do what they want with it,” she said.
