## Proposition 40 headed toward defeat as tech figures credited Brin

The Guardian reported that California voters passed Proposition 42, an initiative that would bar any taxation on billionaire wealth, while Proposition 40 remained headed toward a double-digit defeat. Proposition 42 was placed on the ballot by Google co-founder Sergey Brin and a cohort of California's wealthiest residents; Proposition 40 would have imposed a one-time 5% tax on billionaires in the state. In the hours after polls closed on election day, technology figures credited Brin with what they called his instrumental role in the opposition to Proposition 40.

Technology industry players began praising Brin on social media after polls closed. Garry Tan, the CEO of Y Combinator, wrote, “Huge thanks to Sergey Brin here,” while venture capitalist Ilya Sukhar posted, “God bless Sergey Brin.” David Marcus, a crypto entrepreneur, wrote, “Hero.” Their messages referred to Brin’s instrumental role in the campaign against Proposition 40.

Supporters said the money raised by the proposed tax would fund California's health care losses from Donald Trump's One Big Beautiful Bill Act. Even without Proposition 40's outsized opposition, the pro-Proposition 40 campaign had to persuade Californians that this specific one-time tax, chiefly geared toward health care funding, was the right way to address those losses. Opponents repeatedly told voters that Proposition 40 would instead make health care funding worse. Gov. Gavin Newsom and governor-elect Xavier Becerra were among the major political figures who opposed Proposition 40.

The Guardian's county-level analysis found that counties voting to pass Proposition 40 were typically more Democratic and included areas where many wealthy Californians reside, including Los Angeles, San Francisco and other parts around the Bay Area. Counties that overwhelmingly rejected the measure were mostly red, rural and low-income. Many of those counties swung blue in U.S. House races in the election, while up to 80% of voters there voted no on Proposition 40, according to the latest count.

During the campaign's final month, the Proposition 40 opposition campaign used highly produced ads and mailers to warn voters that the tax was dangerous and would hurt everyday people. Its taglines included “everyone tax,” “reckless experiment” and “you’re next.” Workers attended a rally against Proposition 40 in La Palma, California, on 5 October 2026, in a photograph credited to Eric Thayer/Los Angeles Times/Getty Images.

Campaign finance filings showed that Silicon Valley donors had raised nearly $425 million against Proposition 40, making it the most expensive opposition campaign in California history. Brin, whose net worth was about $230 billion, gave at least $132 million, more than any other donor. Former Google CEO Eric Schmidt, the runner-up donor, gave more than $48 million. Millions more came from executives at Palantir, DoorDash, Stripe and Reddit, along with prominent venture capitalists. The pro-Proposition 40 campaign, sponsored by SEIU-United Healthcare Workers West, known as SEIU-UHW, raised just more than $31 million and was outspent nearly 14-fold.

Suzanne Jimenez, chief of staff for SEIU-UHW, said, “All lies have a shelf life.” She continued: “Billionaire-funded propaganda may mislead voters about the consequences of these cuts today, but as hospitals reduce services, emergency rooms close and families lose access to care, the truth will become increasingly difficult to hide.”

Rob Lalka, a business professor at Tulane University and author of *The Venture Alchemists: How Big Tech Turned Profits Into Power*, compared the Proposition 40 campaign with Uber and Lyft's ballot-measure campaign in 2020. The companies spent more than $200 million to defeat an initiative over labor rights for drivers, Lalka said, and he described the similar tactics as “misleading propaganda and paying for endorsements.”

“The money also seemed astonishing at the time: the amount to influence the outcome, over $200m, was a massive sum,” Lalka said. “Money in politics has only increased in California throughout this decade. That’s the context for Prop 40 this election cycle.”

Francesco Trebbi, a business and public policy professor at the University of California, Berkeley, said political-campaign funding had broken records nationwide during the midterm cycle. He pointed to Elon Musk's $446 million in House and Senate spending across the country and noted that it was ineffective in places such as Texas, where James Talarico, the candidate Musk backed, won. “Maybe it was easier to decouple Prop 40 opposition from President Trump’s agenda, so Brin had an easier target to hit,” Trebbi said. He added: “We need a lot more data and statistical evidence in order to conclusively finalize this discussion.” Exit polls for Proposition 40 had not yet been released and were expected to provide more detail about voters' decisions.

Even as Proposition 40 headed toward rejection, SEIU-UHW and its allies were not giving up on the long game. Jimenez said: “If Sacramento fails to protect patients and deliver a real solution that makes billionaires pay their fair share, healthcare workers will pursue every available avenue, up to and including returning to the ballot in 2028 with a billionaire tax.”
