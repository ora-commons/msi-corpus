## The study tested blood rather than relying on diagnoses

A four-year study in Belo Horizonte, Brazil, found that neighborhoods with Wolbachia-carrying mosquitoes had about a 69% reduction in dengue and chikungunya infections among schoolchildren compared with similar neighborhoods. Researchers presented the result Saturday at a meeting of the American Society of Tropical Medicine and Hygiene.

The study included more than 5,700 children in what the researchers described as one of the world's dengue hot spots. It compared neighborhoods that all received the same pesticide and other mosquito-control interventions, but half were randomly assigned to receive releases of the specially bred mosquitoes.

Because people with mild symptoms may not be diagnosed, the research team tested blood for signs of dengue, chikungunya or the Zika virus. There were too few Zika cases to calculate whether the Wolbachia mosquitoes had any effect on that disease.

Dr. Sri Edupuganti of Emory University, part of the international research team, said the reduction was sustained even during Brazil's severe 2024 dengue outbreak. The study offers new evidence that breeding mosquitoes unable to transmit the viruses can be a viable strategy rather than relying only on pesticides and trying to avoid bites.

The World Mosquito Program, a nonprofit, has pioneered the strategy outside the U.S. Scientists breed mosquitoes to carry Wolbachia, a kind of bacteria that is harmless to people and common in many insects, although it is not found in the dengue-carrying Aedes aegypti mosquitoes. The bacteria dramatically reduce the mosquitoes' ability to transmit certain viruses.

Female mosquitoes spread the bacteria to their offspring, the researchers explained. Releasing enough of the specially bred males and females into an area allows Wolbachia to become established gradually in the local mosquito population.

Field trials and other studies in Indonesia and elsewhere have shown that the strategy can significantly lower dengue diagnoses. The Brazilian study went further by testing blood for signs of dengue, chikungunya or the Zika virus.

David Hamer, a global health expert at Boston University who was not involved in the Brazilian study, said, “This is really exciting.” He predicted that pairing more use of these Wolbachia mosquitoes with existing steps to fight mosquito-borne disease could “knock dengue down to much lower levels” in many places.

Florida is grappling with an unusually large dengue outbreak. Usually, dengue in the continental U.S. is found mostly in people who traveled internationally. Florida health officials had tallied more than 650 locally acquired cases as of Friday, about three-quarters of them in Hillsborough County, home to Tampa. The outbreak appears to be the largest in any U.S. state since at least the 1940s.
