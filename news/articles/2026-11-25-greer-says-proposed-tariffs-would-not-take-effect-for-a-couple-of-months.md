## Administration proposes broad exemption for $30 billion in Chinese goods

U.S. Trade Representative Jamieson Greer said he did not expect the administration's proposed tariffs to take effect before a couple months from now and that he did not expect them to rattle the global economy, in remarks to CNBC reported by the Associated Press. "We've talked to our trading partners about this — they know what we're proposing," Greer told CNBC. "I think a lot of this is baked in, and I don't think any of these would actually take effect earlier than a couple months from now."

Greer also said the administration was proposing a "broad exemption" for $30 billion worth of Chinese goods in line with the trade truce the United States reached with China last year, which is set to expire on Jan. 10, 2027. Trump met Chinese leader Xi Jinping in Shenzhen, China, last week and plans to host him next month at the Group of 20 summit in Florida, an event at which the truce and issues over access to rare earth minerals and China's purchases of American agricultural products could be discussed again.

The proposal Greer described would add 20% to Chinese goods and raise import taxes on products from the European Union, Japan, South Korea, Taiwan and nine other major trading partners. Announced Wednesday, the day before Thanksgiving, it stems from investigations into the trade practices of 12 countries, the 27-member European Union and the self-governing island of Taiwan.

China would face an additional 20% tariff, and for some Chinese goods the new rate would be stacked on other import taxes for a total of 45%. The proposal names Bangladesh, Cambodia, India, Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Switzerland, Thailand and Vietnam among the nations facing new duties. Vietnam faces the same 20% rate as China, pending the finalization of trade talks, the U.S. Trade Representative's office said Wednesday, while tariffs for other trading partners range from 10% to 18%.

The administration said the duties are meant to discourage excessive factory output in economies where subsidies and other trade barriers have boosted production and, in its view, put American manufacturers at a disadvantage. It also said the tariffs would help replace tariff revenue lost after the Supreme Court ruled in February that Trump could not declare an economic emergency to bypass Congress and impose across-the-board tariffs. The legal basis is Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974, which enables the U.S. government to investigate and retaliate against foreign trade practices that violate existing deals, are not justified by market forces or actively discriminate against American goods. A senior Trump administration official, speaking on condition of anonymity, told reporters the new tariffs would be consistent with the trade deals Trump reached last year with dozens of countries after threatening severe tariff hikes.

The new import taxes target specific products and would not stack on separate tariffs already imposed on automobiles, steel, aluminum and other products under Section 232 of the Trade Expansion Act of 1962. Nor would they stack on levies of 10% to 12.5% announced in July for 60 trading partners that the White House said had inadequately enforced bans on goods produced by forced labor.

On Wednesday, the United States separately proposed higher import taxes on Brazil after trade talks stalled. The proposal includes higher taxes on raw and refined sugar and a new 10% tariff on aircraft, which would affect Embraer, Brazil's largest aircraft maker.

China has pushed back consistently on U.S. tariffs. On Wednesday, China's Ministry of Foreign Affairs again stated its opposition while calling for the U.S. to resolve the issue through dialogue. "A tariff war or a trade war serves no party's interests," spokesperson Guo Jiakun said. "The so-called overcapacity is a false narrative, and China opposes using it as a pretext for political manipulation."

The Associated Press characterized the proposal as another controversial turn in Trump's economic policy and reported that voters in this month's midterm elections punished Republicans by handing majorities in the House and Senate to Democrats. About two-thirds of voters said Trump's policies had made the economy worse, according to the AP Voter Poll, a sweeping survey of the American electorate conducted in all 50 states. Trump has promised that import taxes will bring back manufacturing jobs and reduce the federal deficit, but last year's tariffs contributed to higher inflation that angered voters and led to calls by Democratic lawmakers to require congressional approval going forward.

The administration is arguing that the industrial policies of the countries targeted by the duties have undercut American companies and contributed to a trade deficit in goods that has recently exceeded $1 trillion annually.

Mexico and Norway have two other investigations pending, according to an administration official who insisted on anonymity before the U.S. trade representative's office posted the report. The Associated Press confirmed the planned announcement ahead of the call, according to people who insisted on anonymity because they were not authorized to comment publicly. The plans were reported earlier on Tuesday by Bloomberg and The Wall Street Journal. AP writer Huizhong Wu in Bangkok contributed to this report.
