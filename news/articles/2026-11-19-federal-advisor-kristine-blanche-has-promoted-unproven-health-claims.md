## Epstein-Barr and HPV products vanished after reporters asked questions

Kristine Blanche, the wife of U.S. Attorney General Todd Blanche, promoted dietary supplements and online health programs with unproven claims on a website connected to her medical practice, according to a Guardian review published Nov. 19, 2026. The site linked to “Dr Blanche’s Dispensary,” where patients could buy supplement pills she recommended. Blanche was appointed in April to the advisory council of the federal National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health, which has a $170m annual budget and supports research on alternative and holistic medicines and practices.

Blanche, an advocate for alternative medical cures and holistic health, says she has a PhD in breast thermography and an MD degree from the University of Science, Arts and Technology in Montserrat, a British territory in the Caribbean. In a written response, she said she has “a PhD and an MD degree” and complies “with all mandatory FDA guidelines. Any publication to the contrary is false and defamatory.” She also said she had never been licensed as a physician in the United States and had previously practiced as a physician assistant.

Blanche suggested on her site that a liquid herbal compound could be used to treat the Epstein-Barr virus, although she misspelled “Epstein” in the listing. The Food and Drug Administration has no medication approved to treat or cure Epstein-Barr, and the product itself did not claim to treat the virus. The store’s disclaimer said “these statements have not been evaluated by the Food and Drug Administration. These products are not intended to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease.”

Blanche also listed five supplements that she said could help treat human papillomavirus, with prices reaching $63 a bottle. The Guardian reported that the Epstein-Barr and HPV-related products disappeared from her website on the day after The Guardian emailed Blanche questions.

Another treatment Blanche offered online for $595 claimed that it could help men boost testosterone levels and lose weight. Her site still offered the “Man Up” and “Girlfriend” cleanses at almost $600 each, with detoxes, shakes, videos and dietary supplements. The “Man Up” program promised: “Lose weight, boost your testosterone & ditch the toxins. All while eating real food.”

In a YouTube sales pitch, Blanche said, “I’ve had patients lose 40 pounds.” She also said, “It’s not acceptable to you if you’re not really feeling great.”

Peter Lurie, executive director of the Center for Science in the Public Interest, said the website highlighted gaps in regulations governing claims by providers. “That leaves an opening for some to exploit,” he said. Lurie said, “It is not in the interest of American patients, but it is not clearly illegal.”

Lurie said Blanche’s appointment also concerned him because of her financial interest in products within the advisory committee’s field. “It absolutely concerns me,” he said, adding that he had “no confidence that the attorney general’s wife, with a financial interest in the kind of products this advisory committee concerns itself with, would be appropriate on the committee.”

Her April appointment made her a member of the NCCIH advisory council. Former NCCIH head Helene Langevin, a University of Vermont professor, said the council role is “important” because members review every research-grant decision made by independent panels. Langevin retired in September 2025 and declined to discuss Blanche’s qualifications, saying, “I can’t comment on the qualifications of any advisory board member.”

A National Institutes of Health spokesperson defended the appointment, saying “the Trump administration is bringing the best and brightest into HHS.” The spokesperson said “Dr Kristine Blanche was appointed through the established appointment process based on her work and background in integrative health. Her experience provides a relevant foundation for service and a valuable perspective to inform the council’s work, in compliance with the charter.”

Iris International, which invests in medical companies, hired Blanche as chief medical officer this year. A company spokesperson said Blanche “reports to the CEO and provides a view on emerging healthcare businesses in the market.” The scale of business on her website was unclear, though it appears to have been active for more than a decade. Todd Blanche’s 2025 and 2026 financial disclosures listed his wife’s income from “Integrative Healing Center” as between $1,000 and $15,000.

Blanche has promoted thermography, an alternative diagnostic imaging technology that detects heat variations in the body. She said in a podcast that it can predict potential risks years before mammograms, and her website said her PhD thesis concerned “breast thermography.” The FDA has approved thermography when used with mammography but has warned that it cannot serve as a substitute.

Blanche and her husband moved to Florida in 2024. Her website listed a virtual practice in Palm Beach Gardens, where the couple live not far from Mar-a-Lago. Her physician-assistant license lists her home address. Blanche attended her husband’s confirmation hearing before the Senate Judiciary Committee in July.

The month before The Guardian’s review, Blanche appeared on the I Choose Health podcast, run by New York City-based personal trainer Stefan Lessie, who posts interviews with doctors and health activists. Lessie said the podcast had about 20 to 30 listeners a week on Spotify. He also said an unsolicited email from a public-relations executive said, “my client Kristine Blanche would resonate with your audience.” Lessie interviewed Blanche for an hour about her practice and philosophy of medicine.

During the interview, Blanche said, “A lot of women come to me after they’ve been to many doctors over several years of suffering.” She also said, “I love that the Maha movement is out there,” referring to Robert F Kennedy Jr’s Make America Healthy Again movement, and added, “Trying to educate moms on, you know, food dyes are bad.” Lessie said he did not know who Blanche was before the interview. He discovered her husband’s position afterward and said: “I was doing some research after the interview, and I saw her husband, and I was like: ‘He’s the US attorney general!’ And I was like, ‘Wow!’” Blanche had received little, if any, public attention before Stat News reported the appointment in April.
