## Fifteen leading brands advertised real-time vehicle location, report finds

In a report released on Tuesday, University of New South Wales researchers warned that connected-car functions marketed to help owners monitor and protect their vehicles are also being used by perpetrators of domestic violence to track, control and restrict survivors. The report mapped what 15 leading car brands advertised in Australia and found real-time geolocation marketed by every brand examined. It also found digital key access, zone-exit alerts and speed or acceleration restrictions advertised across major brands and said smart-car functions perpetrators could misuse to facilitate abuse were prevalent across major car brands.

The three cases formed part of the report. Earlier this year, a man in Australia was found guilty of using a connected-car app to harass his former partner, including restricting the speed at which she drove, sometimes to 40km/h. In 2022 in the United States, a woman found that her former husband had used a car app associated with her vehicle to track her; the carmaker allegedly refused to terminate the former partner's digital access to the vehicle. In a 2020 US case, a woman found a baseball bat that her former partner had used to threaten her in the back seat of her car.

Researchers said three main scenarios could open the functions to misuse: the abuser owning the car and creating the account; the abuser being granted access as a secondary user; or the perpetrator impersonating the victim-survivor and creating an account on the victim-survivor's behalf. The researchers said many car functions, including tracking and monitoring tools, remote control functions and digital key access, could be controlled remotely through an app.

Geolocation, which reveals a car's location in real time or while it is parked, was advertised by 100% of the 15 brands, with some marketing it as “Vehicle Tracking,” “Vehicle Locator” or “Find My Car.” The researchers said the tool could be misused to track people.

Sixty percent of the top 15 brands also advertised a “zone exit alert,” which allows users to set an alarm if a car leaves a designated area. The report warned the feature could be used to “Set a zone of 3km from the family home to receive alert if the victim-survivor attempts to leave or fails to follow abuser’s rules limiting travel by the victim-survivor.” Twenty percent of brands advertised speed or acceleration restrictions, with carmakers describing them as a way to set limits for young drivers or valet parkers. The report warned it could be used to set a 40km/h speed limit so that a victim-survivor could not safely use highways or escape at speed.

Digital key access, which allows drivers to unlock a car or start its engine from a mobile phone, was advertised by 67% of car brands in the Australian market. The report warned that “This function may also allow an abuser to remotely terminate or severely restrict the victim survivor’s ability to use the vehicle.” It said an abuser could achieve this, for example, by remotely deactivating the victim-survivor’s digital key access or setting speed restrictions on the vehicle. The report said this “could effectively strand the victim-survivor in an unsafe location and/or limit the victim survivor’s ability to escape the abuser’s violence.”

The report said carmakers mainly market the problematic functions as enabling an owner to “keep an eye on” and protect their car. It highlighted an advertisement on Kia's Australian website for “Kia Connect,” which said: “Kia Connect is also bundled with geofence and time fencing alerts which let the owner know when another user strays outside preset limits of geographical areas or time limitations. It’s a perfect feature for anyone dealing with a family member who is borrowing the car.” Kia was contacted for comment.

Katharine Kemp, a data privacy and consumer protection expert at UNSW and a co-author of the report, said the safeguards need to be built in from the start of product design. “There is an urgent need for carmakers to adopt safety by design principles from the early stages of designing both the apps and the cars,” Kemp said. The report recommends that carmakers consider at the design phase how a function could be misused to facilitate abuse, and decide whether a function should not be offered when the risks outweigh the benefits.

Emma Buxton-Namisnyk, also a co-author, warned that the issue is emerging and could get worse. “There’s really not a widespread understanding about how these technologies can be misused,” Buxton-Namisnyk said, adding that there was “almost no accountability” for car manufacturers to limit potential misuses.

Australia's eSafety commissioner, Julie Inman Grant, raised the alarm last year after frontline workers reported that cars were being used to monitor and track victim-survivors of domestic violence. She called for improved industry safeguards, citing an estimate that more than 90% of new cars sold in Australia by 2031 would have embedded connectivity. “We need urgent action to make them safer,” Inman Grant said.

Social services minister Tanya Plibersek said in a statement to Guardian Australia that the government was considering what more it could do about the risk posed by smart devices, after developing a support service on technology-facilitated abuse that helps frontline workers navigate the issue. “We’re seeing new frontiers of family and domestic violence emerge every day – we’ve seen smart fridges, cat feeders and electric vehicles all being weaponised to stalk, control and harm,” Plibersek said. “We’ll consider what more we can do as we finalise our Second Action Plan to End Violence Against Women and Children.”

International helplines are listed at www.befrienders.org.
