## Federal agencies retain tools used to pressure DEI programs

Some people who work on diversity, equity and inclusion say the political environment that pushed much of that work into hiding is beginning to shift after Democrats won this month's midterms. The change appears in the accounts of a diversity consultant, a civil-rights attorney and university researchers. It does not yet appear as a broad restoration: institutions that ended or quietly scaled back their programs are unlikely to move quickly, and the federal pressure behind many of those retreats remains in place.

Since President Trump returned to office, he and his Republican allies have moved aggressively to eliminate diversity, equity and inclusion programs across government and the private sector, NPR reported. Rep. Ro Khanna, a California Democrat, posted a video after the midterms declaring that DEI was back. The post matched an expectation among some advocates that newly empowered Democrats would use their control of Congress to limit some of the political pressure that chilled the work.

Lily Zheng, a consultant who helps companies develop workplace diversity programs, has watched most companies retreat from the work since last year, with some doing so quietly and others ending it altogether. She said, "It was fear of retribution. It was fear of political and regulatory pressure." Zheng said colleagues who had continued the work at a lower profile expressed relief at the prospect of no longer having to move carefully to avoid backlash. She described that reaction as based on a sense, perhaps a hope, that newly empowered Democrats would find ways to rein in Republican attacks on DEI.

Antonio Ingram, an attorney with the NAACP Legal Defense Fund, said, "I think, with this new political landscape, that chilling effect is thawing out." He said the president's Republican allies in Congress had fostered a culture of fear by summoning leaders of universities, companies and nonprofits to hostile hearings. Ingram described the purpose of those hearings: "To cross-examine them and to intimidate them from continuing to do the work they're doing to create a multiracial democracy." He said pro-civil-rights lawmakers now control those committee hearings.

Shaun Harper, who runs the Race and Equity Center at the University of Southern California, said he hopes organizations will find their way back to the field but does not expect them to change course at once. "I don't expect them to overnight flip on a switch and, you know, declare that DEI is back," he said. NPR reported that institutions are unlikely to resume diversity work quickly, much less promote it publicly.

Federal power has not changed with the midterms. President Trump still controls the federal agencies that have used threats of funding cuts and lawsuits to try to force institutions to end DEI programs. In a statement to NPR, White House spokeswoman Allison Schuster called DEI policies "racist and destructive." The White House accused President Biden of allowing those policies to run rampant and said Trump was elected with a mandate to end them.

Texas shows where state-level results could alter the climate. Liliana Garces, a professor of education at the University of Texas at Austin, has studied the effect of state restrictions on DEI programs and race-based curricula. After Republican leaders in Texas banned diversity programs at public universities, she interviewed administrators implementing the bans on their college campuses. Garces found "administrators were applying the law in very restrictive ways and prohibiting programs that weren't even covered by the law," a pattern she linked to uncertainty about the consequences.

Garces expects the political pressure to ease. "That pressure to remove certain readings from your classroom or self-censor is just not going to be as strong," she said. Texans elected a Democratic governor, a Democratic attorney general and a Democratic majority in the State House of Representatives this month, and Governor-elect Gina Hinojosa has said she will not engage in culture wars.

The interviews describe an easing rather than a reversal. The midterm results change who controls congressional committee hearings and several state offices, and people who work in the field said they can feel the difference. They also said institutions that withdrew from DEI work are unlikely to return immediately, while President Trump retains the agencies that used funding-cut and lawsuit threats to press institutions to end their programs.

The relief Zheng described among some of her colleagues and Ingram's expectation both rest on a sense, perhaps just a hope, that newly empowered Democrats would use their control of Congress to rein in the pressure that chilled DEI work. Harper's caution points to a slower recovery in which organizations return to the field quietly rather than announce it. The direction of change after the midterms is visible in those accounts; its pace is not.
