## Worker lawsuit alleges inadequate excavation support at Brooklyn collapse site

**Subtype:** fact

- A lawsuit filed by five injured construction workers alleges that excavation at 2625 Bedford Ave. lacked adequate retaining walls, shoring and underpinning.
- The Buildings Department said it reviewed the excavation and support plans and found they complied with city requirements.

- Tenant Dengane Omari Jack said he was rebuffed when he warned the landlord about cracks near the building’s entrance.
- The city public advocate listed Olufemi Falade, owner of the collapsed building, on its “100 worst landlords” roster six times in the last decade.

## Buildings Department says excavation plans complied with city requirements

The Buildings Department said in a Friday email that it had reviewed the excavation and support plans and found they complied with city requirements. For the question of what was actually installed, the department pointed to city rules requiring private inspectors, hired by developers, to check some stages of construction. It did not answer whether it responded to a preconstruction survey that found the adjoining wall of the now-collapsed building “in poor condition with noticeable cracks.”

Buildings Commissioner Ahmed Tigani vowed to do “everything we can to understand exactly how this collapse occurred, so that the responsible parties can be held to account.” His department is hiring an independent engineering consultant, and Brooklyn prosecutors also are investigating.

A lawsuit filed by five injured construction workers alleges that the dig at 2625 Bedford Ave. lacked adequate retaining walls, shoring and underpinning to keep the adjoining building safe. The suit also says excavation continued after the facade at 1321 Flatbush Ave. began to crack. Lawyer Jonathan Damashek, who sued for the workers, said, “The shoring that they were planning to keep the collapsed building up was severely insufficient.” He questioned whether the city should have approved the plans and whether contractors’ work was negligent. Damashek’s lawsuits accuse defendants, including construction manager Townhouse Homes, of failing “to consider public safety above their own concern of keeping down costs.”

Lawsuits filed since Nov. 11 allege fault by the construction-site owner and contractors, the engineering firm that drew up the excavation plan and the owner of the collapsed building. Several injured workers have also taken steps toward suing the Buildings Department, the city agency that polices construction safety. The central question for investigators is whether work to dig a deep pit for a new building undermined the foundation of the three-story building at 1321 Flatbush Ave. and caused it to topple into the jobsite.

The three-story brick structure at 1321 Flatbush Ave. was erected nearly a century ago and had over 90 unresolved citations for exposed wiring, roof leaks, faulty doors and other alleged housing code violations. Tenant Dengane Omari Jack said in a lawsuit that conditions worsened once construction ramped up next door. Jack said he was rebuffed when he tried warning the landlord about cracks near the building’s entrance. After Jack provided photos of the damage, he was told it was not the landlord’s fault and could not be fixed, the lawsuit said. Jack also lost his extensive record collection and other personal effects.

The Nov. 11 collapse killed eight people, including two children, and families were mourning the dead. City officials said Wednesday that another building adjoining the one that crumbled will have to be demolished after the collapse peeled off an outer wall.

While the investigation proceeds, New York City is checking over 160 construction sites tied to companies involved in the project at 2625 Bedford Ave., a planned apartment and commercial building. Excavation work at the site started, with city permission, in September.

During the Bedford Avenue project’s demolition phase, N Tech Design of America started demolishing an existing structure in March without enough netting and other protections from flying debris. The company was ordered to stop but continued, was caught again in April, admitted both violations and paid a total of $12,500 in fines, according to city records. N Tech representative Aramis Brown told The City Reporter that the company finished its work at the site in May and had nothing to do with the excavation or the collapse.

Townhouse Homes, which also operates as Promont, has worked in New York’s construction industry for more than three decades. In 2016, lawsuits accused the company of damaging an adjacent building during a Manhattan renovation project; Townhouse denied that allegation. The lawsuits also accused it of compromising the structural integrity of adjacent structures while it performed excavation, pile driving and steel beam installation for a Brooklyn hotel. The company’s lawyers have not addressed the hotel allegations. Principals in Townhouse, other firms named in the report and the property owners did not respond to Associated Press requests for comment.

The Bedford Avenue project’s excavation engineer, Jieming Wang, has also faced lawsuits. According to court records, at least a dozen lawsuits have blamed excavation plans designed by his firm, New Jersey-based LMW Engineering Group, for damage at adjacent properties. A 2015 lawsuit alleged that LMW’s work on a Manhattan excavation site caused sloping floors, damaged doors and windows and destroyed utility pipes at an adjacent building. Another lawsuit in 2016 said soil removal undermined the wall of a building next to a Queens excavation project.

A 2020 lawsuit accused LMW of skipping required testing and monitoring at another Queens project. It said a neighboring building’s foundation was left unstable, its gas and hot water systems were damaged and an architectural arch partially separated from the structure. LMW, which has worked on bridges, airports and other projects since its 1998 founding, denied the allegations in the 2015 and 2016 cases and did not respond to the 2020 lawsuit. Other litigation was resolved out of court.

The owner of the collapsed building, Olufemi Falade, has held a slate of Brooklyn buildings under various corporate names and has been listed on the city public advocate’s “100 worst landlords” roster six times in the last decade. Falade, a licensed professional engineer, was jailed for 12 days in 2006 for failing to fix violations in a dozen buildings. Then-Housing Commissioner Shaun Donovan called him “one of the most egregious examples we’ve seen citywide of flouting the law and forcing his residents to live in horrible conditions.”

After his release, Falade told the Village Voice that tenants were not paying rent and said, “People want something for nothing.” Falade has not responded to Associated Press requests for comment since the collapse. At a vigil near the collapse site on Wednesday, housing activists, faith leaders and others called for Falade and the developers to be held accountable. Judith Douglas, a tenant advocate who lives in Flatbush, said, “New housing cannot come at the expense of tenants’ lives.”
