## Voyager’s likely 2030s power loss comes amid mounting damage to Earth

Kim Heacox's commentary in The Guardian uses NASA's Voyager 1 milestone to contrast human engineering with humanity's impact on Earth. On November 18, 2026, the robotic probe was exactly one light-day from Earth, 16.1 billion miles away, after 49 years in flight. No other human-made object had gone farther. Heacox framed the mission as a nonstop journey powered by curiosity and engineering: the probe is the size of a Mini Cooper and travels at 37,800 miles per hour through a vast sea of cold, soundless space, free from friction, while radiation and cosmic rays bombard it and it remains able to communicate with Earth.

Radio signals from Earth travel at 186,000 miles per second and take 24 hours to reach Voyager 1. The probe's faint return signal takes another 24 hours to reach Earth, and scientists at NASA's Deep Space Network must use a massive satellite dish to hear it.

Heacox placed the launch in early September 1977, just weeks after the first *Star Wars* movie reached theaters with Luke Skywalker and R2D2. Voyager 1 photographed Jupiter and Saturn before continuing outward. In 2012, it exited the sun's protective shield of charged particles and magnetic fields, crossed the heliopause and entered interstellar space. Heacox described that moment as turning the spacecraft into a message in a bottle.

Voyager 1's message consists of a 12-inch, gold-plated copper disk engraved with information meant to showcase human culture and the diversity of life on Earth if an advanced civilization discovers it. The disk contains photographs and greetings in 55 human languages, music by Mozart and Chuck Berry, the sound of a baby crying and a whale singing. It also has a timestamp and a map that orients the position of the solar system to 14 pulsars. Heacox described the pulsars as dense remnants of massive stars that rotate rapidly and emit beams of radiation. He said the disk says, in essence: “This is where we live and who we are. We thrive on connection. Come see us sometime.”

Carl Sagan chaired the committee that designed the disk. In the late 1980s, as Voyager 1 hastened through the outer solar system, Sagan tried to convince NASA to turn the probe's sensitive cameras back toward home, but the agency resisted because of concern about damage to the instruments. Finally, on Valentine's Day 1990, the cameras turned. Earth appeared as a fraction of a pixel in the resulting Pale Blue Dot image, which Sagan called “like a mote of dust caught in a sunbeam.” Heacox wrote that the moment began to probe humanity from a distance, forcing people to take a close look at themselves.

“From this vantage point,” Sagan said, “the Earth might not seem of any particular interest. But for us, it’s home … On it everyone you love, everyone you know, everyone you ever heard of, every human being who ever was, lived out their lives.”

The commentary included a NASA and JPL-Caltech composite of the Pale Blue Dot. One photograph showed hundreds of visitors watching images of Saturn being relayed from Voyager 1 at the Smithsonian Institution's National Air and Space Museum in Washington DC on 13 November 1980. Another showed Carl Sagan filming *Cosmos*, while a third showed the Voyager control center at Jet Propulsion Laboratory at the California Institute of Technology in Pasadena, California, in 1980. The images were credited to Len Hawley of Consolidated News Pictures via Getty Images, Science History Images/Alamy and NASA/Getty Images. The Guardian identified Heacox as the author of many books, including *The Only Kayak* and *Jimmy Bluefeather*, both winners of the National Outdoor Book Award.

Heacox placed the spacecraft against a longer account of life on Earth. He wrote that, on a small planet and against long odds, molecules composed themselves into organic matter and evolved over billions of years into complex living organisms. Geologic epochs came and went, continents moved, and species thrived and perished. He described humans as a “remarkable bi-ped” that had only recently begun standing upright and dominating other species, and that was capable of love, compassion and war, making music, felling forests, creating literature, burning oil, building probes and exploring space.

In the commentary, Heacox wrote that scientists say humans have “gravely altered” the chemistry, biology and physical structure of Earth's land and water, with much of that damage occurring since Voyager 1 left. He added: “We (or some among us) have destroyed countless habitats and damaged ecosystems to where wildlife populations are now collapsing worldwide.” He also wrote: “Building a global economy on fossil fuels has so warmed and toxified our planet that few places – if any – are pristine anymore.”

Against that environmental record, Heacox placed author Kurt Vonnegut's warning. Shortly before his death in 2007, Vonnegut said: “Dear Future Generations. Please accept our apologies. We were rolling drunk on petroleum.”

Heacox wrote that it was not always this way. Long before people stared at computer screens and cell phones, they sat around campfires, studied the night sky, found and named patterns among the stars and told stories. Much later, people invented science and turned their rational minds toward a better understanding of themselves and their surroundings. Science, he wrote, tells people that they are made of star stuff and that every element in their bodies was forged in the fiery furnaces of stars. “When we look out into space,” said astronomy professor Brian Cox, “we are looking into our own origins, because we are truly children of the stars.”

Heacox also described what astronauts see after orbiting Earth for months at a time. They see no flags, national boundaries or competing interests, he wrote, but a single home shared by billions: a blue world wreathed in clouds and surrounded by cold, sterile darkness. Astronaut Ron Garan concluded that if humanity intends to flourish far into the future, it must shift its priorities from economy-first to planet-first, followed by society and then the economy. Without clean air, fresh water, stable ecosystems and a suitable climate, Garan said, society and the economy mean nothing and collapse.

Heacox wrote that much of the damage had been caused by ignorance and greed, that global warming was accelerating and that hard challenges lay ahead. He argued that the crisis and other threats could be mitigated if people set their hearts and minds to them, learned to live together and strove to belong rather than dominate. If humanity got things right and survived its technological adolescence, he wrote, thousands of future generations could enjoy a still beautiful, bountiful planet with suitable temperatures and robust, though rapidly diminishing, biodiversity.

Voyager 1's engineering clock is also running down. Heacox said the probe would probably lose all power and go silent in the 2030s. He said it would continue by inertia, not toward Proxima Centauri, the nearest star to us after the sun, but toward the constellation Ophiuchus. In roughly 17,600 years, Voyager 1 would be one light-year from Earth.

Heacox concluded with Sagan's words: “It has been said that astronomy is a humbling and character-building experience. There is perhaps no better demonstration of the folly of human conceits than this distant image of our tiny world.” “To me, it underscores our responsibility to deal more kindly with one another, and to preserve and cherish the pale blue dot, the only home we’ve ever known.”
