## Long-term funding awaits next year's spending review

John Healey is seeking ways to reduce the cost of a support package for unemployed people under 25 proposed in Alan Milburn’s review, according to Whitehall sources who told The Guardian. Whitehall sources said the chancellor was not happy with the level of spending required under the proposals and wanted the system to break even at an earlier date, as the government weighs how to finance it from existing budgets while bringing down the wider welfare bill. The options remain under consideration, and no final spending or benefit package has been announced.

The government has promised to back Milburn’s overall vision for an entirely new welfare system for people under 25. It has also promised spending cuts next year to help finance additional assistance for people out of work to enter education or employment. The cost of those commitments is the subject of the Treasury’s concerns.

One minister said, “Obviously there is trepidation about the upfront costs.” A second said Healey intended to find ways to reduce the cost of delivering the package, citing the political need for the overall welfare bill to start falling faster. Because the multibillion-pound package would have to be funded from existing budgets, ministers have privately admitted that it may be unaffordable.

At least two options are under cross-government consideration. One would initially provide less generous upfront support than Milburn’s report suggested. The other would use planned changes for people over 25 to cut disability payments for a wide group, with as many as one in six at risk of losing them altogether. People over 25 are also likely to be given a less generous offer of support and training than younger people.

Under Milburn’s plan, the government would spend £5.8bn a year by the end of the decade on getting young people into work. Nearly £2bn of that would provide advice for entry into training, education or work; another £2bn would be spent on creating more vocational training courses and apprenticeships; and £1bn would go to companies as incentives to hire young workers.

Spending would increase over time, beginning with £500m next year, which Milburn has promised would come from existing budgets. The Department for Work and Pensions, Department of Health and Social Care and Department for Education are examining how to provide those funds. Longer-term spending is not guaranteed and will be decided in next year’s spending review.

Officials are drawing up plans to redesign the personal independence payment (Pip) system for older adults in England and Wales after work and pensions minister Stephen Timms deemed it “no longer fit for purpose”. While ministers are keen to extend the Milburn principle that disability benefits should be focused on getting people into work, they are understood to have accepted the central proposal in the Timms report not to scrap Pip altogether. Instead, ministers are designing a new assessment system for 90% of claimants over 25, which would involve some people receiving more, some people receiving less and many losing their benefits altogether.

Timms recommended that people who are not deemed to face extra costs because of a disability should not receive Pip. The Resolution Foundation calculated that this could affect as many as one in six claimants, or more than 600,000 people over 25. Officials are working out how the new assessments should operate and whether they should apply to existing claimants. They are also drawing up transitional arrangements for people coming off Pip after turning 25 and existing claimants affected by the changes.

Government sources said Timms’s relatively non-specific recommendations leave room for systems that would save very different amounts. Downing Street officials said Prime Minister Andy Burnham wants to begin reducing the welfare bill as soon as possible. Burnham has said reducing the cost of working-age benefits is his plan, but he has pushed back against what he called “crude cuts”, saying they often create costs elsewhere.

The Conservatives have pressed Burnham to reduce the overall cost of working-age benefits. Until the outstanding choices are made, the government has not settled how Milburn’s youth proposal would be funded or how far Pip assessments would change for people over 25.
