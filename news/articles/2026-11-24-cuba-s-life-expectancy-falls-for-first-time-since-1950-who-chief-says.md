## Cuba's life expectancy falls for first time since 1950, WHO chief says

**Subtype:** fact

- WHO chief Tedros Adhanom Ghebreyesus said Cuba's life expectancy fell from 78.8 years in 2020 to 74.4 years in 2025.
- Tedros said the 2025 figure marked Cuba's first decline since records began in 1950.
- Tedros said Cuba's infant mortality rate more than doubled, rising from approximately four deaths per 1,000 live births in 2020 to 9.9 in 2025.
- Tedros said Cuba's maternal mortality rate increased from 37.4 deaths per 1,000 live births in 2019 to 44.1 in 2025.
- Tedros said Cuba's childhood cancer survival rate fell from 85% to 65% since January.
- Tedros announced $2 million in aid for childhood vaccination programs and essential medical equipment.

## 2025 health figures predate the U.S. fuel blockade imposed in January

WHO chief Tedros Adhanom Ghebreyesus said during a visit to Havana on Tuesday that Cuba's life expectancy fell from 78.8 years in 2020 to 74.4 years in 2025. Tedros described the 2025 result as the country's first decline since records began in 1950, as the island reeled from a severe economic crisis.

The 2025 figures predate the U.S. fuel blockade imposed in January and therefore do not measure the blockade's effects. The Guardian reported that the blockade builds on a six-decade U.S. trade embargo and sharply accelerated the worst crisis in Cuba in decades, leaving state services on the brink of collapse.

At a press conference, Tedros described Cuba's health system as being in “critical” condition. He said the infant mortality rate had more than doubled, from approximately four deaths per 1,000 live births in 2020 to 9.9 in 2025.

Maternal mortality also worsened. Tedros said the rate rose from 37.4 deaths per 1,000 live births in 2019 to 44.1 in 2025.

Childhood cancer survival fell from 85% to 65% since January, Tedros said. The decline came after Washington threatened sanctions on any country supplying fuel to Cuba.

Tedros announced $2 million in aid for childhood vaccination programs and the purchase of essential medical equipment.

Tedros linked the health situation to the “sanctions” but also referred to “internal problems.” He said it was “positive” that Cuba was addressing them. Cuba had announced wide-ranging market-led economic reforms, and Tedros said the WHO supported those changes.

“The reform the government is proposing concerns the economy, the market, health, almost every sector. And we support it,” Tedros said.
