## Distortion could undercut projections of China surpassing United States

Yi Fuxian, a University of Wisconsin-Madison demographer and longtime critic of China's population policies, argues in a new monograph that China's actual population may be closer to 1.27 billion rather than the 1.4 billion officially reported — a gap of as many as 130 million people, roughly the population of Mexico, The Wall Street Journal's chief China correspondent Lingling Wei reported in the publication's weekly China dispatch.

Yi builds his case from health-system and education records. Every newborn in China is required to receive a BCG tuberculosis vaccine within 24 hours of birth. Because multi-dose vials must be discarded 30 minutes after opening, each vial yields only about 1.2 to 1.6 usable doses in practice. Between 2018 and 2020, the government approved roughly 5.8 million vials a year, Yi noted, while official statistics claimed more than 14 million births annually. That would mean each vial vaccinated about 2.4 babies, which Yi said would be medically and practically impossible nationwide.

Yi points to additional discrepancies in the reported size of individual birth cohorts. The National Bureau of Statistics reported that 17.7 million babies were born in 2000. When that cohort reached age 10 in the 2010 census, only about 14.4 million were counted. By the time those children graduated middle school in 2015, only about 14.2 million walked across the stage. About 3.5 million people from a single year were unaccounted for in the official record.

Yi said he cross-checks census records against school enrollments, hospital births, vaccine batches, and household registrations. The picture that emerges, in his analysis, is a pattern of systematic inflation across multiple data sources.

In explaining the gap, Yi describes what he calls a contamination cycle. Census figures were adjusted upward using education and household registration data that had themselves been exaggerated, Yi said. Schools padded enrollment by 20% to 50% once funding shifted from local governments to the central government. Hospital birth records swelled after rural insurance began covering deliveries.

With Beijing now tying birthrates to local officials' performance reviews and rolling out pro-natal subsidies, local governments have every incentive to overstate the numbers — both to win political credit and to secure subsidy funds, Yi said. "Raising the fertility rate is hard," he said. "Revising the statistics is easy."

What makes Yi's forecasts noteworthy, Wei reported, is that they have tracked closer to reality over the past two decades than those produced by China's establishment demographers. China's fertility rate has now fallen below 1.0, meaning the average woman in the country has fewer than one child. Household consumption is stuck at 39% of GDP, well below the 60% global norm.

For the past few years, Xi Jinping has been beating the drum that "the East is rising and the West is in decline," a judgment rooted in the conviction that China's vast population would deliver more workers, more consumers, and an economy that would inevitably overtake America's. Yi's work suggests the demographic headwinds may be even stronger than official figures indicate.

Yi traces a direct line from overstated population figures to China's geopolitical posture. Some state-linked economists, working from population and growth numbers that Yi considers inflated, projected that China's economy would grow to two or three times the size of America's. Those projections helped bolster confidence in Beijing and may have reinforced Xi's belief that China would eventually surpass the United States, Yi said.

On the American side, Yi argued, the same inflated projections fueled warnings that China's overtaking was inevitable and shaped a Washington consensus built partly on what he calls a statistical mirage.

The economic consequences of China's demographic decline are already visible, Wei wrote in a prior edition, with China's growth model cracking under weak demand and mounting debt. The consumption-led rebalancing that Beijing says it wants may not just be a matter of will — if Yi is right about the numbers, a smaller population than officially reported would mean a smaller consumer base than policymakers assume to power the shift. Beijing's governing philosophy says the economic base determines everything above it, Wei wrote. The trouble is what's eroding below.
