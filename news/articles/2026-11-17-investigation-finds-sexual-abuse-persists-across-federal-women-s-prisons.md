## More than 185 cases implicate more than 96 officers at 16 prisons

An investigation by ProPublica and The Marshall Project found reported sexual assault, misconduct or harassment at most of the 27 federal facilities that currently hold women, with more than 185 cases involving Bureau of Prisons employees since 2021. The investigation implicated more than 96 officers at 16 prisons; only 19 had been arrested or convicted. The findings come as a federal consent decree for Dublin nears expiration, Congress has not funded the oversight office created after the scandal, and the Bureau plans to stop housing women at Aliceville.

Reporters searched federal court records and civil lawsuits and interviewed current and formerly incarcerated survivors, prison officials and civil-rights attorneys. They counted only allegations that met the Prison Rape Elimination Act’s definition of sexual abuse and involved a Bureau employee at a women’s facility. The report said it identified more than 185 cases since 2021; its methodology lists 172 involving women and 15 involving transgender men. The report credits Beth Schwartzapfel and Keri Blakinger, with Pratheek Rebala of ProPublica contributing reporting, Gabriel Sandoval contributing research and Sarah Almukhtar handling design and development.

Allegations came from tort claims, lawsuits, criminal filings, internal agency records or survivor accounts corroborated with contemporaneous witness statements, journal entries, letters, sworn statements and other documentation. In some cases, court records alleged that officers withheld food or medical care, or threatened to put women in solitary confinement if they refused to comply with sexual demands. In others, officers locked them in windowless closets or bathrooms, choked them, or forced them to use sex toys or have sex with each other. In more than a dozen cases, women were left bruised and bleeding. Some allegations resulted in arrests and convictions, while others remained pending or unresolved.

Though some abusers have been prosecuted or removed, insiders say the culture of impunity that allows officers to prey on women remains. The investigation found that officials repeatedly refused to acknowledge or failed to act on signs of sexual abuse. More than 25 women said they were abused by officers who had been reported or investigated before, according to lawsuits the reporters reviewed. In some cases, officers covered for abusive colleagues, court records allege. Staff also said they, like prisoners, sometimes hesitated to report misconduct for fear of retaliation.

At the Federal Correctional Institution Dublin, a low-security prison in California, Ana served 15 years while officers preyed on the women in their care. Sexual misconduct became so rampant that staff and prisoners alike called it a “rape club.” After the FBI intervened, prosecutors charged the warden, the chaplain and nine other officers with sexually abusing female prisoners. Hundreds of women, including Ana, came forward not only to investigators but also to outside lawyers who sued the system for changes at the Bay Area prison.

The arrests and subsequent convictions of Dublin staff — 10 in all — marked the largest sex-abuse scandal in the Bureau’s history. Ten officers ultimately pleaded guilty or were convicted at trial, and one went free after two hung juries. A federal judge appointed an outside monitor, multiple agencies issued reports, the Justice Department dispatched attorneys to women’s facilities, and Congress passed the Federal Prison Oversight Act to establish an independent office. In early 2024, the Bureau announced it would close Dublin and transfer more than 600 women to facilities across the country.

More than a dozen current and former federal prison employees told the reporters they had seen few or no meaningful improvements. One Justice Department official said the only obvious sign of progress was new sexual-assault prevention posters. “Nothing has really changed,” said Alix McLearen, a top Bureau official who retired in 2024 after serving as the agency’s first administrator overseeing the women and special populations branch. “We had a horrible crisis, and crisis leads to opportunity, and this is an opportunity that has been completely squandered.”

A federal prison workers union official, who asked not to be named because of fear of retaliation, was more direct: “Nothing has been done to effectively change sexual abuse of inmates within the BOP. Not on a large scale or small scale.” Congress never appropriated funding for the new oversight office.

Ana said she watched the Dublin officer who brought in burritos get walked off the job by investigators. According to an Associated Press investigation and several agency sources, that officer was one of about a dozen Dublin workers in the early 2010s who were quietly removed from their posts but not prosecuted over sex-abuse allegations.

In September 2021, the FBI arrested Dublin Warden Ray Garcia. He was charged with sexually abusing multiple women across the compound and lying to cover it up. In one instance, prosecutors said, Garcia abused a woman while Prison Rape Elimination Act auditors were visiting. Garcia was convicted and sentenced to nearly six years in prison, though his attorney, Kevin Little, said Garcia maintained that he was not guilty. Members of Congress began asking questions, and Bureau Director Michael Carvajal announced that he would step down, saying in an interview that the move was driven by concerns Congress would not support the bureau until he left.

John Bellhouse, a safety supervisor convicted of sexually abusing two women, was serving a 63-month sentence in a federal prison in Louisiana. Bellhouse insisted he was innocent and said lower-ranking staff were “too comfortable” at the facility and too familiar with the prisoners. “It was very junior high school,” he wrote.

Looking back to her first months at Dublin around 2010, Ana recalled seeing women eat Mexican takeout almost every day. At 25, she was beginning a lengthy sentence stemming from her involvement in her husband’s drug-trafficking operation. Ana suspected the smuggled meals reflected an exchange with officers but did not ask questions because she was afraid and the women shared the coveted food. “So I would just eat it and shut up,” she said.

Ana hoped to leave Dublin behind when she was transferred to Aliceville in rural Alabama. The facility had newer buildings but fewer freedoms: roofs leaked, the air sometimes smelled faintly of mold, and shorthanded staff often locked women in their cells for days at a time. Thousands of miles from families they could not call during another lockdown, Ana and Ela became allies. They paced, picked at lukewarm meals and stared through the narrow slit of a window.

Ela was a soft-spoken immigrant from Mexico with children in Utah who had been in prison for only a few years after FBI agents arrested her in a meth bust and she received a seven-year sentence. Her cellmate, Ana, 40, watched Officer Felix Wilder, 35, pay close attention to Ela. Wilder often arrived in gold chains and stylish jeans, and one woman said he moved “like he had just gotten off of a horse from a long ride.” He spoke slowly and watched women cross the prison’s drab buildings and shadeless yards. Ana eventually gave Ela the phone numbers of the lead attorneys in the Dublin class action.

Ela’s civil claim says an officer pulled her into a closet in early 2022, pushed her to her knees, forced her to perform oral sex and then vaginally raped her. Prison officials yelled at her after she reported the incident and fired her from a job in the prison’s safety department, according to the claim. The claim says she began cutting herself and later left a psychiatry appointment feeling worse after the psychiatrist mocked her.

About a year later, Wilder began pursuing Ela, according to her claim. Her claim says he first flirted with her, talked about his own romantic encounters and asked about her family in Utah. In spring 2023, she said he forced her to perform oral sex in a utility closet. A few months later, she said he locked her in the same closet for two hours and returned three times to rape her. Other women had reported similar behavior, but his abuse continued until FBI investigators arrived at the facility in the fall of 2024, court filings and interviews showed. When investigators questioned Ela about her earlier abuser, who had pleaded guilty to assaulting two other women, she was initially reluctant to disclose Wilder’s conduct. She said in emails in Spanish to ProPublica and The Marshall Project: “I was very scared to speak out.” After a second visit, she told investigators everything: “I would not want the chain of victims to continue,” she said. “We had to put a stop to it.” Wilder was walked off the prison property that day. Officers then placed Ela in solitary confinement for nearly two weeks, saying it was for her safety. She described the confinement as a nightmare.

Federal prosecutors charged Wilder with sexually abusing seven women. He has pleaded not guilty and is awaiting trial. Court records and interviews also described Wilder as abusing at least 17 other women, many of whom faced deportation after completing their sentences. Eleven women, including Ela, have also accused Wilder in civil claims, including two lawsuits that have been paused until the criminal case is resolved. Apart from his not-guilty plea, Wilder has not responded in court or to repeated requests for comment. One woman in a phone interview from Fort Worth said: “I’m pretty sure everybody knew what was going on and they just didn’t care.”

As Ana and the other Dublin women, then scattered to other facilities, realized no one was going to stop the abuse, they began encouraging other women across the country to speak out and telling them what they could do about it. The volume of allegations grew rapidly, prompting the San Francisco-based legal team handling the Dublin case to refer scores of potential clients to other attorneys. Some lawyers took on so many federal prison sex-abuse cases that the work became their law practice’s primary focus, and the reporters continued receiving new lawsuits, tort claims and allegations through publication.

That October, lawyers Kara Janssen and Susan Beaty made their first visit to Aliceville. They had come from a legal conference in Atlanta, where they had given a presentation on their work at Dublin, and rented cars to drive four hours to a hotel in Tuscaloosa and another hour toward the prison, which rose from a forest as they neared the Mississippi border. Prison staff seemed unaccustomed to legal visitors and were unsure how to sign them in. “It felt like nobody had come there in a long time,” Janssen said.

Over three days, Janssen and Beaty took statements from more than 70 women, most of them transfers from Dublin. Some described the difficulty of the move or getting medical care, while others reported new abuse at Aliceville, including medical staff forcing women to dance, officers assaulting them in closets and other staff pretending not to see. The women were skittish and rattled by the abuse, retaliation and prodding FBI agents. “A lot of people were really scared,” Janssen said. “It felt just like another Dublin.” She broke down crying after leaving.

In the following months, the lawyers traveled to prisons in California, Connecticut, Texas, Florida and Wisconsin; women described abuse at almost every stop. “There has been so much reporting on Dublin as an aberration,” said Beaty, one of the lawyers leading the class action. “But I think it’s actually incredibly representative. I think it’s happening at basically every BOP women’s prison.”

Under federal law, all sexual contact between prison staff and people in custody is a felony. Prisoners cannot meaningfully give consent when the staff propositioning them control their food, medical care, discipline and access to solitary confinement. The investigation said prison sexual abuse often relies on subtle coercion and grooming. Poor conditions and scarcity behind bars turn necessities into commodities that officers can leverage to create the illusion of a “relationship,” with sexual favors exchanged for privileges and small gifts such as contraband makeup, extra tampons or a few more minutes at visitation.

One high-ranking prison official explained: “The women are getting small trinkets. It’s not big things that we can trace. He’s bringing in a hamburger, a Subway sandwich. It’s heartbreaking to look at the value of the things that are being passed.”

Ana’s Dublin lawsuit described an officer who unlocked doors for her, called her into his office, groomed her through personal attention and eventually kissed and raped her. Ana had previously helped another prisoner file a misconduct complaint. The officer once told Ana he had had a sexual dream about her. Afterward, Ana said, both women were sent to solitary confinement. For two weeks, Ana spent 23 hours a day in a cell barely bigger than an elevator, received cold food and was denied phone calls and visitors.

Prison officials often isolate people who report abuse, ostensibly for their protection, even though the Justice Department has found that the practice has a “chilling effect” that discourages reporting. A federal prison spokesperson said agency policy does not require such confinement but that it can be the only adequate option.

Staff members also fear retaliation. Although Bureau policy requires employees to report misconduct, officers told the reporters and courts that they hesitate to do so. A former correctional officer at the women’s prison camp in Bryan, Texas, said reporting several coworkers in 2024 for sexual contact with incarcerated women led to her resignation after the warden moved to fire her for unprofessional conduct. “You see all the sexual abuse, how they treat inmates. If you bring that to their attention, it’s like you’re doing the wrong thing,” she said.

As of 2022, fewer than 10% of allegations against Bureau employees had been substantiated, meaning investigators found it more likely than not that abuse occurred. In some instances, investigators decided there was evidence the abuse did not occur. More than 75% of the time, investigators said there was not enough evidence to determine whether abuse happened. Internal disciplinary cases can take years, and officers can avoid punishment by retiring or resigning. Bureau insiders said criminal investigations often stop after a resignation. One former prison official said: “Staff would know that if they were having sex with inmates, the worst that would happen is that you would have to find a new job.”

The 2003 Prison Rape Elimination Act mandates employee training and regular audits but lacks a serious enforcement mechanism, and auditors generally focus on policy compliance rather than signs of abuse. “A facility may have widespread sexual abuse, but they’re still meeting compliance on the things they’re required to do,” said Kendra Prisk, a PREA auditor who has evaluated several federal prisons, including Aliceville.

Abuse continued even as facilities kept passing federally mandated sexual-assault-prevention audits. The Texas medical facility had a flawless audit in the same year the FBI arrived to investigate paramedic Preston Emert, whom at least 18 women have accused of sexual assault, records show. Aliceville received perfect marks during the same time period that Wilder was allegedly preying on Ela and other women.

In the wake of the Dublin indictments, prison officials were dispatched across the country beginning in 2022 to inspect other women’s prisons and generate internal reports. That spring, at the low-security prison in Tallahassee, Florida, most of the women surveyed for the “Women’s Institution Cultural Assessment” said they were afraid of reprisals if they spoke freely, according to a copy reviewed by ProPublica and The Marshall Project. The last time officials visited, the women said, officers punished them with “shakedowns” — in which officers ransack cells and destroy belongings — and told them it was in retaliation for talking to the visitors. Despite noting this and “several concerning observations,” the Bureau found that sexual safety was “not determined to be a problem” there.

At least 10 prisoners later filed legal claims saying Tallahassee officers abused them in the same year as the assessment. Six said they were sexually assaulted by a single officer, Lenton Hatten. Hatten had previously been reprimanded at another facility for putting money in women’s commissary accounts, “which was a warning sign of inappropriate relations,” according to multiple lawsuits. He was ultimately sentenced to three months in prison for repeatedly abusing one woman after DNA tied him to the crime.

In legal filings, attorneys for the prison system admitted that Hatten sexually abused that woman but denied that he assaulted any of the others. They also denied that officials had failed to train or supervise Hatten properly. Neither Hatten nor his attorney responded to requests for comment.

At Carswell, multiple prisoners told officials during the summer of 2022 that staff had held town hall meetings before a site visit and threatened repercussions if anyone reported concerns. The assessment team nevertheless concluded that women overwhelmingly felt the facility was a “physically and sexually safe environment.” That year, at least seven women alleged that four Carswell employees sexually abused them in civil lawsuits.

One of those women said in her lawsuit that she was partially paralyzed and required a wheelchair. She alleged that an officer who attended her Bible study group violently sodomized her in the shower. When he heard people coming, she said he dropped her on the floor, leaving her alone to crawl back to her wheelchair, crying and bloodied. Months later, when she reported the assault, the nurse on duty told her she had reported the same officer for sexually harassing other prisoners and that nothing had been done about it.

At the Federal Medical Center Carswell in North Texas, court filings allege that an officer forced two women to have a threesome with him in his office, where he had rigged a doorbell to alert him if anyone came by. A civil lawsuit has been stayed pending a criminal investigation.

All the suits are still pending. Among the staffers named in them, only Hatten has been prosecuted. In many cases, it is unclear whether the Bureau disciplined the other employees. The prison agency would not answer questions about the assessments’ findings, though a spokesperson said it has continued to visit prisons and conduct assessments that it does not release to the public.

Later in 2022, a highly publicized Senate subcommittee investigation found that prison sexual assault was more widespread than previously understood. Around the same time, the Justice Department’s inspector general issued a report criticizing the Bureau’s handling of staff-misconduct investigations.

The Justice Department also formed a working group that recommended prison officials “enhance prevention of sexual misconduct” by strengthening leadership at women’s facilities, improving staff training and installing more cameras. In mid-2023, the department dispatched its own teams to survey women’s prisons and make sure facilities were carrying out the working group’s recommendations.

Nikhil Ramnaney, a former Justice Department attorney who participated in a visit to the SeaTac lockup in Washington state, said the department notified prison officials in advance, giving them time to prepare. “BOP was putting on a show,” he said. Facility leaders stood nearby while the visitors interviewed women, who gave “positive and lukewarm answers” about whether they felt safe reporting abuse. “In hindsight, what else were they going to say?” Another Justice Department attorney visited Carswell, and both attorneys said the department never released the site-visit findings. Neither could identify meaningful changes the agency had made.

Since then, Bureau of Prisons and Justice Department officials have repeatedly said the agency is working to implement the working group’s recommendations. When asked detailed questions about the status of specific recommendations, the Justice Department deferred to the prison agency. Prisons spokesperson Randilee Giamusso said information was “not readily available.” She added: “We have more work to do, and we are committed to doing that work.”

Abuse allegations extend beyond Dublin survivors. In interviews and legal filings, ProPublica and The Marshall Project uncovered accusations of sexual abuse at a prison camp in California, a low-security prison in Wisconsin and a West Virginia prison camp where an officer had been investigated repeatedly for suspected abuse but remained on duty. A lawsuit filed by one woman last year alleges that the officer raped her so forcefully that he tore her vaginal lining. He has been charged with sexually assaulting that woman and two others, has pleaded not guilty and faces trial early next year.

By the end of 2023, women and staff previously interviewed by The Marshall Project and NBC News described officers at the Bryan, Texas, camp groping women and pressuring them into unwanted sex acts. At the federal jail in Chicago, an indictment alleged that an officer groped and sexually assaulted at least four women in December alone. He pleaded not guilty, and the case is ongoing.

Abuse continued at Dublin even while the prison was under the scrutiny of the courts and Congress following Garcia’s arrest. In addition to the hundreds who said they were abused before 2021, at least 47 prisoners alleged abuse by staff after that year. Many of those allegations had not been previously reported, and many of the alleged abusers had not been prosecuted. The abuse continued after the agency replaced Garcia and after a legal team filed a class action in summer 2023 on behalf of Ana and hundreds of other Dublin women. The legal team included attorneys from Rights Behind Bars, Rosen Bien Galvan & Grunfeld and the California Collaborative for Immigrant Justice. The plaintiffs alleged that the Bureau’s systems for preventing and responding to sexual abuse were so inadequate that everyone at Dublin was at risk.

The following year, government attorneys claimed in court filings that sexual abuse was “virtually non-existent” at Dublin and that the facility was “not currently a sexualized environment.” A few weeks before that claim, a Dublin officer had allegedly slammed Antonina Troyer against the wall of her cell and raped her, according to a lawsuit she filed later, which has since been stayed. Troyer said she received a letter three years after reporting the incident saying her allegation was not sustained, without further explanation. The officer, who did not respond to requests for comment, was not prosecuted and remained with the agency as of November. “You have a huge system that just steamrolls everybody, and you’re already considered to be not trustworthy,” Troyer said in an interview. “Our word means practically nothing.”

By March 2024, Judge Yvonne Gonzalez Rogers had criticized the Bureau’s sluggish response and appointed a special master to oversee promised reforms. Days later, the Bureau announced Dublin’s closure and asked the judge to dismiss the case. Gonzalez Rogers said the idea that closure solved the agency’s problems “strains credulity.” Ana and the other women at Dublin were shackled and taken to other facilities around the country in what seemed like an unusually torturous process. In letters and interviews, women recounted how some officers screamed at them, threatened to shoot them or left them without water for hours. One woman wrote: “With their behavior and the noise of the cocked guns, I was sure they were going to kill us.” An internal affairs investigation later determined that all the transport officers had acted “within policy,” even though, in some cases, other prison staff corroborated the women’s accounts.

After leaving Dublin, survivors faced retaliation at their new prisons. According to interviews and court papers, staff at some facilities derisively called them “Dublin girls” or “Dublin bitches” and refused to give them prison jobs. Elsewhere, other prisoners viewed them as snitches and held their decision to come forward against them. Monthly reports filed in federal court recorded almost 40 instances of abuse against the survivors during the first six months of court-appointed monitoring.

In early 2025, over the Trump administration’s objections, Gonzalez Rogers approved a sweeping consent decree, the first of its kind in the federal prison system. It created pathways for early release, increased protections against retaliation and two more years of court-mandated oversight for the Dublin survivors. Despite the persistent abuse documented in the investigation, the Bureau now appears poised to have less oversight than ever. The consent decree is set to expire early next year.

Earlier this year, the FBI arrived at Carswell, a facility one woman described in court filings as “a federal prison that became a hunting ground.” Investigators uncovered a cluster of allegations against paramedic Preston Emert, who was removed from his job after 18 women accused him of sexually abusive behavior, according to court filings and internal Bureau correspondence. Emert targeted women with addiction issues and medical problems, demanding sex in exchange for drugs and medical treatment, according to several women, their attorneys and a top Bureau official.

“I have seizures and would need help and in order to get it I would have to have sex with him,” Olivia Floyd said in a letter to reporters. Emert is one of 12 Carswell employees accused of sexual assault in the last five years. He has not been charged, and Bureau officials said in November he was still employed as a paramedic at Carswell. Last month, Emert’s supervisor — the health services supervisor whom records show fielded at least one of the early complaints about Emert’s behavior — was arrested after he admitted to investigators that he was having sex with a woman incarcerated there. The supervisor has not yet entered a plea.

The Justice Department’s Office of Inspector General, a watchdog with the power to investigate and recommend reforms, has suffered staffing shortages in recent years as the Trump administration pushed to slash its budget. The Bureau recently announced that new officers could begin work with fewer qualifications and lower pay, an approach it presented as a way to shore up staffing levels. “It’s almost as if they’ve learned nothing,” said Shanna Rifkin, general counsel for FAMM, a nonpartisan organization that advocates for criminal-justice reform. “They need staff who are well-trained and well-compensated, and to lower the qualifications when you already have rampant sex abuse and drugs being brought in — what do they think is going to happen?”

The ombuds office required by the Federal Prison Oversight Act still does not exist two years after Congress passed the law. The Justice Department has not released the projected budget and progress report that both chambers directed it to publish by April, and lawmakers have not appropriated money for the office.

Within the Bureau of Prisons, the effects of the intended reforms have been minimal. The agency has installed more cameras and restructured its Office of Internal Affairs, but under the new structure, staff at individual prisons typically still investigate their coworkers. “I think small things were changed — like the signage about how to report [abuse] needed fixing,” said former Justice Department attorney Marnie Shiels. “There were no substantive long-term changes from this that I know of.”

Bureau spokesperson Randilee Giamusso said the federal prison system has “zero tolerance” for sexual abuse and harassment and that the agency takes every allegation seriously. She described a revamped policy intended to ensure allegations are more thoroughly investigated, but the policy eliminates references to transgender and intersex people, whom federal law identifies as particularly vulnerable to sexual abuse in prison. The Bureau did not provide further details or address specific cases because it does not comment on pending litigation. Officers mentioned in the investigation and representatives for the prisons either did not respond or declined to comment on specific allegations.

The federal prison workers union called sexual abuse “completely unacceptable” and blamed persistent staffing shortages for helping create an environment that allows misconduct to go undetected. “If we are serious about protecting inmates and maintaining safe, secure federal prisons, we have to address the staffing crisis head-on,” the union said, noting that more watchful eyes would mean “fewer opportunities for inappropriate conduct to occur behind closed doors.”

Lawyers say civil cases are also becoming harder to succeed. The federal government has agreed to pay $116 million to settle lawsuits brought by the first 103 Dublin victims, while roughly 300 more cases continue through the legal system. Ana, whose abuser was never criminally charged and died while under investigation, has since been released.

Trump’s Justice Department has offered paltry payouts in some cases and reneged on signed agreements that could have avoided protracted litigation in others, according to sources familiar with the situation. Lauren Bis, a White House spokesperson, referred questions to federal prison officials.

In 2024, Judge Scott Coogler dismissed an Aliceville woman’s lawsuit against the Bureau, ruling that the prison system was not liable because the officer was “acting outside the scope of his employment” while assaulting her on duty. The decision is pending appeal. “This case could make it incredibly hard for anyone abused in government custody to seek accountability from the BOP,” said Deborah Golden, an attorney representing dozens of women in federal-prison abuse cases. “I hope that doesn’t happen.”

To the lawyers leading the Dublin litigation, the fallout has been disappointing. “It should not be hard to go to work and not force someone to give you a blow job,” Janssen said. “That is an extremely easy rule to follow. And I can only think that it’s just a complete lack of accountability.” The Bureau has announced plans to stop housing women at Aliceville and convert the facility into a men’s prison. Ela has since been released, while the remaining women are again set to be scattered across the country.
