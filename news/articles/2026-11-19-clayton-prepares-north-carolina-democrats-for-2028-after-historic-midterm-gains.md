## State House control brings affordability and voucher accountability into focus

Two weeks after the Nov. 3 midterms, North Carolina Democratic Party Chair Anderson Clayton was still hoarse from celebrating the party’s gains and apologized for her voice. Democrats captured a U.S. Senate seat as Roy Cooper defeated Republican Michael Whatley by nearly 20 points, flipped seven U.S. House seats, secured a state Supreme Court seat and retook the state House. Clayton credited the results to years of Republican rule in the state and to the volunteers and candidates who stepped up. “I think that a lot of what happened on election night is owed to 16 years of Republican oppression in the state,” Clayton said. “I also think it’s because of the incredible volunteers and candidates that stepped up to run for office.”

Clayton, who became the youngest state Democratic Party chair in the country in 2023, was quick to recognize high-caliber candidates who agreed to run for seats they did not expect to win. She grew up in Roxboro and ran for the post promising to expand Democratic political power in rural North Carolina rather than rely only on conventional urban strongholds. Clayton also emphasized connecting with rural residents and giving them a reason to vote for Democratic candidates. Her post-election message continued that emphasis as the party prepared to defend its newly won seats and offices.

Clayton contrasted the 2026 candidate slate with 2022, when Democrats left 44 state legislative races uncontested. First-time candidates were willing to run because they believed there was a need for accountability at both the federal and state levels, she said. “These people to me are heroes in their communities,” Clayton said. “They wanted to make sure that there was a name on the ballot.”

Support from House and Senate caucuses helped candidates prepare. Clayton credited Sen. Lisa Grafstein, D-Wake, and Rep. Eric Ager, D-Buncombe, with meeting candidates one-on-one to discuss what it meant to run for public office. “It was someone they could call while they were on the job,” Clayton said. “Everybody in the caucuses this year really stepped up to make it possible so that no one felt alone. They felt like they had people to lean on.”

Claire Kempner, a mother of three, signed up for a rematch with Rep. Dr. Tim Reeder, R-Pitt, after losing her 2024 race by more than 5,700 votes. With support, Kempner defeated Reeder to win House District 9. Clayton also pointed to retired deputy Dennis Allen, who unseated Republican “Skeet” Wilborn to become Person County’s first Black sheriff.

Allen’s race brought Republicans, unaffiliated voters and Democrats together in a deep-red county, Clayton said, describing their shared demand: “We want to see a good person in office.” The county backed Republican Michael Whatley for U.S. Senate by five points over Democrat Roy Cooper, while Whatley lost the statewide race to Cooper by nearly 20 points.

Days after the election, Clayton was in Denver for a post-midterm conference where party strategists were celebrating the Nov. 3 outcomes and looking ahead to 2028. The conference was hosted by Majority Democrats. Clayton told the gathering that the results offered two lessons: invest in organizers and infrastructure, and do not write off the South. “Black Americans right now, 50% live in the South,” Clayton said. “When we’re looking at the investments that we make as a party, and we say that Black voters are the base of our party, then I ask, why are we not investing in a region of the country that on Tuesday just proved everybody wrong?”

North Carolina Democrats took a mid-decade redistricting map that Republicans had carefully crafted to deliver an 11-3 GOP delegation, flipped seven of those U.S. House seats and will hold an 11-3 advantage in January. Retired Meredith College political science professor David McLennan said the 2026 investments paid large dividends up and down the Democratic ticket. “The president’s approval was just really an anchor around the Republicans’ neck,” McLennan said. “They took districts for granted.”

McLennan said Republicans would remain vulnerable unless they campaign seriously in every district. He pointed to U.S. Rep. Tim Moore in the 14th Congressional District, the former state House speaker, who did not campaign aggressively until the final weeks as his race against Democratic newcomer LaKesha Womack appeared to tighten. “I think one of the things that Republicans will decide in their autopsy is, ‘We can’t not campaign hard in every district,’ even in years that are favorable for them,” McLennan said.

McLennan said Democrats still face Republican-drawn congressional and legislative districts heading into 2028 and will not have Cooper at the top of the ticket, making this year’s result harder to repeat. Clayton was more optimistic. After 16 years in the wilderness, she said, Democrats in the state House are prepared to deliver on promises about affordability. She also acknowledged that some races should have been wins this year: “To be honest, there were wins that I think we should have had this year,” Clayton said.

“Raising the minimum wage from $7.25 in North Carolina has got to be a priority,” Clayton said. Health care costs were also high on the party’s agenda. “It’s going to be on our state legislatures and our state governments while Congress is figuring out the best way to repeal some of the ACA cuts to offer immediate relief,” Clayton said. The departure of Senate Leader Phil Berger, R-Rockingham, could open room for bipartisan action in some areas, Clayton said, including making private school vouchers more accountable.

North Carolina Republican Party Chairman Jason Simmons said on social media that the party would take an honest look at the results and identify where it needed to improve. Michael Whatley lost the U.S. Senate race to Democrat Roy Cooper by nearly 20 points. “We owe that to our candidates, our supporters, and the millions of North Carolinians who share our commitment to conservative leadership,” Simmons wrote. “We cannot afford to sit back. The work of rebuilding, organizing, and preparing for the next election starts now. 2028 starts now.”

Clayton held post-election debriefs that week in Martin, Duplin, Cabarrus and Wake counties. Besides thanking volunteers, the sessions are intended to identify prospective candidates for district attorney and county commissioner races and build the party’s bench for the next cycle. Her energy and the party’s success over the last three years have prompted some political observers to suggest that she could be recruited for a national role, perhaps at the Democratic National Committee, during the next presidential cycle.

“She’s a rock star right now,” said McLennan, who credited Clayton’s dynamism, enthusiasm and outreach in rural and exurban North Carolina, including her willingness to choose women and women of color when Democrats probably would not have done so 10 years earlier. Western Carolina University political scientist Chris Cooper wrote in a Substack post that the legislature sworn in January will include about twice as many women as it did in 1983. Women will make up more than 52% of the Democratic House delegation and 62.5% of the Democratic Senate delegation, according to Cooper. Clayton dismissed speculation that she might leave North Carolina. “I love North Carolina,” Clayton said. “I love coming home every day to Roxboro to touch the grass and remind Republicans I’m sitting in their backyard.”
