## Strait of Hormuz traffic remains 42% below prewar levels

Oil prices fell Tuesday as traders weighed a potential global supply surplus against persistent threats to energy shipments amid the Middle East conflict. Brent crude futures for January delivery dropped 0.7% to $88.76 a barrel, while U.S. West Texas Intermediate futures for January delivery declined 0.6% to $79.68 a barrel.

Both benchmarks had fallen more than 3% on Monday, when Brent slipped below $90 a barrel as expectations of recovering Middle Eastern supplies and a sizable surplus next year weighed on prices. Tuesday's decline extended that pressure.

Supply and shipping signals pointed in opposite directions this week. Despite the recovery in regional oil exports, attacks on commercial vessels continue to pose risks to energy shipments. Preliminary data from ship-tracking firm Kpler showed traffic through the Strait of Hormuz 42% below prewar levels in the seven days through Nov. 21.

Cyberattacks have emerged as a separate threat to energy shipping. The United States is investigating Iran's possible involvement in cyberattacks that are believed to have targeted as many as 50 ships carrying oil, gas and chemical products, according to U.S. officials and others familiar with the matter.

U.S. authorities also consider Russia a possible culprit. A Trump administration official said the U.S. had not found evidence of Iranian involvement, leaving the investigation without a confirmed attribution.

Giovanni Staunovo, a commodities analyst at UBS, said the recent decline in oil prices reflects recovering Middle Eastern exports and a buildup of crude held at sea. Staunovo also said current prices underestimate the risks posed by depleted inventories, recovering Chinese demand and continued geopolitical tensions.

Muhammad Umair, a senior analyst at FXEmpire, said rising crude supplies are likely to keep oil prices under pressure in the near term. Umair also said delays to Russian oil shipments from Baltic and Black Sea ports could raise transportation costs and limit further declines in prices.

The Organization of the Petroleum Exporting Countries and its allies are scheduled to meet Sunday, and traders will use the meeting to assess the group's production outlook for next year. The projected surplus that has weighed on prices is tied to that outlook.

The result is a market in which falling benchmarks and continuing shipping risk sit side by side. Additional crude supply is expected to weigh on prices in the near term, while reduced traffic through the Strait of Hormuz and attacks on commercial vessels threaten the shipments that carry Middle Eastern exports.
