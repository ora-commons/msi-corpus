## House panel details 879,868 approved chip sales to Chinese-tied firms

Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said Friday that the administration does not need to impose further restrictions on the sale of older Nvidia chips to China, saying the limits already in place are working after the White House moved this week to block China's remote access to the latest semiconductors.

"The proper chip restrictions are in place," Bessent said in an interview with CNBC after returning from a trip to Asia with President Donald Trump. "And the remote access has been curtailed."

Bessent's comments came a day after the House Foreign Affairs Committee released details of the Commerce Department's approval of the sales of more than 850,000 chips from Nvidia and its competitor AMD to Chinese companies. The Pentagon has identified some of those companies as having ties to the Chinese military.

The committee's report tracked applications from Nvidia to sell its H200 chips — one of its more powerful products, though not the most advanced — and AMD's applications to sell its roughly comparable MI325X chip to companies that are either located or headquartered in China, or to entities with parent companies based in China.

The Commerce Department's Bureau of Industry and Security approved the export, re-export or transfer of 879,868 of those chips to companies with Chinese ties across 21 license applications as of May 11, 2026, according to the report. Nvidia and AMD had requested to sell a cumulative total of more than 3.4 million of the chips to Chinese-tied companies across those applications.

The approvals do not necessarily indicate completed sales. They included 75,000 chips for Alibaba, 150,000 for Tencent and its subsidiaries, 43,000 for Baidu and 4,000 for BYD, according to the report. Each of those companies has been identified by the Defense Department as having ties to the Chinese military.

A spokesperson for the Bureau of Industry and Security said in a statement Friday that the Commerce Department has a "long track record of transparency with Congress about licensing activity for the H200 and equivalent chips, including both approvals and rejections."

The spokesperson said the department's approach to H200 licensing is based on a rule published in the Federal Register in January. That rule imposes "significant additional national security-related requirements on license applicants in order to qualify for case-by-case consideration rather than a presumption of denial," the spokesperson said.

An Nvidia spokesperson said in a statement Friday that the "limited shipments we made under the H200 program were legal and authorized" by the Bureau of Industry and Security. "The review and licensing process spanned more than half a year, with approvals subject to strict conditions and limited volumes," the spokesperson said.

Nvidia said earlier this week that it was no longer shipping H200 chips to China. The spokesperson said Friday that shipments had stopped despite the prior approvals.

AMD said it complies with U.S. export control laws and regulations and maintains "rigorous processes to ensure compliance across our global operations." "We remain focused on serving our customers and upholding the highest standards of integrity," a spokesperson said Friday.

The ability of Chinese technology firms to access Nvidia's technology came under scrutiny after CEO Jensen Huang stepped down amid allegations of his affair with an executive at Megaspeed, a Singapore-based company that uses advanced chips to generate computing power that Chinese firms access remotely.

Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick has been under intense scrutiny himself in the turmoil following the Huang affair. Last week, Bessent said he was frustrated with the Commerce Department's delay in placing restrictions on Nvidia chip exports to Megaspeed International, which is under investigation by the United States for potentially diverting restricted Nvidia AI chips to China. A former executive at the company who allegedly had an affair with Huang was reportedly suspected by U.S. intelligence agencies of being a spy for China.

In an unusually public rebuke of a fellow Cabinet member, Bessent said his department wanted to place export controls on Megaspeed, but Commerce was in charge of the matter.

Speaking to reporters on Friday, Trump said he has "not lost confidence" in Lutnick. Calling him a "different kind of guy," Trump said he "has a natural business instinct" and has "done a very good job."

Chinese technology firms have been bracing for impacts on their ambitious AI development plans as scrutiny mounts on export controls in the United States.


Bessent's remarks follow the [Trump administration's Nov. 16 shift on foreign access to American AI chips](/articles/2026-11-16-trump-administration-shifts-stance-on-foreign-access-to-ai-chips/), in which the Commerce Department's Bureau of Industry and Security moved to block remote access to advanced U.S. chips and servers through data centers in other countries — the step Bessent pointed to Friday when he said remote access had been curtailed.
