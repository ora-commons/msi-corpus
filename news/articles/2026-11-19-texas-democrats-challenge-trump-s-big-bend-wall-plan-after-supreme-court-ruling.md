## CBP cites the ruling as authority to resume border construction

A Supreme Court ruling this month cleared the way for U.S. Customs and Border Protection to resume work on the Trump administration’s proposed “smart border wall” along a 500-mile stretch of the Texas border that includes Big Bend National Park. The ruling followed a continuing challenge to the administration’s decision to waive dozens of federal environmental and historic preservation laws to accelerate construction.

Construction began before the litigation halted it. The project has drawn major protests in Texas. Bulldozers began clearing land near Santa Elena Canyon in August, and activists circulated videos that drew a national backlash. CBP Commissioner Rodney Scott paused construction days after it started and traveled to the region to meet with stakeholders. U.S. District Judge Kathleen Cardone then issued a temporary injunction blocking CBP from continuing while the case challenging the waivers remained active. The Supreme Court ruling now leaves CBP free to proceed.

A Republican-dominated Congress put $46.5 billion into border wall expansion in Trump’s “big, beautiful” spending bill last year, and CBP began indicating early this year that much of the money would go to Big Bend. The Guardian reported that Texas Republicans, including outgoing Gov. Greg Abbott, largely stayed quiet as party colleagues orchestrated an unpopular wall expansion that threatened private property, ancient rock art and park landscapes in a region whose cliffs, brush and isolation already make it the section of the border with the fewest unauthorized crossings by far.

The ruling was a major setback, The Guardian said, for environmentalists and park enthusiasts, residents who value the region’s remoteness, landowners worried about property values and businesses that depend on outdoor tourism. Big Bend’s cliffs, brush and isolation have made it the section of the border with the fewest unauthorized crossings by far.

The ruling came less than a week after Republicans suffered losses in the midterms. Texas elected Governor-elect Gina Hinojosa, ending more than 30 years without a Democratic governor, and its first Democratic senator since the 1980s. Democrats also took both chambers of Congress with sizable majorities, flipped the Texas House and weakened Republican control of the state Senate. The Trump administration was also likely to face more scrutiny over the Big Bend expansion when the new Congress convened.

The Guardian said the Republican strategy of staying quiet while the wall expansion advanced almost certainly cost the party at the polls, citing opposition from conservatives and liberals alike. Brewster and Val Verde counties, Big Bend’s two largest, flipped by double digits compared with the 2024 election that sent Trump back to the White House.

Because the wall is a federal project, the White House retains substantial power to push it forward. Even so, CBP will now face open hostility from an incoming Texas government that is preparing legal and legislative challenges. Hinojosa said, “Texas sovereignty and land should be protected.” Hinojosa added, “By letting the federal government bulldoze our land, the supreme court is continuing to put corporate profits before the people of Texas.”

The proposed route runs through several state holdings, including Big Bend Ranch State Park, Black Gap Wildlife Management Area, Seminole Canyon State Park and several small tracts owned by the Texas General Land Office, which manages some state lands and holds extensive mineral rights. State Sen. César Blanco and state Rep. Eddie Morales, both Democrats, filed a bill that would ban border security construction on state parks. Blanco tweeted after the decision was published: “Incredibly disappointed by the supreme court’s decision,” and “Just last week, Texans made it clear they want leaders focused on what matters to their families and communities. Destroying our natural landscapes is not the direction we should be going.”

Democrats now hold the governor’s office for the first time since 1994 and the state House for the first time in two decades, giving the bill a route to passage that had previously appeared unlikely. With Republicans’ state Senate control weakened, the measure could advance even as the federal project proceeds.

Benjamin Flores, the incoming Texas land commissioner who will run the General Land Office, wrote that he “will protect every acre of state land, honor every lease, and ensure no one breaks ground on state property without written permission from the land office.” Flores said the transition team was working to get a full picture of agreements the land office had already made affecting Big Bend state land and would review each with the land office’s legal team once he was sworn in. He said he would act within the law to protect the land and the Texans who depend on it. Texas Parks and Wildlife and the General Land Office did not disclose to The Guardian whether they had yet signed agreements allowing CBP to build.

Scott, however, said CBP would continue to push ahead with construction in the region, citing the Supreme Court ruling as evidence that the agency “has the legal authority and responsibility to secure America’s borders.” He said plans for the park would change, though he did not explain how. In a statement, he wrote that the agency had re-evaluated the terrain, listened to local stakeholders and made “meaningful adjustments” that would secure the critical stretch of border while preserving the unique landscape and public access for generations to come. He said he would share details about the updated plans soon.

A group of 23 Democrats led by U.S. Rep. Veronica Escobar of El Paso, Texas, asked the Office of Inspector General on Monday to investigate whether political donations or ties influenced the award of $7.5 billion in border wall contracts for Big Bend. Their letter said the process CBP used to award the contracts lacked transparency and provided no public oversight of the contracts or their scope.

Advocates are also seeking action from Congress: either to undo the federal environmental and historic preservation waivers issued earlier this year to speed smart wall construction, or to pass a standalone measure to at least exempt Big Bend National Park, whose natural beauty has inspired the loudest public outcry. In an email, Bob Krumenaker, a former Big Bend superintendent who now chairs the non-partisan group Keep Big Bend Wild, wrote: “Are they going to resume bulldozing where they left off? Just because they’ve been given the green light doesn’t make it acceptable.” He wrote, “Will they ever listen to the will of the American people?” He added, “Clearly, the only way to stop the destruction is through legislating the next Congress.” Krumenaker said, “That’s our laser focus right now.”

Laiken Jordahl, a public lands advocate with the Center for Biological Diversity and one of the plaintiffs in the lawsuit challenging the federal waivers, said park advocates would watch any construction closely. “The second a bulldozer enters that national park, we will have eyes on every piece of vegetation that is destroyed, every speck of dirt that moves,” Jordahl said. “It’s so clear that people across Texas oppose this – their newly elected leadership opposes this.”
