## Some companies sued the US government for refunds after ruling

The Trump administration has refunded at least $100bn to companies since August, about 60% of roughly $131bn in tariffs, after the Supreme Court ruled that Trump's "Liberation Day" tariffs were illegal. The court ruled that the tariffs were unlawful because they overstepped his executive authority and should have required congressional approval.

Some companies immediately sued the US government for refunds after the ruling, though Trump signaled that he would "remember" which companies requested reimbursements and praised companies for holding off. The Trump administration has not specified which companies are receiving refunds, despite requests for information, according to ProPublica.

The refund totals and the litigation sit alongside an investigation from ProPublica into where the money is going. After analyzing securities filings and earnings calls, ProPublica identified at least nine companies that said they are receiving tariff refunds and noted they are directing some of those funds to their leadership.

Stanley Black & Decker, a tool company that makes common household products including vacuums, electric drills and measuring tapes, told its consumers they were forced to raise prices because of Trump's import fees, ProPublica reported. The parent company of DeWalt and Craftsman announced price increases across its tools, citing disruptions and costs linked to Trump's tariffs. The company is expected to receive at least $298m in tariff reimbursements and indicated some of it will go to bonuses. Executives at TJMaxx and Marshalls, discount department stores that are set to receive tariff refunds, also said the same.

In financial disclosures, Stanley Black & Decker said the refund it received "was partially offset by directly attributable costs for variable incentive compensation", according to ProPublica. Stanley Black & Decker, TJMaxx and Marshalls did not immediately respond to request for comment.

Though many companies said publicly that they had to increase prices because of tariffs, it has largely been unclear what companies are doing with their tariff refunds. In its analysis, ProPublica found that almost all the companies that have publicly said they plan to use their tariff reimbursements to refund customers are those that deal directly with other businesses, rather than directly with consumers. Some companies have remained "vague" about their plans for the reimbursements and have not specified whether those funds will go towards price decreases or holding back on future increases, according to the report.

Research has shown that US consumers have shouldered some of the cost of tariffs, with the New York Federal Reserve estimating that 26% of tariff increases pass through to consumer prices. The Yale Budget Lab estimated that tariffs cost households about $900 annually under current laws.

Tariffs are one of several of Trump's economic policies that have proven unpopular with voters, reflected in the widespread victories Democrats notched in the November midterms. A year after Trump's tariffs were announced, a survey from the Pew Research Center found that 63% of Americans felt little or no confidence in his handling of tariffs.

Republican candidates across the country were largely punished for Trump's tariffs and other White House decisions that voters widely linked to inflation. In the Associated Press's survey of more than 100,000 midterm voters, two-thirds said that Trump's decisions had fueled inflation, about half of the number of 2022 midterm voters who said the same thing about former president Joe Biden.
