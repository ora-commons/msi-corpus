## Google.org cites 'storytelling problem' at Manhattan event

Amazon said recently that it would no longer use NDAs with local officials on its data-center plans, according to the Journal's reporting by Clara Hudson. Separately, Google reached out to residents of New Mexico's Lea County, where the company is eyeing a possible data center, stressing that its plans are in the earliest stages and that it is eager to field any concerns. A company survey asks what additional investment the area might need, and a website states plainly, "We're listening."

The broader industry, the Journal reported, is sharing data-center plans earlier and promising more transparency to win local support that has proved hard to come by. Tech companies have faced so much criticism for making deals behind closed doors that they have to try a new approach, the newsletter said.

Internal acknowledgment of the trust deficit is also becoming more public. Stuart McLaughlin, head of advocacy at Google.org, Google's philanthropic arm, said at an event in Manhattan last month that "there is a fundamental storytelling problem here" and that "[we] all need to get way better at telling this story."

A Google spokesperson acknowledged to the Journal that announcing data-center plans earlier invites more risk, but said the alternative would be losing public trust. Hudson, the Journal reporter, characterized the company's calculus as "damned if you do, damned if you don't."

Some Lea County residents set up a Facebook page within days of Google's "We're listening" site going live to organize opposition to a possible data center, the Journal reported. Tech companies have also tried hosting open houses, including some at local middle schools, to invite residents to air concerns face-to-face, and have pledged all kinds of funding for communities. Yet the public "still isn't buying it, for the most part," the Journal reported.

The storytelling around water is going especially poorly, the Journal reported. Several months ago, most people were not aware that some data centers can guzzle water to cool electrical equipment; water now ranks among the top concerns communities are raising, particularly in dry and rural regions. To offset those worries, companies are jostling to show that their data centers are the least resource-intensive and more thoughtfully designed than their rivals'.

The New York Times recently published a visualization of how many data-center calculations go into training large language models, the Journal noted. One takeaway: the count is "now well above 'the number of stars in the known universe.'"

"Opponents say offering major community investments and other moves show that tech companies have lost control of the narrative," the Journal reported. "Some tech insiders might agree."
