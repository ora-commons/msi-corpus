## Regulators have not approved Nemaslug pending more native-species research

Oregon State University scientists tested two versions of Nemaslug on banana slugs collected on the Oregon Coast, according to a study published in the journal PLOS One.

Nemaslug is a commercial pesticide sold in many parts of the world and used for three decades in other countries to kill unwanted slugs on farmland.

The scientists found that nearly every banana slug that came into contact with either product died. Nemaslug was particularly lethal to young banana slugs.

Banana slugs are native to coastal rainforests from central California to southeast Alaska. They aid forest health by decomposing organic matter and dispersing seeds. The study said the pesticide could devastate the slugs and the ecosystems they support if approved for sale in the United States.

Emily Taylor, a research assistant at Oregon Health & Science University who was an Oregon State University graduate student at the time of the study, said in a news release, “This research has pretty big implications for biodiversity conservation.” She also said, “The Northwest, due to its rainy climate, is a terrestrial gastropod hotspot.”

The findings came six years after some of the same Oregon State scientists touted Nemaslug as a potential alternative to harsher, slug-killing chemical pesticides being used on Oregon farms.

That interest followed damage from invasive European slugs in the Northwest, which feed on crops and their leaves and seeds. According to a 2021 Oregon State University analysis, those slugs caused upwards of $60 million to $100 million in damage to Oregon's grass seed industry over decades.

Nemaslug is made of extremely small nematodes — tiny parasitic worms that feed on bacteria, fungi and other organisms. The product is not approved by the U.S. Department of Agriculture or Oregon agriculture regulators because of limited research into how it would affect slugs and other species native to North America.

Oregon State University scientists also found Nemaslug similarly harmful to the Pacific sideband snail, a native woodland snail also found along the Pacific Coast from California to Alaska.

Researchers argue that more studies are needed to determine the impact of Nemaslug products before the federal government approves them.
