## Christa Pike survived a lethal-injection dose; a Texas execution lasted over an hour

Attorney General Todd Blanche, Trump’s former personal defense lawyer, told the audience at the Justice Department that the department was seeking the death penalty whenever appropriate. He said it had sought capital punishment 49 times since Trump took office, compared with just once under Biden.

The death penalty came under renewed scrutiny in September when Christa Pike, 50, became the first person to survive receiving doses of a drug during a lethal-injection execution. Last week, the execution of Reinaldo Dennes, 70, in Texas lasted more than an hour as staff struggled to find a suitable vein.

The United States is increasingly isolated in its use of capital punishment. Just 17 countries carried out executions in 2025, while 145 have abolished the death penalty in law or practice. Yet the United States executed 47 people last year, the highest number since 2009.

On Monday, Trump delivered his address from a stage in the great hall of the Robert F Kennedy Department of Justice building. He was flanked by two 12.5-foot aluminum statues from 1936, *The Spirit of Justice* and *The Majesty of Law*. Blanche and FBI Director Kash Patel joined him, and attendees included families of murder victims whose relatives were affected by Newsom’s mass commutation of death row inmates.

Trump said he would sign a presidential directive ordering the Justice Department to seek the death penalty for child murderers, as well as rapists and human traffickers who kill their victims. He also ordered Attorney General Todd Blanche to expedite federal executions despite the recent outcry. “Once juries have spoken, capital punishment will be swift and certain,” Trump said.

Newsom’s decision to commute the death sentences of 548 prisoners — the largest clearing of any U.S. death row in modern history — prompted Trump to call it “one of the most shocking acts of injustice our country has ever seen.” Trump described Newsom as a “terrible” governor and said the commutations “should not be allowed to stand.” He accused Newsom of granting clemency to some of “the most evil monsters on the face of the Earth.”

Trump said the gathering was intended to support victims: “We come together to stand against the travesty and to wrap our arms around the families of victims who were so brutally betrayed by bad government and our political system. What these families have endured is beyond imagination.” In graphic and gruesome terms, he cited cases including serial killer Charles Ng, who tortured and murdered at least 11 people, and Chester Turner, who killed 14 women in Los Angeles.

Relatives of some victims were invited to speak, and several used the event to criticize Newsom. Sandy Friend, whose eight-year-old son Michael Lyons was kidnapped, tortured and murdered, challenged the governor: “Face me, Governor Newsom. Tell me Michael’s life doesn’t matter,” she said.

Newsom’s office stressed that victims and survivors did not speak with one voice on capital punishment and that some supported the governor’s decision. Jess Nichol, whose sister Polly Klaas was kidnapped and murdered, said she had once wanted the death penalty for her sister’s killer but had come to believe that victims deserved more than a promise of justice through vengeance. “When victims are this divided, the death penalty shouldn’t be the answer,” Nichol said, calling instead for counselling, medical care and financial support for survivors.

Newsom announced earlier this month that he intended to commute all 548 death sentences in California to life imprisonment without the possibility of parole. The move does not release any prisoners, overturn convictions or remove restitution obligations, and most of the commutations require approval from the California Supreme Court. Newsom’s office said every prisoner covered would remain behind bars for life.

Newsom has argued that capital punishment is morally wrong and inconsistent with California’s values. He has described the death-penalty system as arbitrary, unequal and vulnerable to wrongful convictions. California has not carried out an execution since January 2006, though voters rejected proposals to abolish the death penalty in 2012 and 2016.

In a statement responding to Trump’s speech, Newsom said he would not accept lectures on accountability from a president who had used his pardon powers to benefit wealthy friends and business associates.

The Guardian reported that the dispute had offered Trump an opportunity to revive Republican attacks portraying Democrats as soft on crime. Newsom, a frequent Democratic critic of Trump who is considering a White House bid, is widely regarded as a potential contender for the Democratic presidential nomination in 2028.

Trump’s tough-on-crime rhetoric dates to 1989, when he called for restoring the death penalty after five young Black and Latino men were wrongly accused in the Central Park jogger case.

Presidential addresses at the department are rare. Trump last spoke there in March 2025 at an event focused on crime and immigration, complaining about criminal investigations under the Biden administration and promising to expose what he called misconduct by officials who investigated him. The Justice Department has since pursued investigations targeting several of Trump’s perceived enemies, although some cases have been rejected by judges and grand juries.
