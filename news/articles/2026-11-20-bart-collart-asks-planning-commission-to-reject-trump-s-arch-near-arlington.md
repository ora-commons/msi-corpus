## Veterans for America First backs the arch amid cemetery objections

During a 10-hour planning commission hearing earlier this month, Bart Collart asked commissioners to “do the right thing” and oppose President Donald Trump’s proposed 250-foot (76-meter) gold-and-granite arch near Arlington National Cemetery. A key planning commission approved the arch at the hearing, where Collart spoke about his son’s sacrifice. He told the commission to “Look across the road at those rows of clean, simple white headstones. Go take the time to walk amongst them,” and to “Show some real respect for the uniform my son died in.” More than 100,000 public comments came in about the project, and many opposed it.

Veterans for America First joined several veteran organizations in a brief in the lawsuit supporting the arch. Jared Craig, the group’s general counsel and national vice president, said, “It was always supposed to be a gateway to connect D.C. to Arlington.” Supporters say the arch completes a plan mandated by Congress in 1925 to add a monument to the circle. Craig argued that the project honors veterans and fallen service members, does not impact the cemetery itself and requires no changes there.

For many veterans and their families, and loved ones of others buried at the Arlington, Virginia, cemetery, the arch has no place so close. That opposition has prompted lawsuits by veterans groups and others, including nine family members of the Kennedy political dynasty. They argue the arch, which would be built on a traffic circle about half a mile (0.8 kilometers) from Arlington’s main entrance, would fundamentally change the experience of visiting the cemetery.

Behind the fences lining Memorial Drive is an active cemetery with more than 430,000 people interred and dozens more added each week. The people buried there include retirees and their spouses, some dependents and a few eligible dignitaries. Among them are two presidents, John F. Kennedy and William Howard Taft, and more than a dozen Supreme Court justices. The dead also include an untold number who died while on active duty and eligible spouses, as well as more than 400 recipients of the Medal of Honor, the nation’s highest honor for valor in combat. The remains of military personnel from every war, dating back to the Revolutionary War, are there.

An estimated 3 million to 4 million people visit annually. Visitors can walk among the stones or take the tour. The Tomb of the Unknown Soldier is one prominent feature. The cemetery also holds the grave of boxer Joe Louis and actor Audie Murphy, who was one of the most decorated U.S. soldiers in World War II.

On a recent visit, a small group of about 10 men came quietly from Section 60, where soldiers who died in Afghanistan and Iraq are buried. The men were young, and inscriptions included “PH” for Purple Heart and references to Afghanistan or Operation Enduring Freedom, reflecting deaths in service at young ages. The setting was otherwise marked by silence and distant sounds, with passenger planes flying over from nearby Ronald Reagan Washington National Airport providing the only interruption.

Micki McElya, a historian at the University of Connecticut and author of “The Politics of Mourning: Death and Honor in Arlington National Cemetery,” said, “I think you could, by any definition, consider it the most hallowed ground in the United States, but I think we’re not even giving it credit for how hallowed, how representative, how much sacrifice and honor is there.” She said the country’s shifting values are represented there in all their complications, including slavery.

Jimi Shaughnessy, a veterans program manager at the nonprofit National Parks Conservation Association, opposed the arch. A veteran, he visits his great-grandparents’ resting place regularly. “The setting is open, it’s quiet, it’s peaceful, it is easy to navigate,” he said. He said there is space to transition “so I can properly acknowledge my great-grandfolks that are buried there.” Shaughnessy said major construction sounds would intrude on the setting and funerals, while creating a tourist attraction at the arch would bring noise and distraction, along with a “looming dark shadow above me.”

Bart Collart spoke about his son, Cpl. Spencer Collart, a young Marine who died at 21 while trying to save fellow crew members in a 2023 Osprey crash in Australia. Spencer Collart’s headstone faces the rising sun, with the Lincoln Memorial to the east and the Marine Corps War Memorial nearby. His stone stands among thousands of uniform headstones and other monuments marking the people buried at Arlington. Bart Collart called the arch “It’s a billboard for an ego. It’s a loud, boastful, gaudy piece of marketing dropped onto sacred ground.” He said Spencer would see it as “dirtying hallowed ground.”

Spencer Collart was killed in August 2023. A crew chief on a V-22 Osprey, an aircraft that functions as both a helicopter and an airplane, he had been deployed to Australia for several months when his aircraft went down. Spencer Collart and the pilots, Capt. Eleanor LeBeau and aircraft commander Maj. Tobin Lewis, died in the crash during a training mission. Months later, the family learned that Spencer Collart had survived the crash but went back into the burning aircraft to try to save the trapped pilots.

Standing at his grave that week, Bart Collart spoke of his son’s love of flying and his pilots. On the day of Spencer Collart’s burial, a fleet of Ospreys flew over the grave in a formation known as the missing man, meant to honor a fallen military aviator. A recent day brought a steady stream of funerals. Planes took off from the nearby airport, though at times the cemetery was otherwise quiet. Section 52, where Spencer Collart is buried, is in a corner of the cemetery and had just one visitor walking and reading the stones.

The arch project is part of a series of renovations that President Donald Trump, a Republican, has initiated to reimagine Washington. When complete, the gold-and-granite arch would stretch taller than the Lincoln Memorial. Opponents say it would break the alignment among the Lincoln Memorial, Memorial Bridge and Arlington House, formerly Confederate Gen. Robert E. Lee’s home, along with the symbolism of a reunited North and South. They also warn that the 250-foot structure could cast a long shadow over Arlington National Cemetery, literally shade gravesites, disrupt solemn visits with construction noise and alter the carefully designed historic landscape of the nation’s capital.

The White House did not immediately respond to a request for comment. A federal judge has temporarily blocked the project, and the court challenges remain ongoing.
