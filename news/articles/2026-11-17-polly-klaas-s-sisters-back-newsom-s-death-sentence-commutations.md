## The sisters say capital appeals have extended their family’s grief for decades

Annie Nichol and Jess Nichol, sisters of Polly Klaas, said in a Nov. 17 opinion essay published by The Guardian that they oppose California's death penalty. They welcomed Gov. Gavin Newsom's decision to commute California's death sentences to life in prison without parole, describing the action as a critical step toward a more just system.

The sisters said they met Newsom a few weeks earlier on the former death row at San Quentin to explain their opposition to capital punishment. They wrote: “A few weeks ago, we met with the California governor, Gavin Newsom, on the former death row at San Quentin to explain why we do not support the death penalty.” According to the sisters, Newsom said the conversation helped shape his decision.

The man who killed Polly Klaas was convicted and sentenced to death, but more than 30 years later remains on death row, Annie and Jess Nichol said. They characterized California's capital-case process as expensive and error-prone and said it prolongs trauma rather than preventing harm. They described California's broader system as one that promises justice but has delivered an expensive and error-prone process.

The sisters wrote that “A death sentence does not bring closure.” They said repeated hearings, legal filings and headlines have kept their family tethered to the man who harmed Polly, leaving them tied to capital proceedings as they try to move on and heal.

Annie and Jess Nichol said they have not forgiven Polly's killer, while acknowledging other survivors who have found forgiveness. They said they still experience moments of intense grief and anger and persistent pain over Polly's absence. The sisters acknowledged that survivors may feel a desire for vengeance but said those feelings should not form the basis of public policy.

Their account of victims' needs contrasted survivor anger with California's reliance on punishment. The sisters said survivors' anger is real and deserves a response. They also said the state can honor survivors' anger without turning to an irreversible punishment. The sisters said capital punishment exploits survivors' most vulnerable moments of rage and pain, and they said the criminal legal system is not built to support the slow, human process of recovering from trauma. They noted that survivors' grief changes shape over time and the meaning they make of their loss changes with it. They argued that survivors have varied needs and that the criminal legal system offers punishment as a one-size-fits-all substitute for the work of healing, accountability and prevention.

Support for survivors was another focus. The sisters said most survivors are given no resources to help them heal or recover from trauma. In California, fewer than one in five survivors receive counseling, medical assistance or financial support after surviving a violent crime. They said many people consequently remain in anger and trauma for their entire lives, are told punishment is the only justice available and receive little more than a promise that someone else will suffer, too. They called for counseling, safety, help paying medical bills and housing, and assistance caring for children, rather than an execution carried out decades later as a substitute for care.

Nonpartisan analysts estimated that ending the death penalty in practice and converting death sentences to life without parole could save California taxpayers up to $150 million annually, largely by reducing the extraordinary costs unique to capital cases. The sisters said those savings could support mental-health treatment, substance-use intervention, gun-violence prevention, trauma-recovery centers and assistance for survivors. They said the investment also could help with solving cases, preventing future victimization and stabilizing families before people fall through the cracks.

The sisters asked California to stop treating survivors' pain as permission for state killing and to build a public-safety strategy centered on healing and preventing harm. They said nothing would undo their loss and that they did not ask anyone to pretend otherwise. They said families like theirs should not remain trapped in an endless loop of promised violence and thanked Newsom for making the commutations part of his legacy. They described the strategy as bigger than vengeance and said it should treat survivors as whole human beings rather than instruments of punishment.

Annie and Jess Nichol described themselves as survivor advocates working to replace punitive responses to harm with healing, accountability and care. They said Polly's murder has been invoked in support of capital punishment for more than three decades, with politicians, advocates and strangers asking, “What about Polly?” as though their loss were an argument that spoke for itself. The sisters said invoking her name did not settle the policy debate for them. They argued execution is not a system responding to victims' needs, but one that borrows survivors' pain to justify what it already intended to do.

In closing, the sisters said they loved Polly, missed her and continued to carry her with them. They wrote: “We deserve better than the death penalty. And so do all survivors.”
