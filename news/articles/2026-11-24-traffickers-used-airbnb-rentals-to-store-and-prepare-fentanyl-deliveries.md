## Most fentanyl pills tracked in DEA operation remain unaccounted for

An Associated Press investigation of a Southwest fentanyl network that authorities alleged was led by Heriberto Salazar Amaya found that traffickers used Airbnb rentals to count cash, store bags of fentanyl pills and prepare deliveries with digital scales. DEA agents watched a backpack holding as many as 100,000 counterfeit pain pills leave a monitored hotel in August 2024 without seizing the load. Almost all of the pills tracked in the operation remain unaccounted for, according to The Associated Press.

Authorities launched an investigation in the summer of 2024 into Heriberto Salazar Amaya, the alleged leader of a trafficking ring that prosecutors say generated as much as $2 million a month in sales. The 38-year-old Mexican had been on the DEA’s radar for a decade. He was arrested in April 2025 on drug-trafficking charges and is scheduled to be tried next year. Salazar Amaya has pleaded not guilty, and his defense attorneys did not respond to requests for comment. Federal prosecutors charged 15 other people in the case, many of them low-level couriers. A dozen pleaded guilty, mostly to drug charges, and none of the group’s foreign suppliers in Mexico was named.

One documented transaction took place in August 2024. Federal agents staked out the parking lot and lobby of a hotel while a surveillance plane circled overhead. A buyer arrived at 2:24 p.m. in a white pickup, greeted a mohawked courier and entered the Home2 Suites. Eight minutes later, the buyer and courier exited separately; one carried a backpack stuffed with as many as 100,000 potentially lethal counterfeit pain pills. The DEA did not seize the load that Sunday. Eight months passed before agents dismantled the major fentanyl ring that the AP said was flooding the Southwest as the U.S. reeled from an epidemic of opioid overdose deaths.

The AP investigation found that dealers trafficked large packages of pills in plastic bins and duffel bags through highly transited hotels, apartment complexes, Airbnb rentals and self-storage facilities in six states. Court records showed that short-term rentals served as a logistical linchpin for the ring. Traffickers stayed at Airbnbs, Salazar Amaya left a public review for one, and members of the network counted cash, stored massive bags of fentanyl pills and used digital scales to prepare deliveries. The DEA frequently surveilled the residences, recording shipments from the street and a reconnaissance plane overhead.

As the dealers passed in and out of hotels and storage units, they took steps to protect themselves. DEA agents found a box of N95 masks at one Airbnb, according to crime-scene photographs reviewed by the AP. Several rentals used by the traffickers remain listed. Airbnb said it first heard from law enforcement after the dealers were arrested and was never told the investigation involved fentanyl. Experts said the DEA should have immediately alerted Airbnb and health officials so the properties could be checked for potential contamination.

Michael Polkabla, an environmental hazard consultant who assists law enforcement in cleaning up fentanyl spills, said, “It’s shocking and frightening.” He added, “Whenever fentanyl is being handled, it’s leaving significant residues.”

The DEA investigation required agents to set up covert cameras on residential streets and at self-storage facilities. Agents nearly compromised the surveillance when a DEA technician stood shoulder to shoulder with a courier in an Albuquerque Extra Space elevator. The DEA confirmed Salazar Amaya’s identity through a series of ruse calls, including one in which agents at a Las Vegas eatery secretly filmed him answering a woman’s call about a washer and dryer for sale. “You have the wrong number,” Salazar Amaya answered before hanging up.

In recordings of intercepted calls reviewed by the AP, the soft-spoken Salazar Amaya sounded more like a switchboard operator than a cartel kingpin. Using a string of aliases and 14 phones, he took orders and dispatched couriers to deliver large drug orders to major dealers from Phoenix to Denver. During one call, Salazar Amaya told a buyer, “I’m super stressed,” explaining that he had gone to urgent care suffering from blurry vision and an exploding headache.

Throughout the investigation, fentanyl reached multiple communities in New Mexico. Agents staked out a shipment of 370,000 fentanyl pills at an Albuquerque park and playground, where a trafficker struggled to remove a large storage container from a vehicle. Couriers driving in tandem cars ferried 200,000 pills to a home in Santa Fe’s historic Agua Fria neighborhood. Agents also watched traffickers access a self-storage unit stocked with fentanyl nearly 500 times.

Almost all of the pills remain unaccounted for. The DEA attributed the loss to the need to safeguard a sensitive investigation while tracing the fentanyl’s source to Mexico. DEA Agent David Zimmerman told a federal jury last year, “One small intervention on our end could cause a whole ripple effect of ruining everything.” He added, “You have to have a strategy on when you can stop people or take stuff off or try to intercept narcotics on these big investigations.”

The broader AP findings said DEA agents permitted at least 1.4 million additional pills to be trafficked between August 2024 and April 2025 as they closed in on the largest fentanyl bust in U.S. history. The AP based its tally on thousands of pages of court documents, investigative records, detailed wiretap reports and 12 hours of DEA surveillance footage. It excluded numerous deals mentioned in the records that lacked specific pill counts or had not been surveilled by the DEA. In June, the AP had reported that agents in New Mexico in recent years permitted hundreds of thousands of fentanyl pills to go unseized. The DEA denied that it ever knowingly allowed the trafficking of a synthetic opioid the White House had designated a “weapon of mass destruction.” The AP said the new findings further contradicted that denial.

The DEA at times has allowed suspected traffickers to continue operating under strict surveillance when agents suspect they are moving cocaine, heroin and marijuana, so agents will not spook bigger targets. The strategy is known as permitting drugs to “walk.” Fentanyl, however, was supposed to be different. The AP said the drug posed such an unprecedented danger that the DEA for years ran a public safety campaign with the slogan “One Pill Can Kill.” The Justice Department began instructing agents in 2017 to seize fentanyl whenever “practicable” because even unintentional contact can prove deadly. Officials loosened those rules in 2024 to give agents more discretion after DEA whistleblower David Howell questioned the morality and effectiveness of letting fentanyl reach the streets.

“Agents are driving around in a car plastered with ‘One Pill Can Kill’ and at the same time walking hundreds of thousands of pills of fentanyl,” said Kevin Small, a former colleague of Howell’s. “They’re hypocrites.”

Defenders of the wait-and-see approach said it could lead to bigger busts and the removal of higher-ranking bosses. Brian Besser, a former DEA senior executive who oversaw the agency’s Rocky Mountain division, said, “Everybody wants the big fish, but the big fish isn’t guaranteed.” He added, “When you’re dealing with a substance that can take this much human life, you can’t gamble like that.”

The AP’s reporting prompted a congressional oversight inquiry and a criminal investigation by New Mexico’s attorney general. Even as the fentanyl crisis subsided from its peak, it continued to batter New Mexico. Officials in Albuquerque recently declared a state of emergency and last week sued the Justice Department for $153 million in damages. Overdose deaths from fentanyl, much of it crudely manufactured in Mexico, spiked 44% last year in the state.

A DEA spokesperson declined to answer questions because of an ongoing review by the U.S. Justice Department’s inspector general. The agency previously said its decisions were lawful and coordinated with federal prosecutors to prevent public harm. DEA spokesperson Amanda Wozniak wrote, “Our response has not changed.” The U.S. attorney’s office in New Mexico and the Justice Department also declined to answer questions.

Tessa DuBerry, a U.S. attorney’s office spokesperson, wrote in an emailed statement, “The Department of Justice is committed to using every lawful tool available to identify, disrupt and dismantle the organizations responsible for trafficking fentanyl into our communities.” New Mexico Gov. Michelle Lujan Grisham, a Democrat, said in an interview, “This is like trying to prevent my house from burning down by negotiating with an arsonist.” Referring to the DEA, she added, “They are fully engaged in the cover-up because they know they went too far and it’s wrong.”

It remained unclear why the DEA took down the ring when it did. After agents made arrests and raided stash houses, then-Attorney General Pam Bondi touted the record bust of 4 million pills as evidence that the Justice Department took the fentanyl threat seriously. At a May 2025 news conference, Bondi removed a small glass vial from her pocket to emphasize the drug’s dangers. “It looks like a few grains of salt,” she said. “This is what it takes to kill you.”
