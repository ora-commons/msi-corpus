## Rockstar tribunal enters closing submissions in union dispute

Rockstar employees across departments are voting this week on whether the union they have built with colleagues should receive recognition. Nestor Uriarte, an animation programmer at the Edinburgh studio, is backing the yes campaign in a first-person column published by The Guardian, saying recognition would give employees a meaningful say over conditions at the company.

Uriarte said that as a child he sneaked into his brother’s room to play Grand Theft Auto: Vice City Stories, and that receiving a job offer from Rockstar later left him thrilled. He described the release of GTA VI as the culmination of more than a decade of effort and called the game “the most anticipated game of all time.” His column also characterized it as “the world’s biggest video game.”

The union is the IWGB Game Workers Union, which Uriarte said was first founded by Rockstar workers among others in 2018 and now represents thousands of members across 300 UK studios. He said the ballot may be many workers’ first introduction to unions, noting that game workers began organizing in greater numbers only over the past decade as they confronted mounting industry challenges.

Some colleagues were nervous that the union would be an outside troublemaking force that threatened the company. Uriarte rejected that view: “We’re not outsiders. We are hundreds of studio employees who live for Rockstar, its output, and the players.” He also argued that recognition at what he called one of the biggest and most influential game studios in the world could open the door for workers across the UK to imagine an industry where employees’ voices carry more weight.

Uriarte cited Grand Theft Auto V’s more than 230 million copies and more than $10bn (£7.6bn) in worldwide revenue, a total he said surpassed any film in Hollywood’s history. He said GTA VI was expected to break those records. Behind the figures, he said, were thousands of workers whose labor made the games possible. He argued that a recognized union could help every department receive a fair share of that success and wrote: “Our games are industry-leading. Our wages, conditions and bonuses should be too.”

Game workers have come to expect intense overtime before launches and mass redundancies afterward, Uriarte wrote. He said Rockstar faced allegations of “crunch culture” in previous years and that employees had already seen some overtime pay introduced. He argued union recognition could prevent a return to the system and ensure “that every hour worked in our industry is an hour paid.”

Uriarte said Rockstar had given no indication that layoffs were coming after the release, but that it was difficult not to worry about the future. He cited an estimate that studios had cut 24,000 roles globally over the past two years and said workers across the sector were unable to resist waves of redundancies until they organized. He said even employees working on GTA VI remained concerned about being laid off.

The rapid emergence of AI is transforming how studios work and raising questions about the industry’s future, Uriarte said. He warned that skills workers spend years developing risk being devalued as creative work is replaced by AI. Rockstar has said it is committed to preserving jobs and that its games will continue to be made by people. Uriarte said union recognition would give employees a seat at the table to manage the industry-wide transition together.

The ballot will coincide with closing submissions in an ongoing employment tribunal over alleged union-busting at Rockstar, following the sudden dismissal of 31 union members last October. A photograph accompanying Uriarte’s column showed protesters at an employment tribunal in Glasgow on 10 September 2026.

Rockstar insists the dismissals were for gross misconduct unconnected with union activity. The company said it makes “the best games possible by giving our talented teams world-class work environments and ongoing career opportunities.” Rockstar also said employee retention is above the industry standard.

Uriarte said the union had grown from strength to strength regardless of the tribunal’s outcome and that every employee should have a hand in shaping Rockstar’s work environments. He called the ballot pivotal for what he described as the UK’s biggest game studio and compared game workers with dockers, miners and teachers, saying unionizing should not be something workers merely admire from afar. “The future of the industry shouldn’t be a game played over our heads,” he wrote.
