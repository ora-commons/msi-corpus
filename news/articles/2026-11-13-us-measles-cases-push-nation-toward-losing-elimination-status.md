## Thirty-nine states fall below the 95% MMR coverage needed for herd immunity

Confirmed measles cases across the United States this year climbed to 5,412 as of Nov. 12, more than double the 2,289 cases reported through all of 2025, according to the latest release of data from the federal Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. The trend is pushing the nation to the brink of losing its official measles elimination status, held for more than 25 years. A panel of experts at the Pan American Health Organization was reviewing cases Friday.

"It's pretty clear we're hitting one grim milestone after the other," Dr. Susan Kansagra, chief medical officer of the Association of State and Territorial Health Officials, a group representing local health officials, told Stateline. "It's really, really just a hard time to see something that we know has been eliminated in the past, and to see this rapid growth that we have seen over these last two years."

The United States launched a national campaign to eliminate measles in the 1960s, around the time the first official measles vaccine was licensed to the public. In 1994, the Pan American Health Organization, a regional office of the World Health Organization representing states across the Americas, set a goal of ending the measles epidemic through a concerted vaccination effort, and by 2000 a panel of experts had concluded that the United States had eliminated measles from its population.

That elimination status means the nation is able to squash instances of measles transmission for 12 months or more. To reverse that status, measles must be spreading for 12 months or more, and health officials say that is where the country is headed.

Katrine Wallace, an epidemiologist at the University of Illinois, Chicago School of Public Health, said sustained transmission would make the disease endemic again. "And that means the disease has become what we call endemic again, meaning it's just spreading in the community," Wallace told Stateline. "I definitely blame it on the anti-vaccination kind of propaganda that's been all over, and the lack of our (U.S. Department of Health and Human Services) really doing anything about these cases."

"There's not been a real push for vaccination efforts to counteract the 5,000 cases or something that we have now," Wallace said.

Measles is a highly contagious respiratory virus that leads to symptoms of fever, rash and other serious health complications, including death, but is entirely avoidable through the two-dose MMR, or measles, mumps and rubella vaccine. Children, who generally don't get vaccinated before a year of age, are highly susceptible.

Among all cases this year, 96% are among people who are unvaccinated, and 11%, or 591 cases, have resulted in hospitalizations. The CDC has confirmed six measles-related deaths so far, though other investigations are continuing within individual states.

According to the latest CDC data, measles cases have been reported this year across 47 states, plus the District of Columbia, and New York City. Among those cases, 58% of them, or 3,134, are among people under the age of 19.

In his time in charge, U.S. Health and Human Services Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. has been an outspoken vaccine skeptic who opposes many vaccine mandates. His agency has called into question the safety and efficacy of vaccinations and recommended fewer vaccines for children. Many Republican-led states have ended vaccine mandates for their populations. Thirty-nine states now have MMR vaccination rates below 95% — the percentage necessary for herd immunity.

Pennsylvania is experiencing a major outbreak, with 1,542 cases confirmed so far this year, including 37 cases since Nov. 9, according to the state's health department. Just since Election Day, Pennsylvania has reported two "measles-associated" deaths, the most recent one announced Nov. 13. Two other deaths, in New York and Wisconsin, have been reported by state health agencies during the same period.

"Revoking our elimination status doesn't really change the reality on the ground, right? We all know, you know, that we have measles in our community," said Dr. Ali Khan, a medical epidemiologist and dean of the College of Public Health at the University of Nebraska Medical Center.

"Measles has always been the sort of ultimate stress test for public health infrastructure," Khan said in an interview. "And we're failing that test … if you can't get people vaccinated, that says something about the quality of your health system."

Khan said the real message behind the potential revocation of the elimination status is that the nation's leaders need to fund and promote public health infrastructure in communities, so that people can protect themselves.

Kansagra, who works with local and state health departments, said it is particularly worrisome that major federal public health grant programs and pandemic-era funds are expiring at the end of next year. "That type of core infrastructure support for public health is so important because these are exactly the types of threats that public health is dealing with and working to respond to," Kansagra said.

Wallace said she is hopeful that Democrats, especially ones steeped in public health like Michigan Senator and Dr. Abdul El-Sayed, can change the direction of the country's public health policy in time to prevent more death and spread of disease. "We're voting these people in," Wallace said. "So it seems like the country is moving towards wanting to rein this in."
