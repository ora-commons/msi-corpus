## Nvidia has agreed to buy an estimated $10 billion of Anthropic stock

Anthropic is pressing ahead with a Thursday initial public offering that could raise more than $60 billion, a haul that could rival SpaceX's June offering depending on the ultimate pricing and other issues. The Claude chatbot maker is proceeding even as investors express concern about an AI bubble, borrowing costs rise and newly elected Democrats pledge tighter regulation of the industry.

Technology stocks have performed well so far this year, but their path higher has been rocky, particularly for AI-related companies. CoreWeave illustrates the swings. The company buys chips and then rents out access to them to customers over the cloud. Its shares plunged 15.5% on Oct. 14 as investors feared the company might not be able to borrow enough money, or borrow it cheaply enough, to fund growth. The stock then surged 25% on Wednesday after an encouraging earnings report before plunging 7% the next day. Most stocks move no more than 2% on an average trading day.

Sector volatility was compounded by Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang's abrupt departure over extramarital affairs, including one with an alleged Chinese spy. Huang built Nvidia into a linchpin of the industry and a major financial backer of other AI companies. Nvidia stock dropped 4% Monday, dragging the broader S&P 500 down for a third consecutive day.

Against that backdrop, Nvidia has agreed to buy an estimated $10 billion of Anthropic stock after the IPO. Nvidia also sells chips to Anthropic, though Anthropic purchases from AMD and other suppliers.

Volatility can cut both ways for a planned IPO: it could signal that the much-debated AI bubble may burst soon, which would make raising capital now more attractive.

Borrowing has become more expensive, partly because of inflation fears and partly because AI companies' massive cash requirements have drawn comparisons with the Gilded Age spending boom on railroads. Lenders fear that some AI companies have taken on too much debt to expand while lacking a clear path to profitability.

Anthropic's financial filings acknowledge that its $6.2 billion in debt is “substantial.” The company remains unprofitable under standard accounting rules, leaving its resources limited for paying interest and principal. It has ample cash but will likely run through it quickly if it cannot raise more soon, given its outsized spending plans. Anthropic said it has signed leases and other deals requiring $900 billion in computing and infrastructure spending in coming years, including $70 billion in the next 12 months.

Political pressure is another complication. Newly elected Democrats have pledged tighter regulation of the AI industry amid a national backlash against data centers, giving new impetus to a congressional effort begun last year for a national moratorium on new facilities. Texas and Georgia, two big states that offer incentives for data centers, have elected Democratic governors who have vowed to stop new data center construction.

Postponing an IPO at the last minute would not be unprecedented. After volatility following President Donald Trump's “Liberation Day” tariffs in April 2025, several companies postponed their initial offerings, including the online ticket marketplace StubHub and the buy-now, pay-later business Klarna. Australian data center company Firmus called off its IPO last month because of market jitters. Anthropic's Thursday sale is scheduled before the newly elected Democrats take office in January, and Anthropic could be betting that selling now is best before tighter regulation begins in earnest.

Demand for IPOs can also disappear quickly. Nearly 400 companies went public in the United States in 2021. When investors grew fearful of a possible recession the following year, demand dried up and only 71 companies made market debuts.

The possible payoff may be substantial if public-market enthusiasm holds. Robert Shiller's measure, which compares share prices with corporate profits over the previous decade, shows stocks have been more expensive only once before, during the 2000 dot-com crash. They are more highly valued now than on the eve of the 2008-09 Financial Crisis.

Stocks currently trade at 40 times their long-term earnings under Shiller's measure, a third more expensive than the average going back before 1900. That pricing backdrop could help Anthropic fetch a high price for its shares this week, though it could also leave IPO buyers purchasing a richly valued stock.

If the offering proceeds, Anthropic also would reach the market ahead of rival OpenAI. That could give Anthropic an advantage if investors decide they already have too much exposure to the sector and do not want to buy the next publicly traded AI company.

For Anthropic, a successful sale would supply substantial capital as the company carries $6.2 billion in debt and has $900 billion in computing and infrastructure commitments. For prospective buyers, the same transaction would place money behind an unprofitable issuer while AI stocks remain volatile and the policy environment for data centers may change in January. The offering therefore tests both public-market demand for Anthropic and the scale of its financing needs.
