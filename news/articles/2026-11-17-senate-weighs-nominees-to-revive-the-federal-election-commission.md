## A quorum of four is needed before the FEC can act

The Senate Rules and Administration Committee held a confirmation hearing Tuesday for President Donald Trump’s Republican nominees Ashley Stow and Andrew Woodson to join the Federal Election Commission. The session marked the first public step toward reviving the campaign finance watchdog after the agency was sidelined during the 2026 midterm elections and lacked a quorum for more than a year.

Only two commissioners, both Democrats, are serving. The FEC needs a bipartisan quorum of at least four to take official action, but without one it cannot act on complaints or issue advisory opinions. Its backlog has grown, leaving candidates and political groups uncertain when, or whether, the agency will respond to alleged violations of federal campaign finance law.

Sen. Bill Hagerty, a Tennessee Republican who chaired the Senate Rules and Administration Committee hearing, said, “Restoring the commission’s ability to function is especially important following the 2026 midterm elections.” Hagerty pointed to the backlog of complaints and preparations for the 2028 presidential race. Restoring a quorum will allow the FEC “to resume its essential responsibilities,” he said.

The FEC enforces federal campaign finance law, assists candidates and donors in complying with it and makes a vast trove of campaign finance data public. The commission marked its 50th anniversary in 2025.

The hearing came as Republicans sought to end the FEC’s dormancy during the Senate’s lame-duck session, before Democrats take control of the chamber in January. After the change in control, Democrats will have much more power to demand that the White House nominate individuals acceptable to Democrats or block Trump’s nominees.

Democrats support restoring the quorum but question whether Trump would permit commissioners to act independently. Sen. Alex Padilla, a California Democrat and the Senate Rules and Administration Committee’s ranking member who is likely to chair the panel in January, said the FEC was established in the post-Watergate era to be evenly balanced between Democrats and Republicans and politically independent.

“It’s absolutely critical that any commissioners who sign on to help lead this agency commit to restoring and defending that independence. Full stop,” Padilla said.

In February 2025, Trump fired Democratic FEC Chair Ellen Weintraub, who had served as a commissioner since 2002. Weintraub has said she does not view the firing as legally valid, though she has not challenged the dismissal in court.

The move against Weintraub came after Republican Sean Cooksey resigned before Trump’s inauguration. Republican Allen Dickerson resigned in April 2025, creating the lack of quorum that has persisted.

The Trump administration has broadly sought to bring independent agencies created by Congress under White House control. The U.S. Supreme Court in June allowed the president to fire members of the Federal Trade Commission without cause. It remains unclear how that decision applies to removals from the FEC.

Stow is a longtime FEC lawyer from Texas. Woodson is a Virginia lawyer focused on campaign finance at Wiley Rein LLP who worked at the FEC from 2013 to 2015. The two Republican nominees emphasized the agency’s bipartisan nature and promised to act impartially.

“Should I be confirmed, I pledge to discharge my duties with impartiality and unwavering commitment to the First Amendment and enforcement of the law as enacted by Congress and interpreted by the nation’s courts,” Stow said.

Woodson said the FEC must always act with sensitivity for First Amendment rights and that its guidance and regulations should “promote freedom, not frustration.”

The Republican nominees did not specify how they would act toward Trump’s political operation, MAGA, Inc., because potential complaints about the super PAC could come before the commission. Woodson said, “Everyone that will come before me, I will treat exactly the same — impartially,” whether they hold “the highest office in the land” or are “just an ordinary congressperson.”

Trump nominated the two Republicans, Stow and Woodson, in February, but Tuesday’s hearing was the Senate’s first public step toward advancing their nominations. On Nov. 5, a dozen former Republican FEC commissioners wrote to Senate Majority Leader John Thune, a South Dakota Republican, asking him to prioritize their confirmations before the end of the current Congress.

The former commissioners called the FEC’s situation “increasingly untenable.” They also said hackers had targeted the agency, crippling its online system for reporting campaign finance information during the closing days of the midterm elections.

The group called the incident a “stark reminder” that the FEC maintains critical systems that provide campaign finance information to the public. “An agency carrying responsibilities of that magnitude should have a functioning Commission at its head,” the former commissioners wrote.

Tuesday’s hearing placed the Republican nominees’ promises of impartiality on the record, but the FEC still lacked the quorum required to act. Democrats’ concern over whether Trump would allow commissioners to act independently also remained unresolved.
