## Danish parliament ratifies Greenland security agreement with US

**Subtype:** fact

- Denmark's parliament unanimously ratified an Arctic security agreement with Greenland and the United States.
- The security agreement granted the United States underwater access to Greenland.
- The security agreement also granted the United States permission to construct a missile-defense system.
- Greenland's parliament approved the agreement earlier in November.
- President Donald Trump still must notify Congress before the agreement can enter into force, according to Danish Foreign Minister Lars Løkke Rasmussen.

## Congress notification remains the final step for Arctic agreement

The Danish parliament unanimously ratified the Arctic security agreement on Thursday, with U.S. Ambassador to Denmark Ken Howery saying the vote was 109-0. The decision followed approval by Greenland's parliament earlier in November and leaves the pact awaiting a notification from President Donald Trump to the U.S. Congress.

The agreement was signed in September after months of talks by President Donald Trump, Greenlandic Prime Minister Jens-Frederik Nielsen and Danish Prime Minister Mette Frederiksen. It updates a 1951 defence agreement and gives the United States underwater access to Greenland.

The pact also gives the United States specific permission to construct a missile-defense system. Its terms recognize Greenland, a former Danish colony and a largely autonomous territory, as an equal partner. If Greenland becomes fully independent of Denmark, the agreement stipulates that it would remain in Nato.

Løkke Rasmussen said the Danish vote left only Trump's notification to Congress before the agreement could take effect.

“We have now resolved our part of the equation,” Løkke Rasmussen said after the bill was passed. “This is a positive development, as it removes all uncertainty surrounding Greenland and establishes a proper framework for future cooperation.”

The Danish foreign minister nevertheless said trust between the United States and its Arctic partners had been damaged.

“Consequently, there is a significant task of rebuilding ahead. But this will take place within the framework of an agreement, which is a good thing,” he said.

Before the pact was signed, Trump said the United States would “immediately begin the process of developing a large military presence in the appropriate part of Greenland”. On Thursday, Løkke Rasmussen said the United States had yet to inform Denmark of any plans to establish new military bases.

Howery said on social media that he was pleased by the unanimous vote. In a statement, he said: “On behalf of the United States, I welcome the strong support in Denmark’s parliament for the ratification of the historic US-Denmark-Greenland defence agreement, paving the way for it to enter into force.”

He added: “The agreement is a tremendous accomplishment, one that modernises our partnership for the security challenges of the future and bolsters the security of the United States, the kingdom of Denmark, including Greenland, and all of Nato.”

The agreement followed months of tension between the United States, Denmark and Greenland over Trump's repeated threats to take over the largely autonomous Arctic territory. The threats prompted Denmark to prepare for a possible U.S. invasion and placed strain on relations among the three governments and the Nato alliance.

Copenhagen reportedly sent blood bags and explosives to blow up runways in case of a U.S. attack, reflecting the threat it perceived in January.

The agreement now has legislative approval in Greenland and Denmark, but it has not entered into force because Congress notification remains pending. Its terms establish rules for underwater access and missile-defense construction, while the location and scale of any additional U.S. military presence remain unspecified.
