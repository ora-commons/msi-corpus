## About half of Venezuela's debt obligations stem from PdVSA

Venezuela’s interim authorities disclosed Tuesday that the country’s national debt load was $237 billion, a significantly higher-than-expected figure that underscored the financial challenges facing the U.S.-backed government. The amount appeared in a 98-page economic report titled “Venezuela Reborn,” which said the debt was far greater than the roughly $180 billion that Wall Street analysts and independent economists had long estimated. Venezuela has one of the highest debt-to-GDP ratios in the world.

The disclosure prepares the stage for one of the largest and most complex sovereign-debt restructurings in modern history. Since U.S. commandos captured Venezuela’s former leader, Nicolás Maduro, in January, acting President Delcy Rodríguez has forged a partnership with the Trump administration and made refinancing Venezuela’s loans a priority.

Some economists and former government advisers have worried that the debt figure would be inflated, allowing insiders to skim from the eventual restructuring. Hermes Pérez, an economist and professor at the Metropolitan University in Caracas, called the disclosed total “massive, it’s outrageous, and it needs to be closely analyzed.” He said, “Without a serious auditing, it’s going to be very hard to refinance the debt.”

The report said, “Venezuela’s public debt is in a state of protracted default and is unsustainable, reflecting the constraints imposed by financial sanctions, the decline in hydrocarbon revenues and years of macroeconomic imbalances.” The financial report marks one of the most comprehensive data releases yet by a government that hasn’t published audited economic reports in more than a decade. Rodríguez and her allies, despite leading an unelected caretaker administration, say executing a speedy debt renegotiation is vital to reopening the economy to international credit markets and restoring the confidence needed to attract overseas investment.

The report painted a picture that was more dire than many economists anticipated. The numbers offer “a bare minimum starting point and gives us a general view of how authorities are perceiving the country’s financial status,” said Giorgio Cunto, an economist in the capital, Caracas. He also said, “We still can’t say that Venezuela has the solid financial infrastructure we’d hope for.” He added, “But it’s better than nothing.”

About half of the country’s debt obligations stem from Petróleos de Venezuela SA, or PdVSA, the state oil company that has long been Venezuela’s main income generator. “At some point you have to ask yourself if it’s better to just liquidate that company and start from scratch,” Pérez said.

Among the largest arrears reported Tuesday were $110 billion in euro-denominated bonds, including principal and interest that the government stopped paying years earlier. The country also owes nearly $17 billion to China for development loans, $5 billion to Russia and more than $26 billion to foreign companies that sued the government years earlier in international arbitration courts to seek compensation for nationalized assets.

Pérez said he was most surprised by nearly $67 billion in so-called nonfinancial debts. The category includes unspecified government promissory notes, debts to suppliers, pensions and IOUs between various state agencies.

The restructuring is part of a broader economic recovery program that aims to double gross domestic product to about $200 billion in the next five years, the report said. Rodríguez’s government says it is working to dig itself out of the debt hole by advancing a U.S.-supported plan to reopen Venezuela’s massive oil and gas reserves to foreign investors after years of isolation.

The government said it would raise average public-sector wages to $561 a month by 2032 from $200 a month in January.

The government aims to nearly triple oil output to about 2.9 million barrels a day by 2036, the report said.


[MSI previously reported](/articles/2026-01-22-venezuela-advances-oil-reform-allowing-private-company-investment/) that Venezuela’s legislature had advanced legislation opening the oil sector to private investment and international arbitration for investment disputes. Tuesday’s disclosure quantifies the debt the government must address under that reform push as it seeks foreign capital, though economist Hermes Pérez said the total still needed close analysis and a serious audit before refinancing.
