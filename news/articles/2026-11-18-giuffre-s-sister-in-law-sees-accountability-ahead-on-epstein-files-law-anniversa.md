## Bensky said an unredacted memo exposed her phone number

On the one-year anniversary of the House's passage of the Epstein Files Transparency Act, Annie Farmer and Dani Bensky described a law that required the Justice Department to release millions of files connected to the investigation as an effort to shed light on the crimes, but the release also put names and phone numbers of some women into public view when the files were made public last year. NPR's Ava Berger spoke with Farmer, Bensky and Amanda Roberts about the release's consequences and Roberts's statement that continued momentum could produce real accountability for all abusers.

Farmer said Jeffrey Epstein and Ghislaine Maxwell abused her when she was 16. Bensky said she met Epstein when she was 17. Roberts identified herself as a survivor of childhood sexual abuse and said she was Virginia Roberts Giuffre's sister-in-law. Roberts described Giuffre as one of the most prominent survivors of Epstein and Maxwell, while host Sacha Pfeiffer called her one of Epstein's most outspoken accusers and described Epstein as a convicted sex offender.

Farmer described the previous 12 months and the effort to pass the legislation as "very busy and very chaotic." Bensky said she sat in the House gallery and watched the votes change to yes. "It was so surreal to sit there and just watch the board and watch everything start to flip to yes," she said, adding that "tears were streaming as we passed this new law." Roberts said the pain survivors carried gave way to unity and courage that "drove this entire year."

Farmer called the day the files were released "The moment of joy that comes to mind for me from this year." She tied that reaction to growing public awareness of both the extent of the crimes and the amount of information the government had gathered about them, which she said created a sense of validation.

Bensky said she later found her own name in a memo pad in the released material, along with her phone number. "It certainly wasn't redacted, as it had my phone number in it," she said. Bensky said she and other survivors could find the names of many victims and survivors but could not find the names of perpetrators and predators. She said the disparity made survivors feel that perpetrators were protected and that the situation felt awful. Pfeiffer said the release effort had, in some cases, revictimized the women Epstein abused by making their names and phone numbers public.

"There are a thousand girls, more than a thousand girls and women that have been abused in those files," Bensky said. She said the records could involve powerful people but also represented "over a thousand lives that have been completely derailed."

Roberts said there was "such an appetite for justice in this community, in our country right now." She said she believed that keeping the momentum moving would produce "real accountability for all abusers out there." Roberts described a broader societal shift under which power, position and money would not shield abusers and everyone would be held accountable to the same laws and rules.

Farmer said she hoped for a future in which she never had to speak the names Epstein and Maxwell again. She said many survivors wanted their lives to move forward and to spend less time looking back at what had happened.

While crying, Roberts also addressed Giuffre directly. "If Virginia was here to see this, I would tell her that I'm so proud," Roberts said, adding, "I wish that you would have held on just a little bit longer to see this." Roberts said she understood why Giuffre felt she could not continue because she "had been failed by every single system in her life." Pfeiffer said Giuffre died by suicide last year.
