## U.S. loses elimination status after transmission continued for more than a year

On November 17, the American Academy of Pediatrics joined nearly 150 other healthcare organizations in a letter urging Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. and CDC Director Erica Schwartz to step up federal leadership in the fight against measles.

The action came as the Pan American Health Organization announced that measles is no longer considered eliminated in the United States, classifying the disease as endemic after transmission continued without interruption for more than a year. The status change erased a public-health milestone that took decades to achieve and that the country had held for 26 years. The United States is reporting more than 5,400 measles cases and 10 associated deaths.

Technically, the loss of elimination status means measles has been circulating in the United States without interruption for more than a year. NPR health correspondent Maria Godoy called it a blow to America's standing as a global health leader and said it raises questions about the country's ability to meet other infectious-disease challenges.

Dr. Amesh Adalja of the Johns Hopkins Center for Health Security said, "It's about what it signifies in terms of the United States' ability to control infectious diseases in the current environment. It's more emblematic of the fact that this is backsliding, that this is not an advance of civilization but a decline of civilization."

Godoy attributed the loss to falling childhood vaccination rates. The rates had been sliding for years, and the decline accelerated during the pandemic. The share of children with nonmedical exemptions to school vaccination requirements has since hit an all-time high, while nationwide kindergarten vaccination rates have dropped below the 95% threshold needed to prevent measles from spreading.

Godoy said many communities now have enough unvaccinated people for the virus to spread quickly. Measles is so contagious that 9 out of 10 people without immunity will get infected.

Godoy said she thought the public health community had expected the announcement because measles cases had surged since early 2025. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention issued a statement in which Director Erica Schwartz said, "The measles elimination designation is not what protects Americans. Vaccination does."

The American Academy of Pediatrics described the loss as a preventable failure to protect children. Dr. Adam Ratner, a member of the Academy's Committee on Infectious Diseases, said, "It's sad that we're at a point where we have children who are getting hospitalized and children who are dying from a disease that we know very well how to prevent."

The healthcare organizations asked for more support for state and local public health departments and much stronger federal messaging about the need to vaccinate against measles. The coalition also asked the CDC to remove information from its website that falsely implies a link between vaccines and autism. The organizations said they were ready to work with the administration.

To win back elimination status, the United States must submit a plan in the next few weeks, stop transmission of measles for at least 12 months and then provide proof to the Pan American Health Organization, Godoy said.

Godoy summarized the public-health response this way: "The bottom line, though, is we know how to stop measles. It's all about vaccination."
