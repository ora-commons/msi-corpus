## Auto suppliers weigh thin margins, reputational risks and uncertain military demand

The Pentagon is holding preliminary discussions with General Motors and other manufacturers about supplying parts for military drones, according to people familiar with the matter. Defense officials have met with leaders at Ford Motor, Magna International and manufacturers outside the automotive industry. People involved described the discussions as preliminary, and no deals have been finalized.

The drone effort expands a broader Defense Department push to enlist automakers and their established supply chains in military production. Defense Department leaders have spent months working with automotive companies on everything from missiles to tactical trucks, and they now want those companies to help ease shortages of unmanned systems, including surveillance and strike drones used in global conflicts.

Under potential arrangements, automotive companies would supply parts to drone companies. The parts under discussion include actuators, engines, sensors and lidar. General Motors is weighing whether to manufacture drone motors closer in size to something that would power a riding lawn mower than a car.

The Pentagon says it plans to buy millions of drones in the coming years. Defense Department leaders say many American drone companies are small and lack sufficient manufacturing experience or capacity to work at that scale.

The United States has only begun to onshore some of this manufacturing, resulting in slow and expensive building processes for American drone makers that need to meet military specifications. China has dominated the global drone industry for more than a decade and has been the primary or lone supplier for many parts.

Automotive executives have given mixed reactions to the Trump administration's outreach, people involved said. Drones, particularly the small and expendable models that have come to define the war in Ukraine, are a low-margin business that automakers may lose money on. Companies also worry defense work does not fit their core operations.

The companies also cited reputational risk from association with weapons production, choppy demand from the Defense Department and uncertainty over annual congressional approval of funding. Ford has steered clear of weapons manufacturing, instead supplying the Pentagon with vehicles and equipment more closely aligned with its existing commercial lineup.

Potential political pitfalls have grown since the midterm elections, with Democratic scrutiny of Pentagon officials and the deals they have struck with private industry.

To consolidate the effort, the Pentagon is running it through a four-star combatant command created this fall by Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth. The command aims to consolidate drone production and development across the military branches. The department's theory is that bigger contracts are more likely to be profitable because of their scale.

U.S. Navy SEAL Senior Chief Petty Officer Max Strasiser, who was appointed to help set up the drone initiative, said, “We had to unify our buying power to put out a demand signal that our partners could get behind.” He also said, “In order to get them in, there has to be a dollar amount that justifies them switching over a factory.”

The Pentagon sees the military drone market reaching $20 billion to $30 billion annually. People familiar with the matter said the department is prepared to offer automotive companies large sums of money.

Strasiser said military drone development can be convoluted and costly, deterring companies from participating. At one point, six separate processes within the Defense Department were underway to develop a one-way attack drone. Each sought a system designed to stay airborne while searching or waiting for a target, but varying specifications complicated the work.

General Motors has shown the strongest interest among the automakers, according to people familiar with the matter, following requests from high-ranking defense officials. GM said in a statement that it has demonstrated a commitment to helping reinforce the U.S. defense-industrial base. Earlier this year, the Detroit automaker signed an agreement to manufacture housing components for Lockheed Martin's Patriot air-defense missiles and is in talks with RTX and L3Harris Technologies on similar deals, people familiar said.

Some smaller automotive companies are also exploring the sector. Family-owned Michigan auto-parts supplier Lacks Enterprises entered the drone space in April with an equity stake in a defense contractor. A company representative said Lacks had held talks with about 30 drone manufacturers and was starting to produce components.

Washington has also considered tapping automakers to help manufacture the Lucas drone, modeled on Iran's successful Shahed, a former senior defense official said. Under that approach, the Pentagon would own the design and farm out production to companies with established assembly lines.
