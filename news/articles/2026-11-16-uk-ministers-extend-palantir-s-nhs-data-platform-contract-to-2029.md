## MPs criticise the decision not to exercise the 2027 break clause

Ministers have decided to end the NHS in England's relationship with Palantir, the US technology firm run by the Donald Trump-supporting US tech billionaire Peter Thiel, but not until 2029. The Department of Health and Social Care has chosen to extend Palantir's contract to provide the federated data platform — the software that links different healthcare providers — for two more years rather than exercise a break clause that would have let them end it next spring.

Ministers have come under growing pressure to end the NHS in England's relationship with Palantir. Labour and Liberal Democrat MPs criticised Palantir being given a further two years.

James Frith, a health minister, said Palantir's role would end in 2029 to boost public trust in NHS tech, end its "over-dependence" on one supplier and let UK companies start providing crucial digital infrastructure. The Department of Health and Social Care will in the meantime select UK tech firms to replace it, and will soon invite British tech outfits to bid to be part of whoever replaces Palantir.

"The current contract will remain for two years, before coming to an end," Frith told MPs. He added: "A process this important must command public trust as well as clinical confidence."

Chi Onwurah, the chair of the Commons science and technology select committee, said the decision went against her committee's repeated calls. "My committee has repeatedly called on the government to exercise the 2027 break clause in Palantir's federated data platform contract with the NHS," Onwurah said. "It's deeply disappointing to hear that it won't do so and is instead choosing to extend the contract for two years, during which time it says it will procure a UK-based supplier."

Martin Wrigley, a Liberal Democrat MP, said the government had finally admitted the contract had to end but had delayed the consequence. "After months of campaigning by the Liberal Democrats, the government has finally admitted that the Palantir contract has to end," Wrigley said. "However, ministers have kicked the can down the road to March 2029. That leaves a company co-founded by Trump ally Peter Thiel, and criticised by human rights groups over its work with ICE [the US Immigration and Customs Enforcement agency], holding the keys to our health data for years to come."

Campaign groups such as Amnesty International and Just Treatment have been urging ministers to activate the break clause in February in the contract that NHS England agreed with Palantir in 2023. Photographs accompanying the Guardian's report showed campaigners protesting outside the Bristol Royal Infirmary against NHS England's data contract with Palantir.

Adoption of the platform inside the health service has been contested. Many NHS care trusts have declined to use the federated data platform, amid widespread doubts in the service that it is as good as Palantir insists. NHS England last week deleted a claim from its website that 137 trusts were benefiting from the platform after the Guardian disclosed that 29 were using it never or rarely.

Ben Coleman, a Labour member of the Commons health select committee who last week urged ministers to replace Palantir with a group of British firms, welcomed the end of the contract while warning about the transition. "I'm glad the government is ending reliance on a single supplier and shaking off the Palantir albatross. This must be done without disrupting patient care or creating new dependence [on a new single supplier]," Coleman said.

Amnesty International said the decision was "a victory for healthcare workers, patients and campaigners who refused to give up". Tom Guha, the organisation's crisis campaigner, tied the decision to Palantir's conduct beyond the NHS contract: "Doctors and nurses … should never have to work in a health service that partners with a company supporting a military committing genocide. Palantir has openly acknowledged supporting Israel's military in 'war-related missions', while Israel is committing genocide against Palestinians in Gaza. 'First, do no harm' should apply not just to NHS staff, but to the companies the NHS contracts with," Guha said.
