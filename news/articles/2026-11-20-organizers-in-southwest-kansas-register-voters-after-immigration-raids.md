## Johnson County turnout tops 300,000; Holscher wins more than 60%

After the Nov. 3 election, Kansas had elected a Democratic senator for the first time since 1932, kept the governorship in Democratic hands and ousted Republican Attorney General Kris Kobach. The News From The States analysis, by Claudia Amaro and produced by Planeta Venus, described the victories of Adam Hamilton, Cindy Holscher and Chris Mann as a coalition that united the strength of cities with gains in southwest communities, where immigration raids had become a major local issue.

Adam Hamilton's victory over Sen. Roger Marshall ended nearly a century without Kansas electing a Democrat to the U.S. Senate. Hamilton, a pastor of a Methodist congregation in the Kansas City area, ran a campaign that reached beyond his party's traditional strongholds. AP attributed his victory to strength in Johnson County and change in the southwest. A Kansas Reflector photograph by Anna Kaminski showed Sen.-elect Hamilton delivering his victory speech on Nov. 3, 2026, in Overland Park after he defeated Republican incumbent Sen. Marshall in the 2026 midterm election.

The gubernatorial race was much closer. NPR's tally updated Nov. 13 listed 552,328 votes for Holscher, or 50.3%, and 545,128 for Ty Masterson, or 49.7%, a difference of about 7,200 votes. AP declared Holscher the winner on Nov. 12 after several days of counting. Holscher was set to replace Laura Kelly, who could not seek reelection. Her running mate, Wichita Congressman K.C. Ohaebosim, was elected lieutenant governor and became the first African-American official elected to statewide office in Kansas since 1887.

Chris Mann beat Kobach in a rematch of the 2022 Kansas attorney general election. The result carried significance in the immigration debate because Kobach has advised governments on restrictive immigration laws, but the analysis said that relationship did not make it possible to determine how much immigration influenced each vote for Mann.

Post-election testimony in Ford, Finney and Seward counties described people who registered for the first time, voted again or helped mobilize their neighbors after the September immigration raids. The analysis cautioned that attributing the entire statewide result to the Latino vote alone would simplify an election also shaped by suburban voting, the cost of living, candidate profiles and support from some Republican voters.

The analysis said participation did not automatically follow the protests. In Dodge City, community organizers worked to translate the protests into voter registration and turnout. AP described how Amy Soberanes and others encouraged residents to channel their frustration into voting. One account came from José del Real, an Army veteran who had not voted for a decade; after the operations, he called on his neighbors to participate. His case illustrated that mobilization depended not only on new voters but also on people who had abandoned electoral participation.

In Seward County, a New York Times report found that during the three weeks after ICE arrived, about as many people registered to vote as in the previous nine months. The analysis said the data showed an acceleration in registration but did not show whether those people voted for or supported the same candidate.

Organizers from Wichita and Lakin, Kansas, worked to register new Latino voters, mobilizing parents to encourage their U.S.-citizen children to vote. The effort was nonpartisan and supported by The Voter Network, a nonprofit dedicated to strengthening Kansas communities through voter education and engagement. The analysis said the nonprofit's “Voter to Voter” tool, bilingual content and interpretation support at polling locations in Wichita—in areas with a large Latino population—increased participation among new voters, most of whom were children of immigrants.

The analysis identified three ways Latino participation influenced the result: incorporating new voters, reactivating people who had not voted in a long time, and organizing communities around shared experiences. It also noted that Latino presence is closely linked to the labor and demographic history of these communities. The analysis distinguished between showing influence and accurately quantifying it.

The analysis said results from a county with a large Latino population do not automatically reveal how its Latino residents voted, and the total population, voting-age citizen population, registered persons and those who actually cast a ballot are not equivalent figures. According to an AP Voter Poll, more than half of Kansas voters in the Senate race disapproved of the way ICE conducts its work, but that figure reflected the general electorate in that election rather than Latino voters alone. The analysis said Latino mobilization was an important part of the change but worked alongside other sectors of the electorate. The sources verified for the article did not provide sufficiently detailed measures to establish Latino state participation, its distribution by candidate, or how many voters changed parties.

Johnson County was instrumental to Holscher's victory. The scoreboard showed a Democratic lead of about 23 points in the gubernatorial race, and AP noted that Holscher won more than 60% of the county's vote, offsetting wide Republican margins in rural areas. Turnout was elevated as well: KMBC reported that more than 300,000 people voted in Johnson County, the third-highest record in its history, behind only the 2020 and 2024 presidential years. As of Nov. 13, officials had approved approximately 5,000 additional provisional ballots.

The analysis called the relationship between the two geographies essential: suburban communities contributed many votes, while southwest Kansas reduced Republican advantages or produced Democratic victories where there normally were none.

To compare the results, the analysis said the reference point had to be specified. The New York Times reported that Seward County shifted 52 points toward the Democratic nominee in the Senate race compared with the 2024 presidential election, the biggest change on record in Kansas. The analysis cautioned that the shift did not mean 52% of people had changed their votes. The figures did not compare the same office or necessarily the same voters, and they described shifts in party margin rather than individual transfers of votes.

The change also reached the governorship. Holscher won Seward County, which Donald Trump carried with 68% in 2024. According to KCUR, Holscher's victory was the county's first by a Democrat since 1974. Seward stood out for both the size of its Senate shift and its gubernatorial result, the analysis said.

In southwest Kansas, immigration was not only a topic of speeches and campaign advertising. The late-September raids targeted communities linked to feedlots, meatpacking plants and other activities that rely on immigrant labor. AP documented businesses closing, families afraid to leave home and children who stopped attending school. The analysis said the dispute over the border, once distant, began to involve co-workers, neighbors, family members and how communities function.

On Sept. 25, 2026, protesters gathered outside U.S. Rep. Tracey Mann's office in Dodge City, chanting, “Tracey Mann, where are you? We need you?” A Kansas Reflector photograph by Morgan Chilson showed the group protesting U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement actions in the community.

The reaction was not limited to Democrats or Latino residents. AP drew criticism from local Republican leaders for the intensity of the operations and the lack of coordination, and Marshall asked the federal government to consult local authorities before conducting raids.

A Wichita Eagle investigation collected by AP found that none of the five people the government presented as examples of captured criminals had been arrested during that operation: four had been deported months earlier, and the fifth was already in prison. The federal agency reported 104 arrests in the southwest, but local authorities quoted by AP said they had not received a list. The analysis characterized these as findings and journalistic statements, not a judicial resolution.

Immigration alone did not explain the result. Holscher made the cost of living a campaign linchpin and, after winning, said she heard the same concern across the state, that everything costs too much. She also proposed a temporary moratorium on opening new data centers. Reuters identified the rejection of those projects as a relevant factor in the contest. The issue strained alliances, with labor organizations that supported data center development backing Masterson. The analysis said this dimension made it insufficient to interpret each Democratic vote as a referendum only on immigration policy.

Kansas in 2026 leaves two complementary images, the analysis said. In the suburbs, a broad electoral base largely offset Republican advantages in rural territory. In the southwest, immigrant and Latino communities, along with other residents, responded to the raids by organizing and participating politically. The analysis concluded that the Latino vote mattered not because it acted as a uniform bloc but because it influenced competitive communities, narrowed margins and helped change outcomes.
