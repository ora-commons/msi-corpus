## Perlroth warns of cascading failures in systems few people understand

Guardian US columnist Margaret Sullivan, who writes on media, politics and culture, argued that the Trump administration had lost public trust so thoroughly that its spokespeople could not, and should not, be believed after their responses to recent technology outages. She singled out Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy, whose changing explanations for a nationwide flight grounding were disputed by dozens of air-traffic controllers.

Sullivan said the clearest analysis in recent days had come from experts far from the Oval Office rather than Trump or administration members. She singled out Nicole Perlroth, who for years led cybersecurity coverage at the New York Times and wrote the 2021 cyberwarfare book *This is How They Tell Me the World Ends*. Perlroth filed a detailed post that Sullivan contrasted with Trump’s reassurances.

Perlroth’s post responded in part to a Washington Sun report that Wall Street executives and the NSA had told Congress the outages were caused by efforts to quickly repair vulnerable systems identified by AI. Sullivan described the result as a rush to patch known weaknesses that overwhelmed various networks during the initial period of known vulnerability. Perlroth did not dispute that account but went further in her assessment.

Perlroth wrote: “We are now entirely dependent on software systems that almost nobody understands end to end, that increasingly few people can repair, and that attackers can now probe at machine speed.”

She continued: “We are entering a period of unprecedented technological instability … a period of cascading failures, whether triggered by attackers, bad updates or our own accumulated complexity.”

Behind that analysis was President Donald Trump’s response to recent technology outages. Trump dismissed the disruptions as “fine” while answering reporters in the Philippines. In less than a week, Microsoft went down, the FAA grounded all flights, banking systems were roiled and the National Security Agency’s internal network failed. Sullivan said Americans were searching for answers as the sequence raised questions about random coincidence or the start of “digital Armageddon.”

She asked whether the disruptions were random but troubling coincidences or the start of a burgeoning cyberattack that might take everything down. Sullivan wrote that, in normal times, the president and subject-area experts within the administration should have provided serious answers, either warning or reassuring the nation, while acknowledging what they did not know, describing investigations and promising transparency.

She said the responses instead consisted of Trump’s broad dismissal and narrower accounts from Duffy and Secretary of State Marco Rubio. Sullivan argued that Trump and many in his administration were “practiced and inveterate liars” and that falsehood was too often a predictable part of what people heard from on high. She called shrugging and hoping someone else eventually revealed the truth the public’s only reasonable response.

Trump’s answer was: “It’s life … When you’re big and powerful and you have all the money, things like that happen, it’s fine.” Sullivan called the statement glib and said it had a darker side. She said Trump had either declared everything fine or blamed Joe Biden and former Transportation Secretary Pete Buttigieg for problems. She wrote that his statement should not be believed except by “Maga diehards.”

Duffy first cited overloaded switches for the nationwide flight grounding and later said the conversion of copper to fiber-optic equipment at a remote facility had caused the stop. Dozens of air-traffic controllers expressed disagreement after Duffy’s various explanations. Sullivan said it was easier to credit the controllers than Duffy because, at least, they appeared to have some expertise.

She argued that the Trump administration and its spokespeople had lost public trust so thoroughly that they could not, and should not, be believed. To support that judgment, Sullivan cited Glenn Kessler of the Washington Post, who documented tens of thousands of misstatements and falsehoods by Trump during his first administration. She also described Trump’s refusal to admit that Joe Biden won the 2020 election as “the Big Lie” and referred to the deadly insurrection at the US Capitol.

Sullivan invoked German-born political philosopher Hannah Arendt, a student of 20th-century authoritarianism. Arendt wrote: “The result of a consistent and total substitution of lies for factual truth,” … “is that the sense by which we take our bearings in the real world” is destroyed. Sullivan said political disinformation can disorient and confuse a baffled populace, making it more malleable.

Sullivan described Perlroth’s prediction as far removed from Trump’s dismissive response and more sophisticated than Rubio’s assurance that the outages were “not interrelated.” She said Rubio’s assurance could be narrowly true because malign actors did not seem to be involved, as many had feared. In the broader sense, she argued, the breakdowns were interconnected and part of a pattern fueled in part by AI’s growing capabilities.

Sullivan concluded that one piece of good news was that the episode had reminded people whom not to trust or believe during a crisis. “If there’s any good news in this situation, perhaps it’s that we’ve been reminded of whom, in a crisis, not to trust or believe,” she wrote.
