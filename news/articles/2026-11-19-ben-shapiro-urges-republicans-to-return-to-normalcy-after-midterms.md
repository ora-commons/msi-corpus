## Shapiro doubts Vance can hold Trump’s coalition together

Republicans are trying to regroup for 2028 and for possible presidential candidates after President Trump, and Ben Shapiro doubted Vice President Vance could hold together Trump's coalition. He said Vance was extraordinarily online, spent an inordinate amount of time worrying what podcast figures such as Tucker Carlson thought and steered directly into a militantly antiestablishment affect designed to appeal to 21-year-old Joe Rogan listeners. Shapiro said that was not how to win a broad-scale national election. Joe Rogan listeners were important to Trump in 2024, Shapiro said, but Hispanic voters were more important by the numbers.

Shapiro advised Democrats not to read the Republican shellacking as vindication of all their central principles. Instead, he said, Democrats should emphasize consensus measures on affordability, investigate only the most egregious cases they can evidence and “act in thoroughgoingly normal fashion.” His advice to Republicans was that “all the American people want is normal.” He said voters had spent the last 20 years asking for whichever candidate seemed more normal without being offered it.

Conservative commentator Ben Shapiro went on “The Ben Shapiro Show” the day after Republicans lost the midterm elections and asked why President Trump had spent the preceding day at the reflecting pool. Steve Inskeep reported that Shapiro called the result a referendum on Trump and used the next 22 minutes to answer what had happened, citing tariffs, the war in Iran, corruption and a president focused only on himself.

When NPR interviewed Shapiro days later, Inskeep recalled that Shapiro had campaigned for President Trump only two years earlier. Shapiro said, “I regret the way the president has performed. I don't regret my vote,” adding, “I don't have a time machine.” He said he had spent the last 20 years facing binary choices he did not particularly like.

Shapiro traced his own political choices: he voted for neither candidate in 2015 and 2016, endorsed Trump in 2020 and was extremely critical during the election-denialism phase at the end of that year. He said Joe Biden had done a terrible job, Kamala Harris had been a horrible nominee and he campaigned for Trump in 2024. Shapiro said none of that represented a fundamental shift in his values, that his feelings about President Trump had not really changed very much in certain ways from 2016, and that a lot of the ills he had diagnosed from a Trump presidency had come to pass. He also said Trump had given him a lot more policy wins than expected.

NPR described Shapiro as having built his political writing into an empire and identified him as a co-founder of The Daily Wire, which also publishes feature films. NPR described conservative media as a business in which Shapiro has thrived since age 17. Shapiro said one goal of his work has always been to provide another side to the cultural influences people see and that he was trying to hold to traditional conservative values. NPR also noted that he wrote the screenplay for “Confession,” a film about a Christian man trying to hold to his values during an interrogation in Stalin's Soviet Union.

Ron DeSantis, governor of Florida, used his postmortem of the election to call for a big change in conservative media culture, saying “a lot of it is a grift,” “pay-for-play” and “not serious about ideas.” Shapiro said he agreed. He argued that an enormous number of people in the right-wing media ecosystem were “weather vanes” rather than “actual light towers,” called the pattern “wave surfing” and said very few were willing to stand where they stood and remain there.

Asked which figures Shapiro meant, he called Megyn Kelly the most obvious weather vane but said Tucker Carlson was not one. Shapiro said Carlson had a belief system he viewed as nefarious and described him as an antisemite. He said Carlson had promoted a right-wing version of grievance politics that ought to be justified and fed and ultimately came down to conspiratorial theories about elites with disproportionately Jewish last names.

Inskeep said Carlson, when he appeared on NPR in September, denied the antisemitism charge. Carlson said he was critical of Israel, suggested Trump followed Israel's direction and contended that a “uniparty” of Republicans and Democrats caused a war that was not in U.S. interests. He also said he had noticed “the undue influence of Israel” through its lobby in Washington.

Shapiro said, “Antisemitism is a conspiracy theory about the world,” and defined it as a claim that Jews bear inordinate responsibility and use inordinate power to control the mechanisms by which the world works. When Inskeep asked how Israeli influence in the U.S. government should be described, Shapiro said many people in the United States are pro-Israel. Inskeep said Shapiro denied that Israel bends U.S. policy. Inskeep said the exchange illustrates a divide among conservatives.

On the current war in Iran, Shapiro said Israel's end goal is toppling the regime, while the United States' goal is not the regime's total toppling. He said President Trump has said so repeatedly, though Inskeep noted that Trump once talked about toppling the regime as he declared the beginning of the war. Shapiro said, “I think that was his hope,” but not the war's explicit goal.

Shapiro said it was impossible to judge whether the war had worked until it ended, though he contended that President Trump had executed it badly and that it could still work. He said Iran's leadership was suffering, but Trump did not strike hard or long enough before trying a ceasefire and did not deliver what Shapiro called the final hammer blow needed to end the Iranian regime's capacity to recover.

Shapiro said the problem was the choice of targets rather than the length of bombing. He named Kharg Island, the South Pars gas field and Pickaxe Mountain as places where Trump had hesitated and argued that the United States should have gone after Kharg Island and South Pars to effectively disable Iran's ability to produce oil instead of blockading it. When Inskeep asked whether oil would rise to $200 a barrel, Shapiro said it would come back down. He said the high costs of the war hurt Republicans in the election.
