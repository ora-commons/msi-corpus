## Georgia SNAP participation falls as first-time food-bank visits rise

Volunteer Nassar said the volunteer-run Enough to Share pantry in DeKalb County was receiving less than half the food it obtained for the same price it paid a few years ago. At its Nov. 18, 2026, distribution a week before Thanksgiving, the pantry was handing out lighter bags as families faced rising grocery costs and reduced access to SNAP.

Enough to Share operates out of the Atlanta Masjid of Al-Islam mosque in DeKalb County, where people line up in cars and on foot every two weeks for food distribution. The volunteer-run pantry started in 2017 when five volunteers pooled $20 each to buy groceries at a dollar store for about 30 families. It now serves about 240 families every two weeks. Longtime volunteer Nafeesah Madyun said, “The demand has increased tremendously,” as she prepared to check in “neighbors,” the term the organization uses for the people it serves, for its food distribution event a week ahead of Thanksgiving.

Organizers said a bag once covered three to four meals for a family, while each now stretches to about two. That reduction forces families seeking assistance to rely on multiple pantries and pop-up events to piece together groceries during the holidays.

Community pantries like Enough to Share purchase much of the food they distribute from food banks. Nassar, the husband of longtime volunteer Nafeesah Madyun, is also a volunteer who manages the pantry’s order with the food bank. He said the pantry now gets less than half the food it obtained for the same price it paid just a few years ago. About $400 bought 12,000 pounds of food then; the same $400 now buys about 5,000 pounds. He said the pantry fills the gap by seeking donations from local greenhouses, partner groups and farmer markets and by using funds raised through fundraisers to buy groceries from supermarkets.

“Our primary source being the food bank, and when the food bank doesn’t have food, we don’t have food,” Enough to Share vice president Amina Ali said.

The Atlanta Community Food Bank distributes food to hundreds of organizations across 29 metro Atlanta and North Georgia counties. Interim president and CEO Jon West said post-pandemic food inflation had largely eroded the purchasing power of both households and nonprofits, creating two effects: tighter family budgets pushed more families toward food banks while the food bank had to find creative ways to fill the gap. “Those dollars just aren’t going as far for us either,” West said. “As need goes up, resources are constrained and tight – and tight for us – we’re just not getting as much bang for our buck as we used to. There’s a lot of parallels between what’s happening at the household level and then what’s happening with us and our operations right now as well.”

West said about 30% of visitors who received assistance over the past three months were first-time visitors. He said the growing demand was coming from families with no record of ever using food banks.

Outside metro Atlanta, where there are fewer food banks and nonprofits, West said residents must travel farther for assistance. He said food insecurity rates were higher in rural areas than in urban areas and cited a variety of economic reasons for the difference, leaving more hungry people in places with fewer resources available to help them. He pointed to Haralson County to illustrate the issue. “We just have fewer partners in that county because there are fewer organizations for us to connect with and partner with, and so you have to travel farther to get to resources, which takes more time, energy and fuel, and just makes it really harder to navigate,” he said.

Georgia Interfaith Public Policy Center executive director Wesley Myrick said rural providers can be overwhelmed when significant demand meets limited availability. “In our rural communities, you’ve got one, maybe two providers that service the whole county, and that one provider is bombarded as soon as they offer it,” Myrick said.

At the federal level, assistance on which low-income families rely is shrinking. Joseph Llobrera, senior director of research on food assistance at the Center on Budget and Policy Priorities, said the Trump administration cut SNAP funding by about 20%, or about $187 billion, over the next decade through H.R.1 last year. He said the change had already caused 5.5 million people to lose access to the food safety net program, a 13% drop. “I think we are seeing historic, unprecedented changes to food assistance in this country,” Llobrera said.

In Georgia, SNAP participation had declined by almost 10%, or roughly 150,000 people overall, and by about 8% among households with children, Llobrera said. He said the decline among families with children was particularly alarming because households with children under 14 are exempt from the new law’s stricter work requirements.

Elaine Waxman, a senior fellow at the Urban Institute, said state agencies were scrambling to avoid potential cuts to their federal SNAP funding. She said the new law could shift a share of the cost of administering benefits to states if they failed to quickly lower payment error rates, which measure administrative and paperwork mistakes rather than fraud, a focus of the current administration. States have demanded more frequent documentation to lower those rates, creating a backlog that has knocked eligible families off SNAP rolls, Waxman said. “[State officials have] been so focused, understandably, on trying to reduce the risk of having to pay benefit costs that the scramble to do that has led to people falling through the cracks,” she said.

Back at the mosque, Brenda Rasheed, another longtime volunteer and the pantry’s volunteer coordinator, said the mood in the drive-thru and walk-up lines had not changed despite slimmer bags and less funding to cover the gap. “You see the gratitude on faces, the relief on faces,” Rasheed said. “People are grateful to have some support, some supplementing with their own foods that they’re able to secure,” she said.

The new reporting shows that the same cost pressure was reaching Georgia food pantries, where food-bank purchases had contracted and first-time visits were increasing.
