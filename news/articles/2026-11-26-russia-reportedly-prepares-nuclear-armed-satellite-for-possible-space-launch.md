## McDowell: Low-orbit detonation could damage satellites, create radiation belt

Russia is reportedly preparing to launch a nuclear-armed satellite named Sputnik-S, with German media citing Western intelligence assessments that it could be launched within 60 days and become operational shortly afterward. German broadcasters NDR and WDR and the Süddeutsche Zeitung reported that Western intelligence services, including Germany's Bundesnachrichtendienst and armed forces, had exchanged information showing that Sputnik-S was assembled several years ago and was stationed in the Moscow region. Those reports placed a possible launch later in 2026.

A German government spokesperson said the intelligence reports were being taken “very seriously.” The report said Sputnik-S could become operational shortly after launch and that a detonation could pose a significant threat to global communications.

In space, where there is no atmosphere, a nuclear explosion would not produce a mushroom cloud. Its energy would be released as radiation, and the effects would be particularly damaging in low Earth orbit, which the report places as high as 1,200 miles above Earth and where most satellites operate.

Jonathan McDowell, an astrophysicist and honorary professor at the Durham University Space Research Centre in the UK, said the main effects would be twofold. Everything within line of sight could be zapped by X-rays and other forms of radiation, potentially knocking out or damaging satellites within thousands of miles. Particles released into the lower atmosphere could form a new “Van Allen belt,” McDowell said. The term refers to two belts of radiation that circle Earth and shield it from solar storms.

The scenario has a direct historical parallel in Starfish Prime, a 1962 U.S. test of a thermonuclear bomb detonated 250 miles above Earth. Although many fewer satellites were operating then, the test shortened the lives of Telstar 1 and Ariel-1. McDowell said Starfish caused severe radiation for months and reached at least half the satellites then working on orbit.

Keith Ryden, a professor of space engineering at the University of Surrey in the UK, said low Earth orbit satellites would be vulnerable to bursts of radiation. He explained that natural radiation levels there are relatively low and the satellites are not designed for high radiation levels, leaving them exposed to unexpected increases.

About 17,000 satellites operate in lower Earth orbit, according to the report. They include craft used for weather forecasting, climate monitoring and military work, along with Elon Musk's Starlink broadband network. The report said Starlink is crucial to Ukraine's efforts to repel Russia's invasion. Russia is also a partner in the International Space Station, which is in low Earth orbit, and Musk's proposed constellation of space datacentres would operate there as well.

Damage would not be uniform across orbit. Satellites handling television broadcasts, GPS and other communications are in higher orbits and would be less easy for a nuclear weapon to affect, according to the report. Those higher-orbit systems would be less exposed than the roughly 17,000 satellites in lower Earth orbit.

McDowell said the consequences would be immediate and lasting. He said, “There will be immediate impacts from a detonation but there are lasting environmental effects that will damage satellites in the longer run. It could take several years, up to a decade, for the trapped particles to diffuse out of the radiation belt.” The artificial Van Allen belt created by Starfish Prime remained around for five years.

The 1962 test also showed that effects could reach the ground. Hundreds of miles below the explosion, streetlights in Hawaii were knocked out, burglar alarms were set off and phone calls were disrupted. McDowell said computers and electronic systems might be affected in the region of Earth where the explosion was visible. Ryden said the detonation's location was crucial because Starfish Prime and other nuclear tests took place over sparsely populated areas.

Starfish Prime also created auroras in shades of green, yellow and red, with one report describing “tremendous white rainbows.” McDowell said a new detonation would have “huge auroral effects” and added, “It’s hard to be subtle with nukes.”

The loss of satellites could also outpace the industry's capacity to respond. Ryden said, “Just as important, the space industry would not have the capacity to rebuild and launch the required number of spacecraft in a short time.”

A European security official told The Guardian that they were doubtful Russia would detonate a nuclear device in space. They said an explosion would be hard to control, could cause significant damage to Western economies and would probably damage Russia's own satellites, as well as potentially China's. The official said, “It would be shooting themselves in the leg.”

The official characterized Russia's earlier nuclear blackmail in the war in Ukraine, including the 2022 high alert for its nuclear forces followed by movements of troops and weapons. “They were bluffing then – and this looks like a bluff now,” the official said. McDowell was also skeptical, saying a detonation would be viewed as an act of war.
