## Immigration courts expedite hearings as 94% of asylum cases denied in June

**Subtype:** fact

- Federal officials are moving immigration hearing dates forward and reassigning cases to judges far from where migrants live, attorneys say.
- A New Orleans immigration court gave attorney Corinne Beth four days to prepare a family of six for an asylum hearing after switching their judge to Cleveland, Ohio.
- Asylum cases were denied at a 94% rate in June, according to the Transactional Records Access Clearinghouse.
- 279 immigration judges — 38% of the bench — resigned or were fired between October 2024 and the end of September 2026.
- The government refers to the immigration judge role as "deportation judges" in some of its recruitment literature, and a new plan has unaccompanied children's cases heard remotely across state lines.

## 279 judges left the immigration bench over a two-year period

Four days. That is all the notice the New Orleans immigration court gave attorney Corinne Beth to get a family of six ready to defend their asylum case — an intensive process lawyers estimate requires at least 50 to 75 hours of dedicated preparation.

The court had already brought forward the family's next hearing once, from a date in July 2028, saying it was "a priority case" and must be completed by the end of November without explaining why.

Then, on a Thursday in late October, Beth learned that officials had switched her clients' judge to one in Cleveland, Ohio, without their permission, and they now had a virtual hearing come Monday.

A government attorney arguing for the family's deportation acknowledged the circumstances were strange. But instead of agreeing to give them more time, the administration's lawyer proposed "pretermitting" their asylum application — jargon for denying their request and deporting them without ever holding a full evidentiary hearing.

"It just doesn't make sense why we're behaving this way as a human race," Beth said.

Beth was able to convince the judge to move her clients to a different hearing date, later this month, the same week it had been rescheduled the first time. Yet even the new court date — so much sooner than in the past, when she would at least get six months' notice — would not give the family enough time to prepare, she said.

The family's struggle is an extreme example of what migrants and asylum seekers across the United States are enduring, as federal officials rapidly expedite hearings, send cases to far-flung judges and use every tool in their legal toolbox to prioritize mass deportations without, immigration attorneys protest, any semblance of due process.

"It's like: 'Your hearing's been moved up. Oh, now your hearing's cancelled. Oh, now you have a new judge. Oh, that judge just quit, so now here's another one. Oh, we're randomly going to move it to this other judge just because,'" said Genevra Alberti, a lawyer in Kansas City. "It doesn't make any sense. There's no rhyme or reason to it."

Immigrants without attorneys are faring just as poorly, if not worse, with hearing dates set for next year suddenly pulled forward to just a few weeks' time, giving them almost no notice to seek legal representation or prepare, according to a source with knowledge of the situation, who could not be named for fear of government retribution.

The rate at which US immigration judges grant asylum has already plummeted, with 94% of cases denied in June, according to the Transactional Records Access Clearinghouse, in stark contrast with the Biden administration, when at certain times asylum grants eclipsed denials. Children and some nationalities targeted by the second Trump administration, including Somali immigrants, whom as a group Donald Trump has called "garbage" in past statements, have been harmed even more than others, a number of attorneys said.

The reported acceleration has collided with large-scale personnel departures: 279 judges — 38% of the bench — resigned or were fired between October 2024 and the end of September 2026. Those experienced officials are now being replaced by the administration's "deportation judges," as the government refers to the role in some of its recruitment literature — often former military lawyers, immigration enforcement attorneys or prosecutors. The Guardian reported that their tenure has coincided with an abundance of removal orders.

"The job has become so non-neutral that they can't find anybody that wants to keep the job unless it's these judges that they're appointing to the 'Somali rocket docket' or to the kids' dockets," said Hannah Sullivan, an attorney outside St Louis, referring to expedited proceedings for Somalis and in many children's cases, in front of a small number of chosen judges. "They're known to deny and move cases as quickly as possible. So those guys are still on the bench. The ones that were good and wanted to, you know, be a neutral arbiter have not stayed around," she added.

Sullivan said the court where she practices in Kansas City lost two veteran judges back-to-back in recent months. And even newer ones — Scott Ison, who was appointed in April, and Katherine Estes, who was appointed in March — left almost as soon as they arrived.

"It's been very difficult to get information from the court because nobody's available to answer phones or emails," Alberti said. Sometimes she receives an answer to her urgent questions weeks later, and other times she never hears back, she said.

As short-staffed courts nevertheless contend with what immigration attorneys describe as pressure to rush immigrants toward deportation, many people are being ping-ponged to judges across the country, often nowhere near where they live, and sometimes outside the court circuits with specific case law that should apply to their situations.

That includes children. A new plan has those who came to the US unaccompanied by a parent or legal guardian appearing before a select bench of judges who are not necessarily even hearing juvenile cases in their own courts, but instead adjudicating remotely across time zones and state lines.

"I don't understand why they're messing with the kids. Like, why go after children? It doesn't make any sense to me. But that's what's happening," Alberti said.

Children will still have to show up in person to their home courts, despite judges being virtual and based elsewhere, and adjudicators are being double-booked to funnel through two courts' full caseloads at once, said a source familiar with the situation who could not be named for fear of government retaliation.

"It's insane. Just insane," the source said.

The latest developments come amid a series of adverse legal and policy changes affecting these children, even as a growing number of lawyers rallies to try to protect them.

The children are being denied the time they need to access pathways to citizenship for which, a number of advocates stressed, they qualify, as the administration sets what appear to be arbitrary deadlines for judges to end their cases — while also defunding many of their attorneys.

All this can mean immigration lawyers having to rush to the point where children are being pulled out of class at school so that they can practice describing some of their greatest life traumas that are at the heart of their asylum cases. Sometimes, lawyers recounted, they may not have processed the effects of what happened to them yet but still have to confront those memories in preparation for an accelerated court hearing that could change their whole life.

"[Government officials] are not giving us the chance to build the record. They're not giving us time to prepare the cases. They're saying, you know, basically, without [actually] saying it: 'We're going to speed up these cases for kids because we don't believe them,'" Sullivan said.

The courts' new policies — which make it harder to submit evidence, ask for more time, pause proceedings while applying for a legal status through other agencies, and otherwise receive meaningful due process — have rendered it nearly impossible, lawyers said, to successfully fight for a client to stay.

"If somebody wins a case at all at trial, it is considered a unicorn," Alberti said. "It really is becoming a deportation machine … just hiring deportation judges, right? To help fuel this deportation machine."
