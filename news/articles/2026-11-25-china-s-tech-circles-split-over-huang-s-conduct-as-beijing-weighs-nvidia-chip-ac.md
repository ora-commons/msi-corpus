## NPR reports U.S. congressional staff briefed on China spy allegations

BEIJING — Jensen Huang's return to NVIDIA has met with a mixed response in China as Beijing weighs whether it wants NVIDIA's best computer chips at all. Chinese technology circles are split over Huang's conduct, while government officials and industry observers separately weigh Chinese access to advanced NVIDIA chips and U.S. export controls.

Huang has said he wants to be able to sell chips directly to China. Jennifer Pak said there may be hope for smoother access to high-end U.S. chips among some Chinese AI companies, but industry watchers she spoke with expected Washington to tighten export controls, which she said had already occurred.

Beijing's own signals have conflicted. Officials have criticized the United States for its export controls. At the same time, when the U.S. approves chips for sale to China, Beijing has told Chinese companies not to buy them and to buy local products instead.

Steven Hai, an assistant professor of technology entrepreneurship at Xi'an Jiaotong-Liverpool University, said China's central government should not be treated as a monolith. He described the strategy this way: "They want normal global trade, but they want so-called, like, its own developmental strategy in the long run."

In the short to medium term, Hai said, China wants access to NVIDIA's high-end chips until it can build its own replacement, something Huang has wanted to avoid. Huang has separately said he wants to be able to sell chips directly to China, placing the two positions in opposition over whether advanced NVIDIA hardware will remain part of Beijing's near-term technology strategy.

NPR correspondent Jennifer Pak said some in the general public compared Huang's swift fall and sudden return to a microdrama, a popular format similar to soap operas and broken into fast-paced episodes of two to three minutes. Pak said the sequence of events had many twists and turns.

In China's technology circles, Du Chen, a technology media editor at Ifanr in Beijing, described a sharper split. He said one group believes Huang's conduct raises serious ethical violations and that he should not remain in charge. A second group, made up of people who have had business dealings with NVIDIA or interacted with Huang directly and indirectly, generally trusts Huang's ability to serve as CEO. Jennifer Pak, reporting Du Chen's account, said the group believed Huang would return, but not as fast as it happened, "like 11 days."

The allegation that China launched a spy operation targeting NVIDIA and Huang was denied by China's foreign ministry, Pak reported.

NPR reported that it had heard that U.S. congressional staff had been briefed by American intelligence officials about the China spy allegations. A person familiar with the matter said there was no evidence that Alice Huang, the woman who had an affair with NVIDIA chief Jensen Huang and is unrelated to him, was a Chinese spy. The person said she was believed to have passed information to China after the affair began.

Du said there was surprisingly little discussion of the allegation on the ground in China, noting that the limited talk was not simply the result of censorship. He said people in China did not believe Alice Huang was a spy and noted that she and Huang had been photographed openly.

Du also said he did not know the specific details of allegations that Alice Huang and her company, Megaspeed, helped smuggle NVIDIA chips into China. He said NVIDIA chips entering China through "gray channels" were not a secret in China at all, especially within the technology industry. Many people in the West, he said, may have heard about those channels for the first time through U.S. media.
