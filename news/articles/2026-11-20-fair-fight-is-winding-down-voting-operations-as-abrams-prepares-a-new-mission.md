## Cross-state coordination will continue under Fair Fight's new structure

Fair Fight, the organization Stacey Abrams formed after her 2018 loss in Georgia, is winding down its voting-focused operations. Abrams said she plans to unveil a new structure and mission in the new year, one that will strengthen democratic processes and confront what she describes as authoritarian maneuvers in state and local governments. She told The Associated Press the change is necessary as President Donald Trump's second term enters its final two years and Democrats take control of Capitol Hill in January. “I don’t think it’s getting out of the game. I think it’s changing the approach,” she said.

Abrams pointed to gerrymandering of political districts as a reason for the change, including congressional redistricting that she said was supercharged by the Supreme Court's gutting of the Voting Rights Act earlier this year. She also cited state-level moves that she said are “not going to end with Donald Trump’s exit.” “Voter suppression is not solved, but it has enough lieutenants and knights and advocates who are fighting,” she said. “So we can take this moment to shift the focus because this next threat is as dangerous, if not more.”

Abrams said the change does not mean she is abandoning voting rights, because ballot-access issues are distinct from how government itself functions. She expects redistricting fights to continue, especially in Southern statehouses where Democrats have not won legislative control, and said she wants to better educate citizens about how the makeup of a state legislature and its rules shape budgets, healthcare, education and other policies that affect people's daily lives. “Where voter suppression is about how you access democracy, the rise of authoritarian control is about whether there is a democracy, and that’s where we want to put the focus,” she said.

Other advocates who worked with Fair Fight praised its role in helping marginalized voters and raising money to build a more stable voter-protection infrastructure within the Democratic Party and among left-leaning nonprofits. Some Abrams supporters said donors and activists on the left cannot treat the organization's shift, or Democrats' sweeping victories in November, as a reason to let up.

“I want to be very clear about this: It is an institutional loss,” said Cliff Albright, a co-founder of Black Voters Matter. He said how the loss plays out and what its impact is will depend on whether people still believe it is an important issue deserving attention and funding. Albright said Fair Fight was a leader this year in pulling national, state and local organizations into a Signal chat and weekly Zoom calls after the Supreme Court's decision in the Voting Rights Act case led several Republican legislatures across the South to eliminate congressional districts with Black representation.

“That was critical to us sharing ideas and combining our resources,” Albright said, adding that the early organizing led into a campaign season in which strong turnout, especially among Black voters, fueled Democratic victories.

Lauren Groh-Wargo, who managed Abrams' two unsuccessful runs for Georgia governor in 2018 and 2022 and ran Fair Fight for much of its existence, cited the group's role as coordinator of organizations across multiple states and said that work would continue.

Fair Fight began soon after Abrams lost the 2018 Georgia governor's race to Republican Brian Kemp by fewer than 60,000 votes, around 1.4 percentage points. She used her national profile and fundraising prowess to start the organization to confront ballot access and voter disenfranchisement around the country. At the time, she argued that Kemp had used his role as secretary of state to suppress her would-be voters; Kemp denied the allegations. When she ended her election challenges more than a week after Election Day, she said, “Let’s be clear, this is not a speech of concession.” Her lawsuit alleging “gross mismanagement” of Georgia's elections eventually sputtered out after a judge tossed out most of its claims.

Other groups Abrams founded years ahead of that race — the New Georgia Project and its action fund — shut down in 2025 after the state Ethics Commission fined them for violating state campaign laws through activities supporting her 2018 campaign.

Abrams' elevated national profile after her first run for governor translated into rapid growth for Fair Fight. The group raised almost $220 million from thousands of donors over eight years. It has 22 employees now, down from a peak that approached 60 before financial troubles forced layoffs after the 2022 elections.

The organization also has an extensive donor list and reams of policy research, opposition research on candidates, and strategy and communications plans. “We’re going to take the time over the next couple of months to make sure those pieces get transferred or get leveraged for allies or get moved” to the new organization, Groh-Wargo said. Abrams said she wants a new Fair Fight iteration to have the same long-term effect as the first: seeding other organizations doing similar work.

“It’s the ripple effect,” said Ben Wikler, a former Wisconsin Democratic Party chair. He recalled Abrams helping raise money to fund the state party's first full-time voter-protection staff member. That became a permanent team ahead of the 2020 presidential election, in which Republican incumbent Donald Trump was defeated. “We built the machine,” Wikler said. “Fair Fight kickstarted it.”

Fair Fight also worked with Joe Biden's successful 2020 campaign on voter protection in 18 states. Marc Elias, whom The Associated Press describes as Democrats' most high-profile election lawyer, said those efforts can be used to leverage citizen pressure on government at all levels. “You can’t have a democracy where lawyers like me have to litigate against the Department of Justice and win dozens and dozens of cases in order to have free and fair elections and government,” he said.
