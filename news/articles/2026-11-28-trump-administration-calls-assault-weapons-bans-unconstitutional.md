## Connecticut attorneys cite “weapons of war” and “catastrophic damage”

The Trump administration and gun-rights advocates are arguing that bans on the nation’s most widely owned rifle are unconstitutional because such weapons are in common use by law-abiding citizens. A 2026 survey cited by the challengers estimates Americans own 40 million AR-15-type rifles, making them more common than Ford F-Series pickup trucks. The conservative-majority Supreme Court will hear arguments Wednesday, placing the court at the center of one of firearms law’s most contested issues; a decision is expected around the end of June.

Gun-rights advocates backed by the administration also say assault-style rifles are involved in a relatively small portion of overall gun violence. Bill Sack, senior director of legal operations at the Second Amendment Foundation, said, “This is the biggest and most pernicious problem we have in the gun control space,” adding, “We all view this as the Super Bowl of gun rights for the time being.”

Attorneys defending bans in Connecticut and Illinois counter that semiautomatic rifles were originally designed as “weapons of war” and remain especially dangerous. Connecticut’s attorneys said the rifles fire one round for each squeeze of the trigger but that their high-velocity rounds cause “catastrophic damage” to the human body.

The defenders’ argument is that widespread sales do not prevent democratically elected lawmakers from regulating the weapons. “Once it becomes clear that a new weapon poses an unacceptable threat to human life, legislatures should be able to act,” lawyers for Cook County, Illinois, wrote.

A central issue is whether widespread ownership of semiautomatic rifles prevents governments from imposing blanket bans. The administration argues that courts have upheld bans on especially dangerous weapons such as machine guns, but it sees no historical basis for similar restrictions on weapons “in common use” by law-abiding citizens.

The Supreme Court’s 2022 decision expanded gun rights and required firearm restrictions to be rooted in the nation’s history. The opinion prompted challenges to gun laws across the country, although the court has upheld some regulations since then. Lower-court judges have expressed confusion about how to apply the historical test.

About a dozen states have similar laws banning weapons such as the AR-15, and those laws cover major cities including New York, Los Angeles and Chicago. A county-level ban in Cook County is also before the court, and a federal law restricting the sale and manufacture of the weapons expired in 2004.

The rifles have increased sharply in popularity as they became widely available at lower prices. Gun retailer surveys show sales of AR-15-type weapons second only to semiautomatic handguns, according to court documents. AR-15-type rifles have also been used in several high-profile mass shootings, including at an elementary school in Uvalde, Texas; a country music festival in Las Vegas; and an Independence Day parade in Highland Park, Illinois.

Nicole Hockley carried a photograph of her 6-year-old son Dylan to the Connecticut Capitol. The image of his bright blue eyes and sweep of brown hair was marked with the year he was born and the year he was killed. Twenty children, including Dylan, and six staff members at Sandy Hook Elementary School were killed in 2012 by shots from an AR-15-style rifle.

Hockley was among the people who helped persuade Connecticut to expand its ban on firearms like the weapon used. More than a decade later, she remains convinced the assault weapons ban has helped prevent more mass school shootings. “This isn’t just about laws. This is about people, and this is about ensuring that people are protected,” said Hockley, who co-founded the group Sandy Hook Promise. She is heading to Washington next week with other families and survivors of deadly mass shootings.

Sheila Gutman was one of dozens of people wounded on July 4, 2022, when a gunman fired 83 rounds into the Highland Park parade crowd in less than a minute, killing seven. A single round created a wound the size of a baseball on her foot. Gutman still walks with a limp and suffers from near-constant nerve pain despite weeks in the hospital and 17 surgeries. “I had no idea the damage it could do,” she said.

Several of the nation’s deadliest mass shootings have involved semiautomatic rifles, said James Alan Fox, a Northeastern University criminology professor who maintains a mass-killing database in a partnership with The Associated Press and USA Today. Such attacks have killed nearly twice as many people on average as other mass shootings. “Assault weapons don’t change the likelihood of committing the crime but they do impact the level of carnage,” Fox said.

Fox also said the majority of mass shootings, particularly those away from public places, do not involve semiautomatic rifles. Handguns are more prevalent in gun violence overall and in mass shootings.

Adam Winkler, a law professor at the University of California, Los Angeles, questioned how much the decision will affect gun violence more broadly. “I don’t think it’s going to have a big impact on public safety either way,” he said. The ruling could nevertheless have a major impact on other gun laws, even if its broader effect on gun violence remains uncertain.

“We’ve agreed, as a society, that certain policy choices are off the table in terms of trying to address a problem,” Sack said. The assault weapons case could be another milestone in the court’s 2022 expansion of gun rights, and a decision is expected around the end of June.
