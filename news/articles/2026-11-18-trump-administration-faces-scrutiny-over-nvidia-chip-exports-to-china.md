## Commerce Department moves to restrict chip sales after enforcement gap

The Trump administration's handling of Nvidia's advanced AI-chip exports has become a national-security and congressional-oversight issue, according to NPR's Ryan Lucas and Shannon Bond. The rolling crisis around Nvidia had lasted more than two weeks. The Commerce Department moved this week to restrict sales to Malaysia and Thailand after a recommended license requirement went unenforced, while Huang and Nvidia's top lawyer are set to answer questions from lawmakers after Thanksgiving.

News reports said Huang was pushed out after extramarital affairs, including one with a woman U.S. intelligence agencies suspected of being a Chinese spy. The personal scandal has since spilled into a wider examination of how the administration allowed America's top AI chips to end up in China's hands.

Lucas described China as a superpower whose economic and military might put it almost on equal footing with the United States. He said the two countries were racing to develop artificial intelligence and that the technology had implications for cybersecurity, the military battlefield and technological development in the economy. Protecting the American lead in AI, he said, is a national-security issue.

Bond said Trump sees the AI race with China as a binary win-lose contest. In an archived September recording, the president said:

> "We're leading China in AI. We're the most sophisticated country in the world. And frankly, I want to keep it that way, because whoever wins AI wins."

Bond said Nvidia's chips are crucial to AI development and Huang has long argued that American companies should sell them to China so Chinese AI development relies on U.S. hardware. The position was not universally shared, including within the government, but Huang had successfully pushed it during Trump's second administration, NPR reported.

The Biden administration took a hard line, severely restricting sales of the most advanced American AI chips to China to protect U.S. national security and the country's technological edge in AI. Trump took office in January 2025, and Lucas said Huang actively lobbied his administration to loosen the restrictions. For the past 18 months or so, Nvidia's most advanced chips had been flowing to China, Lucas said.

Chris McGuire, who worked on AI and export controls at the National Security Council, said:

> "These chips are the lifeblood of AI. They're the most important thing that you need for AI systems and AI development, and they're also the single biggest differentiator between the U.S. and China."

He said China could not make the top-end chips itself. Despite national-security concerns, McGuire said about $60 billion in AI chips had gone legally to Singapore, Malaysia and Thailand over the last 18 months. He said those countries could not support that level of AI buildout on their own, so some of those chips were suspected of having been smuggled into China. The rest were in data centers primarily used by China's top tech companies to train their own AI and make those models better, according to McGuire.

McGuire said the Trump administration had failed to enforce the license requirement applicable to those countries. He said:

> "The reason that this happened is that the Trump administration did not enforce the license requirement that would have applied to those countries, and it's just enabled a massive technology transfer out the door."

He described the result as potentially the biggest transfer of export-control technology to an adversary in U.S. history.

Lucas said there was an argument that Huang's personal relationship with Trump played a role. Huang was not among the tech chief executives who attended Trump's inauguration, but Bond said he launched a campaign of personal diplomacy. In September, Huang was on stage at a large technology conference when Trump called his personal cellphone. Huang said:

> "Mr. President, oh, yes, sir. I got to tell you something. I - if it wasn't because of you calling, I would - I'm on stage."

Bond said Huang put Trump on speakerphone and praised his leadership on AI. She said the cultivation of that relationship helped make Huang one of the administration's most influential voices in AI policy.

Lucas said the administration lifted restrictions on two specific Nvidia chips to allow direct sales to China. It also secured a multibillion-dollar deal giving the United Arab Emirates access to Nvidia's most advanced chips, despite concerns that the chips could end up in China again. Separately, Lucas said he had been told a summer 2025 recommendation called for export licenses on AI-chip shipments to Malaysia and Thailand. The Commerce Department did not move forward, and the sales at the center of the scandal continued.

In response to the rolling scandal, the Commerce Department moved this week to restrict those chip sales and close the loophole. Nvidia's board said this week that it had formed a committee to oversee the company's national-security policies and compliance with export controls. Bond said lawmakers would ask Huang and Nvidia's top lawyer about his relationship with the woman suspected of being a Chinese spy and about allegations that Nvidia might have found ways to evade export controls.

Bond said Democrats were now in control of Congress and expected more questions for Huang and administration officials about the export-control decisions, the thinking behind them and who might have influenced the policy. The Commerce Department's move and the planned Capitol Hill testimony leave enforcement, compliance and influence questions unresolved.
