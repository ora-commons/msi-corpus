## Coyotes use Los Angeles freeway crossing ahead of official opening

**Subtype:** fact

- The Wallis Annenberg Wildlife Crossing has carried wildlife over U.S. 101 before its Dec. 2 opening ceremony in Agoura Hills.
- Motion-sensor cameras captured a lone coyote believed to be the first mammal to cross the span safely two weeks before a recent tour of the span.
- Wallis Annenberg's foundation donated $25 million toward the crossing's $114 million total budget.
- A University of California, Davis report found that vehicles kill at least 780 mountain lions each year, equal to 18% of California's mountain lion population.

## Biologgers will measure animals’ heart rate and activity on the span

During a tour last week, Beth Pratt, one of the project's leaders and president of the nonprofit Wildlife Crossing Fund, stepped carefully through freshly planted native vegetation and held up a hand for silence. “Hear that? We’re listening to birdsong right now, on top of one of the busiest freeways in the U.S.!” she said. “That makes my heart glad.”

The birdsong was the latest sign that the Wallis Annenberg Wildlife Crossing was already in use by animals ahead of its official Dec. 2 opening ceremony in Agoura Hills. The structure is about 35 miles (56 kilometers) northwest of downtown Los Angeles and spans U.S. 101 and a feeder road. About 300,000 cars a day travel the stretch of U.S. 101 in Agoura Hills, a small city surrounded by a patchwork of protected wildland.

Just two weeks before that tour, motion-sensor cameras captured a lone coyote believed to be the first mammal to cross safely. Days after that crossing, three juvenile coyotes were seen on camera playing in the brush beneath the 10-foot (3-meter) sound-dampening walls that line the span. Pratt said of the young coyotes, “They don’t care about ribbon cuttings.”

Pratt said the crossing's real official opening would come when a mountain lion, a species listed as threatened in California, reaches the overpass. The Dec. 2 ceremony will formally recognize the project, while animal use is already underway.

Fencing along the freeway funnels animals toward the crossing, which links the protected wildland around Agoura Hills to the nearby Santa Monica Mountains and beyond. The span stretches 1,250 feet (380 meters) in two sections over U.S. 101 and the feeder road, giving animals hemmed in by urban sprawl a safe path. It is the world's largest wildlife crossing. Its landscape is indistinguishable from the surrounding hillsides. Sound-dampening walls 10 feet (3 meters) high line the span, and freshly planted native vegetation covers the new terrain.

Named for the late philanthropist Wallis Annenberg, the project has a total budget of $114 million, including a $25 million donation from Wallis Annenberg's foundation. The cost rose from an initial $92 million. Donations came from 6,000 members of the public, grants from nonprofits, and state funds set aside for conservation. Leonardo DiCaprio was among the earliest donors after the funding drive began a decade ago.

The campaign's centerpiece was P-22, a mountain lion that traveled across freeways and made a home in Griffith Park. The cat was euthanized in 2022 after likely being hit by a car, and thousands attended a memorial service. P-22 became a symbol of the shrinking genetic diversity and road risks facing wild animals trapped by sprawling development. During that tour, Pratt wore a shirt bearing an image of the cougar and said she hopes eventually to erect a statue of P-22 on the side of the new bridge.

Scientists who tracked mountain lions with GPS collars found over decades that roadways largely confined the animals in mountains running along the Malibu coast and across the middle of Los Angeles to Griffith Park. Scientists and state wildlife officials said isolation, vehicle deaths and rat poisoning leave wild pumas at risk of inbreeding into extinction.

A University of California, Davis report from the previous week found at least 780 mountain lions, or 18% of the state's mountain lion population, are killed by vehicles each year.

Mari Galloway, California program director for the nonprofit Wildlands Network, said P-22's story demonstrated the danger facing mountain lions in the area. “The story of P-22 was just so compelling,” she said. “We saw the peril in what happened to that particular cat. It was demonstrative of what these mountain lions are facing in this part of the landscape.” The Wildlands Network, which was not involved in building the Annenberg crossing, hopes the new bridge will spur construction of many similar crossings across California and the nation. The group is working with state transportation officials in the early stages of planning a wildlife bridge for a stretch of highway in the Mojave Desert east of Los Angeles where bighorn sheep roam.

Wildlife crossings are common in western Europe and Canada. A famous crossing in Banff National Park in Alberta spans the Trans-Canada Highway and is frequently used by bears, moose and elk. In Colorado, a large overpass above Interstate 25, together with underpasses and fencing, contributed to a 91% decrease in wildlife collisions, the state parks and wildlife department said in September.

Anthony Deascentis, a field inspector for the California Department of Transportation, waved Pratt over to some scrub brush and pointed out an orb-weaver spider. The spider, as big as a silver dollar, was building an elaborate circular web. Deascentis said bees were using the native plants and that he had spotted an oak titmouse, sparrows and hummingbirds. The observations came as crews using heavy machinery continued to fill in the landscape on the bridge's south side.

Jeff Sikich, a biologist for the National Park Service who collars and tracks cougars and other wild animals, said the next step will be to implant “biologgers” that allow scientists to monitor physiological data including heart rate, body temperature and activity levels. With that information, scientists will be able to tell whether animals are stressed while using the wildlife bridge or “if they’re relaxed up there.”

In recent weeks, scientists have detected multiple mammals in the hills near the new span, including bobcats, deer, gray foxes and a female mountain lion with two kittens. Sikich said they were “sniffing around,” and he hoped they were preparing to make the crossing.
