## A May study found close to zero effect on test scores

A large-scale study published Tuesday and led by Harvard’s Center for Education Policy Research compared schools that required students to keep phones in locked pouches for the entire school day with similar schools that did not. It found modest improvements in academic growth in the restricted schools and estimated that the gains were equivalent to roughly one or two additional weeks of learning over a school year.

School cellphone rules have expanded through state legislation. In the past few years, more than 40 U.S. states have passed laws restricting cellphones at schools, according to the Phone Free Schools State Report Card, which tracks state phone legislation. The laws vary widely and often let schools design their own policies, resulting in a patchwork of rules and enforcement across the country.

The Harvard analysis used math and reading test data for grades four through 10 from the past five academic years, covering 3.3 million students at 4,600 schools nationally. The researchers cautioned that schools should not expect large immediate improvements in achievement.

Thomas Kane, a Harvard professor and one of the study’s authors, said: “Our answer is yes, phone bans do have a positive effect on growth. But it’s not a panacea.” The study described storing students’ phones as a cost-effective way to add one or two weeks of instruction each year, which could add up over time.

The research followed a global decline in student achievement that began in 2013, roughly when social media-enabled smartphones took off. The researchers sought to determine whether schools taking phones away had begun to reverse that decline. Kane said learning loss had developed gradually over a decade, making gradual gains unsurprising. The study estimated that the boost amounted to about half of the annual decline in test scores since smartphones became mainstream.

A separate National Bureau of Economic Research study published in May compared schools with bans with tens of thousands of other schools. It used a different set of standardized test scores, along with GPS phone activity, teacher surveys and data from Yondr, described as the leading provider of lockable phone pouches, at 4,600 schools that use the pouches.

Researchers from Stanford, the University of Michigan, Duke and the University of Pennsylvania found “modest positive effects” in high school test scores over three years, particularly in math. But the report’s authors were hesitant to conclude that bans produced overall learning benefits. Thomas Dee, a Stanford education economist and an author of the study, said both studies pointed to a similar conclusion: “The early results from school phone bans are largely disappointing and raise unresolved questions.”

Noting that the Harvard study showed learning gains may accumulate over time, Dee said: “I hope they’re right. However, I’m not comfortable making that extrapolation from estimates that are small, sensitive and based on just a few years of phone bans.” He offered two possible explanations: students may remain distracted on other devices, or “it may be that we see these disappointing results because out-of-school phone use is harming child development.”

Jean Twenge, a psychologist at San Diego State University and author of “10 Rules for Raising Kids in a High-Tech World,” said phone bans are not necessarily enforced. The Associated Press reported that some phone bans were largely cosmetic, noting that teens have come up with creative workarounds, including stowing burner phones or even phone-shaped rocks in their locked pouches. At schools that rely on students to keep phones in backpacks, adherence to the ban can be spotty.

“It happens pretty often that there’s a policy of no phones during the school day, bell-to-bell, and you go into the school and phones are everywhere,” Twenge said. Even without phones, she asked: “Do they have laptops at school? Are they just doing social media on laptops instead?”

Twenge also said access to a phone while studying outside school could affect learning: “If they have that phone available when they’re studying, how much are they really taking in?” Kane called out-of-school cellphone use the “big question.” He said researchers had not been able to investigate parents’ and educators’ suspicions that late-night binges on YouTube and other platforms were costly for students.

“I find it astonishing that social media companies know how much time kids spend on their platforms, but that data has never been linked to student outcomes,” Kane said. “We can’t provide answers until the social media companies share data.”
