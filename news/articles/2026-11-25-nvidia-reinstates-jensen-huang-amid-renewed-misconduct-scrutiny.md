## Experts and advocates question accountability after Huang's return

Nvidia's 10-member board unanimously reinstated Jensen Huang on Monday after a pressure campaign by Huang's technology allies and President Donald Trump. The board said it had received assurances from the U.S. government that Huang was not a national security concern, but its statement did not mention the sexual misconduct allegations at the center of the scandal. The Associated Press reported: “The company did not respond to requests for comment for this story.”

The Associated Press reported that Huang resigned after he admitted to an extramarital affair with a woman suspected of being a Chinese spy and following reports that he was involved with a second woman who was his subordinate. Huang acknowledged that he violated Nvidia's code of conduct, which warns against personal relationships that may create conflicts of interest.

Just over a year earlier, the chief executive of an Ohio technology company was forced to resign after video captured him embracing an employee at a Coldplay concert. The McDonald's chief executive was among several pushed out over a consensual relationship at the height of the #MeToo movement. Huang's reinstatement despite similar allegations has revived questions about whether powerful executives can face consequences for workplace misconduct.

Trump addressed Huang's personal conduct, predicting the scandal would only help Huang's reputation. The president, who has a history of marital infidelity and has faced sexual-assault allegations he says are false, declared in a CNBC interview, “Sex sells, fellas,” and also posted a doctored image of Huang raising a red bra in triumph. That pose parodied a famous photograph of Trump himself from moments after he survived a 2024 assassination attempt in Butler, Pennsylvania. The White House had not immediately responded to an Associated Press request for comment on Wednesday.

Cheyenne Hunt, an attorney who founded the advocacy group Reckoning Action after her role in bringing to light the sexual-assault allegations that brought down Democratic Sen. Eric Swalwell and Maine Senate candidate Graham Platner, said Trump's response reinforced the impression “that there is a class of men who see violence against women, coercion of women as a currency of power.” Hunt said, “This sends a horrible message to men everywhere that it’s open season on their direct reports.”

Randall Peterson, a professor of organizational behavior at the London Business School, said the #MeToo movement had reinforced the idea that company leaders who engage in relationships with subordinates “are in serious trouble because you cannot rule out coercion. You just cannot.” Peterson said the messaging now is that “you are at risk, as in the days before #MeToo.” He added, “I’m sad about that. I thought we had moved on from that.”

The day after returning to Nvidia, Huang apologized to employees, saying he had made mistakes and was in daily therapy, according to a livestreamed meeting reviewed by The New York Times. Huang warned that his reinstatement did not mean other employees could violate the code, saying “sometimes, it’s appropriate to forgive” and “sometimes it’s not.” He also addressed a report by The Times about his management style that included publicly berating and threatening violence against employees. Huang acknowledged he had sometimes gone too far and promised Nvidia's human resources department would henceforth collect feedback about his conduct.

Ann Lipton, a University of Colorado Boulder law professor who specializes in corporate and securities law, said the saga had “made very clear that Huang is untouchable.” She said it suggests that people who achieve a certain level of success can get away with almost anything, particularly in the male-dominated technology industry. Lipton cited Meta founder Mark Zuckerberg's comment last year that corporate culture needed more “masculine energy.” The case has brought scrutiny to the independence of Nvidia's 10-member board and to questions about the company's CEO-centered culture.

Thousands of people, including former and current employees, signed a petition calling for Huang's return. Nvidia's collapsing stock had fueled fears that the AI bubble would pop, while Huang's supporters argued that his vision, connections and deal-making skills were indispensable to the entire industry. Shares rocketed up 31.5% upon his reinstatement on Monday, although they have since fallen, and employees chanted Huang's name as he returned to headquarters.

Freada Kapor Klein, an investor and activist in equity and access issues around tech, said Nvidia and other successful technology startups “become cult-like. And people get addicted almost to being part of this inside powerful club.” Klein noted that America has a history of industries idolizing figures such as Henry Ford and Andrew Carnegie. Technology culture adds to that, she said, by “glorifying geniuses. And so there is something extra special about technological prowess, more than or on top of just business success.”

According to Nvidia's latest sustainability report, women make up 21% of the company's global workforce and an even smaller share of its managers and leaders. The report cites efforts to achieve gender pay parity, employee resource groups for women and other groups, and family-supporting policies such as generous paid parental leave. Nvidia's code of conduct says employees “must report” suspected violations and offers extensive guidelines and reassurances about protection against retaliation.

Fatima Goss Graves, president and CEO of the National Women's Law Center, which runs the Time's Up Legal Defense Fund to support workers facing sexual harassment, said Huang's reinstatement was exactly the sort of outcome that silences victims of sexual misconduct. Goss Graves said the legacy of the #MeToo movement continues to inspire women to speak out about sexual harassment and misconduct. She added that public scandals like Nvidia's sometimes provoke outrage that leads to an uptick in complaints to Time's Up and other organizations.

On the same day Huang was reinstated, Democratic Rep. Jimmy Gomez of California resigned from Congress after the House Ethics Committee released a report finding “substantial evidence” that he made unwelcome sexual advances toward a House staffer and subjected the staffer to unwanted sexual contact. Gomez was the latest in a string of lawmakers or candidates brought down by misconduct allegations in recent months. Trump's role in getting Huang's job back drew immediate bipartisan fire, and Huang is expected to testify next week before the Senate Banking Committee.

Goss Graves said, “What comes next really matters.” She warned, “If everybody shrugs their shoulders and says some people are too important to be held accountable, then we will have a problem.”
