## Federal Emergency Management Agency supports recommended Middletown relocations; environmentalists cite modeling

Army Col. Aaron Teller, a munitions emergency response specialist, said in a court filing that the Army will use a combination of C-4 explosives, explosive charges and diesel fuel to blow up the stockpile. The diesel will enhance consumption of the lead azide, while the charges are intended to direct the blast effects downward, Teller said.

The 68 barrels at the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant in Middletown were transferred there in 2009 from an Army facility in Texas and represent the nation's entire stockpile of the compound, the Army said. The material is stored in reinforced structures called magazines.

The Army plans to detonate 5 tons of degraded and dangerous lead azide on Wednesday in southeastern Iowa, in what military officials describe as an unprecedented effort to prevent a spontaneous, uncontrolled explosion at the site. The plant is not far from the Mississippi River and the Illinois and Missouri state lines. The Army and other federal agencies say their safety measures are intended to limit the plume of lead particles, while public concerns remain about potential health and environmental effects.

The Army says the lead azide is degrading and that crystallization inside the containers increases the risk of an explosion as outside temperatures change, especially below freezing. Lead azide is a highly sensitive compound containing lead and nitrogen that has historically been used as an initiating explosive in munitions and in detonators to set off other explosives. The military moved away from the compound because of its hazards, and officials said it is no longer needed for munitions production. Lead is a neurological toxicant that can harm the brain, nervous system and other organs.

According to the Army's emergency application to detonate the Iowa stockpiles, production of lead azide increased sharply during the Vietnam era, especially for use in gravel mines. Mass production began declining in the early 1980s and eventually ceased, the application said.

The Army and other federal agencies placed sand barriers about two stories high around the magazines, along with walls consisting of four 16,000-gallon (60,000-liter) water tanks. Special screening to contain the lead has been installed. A 2-foot (61-centimeter) layer of foam will flood the area, dissipate the blast and capture particles blown from the stockpiles, Army officials said. The ground above and surrounding the storage magazines will be saturated with water before and during the detonation to reduce particle dispersal.

The Army planned extensive drone surveillance of lead levels before and after the operation to determine whether levels rose and to identify areas that might need cleanup. Officials expect most blast particles to be contained at the site. During the Army's last lead azide detonation in Colorado in 1977, no mitigation measures were used and 75% of the lead particulate remained at the site, officials said.

The plume is likely to head southwest, away from nearby Burlington, a city of just under 25,000 people. The National Weather Service forecast for Wednesday called for winds of 5 to 10 mph (8 to 16 kph), blowing to the southwest.

There were no mandatory evacuations as of Tuesday. The Federal Emergency Management Agency said it was supporting a recommended relocation of all residents of Middletown, Iowa, and the immediately surrounding areas beginning Tuesday. The recommended evacuation zone is a 5-mile (8-kilometer) cone extending southwest of the Iowa plant.

A separate emergency notification sent to cellphones Tuesday morning told people as far as 20 miles (32 kilometers) away to remain indoors with windows closed “to avoid airborne lead dust, noise and vibration.” Government officials said support would be provided to people who chose to leave the area, including transportation, short-term lodging, food, water and other supplies. People who evacuated would be notified when it was safe to return.

Environmentalists who sued last week to stop the detonation said in court filings that there is no safe exposure level for lead, which is especially harmful to children. They said lead has been shown to permanently damage children's brains and development. They also said lead is harmful to adults and has been linked to heart, kidney and liver disease, reproductive harm, cognitive decline and cancer.

The environmentalists said the Army's own modeling showed that a large amount of farmland in the area as well as waterways could be contaminated with lead dust from the detonation. One filing stated: “Once lead dust is dispersed, there is no undoing the harm.” The filing continued: “Lead persists in the environment and spreads easily between media. Lead dust in the air will not just disappear once it settles to the ground—people, cattle, chickens and wildlife will continue to face exposures to lead from soil, water runoff, and deposition into waterbodies.”

Federal officials said they were taking precautions to keep people safe. Their recommendations included cleaning outdoor surfaces, including areas children have touched. The Army said: “The team overseeing this operation conducted extensive safety and environmental reviews, analyzed potential blast impacts, practiced the planned detonation procedures, and put safety zones, monitoring systems, and emergency-response plans in place.”
