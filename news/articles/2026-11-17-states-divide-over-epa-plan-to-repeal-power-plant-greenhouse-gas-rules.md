## States divide over EPA plan to repeal power plant greenhouse gas rules

**Subtype:** fact

- The Trump administration's U.S. Environmental Protection Agency has proposed rescinding its 2015 findings that it may regulate power plant greenhouse gas emissions under the Clean Air Act.
- If finalized and upheld by courts, the EPA proposal would repeal the remaining greenhouse gas regulations for fossil-fuel-fired electric generating units.
- Attorneys general from 22 mostly Democratic-leaning states plus Washington, D.C., and officials from New York City, Denver, Chicago and California's Bay Area argued that EPA has no legal basis to abandon the regulations.
- Attorneys general from West Virginia and 24 other largely Republican-leaning states said the proposal would allow states to secure affordable, reliable and environmentally responsible energy.
- More than 70 medical and public health organizations opposed the rollbacks, citing disproportionate health risks for children and other vulnerable groups.

## Medical groups warn the proposal could harm vulnerable populations

The U.S. Environmental Protection Agency said in its proposal that it does not have authority to regulate power plant greenhouse gas emissions to address global climate change concerns. The agency proposed rescinding its 2015 findings that it had authority to do so under a section of the Clean Air Act. If the proposal is finalized and upheld by courts, it would repeal the remaining power plant greenhouse gas emissions regulations for fossil fuel-fired electric generating units. EPA said the change is intended to maintain the affordability and reliability of energy supplies.

The proposal follows a final EPA rule that took effect Monday and partially repealed 2024 emissions standards for fossil fuel-fired plants, along with requirements that states develop related plans. A comment period closed earlier this month, generating more than 165,000 comments from states, health and environmental groups, and private citizens. States and public-health and environmental groups have also filed legal challenges to EPA's rollbacks.

The attorneys general of 22 mostly Democratic-leaning states, together with Washington, D.C., and officials from New York City, Denver, Chicago and California's Bay Area, submitted a joint comment last week in a letter of more than 160 pages. Their letter cited a consensus study report from the National Academies of Science, Engineering, and Medicine from last year. The report concluded that human activity-initiated greenhouse gas emissions will lead to more climate change, “with the severity of expected change increasing with every ton of greenhouse gases emitted.”

The states said EPA was pushing the proposal despite “unequivocal evidence, and the increasingly devastating climate harms” that state and local governments experience. They added that the proposal “has no basis in fact or law, would result in large-scale emission increases that will harm the States and Local Governments, and is an unlawful abdication of EPA’s responsibility under the Clean Air Act to protect public health and welfare.”

Attorneys general from West Virginia and 24 other largely Republican-leaning states took the opposite position. Their comment said the proposal “properly reverses course on past EPA attempts to reshape the Nation’s electricity-generating sector.” It would “allow States to secure affordable, reliable, and environmentally responsible energy for their residents,” the states said. Some states said EPA's regulatory authority over fossil fuel emissions was overreach and would harm their economies, while others said federal regulations were needed as states, tribes and local governments grapple with the health and costs of air pollution and climate change.

North Dakota, which signed the letter from the 25-state group, also submitted its own comment saying it strongly supports the supplemental proposal. The state cited its interest in the issue because it collects a coal conversion tax when power is generated from coal within the state. The revenue helps pay for school districts and other government services.

More than 70 medical and public health organizations submitted comments opposing the rollbacks. The groups included the American Medical Association, the American Lung Association and several state American Academy of Pediatrics chapters, which argued that the changes would harm children and other vulnerable groups. The groups wrote that “Children, older adults, people who work outside, individuals with preexisting health conditions or who are pregnant, low-wealth communities and communities of color often experience disproportionate health harms stemming from climate change and extreme weather events.”

The National Tribal Air Association, an organization of 162 tribes focused on air quality management policies and programs for American Indian and Alaska Native tribes, also opposed EPA's actions. The association said: “Many fossil fuel-fired electric generating units are located on or near American Indian and Alaska Native communities, and Tribes and Tribal members may disproportionately bear the public health and environmental consequences of the increased greenhouse gas and other emissions that this proposal would permit.”

Carrie Jenks, executive director of the Environmental & Energy Law Program at Harvard Law School, said some ongoing court cases may affect the EPA rules. She said states that support EPA's authority to regulate greenhouse gases typically also support their own authority to regulate them within their state programs. Jenks also said other groups of states want EPA's authority to be limited, if not eliminated, while some argue that states cannot regulate their own sources of greenhouse gases.

The proposal remains contingent on finalization and judicial review. If EPA finalizes it, courts would still have to uphold it before the remaining regulations would be repealed; states and public-health and environmental groups have already filed legal challenges. EPA cites energy affordability and reliability as its rationale, while opposing states, the National Tribal Air Association and health organizations emphasize public-health and climate harms.
