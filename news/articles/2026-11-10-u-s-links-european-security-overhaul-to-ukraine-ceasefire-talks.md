## Officials played down breakthrough odds after five hours of Abu Dhabi talks

The Trump administration has placed a longstanding Russian demand at the center of its push to end the war in Ukraine: renegotiating pan-European security in ways the Kremlin hopes will give it more influence in the region. Negotiators from the American, Russian, Ukrainian and European sides met in Abu Dhabi on Tuesday at the start of meetings scheduled to run through Wednesday, and U.S. officials said work on a ceasefire must proceed alongside discussions about Europe's future security arrangements.

Many European governments fear the approach could hand Moscow influence over the continent's defense arrangements, believing Russia aims to re-establish what they describe as a sphere of influence in Europe's east, giving it a say over other countries' defense and security policies. European officials and analysts say the new White House approach risks turning Russia's invasion of Ukraine into an opportunity for Moscow to win concessions on Europe's defenses that it couldn't obtain until now.

U.S. officials have said in recent days that the long war in Ukraine shows that Europe's existing security arrangements haven't worked well. "We have to build something new that works today," one U.S. official said on Monday. Trump said last week that he envisioned a broad agreement with Moscow that might involve all of Europe. "It's going to be a big, beautiful agreement, and everyone's going to be thrilled," he told reporters in the White House.

Moscow has welcomed Washington's approach. "The idea of formulating clear parameters for the future security architecture, and only then moving on to resolving the Russia-Ukraine conflict itself, is absolutely reasonable," Russian Foreign Minister Sergei Lavrov said on Tuesday.

European policymakers have pushed back. "We already have a European security architecture in place," the European Union's foreign-policy chief Kaja Kallas said on Monday. "The problem is that Russia is not respecting these agreements."

Witkoff and Kushner sounded a note closer to the European and previous U.S. position on Tuesday. In a joint statement with European allies and Ukraine, they said they were discussing "how to build upon existing European security institutions and agreements." U.S. officials stress that NATO would remain central to European defense and that countries would retain independent decision-making. Washington is working on guarantees for Ukraine resembling NATO's collective defense, subject to Trump's approval.

Andrew Weiss, a former U.S. national-security official and a Russia expert at the Carnegie Endowment for International Peace, a Washington think tank, said that for decades U.S. policy under both Republican and Democratic administrations has emphasized that Russia should have a voice but not a veto about the security landscape in Europe. "No wonder the Ukrainians and the Europeans are deeply worried about deals possibly being cut behind their backs," he said.

European governments believe that a rebuilt Russian sphere of influence would rewrite Europe's post-Cold War order, sponsored by the U.S. under both Republican and Democratic presidents, which guaranteed all countries' sovereignty, including their freedom to choose their own alliances, while deepening arms control agreements. Russia signed up to those principles in a series of treaties in the 1990s, but under President Vladimir Putin it has turned instead to a massive military buildup, territorial expansion and an effort to restore its political sway over Ukraine and other neighbors.

Putin has for years demanded restrictions on Western military deployments in Europe and on countries' freedom to choose their own alliances. In late 2021, shortly before launching the full-scale invasion of Ukraine, the Kremlin demanded an effective rollback of the North Atlantic Treaty Organization in countries that were formerly Soviet satellites or part of the Soviet Union, such as Poland and the Baltic states. Moscow also demanded a ban on admitting any new members to NATO and the removal of U.S. nuclear weapons from Europe. Washington and NATO rejected those demands. Putin has said no peace deal can be reached without addressing what he calls the conflict's "root causes," including NATO's expansion.

The pairing also follows a series of U.S. concessions and inducements to Moscow. The U.S. position has moved significantly since Trump returned to the White House, and his administration has argued that its concessions and inducements to Moscow would encourage Putin to compromise. The administration has pressed Ukraine to surrender territory in its east that Russia has been unable to conquer, pressured Kyiv to stop attacking Russian oil refineries, and offered Moscow a series of potential energy and other economic deals. In recent weeks, Kushner and Witkoff have argued that expanding the scope of a peace deal to encompass a rethink of European security arrangements would increase the chances of success.

Talks in Abu Dhabi concluded Tuesday after around five hours. U.S. officials said the talks are intended to identify remaining disagreements and lay the groundwork for further negotiations, and Kremlin foreign-policy adviser Yuri Ushakov described the meeting as a "trial run" to see if talks are making any progress from Russia's perspective. Officials from all sides played down the chances of a breakthrough at the two-day meeting.

European officials believe the Kremlin has no intention of ending the war, noting intensifying Russian attacks on Ukraine and Putin's unchanged war aims. But with the U.S. pushing for discussions about their continent's security arrangements, European countries want to be at the table to prevent agreements being imposed on them, senior diplomats said.

Witkoff and Kushner have sought to mediate between Russia and Ukraine since last year. But with Putin regularly reaffirming war aims that The Wall Street Journal describes as seeking Ukraine's subjugation, and Kyiv refusing to capitulate, the newspaper assessed that no end to the war is in sight.
