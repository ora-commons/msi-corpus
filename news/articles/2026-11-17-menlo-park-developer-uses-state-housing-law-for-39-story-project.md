## Developer lawsuit tests California’s Builder’s Remedy against local zoning

N17 Development wants Menlo Park to sign off on Willow Park, a group of high-rises proposed for a nearly 7-acre parcel in Linfield Oaks, a neighborhood of midcentury homes, manicured lawns and tree-lined streets. Developer Oisin Heneghan, 45, says the 665 homes, offices, hotel and shops would help address Silicon Valley’s severe housing shortage. The tallest tower would rise 39 stories, or 458 feet, about as tall as the Great Pyramid of Giza. According to the city, height limits in the property’s district are 35-40 feet.

Menlo Park says it tried to adopt a long-term housing plan but could not meet the Jan. 31, 2023, deadline, which it attributed to administrative delays in Sacramento. California’s Builder’s Remedy permits developers to bypass local zoning in cities that lack a long-term housing plan satisfactory to the state. The missed deadline left Menlo Park out of compliance and allowed Heneghan’s company to seek speedy city approval of Willow Park under the provision that same year.

The state rules are not new, but California is enforcing them more aggressively. The Wall Street Journal reported that California sits at the center of America’s housing and affordability crisis, which experts blame heavily on local barriers to construction. One wealthy Bay Area suburb tried to declare itself a mountain lion refuge to block apartments.

Menlo Park has repeatedly refused the project since N17 began that pursuit. Approval would set aside 15% of the units as affordable in an area near Meta Platforms’ headquarters and Stanford University; a city analysis put an affordable two-bedroom at $2,556 a month. Before approving, local leaders want environmental reviews, including the project’s effect on a creek known as a home to steelhead and the California red-legged frog.

California Attorney General Rob Bonta’s office, which has sued other California cities over housing production, warned Menlo Park in July to step aside or else. “When it comes to housing, one of our most important jobs is to make sure that local governments follow state law,” the office said. In October, N17 Development sued the city on behalf of property owner Willow Project LLC, seeking $6.65 million in fines that would go into a local or state housing trust fund.

The lawsuit could be a major test of the Builder’s Remedy, which some cities describe as a draconian loophole that lets developers skirt local rules, including height limits. “An adverse ruling against Menlo Park’s arguments would likely force cities to roll over on Builder’s Remedy projects,” said Pat Burt, a city councilman in neighboring Palo Alto, which has agreed to file a brief backing Menlo Park. “Is it a loophole when you take the keys back from your child for driving recklessly? No, it’s a consequence,” said Sonja Trauss, executive director of YIMBY Law, a pro-housing group in San Francisco. “People need to get used to the idea we need a tremendous amount of housing because we are in a horrible crisis.”

Developer Oisin Heneghan dismissed the backlash as the same Nimbyism that often blocks the housing renters like him need to become homeowners. Nor did he see an issue with Willow Park’s height. “I think tall buildings are good,” said Heneghan, 45 years old. “I challenge anyone to go and find me a 600-unit, eight-story building that looks good.”

Many residents and local lawmakers describe the proposed towers as grotesquely oversize. “Horrific,” said Sean Moynihan, ringing up groceries at the Willows Market across the street. “I mean, look at traffic right now. I can only imagine what it would do, bringing in that many people.” Critics also objected to the nearly 7-acre parcel’s owner, an entity controlled by Vitaly Yusufov, the son of former Russian Energy Minister Igor Yusufov. At one demonstration, a protester’s sign read, “Just say nyet to the towers.”

In late September, U.S. Rep. Sam Liccardo, a California Democrat, urged the Treasury Department to review Willow Park on national-security grounds. He said the land was “owned by a Russian citizen, Vitaly Yusufov,” the son of former Russian Energy Minister Igor Yusufov. Liccardo wrote that Treasury should “ensure Kremlin officials and their proxies cannot obtain a foothold in the epicenter of American innovation.” A Treasury Department official said the agency takes such allegations seriously but did not say whether it would examine the project.

The Wall Street Journal previously reported that Deutsche Bank AG, which formerly owned the parcel, sold it to a Yusufov-controlled company in 2018 despite some internal concern from a reputational risk committee. The bank identified no evidence the deal “would violate money-laundering or sanctions laws. As a result, the discussion of whether to move forward with the transaction was focused on reputational considerations,” a spokesman for Deutsche Bank said at the time. Daria Pustilnik, a lawyer for Yusufov, said those concerns came from a minority of committee members that approved the deal. She described Yusufov as a real-estate developer with projects in Europe and said he has no Kremlin ties.

In an Oct. 3 letter, Pustilnik warned Liccardo that suggesting her client or his family had unsavory ties was false and defamatory. “It’s basically a smear campaign against them that’s been going on for a while, including articles by extortionist publishers,” Pustilnik said in an interview. Liccardo’s spokeswoman, Izzy Connolly, called the letter “a sad attempt to divert attention from a bad client.”

The property, once home to the headquarters of Sunset Magazine, has oak and pine trees around a courtyard and an adobe homestead-style building. During a walking tour, Heneghan pointed to a planned swim club beside redwoods and a Montessori school near a giant live oak. He said neighbors could use the club and that the plans also include a rooftop pool and a five-star hotel atop one tower. Heneghan said the hotel bar could create activity: “You can go to the hotel bar and have, you know, a burger and a beer and there’s people there and it creates that activity.”

Days earlier, Menlo Park Mayor Betsy Nash and other critics toured the site and saw the plan differently. “We’re all for building more housing, because we all have kids who want to live here,” said Elizabeth Burr, a local homeowner on the tour. “What we don’t need is more office space in Menlo Park. We have plenty, plenty, and we don’t need a new five-star hotel and spa.” Other critics argue the project would worsen the housing crunch by creating more jobs than homes and add cars to roads already congested by commuters.

John O’Brien, a retired real-estate executive whose children attend Menlo Park’s highly ranked schools, welcomed the proposal because it could help families afford to live there. “Creating housing to allow parents to bring kids into a school district that will benefit kids, that’s a good thing,” he said. The legal question in N17 Development’s suit is whether Menlo Park’s refusal violates California’s housing law and whether the Builder’s Remedy can overcome local zoning and environmental-review objections.
