## Harrell outlines prosecution, settlement or pardon options for Nvidia

Greg Ip, writing in The Wall Street Journal's Economics newsletter, argued that Nvidia had become, for now, effectively a government-sponsored enterprise. He said the episode had all but erased the line between the chipmaker's boardroom and the state, leaving Nvidia's mission and governance effectively steered by the federal government. Ip wrote that Huang owed his job to Trump.

Nvidia's board said it rehired Huang after receiving assurances that he was not a national security risk, and Trump said the Justice Department knew of no unlawful conduct. Ip placed Nvidia alongside Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac, the original government-sponsored enterprises.

Ip cited Intel as a recent example of the administration's leverage over corporate decisions. Last year, Trump called on Intel to fire its CEO over ties to China, then relented after the company granted the federal government a 10% stake. Ip used the episode to argue that the administration can use regulatory and law-enforcement influence to obtain corporate cooperation.

Even so, Ip cautioned that Nvidia's legal shield lasts only as long as Trump is willing and able to provide it. He said investigations still surround Huang and the company. Peter Harrell, an attorney specializing in international finance who advised former President Joe Biden, said the Justice Department, perhaps under a future administration, could still prosecute Nvidia. Harrell said the company may seek a settlement now, while Trump may pardon it.

The arrangement with Trump predates the current scandal, Ip wrote. Huang had personally lobbied Trump to allow Nvidia to sell chips to China, and Trump agreed in return for a cut of chip sales. Ip said that close alignment turned Nvidia into what he called the poster child for America's drift into state capitalism and helped create conditions for the current scandal. He wrote that state capitalism remained alive.

The episode exposed a division inside the Trump administration over the goal of export controls, Ip wrote. He said Secretary of State Marco Rubio and, more recently, Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent were what he called China hawks and wanted to deprive China of the most advanced U.S. technology. He called Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick a dove who wanted to wring as much money from China as possible. Ip said the hawks seized on Huang's downfall to push through tougher controls, making his return a setback but not a defeat.

Ip said Huang could still persuade Trump to weaken the new controls or look the other way as customers circumvent them, though Huang's diminished standing may make that harder. The congressional mood has also shifted: Republican senators Tom Cotton and John Kennedy have criticized Lutnick and Huang, and Ip said Democrats plan sweeping investigations in January of the ties between Trump and Silicon Valley. Ip said bipartisan support is growing for attaching tougher export controls to must-pass defense funding.

Eddie Fishman of the Council on Foreign Relations said enforcement remains with the administration. “If tech companies think they can make decisions that undermine U.S. national security with no repercussions, even more stringent export control laws may not materially affect their corporate decision-making,” he said.

Ip called markets the ultimate arbiter and said Huang mattered to Trump not only because of his importance to AI but because of his effect on the stock market. Huang told CNBC Monday, as the market roared back, “Investors should be very happy.”

Ip placed the episode within the administration's broader management of economic markets. He cited rising bond yields caused by inflation and deficits, followed by Bessent's response of buying back some longer-dated issues. He also cited oil-price spikes caused by the war with Iran and said Trump repeatedly talked prices down with promises of ceasefires and victories that never happened. Ip argued that interventions of this kind would not remove the underlying problems disturbing markets or lift what he called the shadow over AI.

AI demand remains the larger economic concern. Ip said demand for Nvidia chips and computing power has been driven by hyperscalers such as Alphabet, Meta and SpaceX and frontier labs such as Anthropic, with that demand financed not by commercial revenue but by the debt markets and by Nvidia itself. He said Huang's return would not resolve doubts about whether that investment can be sustained.

Those unresolved questions leave the episode short of closure. Ip said his speculation last week that the scandal might halt the drift toward state capitalism now appears premature, and he wrote that the story is not over.
