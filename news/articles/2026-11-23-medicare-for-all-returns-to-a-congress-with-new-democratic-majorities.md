## KFF polling shows stronger support as Democrats confront policy costs

New Democratic congressional majorities are bringing Medicare for All back into the health-policy debate after the midterm elections. Among the election winners were candidates who made universal, government-funded coverage, or some variation of it, a central pillar of their campaigns. That group included new Democratic U.S. senators-elect from Michigan, Maine, Iowa and Texas.

Those candidates, along with many of their soon-to-be colleagues, say they will push the new Democratic majorities in the House and Senate to solve the health-care affordability problem many voters raised on the campaign trail.

Although virtually every elected official supports making health care available and affordable for all Americans, the route to that goal has repeatedly divided Democrats when health care reached the top of the national agenda. The Clinton health reform effort in 1993-94 fractured over differences within the Democratic majority: people who wanted a dramatic overhaul such as Medicare for All argued with those who saw incremental changes, including a public option, as more politically feasible. The Affordable Care Act made incremental reforms despite heated politics and barely squeaked into law in 2010 after similar disputes.

The renewed debate begins with a definition problem. Most Americans have no real idea what the phrase "Medicare for All" actually means. "It's cosplay," said Nicholas Bagley, a law professor at the University of Michigan who has written about health policy for years. "When you say 'Medicare for All,' people think 'Healthcare — good. We should have more of it.'"

Support also depends on how the proposal is described. Mollyann Brodie — who oversees KFF's public opinion and survey research program as executive vice president and chief operating officer of the health information nonprofit — said support rises and falls dramatically depending on the description. "I can move it up to three-quarters [percent approval] if I talk about it being a guaranteed right to healthcare or eliminating insurance premiums and reducing out-of-pocket costs," she said of survey respondents' support, on a recent episode of the What the Health? From KFF Health News podcast. "I can plummet that support down to the 20s if I talk about it requiring people to pay more in taxes or threatening the current Medicare program."

Medicare itself presents a second problem for Democrats. The program covers adults 65 and older and some people with disabilities. As of this year, the trust fund underpinning its hospital insurance part is projected to become insolvent in 2033. Costs are rising for beneficiaries, who face significant out-of-pocket spending on premiums, deductibles and coinsurance. Traditional Medicare also fails to cover services such as dental, vision, hearing and, most expensively, long-term care. Expanding Medicare benefits would be expensive and likely require large tax increases, according to most outside estimates.

The new Democratic majorities in Congress will have to decide whether their priority is to reverse the spending cuts made by the Trump administration to Medicaid for low-income and disabled people and to Affordable Care Act health plans, or to expand coverage and lower costs, turning back time to before President Donald Trump's second term began. Brodie said, "Expectations are super high among voters right now, and they are demanding action on healthcare costs."

There are already indications that Democratic leaders are listening to the Medicare for All wing of their party. The day before Rep. Hakeem Jeffries of New York was officially nominated as the next speaker of the House, he voiced support for expanding government-funded coverage, including lowering the age of Medicare eligibility and covering all children.

Even if Democrats can pass legislation to restore what was cut, there is every expectation that Trump would veto those efforts. He has referred to Medicare for All in the past as "a socialist takeover of our healthcare system." The new majorities are large, but they are not veto-proof.

The larger the majority, the more ideologically diverse it will be, a reality both parties often forget. The next Congress will include more progressives pursuing Medicare for All, but it will also have more moderate members from swing districts currently represented by Republicans. That is the reverse of the splits that divided the GOP in the current Congress, with conservatives from safe red districts pitted against moderates from purplish ones.

One big difference in the coming Democrat-on-Democrat debates, compared with those in 1993 and 2009, is the readiness for something bigger suggested by KFF's September tracking poll. In those earlier debates, one thing that hindered Democrats trying to fix the health system was resistance from a plurality, if not a majority, of Americans who were relatively satisfied with their current health coverage. During the Clinton-era health reform debate, the health insurance industry sponsored the famous "Harry and Louise" ads, showing a couple at their kitchen table worrying that proposed changes would leave them worse off financially.

KFF's September tracking poll found not only that professed support for Medicare for All was at its highest since the organization began regularly asking about it, but also that a majority of respondents would support the policy even if it meant eliminating the private insurance industry. The majority finding was a first and a significant change, suggesting the public is so dissatisfied with the status quo that it would rather risk something completely new than keep current coverage.

That sentiment could change fast when industry opposition revs up a counter-campaign. For now, the survey results suggest the next health-reform debate could start with a public ready for something bigger. Whether the people who could make it happen can agree on a proposal remains an open question.
