## Boris Pistorius says U.S. cooperation will not change after Democratic win

**Subtype:** fact

- Foreign leaders and international newspapers responded to the Democrats' 2026 U.S. midterm victory with reactions ranging from calls for cooperation to diplomatic warnings.
- Spanish Prime Minister Pedro Sanchez said American society voted against impoverishing policies, hate, machismo, climate denialism and the privileges of elites.
- South African President Cyril Ramaphosa said the result could introduce a new era in world relations and expressed hope for bilateral cooperation.
- Russian Deputy Foreign Minister Sergei Ryabkov said the Democratic Party's activist wing remained "extremely negative" toward Russia while signaling no major shifts in Russian foreign policy.
- German Defense Minister Boris Pistorius called the result an "historic victory" and said Berlin's cooperation with Washington would remain unchanged.
- Japan's Chief Cabinet Secretary Minoru Kihara said the U.S. alliance remains essential for global peace and stability regardless of the election outcome.

## Norway cites emerging American voices closer to Norwegian positions

Norway's Prime Minister Jonas Gahr Stoere called the results a "powerful political signal about a change of course among American voters." Speaking to Reuters, he said "voices are now emerging in American politics that are closer to us than the president and the administration have stood for, for example on climate and environment, and in foreign policy and development aid policy."

Germany's Defense Minister Boris Pistorius congratulated Democrats on an "historic victory." He said Berlin's commitment to cooperating with the administration in Washington remains unchanged and that Berlin would reach out to both Democrats and Republicans.

Japan's top government spokesman and Chief Cabinet Secretary Minoru Kihara told a press conference that Japan's alliance with the U.S. remains essential for global peace and stability, regardless of the outcome of the midterm elections. He said the alliance remains "the cornerstone of Japan's foreign and security policy" and is crucial to "maximizing its national interests" amid an increasingly volatile and uncertain global situation.

China's foreign ministry spokeswoman told a daily press conference that the midterm elections "are an internal affair of the United States." She said China was willing to continue working with the U.S. to "strengthen communication, properly manage differences, expand cooperation, and build a constructive, strategically stable China-U.S. relationship based on the principles of respect, fairness, and reciprocity."

Russia's Deputy Foreign Minister Sergei Ryabkov said, "The approach of the activist wing of the Democratic Party towards Russia is extremely negative. There's nothing new in that." He said a Democratic return in both houses of Congress boded ill for U.S.-Russian relations while signaling that Russian foreign policy would undergo no grand shift.

Iran's Parliament Speaker and top negotiator Mohammad Baqer Ghalibaf commented on X: "Conscience or the cost of living, Americans said NO TO WAR, and we endorse this message. If the US govt betrays their public will to serve international paymasters and blackmailers, it will learn our NO comes with a Nightmare."

Spanish Prime Minister Pedro Sanchez, whom NPR described as having an acrimonious relationship with Trump, was forthright on X. He said, "Yesterday, American society voted against policies that only impoverish. Against hate. Against machismo. Against climate denialism and against the privileges of the elites." In South Africa, NPR reported that diplomatic relations with the U.S. were at an all-time low after Trump falsely accused the South African government of persecuting its white minority. President Cyril Ramaphosa, casting a ballot in South Africa's municipal elections Wednesday, said, "The people of America have spoken." He said he hoped the result would "introduce a new era in world relations" and added that he hoped for "a coming together of minds" in bilateral relations.

There was no immediate reaction from UK Prime Minister Andy Burnham. London Mayor Sadiq Khan, whom NPR described as someone Trump had frequently sparred with, said on X: "Congratulations to Democrats on a huge victory last night. With a focus on hope, unity and supporting families with the cost of living - their campaign was a true rejection of the damaging and divisive politics of Donald Trump."

International headlines were dominated by the Democratic result and blue-wave language. France's Libération called the win "une vague bleue," or blue wave, while Britain's Daily Mail dubbed it "a historic blue tsunami." The Times of India ran the headline "midterm blues for Trump."

Countries at war were also watching closely. The Kyiv Independent's online headline was hopeful: "Democrats seize control of Congress in blow to Trump, potential boost for Ukraine." Russia's state-run Rossiyskaya Gazeta went in the opposite direction, suggesting the result would instead be a blow to Ukraine, which it said had firm support from the EU. Its headline read: "Brussels, brace yourself. After the Republicans' defeat, Trump's wrath will fall on Europe."

The Tehran Times put the Republican defeat down to President Trump's decision earlier in the year to attack Iran. Its headline read: "The Iran war comes home to hit Trump at the ballot box." NPR reported that the war, which Trump had promised would be over quickly, had dragged on, sent oil prices soaring and affected many Americans' daily lives with the affordability crisis.

In Israel, which had just held its own elections the previous week, headlines focused on what the Democrats' victory meant for U.S. support for the Jewish state and the war in Gaza. The Times of Israel ran a headline focusing on Democrat and Muslim candidate Abdul El-Sayed's triumph in the Michigan Senate race: "Anti-Israel El-Sayed wins Michigan Senate race, pledges no money for 'apartheid, genocide.'" Haaretz's headline read: "Abdul El-Sayed Shows Opposition to Israel Is No Barrier to Winning Battleground States."

NPR described Canada as being in the midst of a contentious trade war with the U.S. when it reported the Toronto Star's "rebuke" to Trump. Across the southern border, Mexico's El Universal ran the headline "Setback for Trump." Many foreign leaders shied away from commenting directly because, unlike a U.S. presidential election, they saw the midterms as a domestic American issue rather than an occasion for congratulating the winner.

For Israel, the U.S. result came during political uncertainty and coalition-building talks after incumbent Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu, whom NPR described as a Trump ally, lost his parliamentary majority in the Oct. 27 Israeli elections. Israel-American analyst Mairav Zonstein said, "In some ways Netanyahu lost two elections in the last week." Netanyahu's rivals used the U.S. result in their domestic arguments. Yair Golan said the U.S. results revealed "just how toxic Netanyahu is among both Democrats and Republicans." Yair Lapid said the results "eliminate any possibility of a unity government led by Netanyahu."

Germany and China said they would continue working with Washington, while Japan said its U.S. alliance remained essential regardless of the election outcome and Norway cited emerging American political voices closer to Norwegian positions. Separately, Ryabkov warned about U.S.-Russian relations and Ghalibaf warned against U.S. betrayal of the public's antiwar will.
