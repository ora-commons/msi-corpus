## Denmark's 110% target follows priority on deep emission reductions

Early versions of the end-of-conference communique at the United Nations climate talks known as COP31 acknowledge that the world has failed to keep long-term warming below 1.5°C, will enter a period of decades of temperatures above that threshold and then bend the curve back below it. Doing that requires sharp cuts in carbon dioxide emissions and the removal of carbon from the atmosphere. Carbon dioxide emitted by burning fossil fuels such as coal, oil and gas traps heat in the atmosphere and causes the planet to warm. The negotiations in Turkey are scheduled to end Friday, though they rarely finish on time.

For the first time, the talks are likely to conclude that the world will have to remove heat-trapping gases from the air as well as cut carbon pollution because society has failed to keep long-term warming below the 1.5°C Paris threshold. Scientists and United Nations officials said the work would cost tens of trillions of dollars and rely on still-unproven technology, but described removal as “the best the globe can hope for” because exceeding the 1.5°C threshold set by the 2015 Paris climate agreement and confirmed by subsequent scientific reports was now inevitable.

The sequence begins with emissions cuts. The world will need to remove massive amounts of carbon dioxide, “but there’s not much point of thinking about that until we have deep emission reductions,” Climate Analytics CEO Bill Hare said at the conference. Unless policies around the world change, Hare said, the world is heading for at least 2.7°C of warming by 2100, and there is no way to remove enough carbon from the air to compensate for that warming. Inger Andersen, director of the United Nations Environment Programme, said the world had to seriously consider carbon removal “because there really are no good outcomes above 1.5 degrees,” noting that warming is already causing stronger heat waves, droughts, floods and extreme storms. Andersen and other speakers said carbon removal, sometimes called negative emissions, was an addition to steep emissions cuts rather than a replacement.

Officials, analysts and scientists said years of calls for steep emissions cuts had gone unmet, leaving the planet reliant on carbon removal. “This is something I don’t think everyone has internalized yet. If you’re a champion of 1.5 degrees C, then you’re also a champion of reaching net-negative emissions,” said World Resources Institute global climate and economics director Melanie Robinson. Even after describing the difficulties of carbon removal, Rockström said: “we must keep this as our safe landing temperature, now that we fully understand how dangerous it is to exceed 1.5°C.”

Inger Andersen said at the COP31 conference this week that the world needs to remove 320 billion metric tons, or 350 billion tons, of carbon dioxide from the atmosphere this century to bend the curve back down. She described the requirement as eight years of global emissions. Berkeley Earth climate scientist Zeke Hausfather calculated that reducing global temperatures by every tenth of a degree Celsius, or .18°F, through carbon removal would cost about $22 trillion. Even if the world were to peak at two or three tenths of a degree Celsius, or .36 to .54°F, above the Paris threshold — the most optimistic scenario — it would cost $44 trillion to $66 trillion by 2100 to return below the threshold, Hausfather said.

Planting more trees would not remove anywhere near enough carbon, experts said. Andersen said increased tree planting and other nature-based solutions removed at most 2 billion metric tons of carbon dioxide in the past decade. The required scale calls for mechanical methods, including huge devices resembling giant fans that suck carbon out of the air, some of which are being tested at pilot scale.

“Carbon removal is the magic eraser we’re left with when we’ve busted the world’s carbon budget, such as the 1.5C target,” Stanford University climate scientist and emissions tracker Rob Jackson said in an email. “Is carbon dioxide removal feasible technically? Yes. Is it likely to happen at the billion-ton scale needed to make a difference? No.” Atmospheric carbon removal differs from carbon capture and storage technology used by some industries to prevent their own pollution from escaping into the air. The climate talks are also likely to encourage more of that type of carbon capture.

Johan Rockström, director of the Potsdam Institute for Climate Impacts Research in Germany, said the nascent mechanical technology currently removes as much carbon dioxide in a year as fossil-fuel-burning plants put into the air every half hour. He said the current hope was that it could scale to 5 billion metric tons by 2050.

“We need deep emission reductions. That is, and will always remain, the most important tool at our disposal,” said Denmark's environment minister Samira Nawa. Nawa said that priority was why Denmark was aiming for a 110% reduction in emissions, going beyond net zero by leaving 10% less carbon in the air than oceans and forests naturally remove. Denmark and Ethiopia are leading a newly formed coalition called the Group of Negative Emitters, or GONE, and Ethiopia will chair next year's climate talks.

A United Nations annual report issued earlier in 2026 had already acknowledged it was certain that the planet would breach the Paris threshold of keeping long-term warming below 1.5°C, or 2.7°F, compared with pre-industrial levels dating to the mid-1800s. That threshold is measured over a couple of decades, while short-term warning signs continue to mount.

October's global average temperature set the record for the greatest departure above pre-industrial levels for any month, at 1.8°C, or 3.2°F, above that baseline, Berkeley Earth scientists said. They predicted that 2026 would pass 2024 as the hottest year on record.
