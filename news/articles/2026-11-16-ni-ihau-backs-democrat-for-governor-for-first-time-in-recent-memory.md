## Turnout on Ni‘ihau falls to lowest level on record

The island of Ni‘ihau, about 17 miles off the western coast of Kaua‘i, has long been a Republican stronghold in deeply Democratic Hawaiʻi, but it voted for a Democratic gubernatorial ticket this month for the first time in recent memory, delivering 14 of its 21 ballots to Green and Kawakami. The result arrived in an election in which Democrats retook the House and Senate.

The Democratic vote came against a Republican habit that reaches back to the early 1900s, when Ni‘ihau ballots were sometimes relayed to Kaua‘i by carrier pigeon. A 1939 Honolulu Star Bulletin headline read: "Ni‘ihau Casts 59 Votes All For GOP as Usual." Bush beat Kerry 39-0, Reagan beat Carter 56-1, and Nixon beat Kennedy 99-0.

In 2020, all 43 Ni‘ihauan voters cast ballots for Donald Trump. In 2022, when Green first ran, he won only four of the island's votes to his Republican challenger's 26. Records are spotty before the turn of the century, but they show no Ni‘ihau support for a Democratic governor in the 21st century before this year; the most recent example found was 1978, when George Ariyoshi won 74 of the island's 86 votes. Ni‘ihauans narrowly backed Barack Obama in 2012 and supported Democratic House and Senate candidates in 2016, according to the Hawaii Office of Elections.

The Guardian describes Ni‘ihau, also known as the Forbidden Island, as "one of the most secretive places on Earth." Ni‘ihau itself is unlike anywhere else in the state. The 40,000-acre island has no hotels, condos, paved roads, electrical grid or running water; residents travel by horse or bicycle and collect rainfall in catchment systems. It retains an element of 1800s Hawaiian-ness that has been lost throughout the rest of the state. Its main economic engine is a small U.S. Navy installation, and it is the only island where the Hawaiian language remains the primary mode of communication.

The island's character reflects a century and a half of management by the Robinsons, a Scottish plantation family that bought Ni‘ihau from King Kamehameha V for $10,000 in 1864. Nobody may set foot there without the family's permission — a condition now administered by octogenarian brothers Bruce and Keith Robinson, with Bruce's Ni‘ihauan wife, Leiana Robinson, handling much of the day-to-day management. Ni‘ihauans live rent-free as the family's invited guests but follow a strict code of conduct aligned with the family's Calvinist values; drugs, alcohol, tattoos or long hair on men can be grounds for permanent exile, as can speaking about the Robinsons or the island out of turn. While Ni‘ihauans are allowed a certain degree of self-governance, the final say on significant issues always goes to the Robinsons.

Ni‘ihau's conservative voting patterns mirror the beliefs of the family, which has long been staunchly Republican. Keith Robinson, 85, describes himself as a "rightwing extremist" and has frequently railed against leftwing environmentalists, whom he calls "eco-nazis." Researcher Edward Stepien wrote in a 1984 University of Hawaii master's dissertation that "the political leanings of Ni‘ihau are as predictable as the tides or trade winds."

Asked about Ni‘ihau supporting a Democrat for governor, Keith Robinson said, "That's their business, not mine." He appeared broadly disheartened by the midterm results, in which Democrats retook the House and Senate. "The United States is declining like ancient Rome," he said. "I did my part to try to shore up the system. I worked for many years hard and faithfully, but there comes a time when you just have to give up on the whole mess."

One possible explanation for the island's Democratic votes lies in local ties. Kawakami, who is part native Hawaiian, served as mayor of Kaua‘i county, which includes Ni‘ihau, and has drawn Ni‘ihauan support in previous nonpartisan mayoral races. Democratic congresswoman Jill Tokuda, who lacks close Kaua‘i ties, did not draw the same level of Ni‘ihauan support.

Kawakami's campaign manager, Dan Giovanni, said the candidate has worked with the island "on their terms, respecting their privacy and way of life," and that the Kawakami administration collaborated with island management after Hurricane Lowell battered Ni‘ihau in September. "The success of the Green-Kawakami ticket on Ni‘ihau is rooted in a record of listening and working together, especially in times of need," Giovanni said.

Green, who won re-election easily, said he had met personally with members of the Robinson family. "I have a very warm feeling from them," Green said. "We share the same values and I think that's why I was able to humbly earn their support."

Election records also show the island's population is shrinking. Historical records generally list approximately 100 registered Ni‘ihauan voters; this year there were 48, and 21 cast ballots — the lowest turnout in the years on record. Keith Robinson said he was unhappy about Ni‘ihauans leaving the island. "It's been gradually happening over the years and I'm not too happy about it," he said. "Some people like a nice quiet life, and other people are more attracted to city lights and entertainment."
