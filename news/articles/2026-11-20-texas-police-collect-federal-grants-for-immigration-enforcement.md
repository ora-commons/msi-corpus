## Smith County received the most funding among 93 agencies KXAN reviewed

A ProPublica adaptation of an original KXAN investigation, co-published with The Texas Tribune, found that 429 Texas law-enforcement agencies had entered agreements with Immigration and Customs Enforcement as of mid-September. The co-publication was part of an initiative to report on state and federal efforts affecting local control. Texas led the nation in the partnerships, known as 287(g) agreements, which authorize local officers to make immigration arrests during their normal course of duty. The agreements came as President Donald Trump sought to accelerate deportations, and federal funding had long been a key incentive for local governments across the country to participate.

The federal program directs millions of dollars to local law enforcement while requiring few measurable results and providing little tracking of how the money is spent. Agencies can receive federal reimbursement for salaries, overtime and benefits for “conducting purposeful and intentional” immigration-enforcement activities once the contract starts, according to a service agreement obtained by KXAN. Agencies can also receive recurring quarterly incentive payments and one-time stipends for equipment, technology and vehicles, though federal memoranda and agreements obtained by KXAN did not specify what agencies could purchase.

In April, Pantego Police Chief Robert Rife took the proposal to the town council in the North Texas town of 2,500 residents a few miles east of Fort Worth. Rife said, “I won’t say there’s unlimited funding, but there’s quite a bit of money in the fund,” as council members approved an agreement with Immigration and Customs Enforcement. After a single immigration arrest in June, Pantego received $100,000. Rife projected an additional $720,000 in federal grants; combined with the money already received, that would equal roughly 40% of Pantego’s $2.1 million police budget last year.

KXAN reviewed minutes from nine local government meetings and found that Texas law-enforcement officials repeatedly emphasized the program’s financial benefits for departments facing constrained budgets when pitching local elected leaders. In the same month Pantego joined, the police chief in the small suburb of Hollywood Park near San Antonio made a similar pitch to the city council, saying the city “actively profits on payroll for the time spent.” In Smith County, Sheriff Larry Smith told county commissioners that “equipment, use of fuel, mileage and things of that nature” could be reimbursed, along with $100,000 for two new vehicles and money for overtime.

Smith County Commissioner John Moore asked Smith, “How often do the feds offer us money for anything?” Smith replied, “Not very often.” Smith declined an interview with KXAN. Moore, the only member of the Smith County Commissioners Court who agreed to speak with KXAN, explained why he thought the commissioners wanted to partner. His first word was “budgetary.” He said, “Any kind of training that any of our departments need to have, or are required to have, costs money, and it’s rare that we get an opportunity to get funding for training from outside county taxes.” He added, “Any time we can save the taxpayers money, I’m all for it.”

KXAN’s review found little tracking of how participating agencies spend the federal money. Austin-based immigration attorney Kate Lincoln-Goldfinch said she worried local law enforcement would become dependent on the financial incentives and have difficulty leaving the program later. “I’m concerned about what this money is going to be doing to the mindset of these small police departments,” she said. Other local city and law-enforcement officials said the additional money helped officers protect their communities and carry out core duties. Immigration and Customs Enforcement did not respond to an interview request but provided a statement describing the program; its website says the program targets “dangerous criminal offenders.”

In the Texas Panhandle, Hartley County Sheriff Chanze Fowler said nearly all the federal funding his department receives supports regular operations in addition to immigration work. Records obtained by KXAN showed that the office recorded 54 arrests under 287(g) authority from January to early July, most for minor traffic and vehicle violations, and received more than $205,000 during that time. By summer, the office had spent about $30,000 on things like wages, extra pay for officers who became 287(g) certified, payroll taxes, retirement funds and “fleet purchases.” Fowler said almost all of the 287(g)-related funds “go towards items needed for our everyday job.”

Pantego officials have declined to answer KXAN’s questions about the department’s involvement since August despite repeated attempts to reach the mayor, town council, city manager and police chief by phone, email and in person. City Manager Leslie Galloway, the only official to respond, said, “We are not interested in interviewing at this time.” Ammon Blair, a senior fellow at the conservative Texas Public Policy Foundation, which supports the program, acknowledged the money can help departments operating on thin budgets, including with overtime. Blair also said federal spending should receive more oversight.

KXAN obtained service agreements between Immigration and Customs Enforcement and the Smith County Sheriff’s Office and the police department in Bullard, a town of about 5,300 people in East Texas. The agreements allow either party to terminate with 60 days’ notice, and none indicates that the federal government can reclaim funding if an agreement ends. Pantego could also leave the program without penalty and retain equipment purchased with federal money if it made at least one immigration arrest, Rife told council members. Between the end of April and mid-June, Pantego made two arrests.

Local law-enforcement agencies working with Immigration and Customs Enforcement can choose among three agreement models, two of which focus on identifying people already in jail who may be subject to deportation. Trump revived the third, known as the task-force model, at the beginning of his second term. Civil and immigrants’ rights groups have raised concerns about the model’s track record. Immigration and Customs Enforcement began phasing task-force agreements out in 2012 after Department of Justice investigations revealed that sheriff’s offices participating in the model had committed civil-rights violations. The Department of Homeland Security later stopped renewing task-force agreements during the Obama administration after federal investigations involving sheriff’s offices in Arizona and North Carolina.

Since Trump’s return to office, the task-force model had become the most common of the three agreement types. At least 241 task-force agreements were signed in Texas in 2026 alone, compared with 30 combined for the other models, according to Immigration and Customs Enforcement participation data from September. Smith County was among the first Texas agencies to sign after the model was reinstated in early 2025.

Records obtained by KXAN showed that Smith County in East Texas received more federal 287(g) funding than any of the 93 Texas law-enforcement agencies whose funding the station reviewed. The sheriff’s office received nearly $2.2 million from January 2025 through June 2026, equal to about 12% of its fiscal year 2025 budget.

About 120 of 198 licensed officers in the Smith County Sheriff’s Office participated in the program. Together, they conducted 124 immigration-enforcement actions between October 2025 and July. KXAN requested a list of 287(g) stops from the department and any associated charge. Smith County listed no associated criminal charge for more than 100 of the stops. Sheriff Smith told the Commissioners Court last November that although vehicles purchased with the money would be used for the 287(g) program, “that doesn’t mean, if we’re out here, we won’t run to another call or take another call that comes across the radio. We will do that. We try not to separate 287(g) from our other law enforcement duties. We do it all together.”

One afternoon in late June, Jose Rodriguez and his 23-year-old son, Pablo Rodriguez, drove along Loop 323, a circular segment of highway in Tyler, a city of 113,000 people in East Texas. The pair had spent hours working in the summer heat at a construction job and were headed to Chick-fil-A to pick up lunch for their family. Then red and blue lights appeared behind them. A law-enforcement agent said he pulled their black extended-cab work truck over because the attached trailer lacked a license plate and the pickup lacked a dual-wheel mud flap.

Body- and vehicle-camera footage showed the detective requesting that Jose Rodriguez get out of the truck. “Any English at all?” the detective later asked. Jose Rodriguez raised his right hand, holding his thumb and index finger close together. “Un poquito?” the detective asked, followed by, “Are you a U.S. citizen?” Rodriguez shook his head and answered, “No.” Seconds later, the detective pulled Rodriguez’s hands behind his back and put him in handcuffs.

Rodriguez was eventually taken from the Smith County jail to an Immigration and Customs Enforcement detention facility about two hours west of Tyler. Pablo Rodriguez, a U.S. citizen, said his father was detained for about three months before being deported to Mexico. KXAN could find no criminal convictions or arrests on Rodriguez’s record when the Smith County detective took him into custody in June. “Just like that, he’s gone,” Pablo later told KXAN. “My dad’s gone.” Pablo said law-enforcement officers “portray us as criminals, rapists, violent people. We’re not that. We’re just regular people trying to find a better life. The only difference is our skin. That’s pretty much it.”

November’s elections left the 287(g) program’s future uncertain, at least in Texas. While the Trump administration had reached agreements with local law enforcement to work with immigration officials, Congress has broader oversight authority, including decisions about funding. In January, Democrats will take over both chambers of Congress, and many incoming members, including freshman Texas Sen. James Talarico, have criticized Immigration and Customs Enforcement tactics. It was unclear how much of their focus would be on immigration enforcement and specifically on the 287(g) program.

At the state level, Republican Sen. Charles Schwertner retained his seat after authoring a 2025 measure that requires most Texas sheriff’s offices to participate in the program. He wants to extend the mandate during the coming legislative session to other law-enforcement agencies, including police departments. The Texas Public Policy Foundation also supports the expansion. Support for such legislation may be harder to get after November’s midterm election. Republicans maintained their majority in the state Senate, but Democrats took control of the governor’s office and state House for the first time in a generation. Any new bill would require approval from both chambers and the governor, or enough legislative support to overcome a veto.

In a press release well before the election, Democrat Gina Hinojosa called on Gov. Greg Abbott to “demand accountability from the Trump administration and stop enabling ICE operations that are terrorizing communities and violating the law.” Her release did not mention the 287(g) program by name. Hinojosa defeated Abbott and will take office as governor in January; she did not respond to a request for comment.

The state program could also be affected by November’s election of Democrats Nathan Johnson, who won the race for state attorney general, and Sarah Eckhardt, who won her bid to become state comptroller. Incoming Attorney General Nathan Johnson opposed the mandate as a state senator and will inherit responsibility for enforcing a law that requires most sheriffs to seek Immigration and Customs Enforcement agreements by Dec. 1. Johnson said he would use his discretion when deciding whether to sue sheriffs who fail to comply if they have “legitimate law enforcement reasons for not having a 287(g) agreement.” He also questioned the attorney general’s continued participation; Ken Paxton signed a task-force agreement in February 2025. Johnson said, “The attorney general’s office has tremendous, vast responsibilities,” and that “Never included among them since the creation of this state was outsourcing your employees to a federal agency for a purpose in which we’re not trained or qualified and which distracts me from the mission of my office.” He did not entirely rule out working with Immigration and Customs Enforcement in some capacity if his office could investigate crimes committed by ICE agents in Texas.

Incoming Comptroller Sarah Eckhardt will oversee a $20 million state grant program that state lawmakers created two years ago to help sheriff’s offices cover participation costs not reimbursed by the federal government. By late September, the comptroller’s office had awarded more than $6.5 million to 78 sheriff’s offices, according to figures obtained by KXAN. Eckhardt warned during her campaign that program expenses could eventually fall on local taxpayers, calling the arrangement “unsustainable.” After winning her seat, she said she would “do a 287(g) audit to show how that expense is driving up property taxes.”

Even if state leaders do not expand the participation requirement or remove the current mandate, departments can choose to keep collecting federal money to enforce immigration law. In Fayette County southeast of Austin, Sheriff’s Lt. David Beyer told KXAN that the 287(g) funding his office had received totaled about $800,000 and helps purchase equipment that can also be used for ordinary police work. “If they want to pay us extra to do that job,” Beyer said, “we’re more than happily going to accept those funds, because that saves our taxpayers money.”
