## Rubin chips generated $20 billion in revenue during their first quarter of production shipments

Nvidia said its backlog of sales to cloud-computing companies alone reached $2.5 trillion, up from $2 trillion three months earlier. During Tuesday’s analyst call, Colette Kress said TSMC’s leaders were in Nvidia’s offices that week and “fully engaged in helping us.” She also said Rubin-based chips generated $20 billion in revenue during their first quarter of production shipments. UBS analyst Timothy Arcuri projected that the world’s biggest operators of computing infrastructure will together spend about $1.76 trillion next year, much of which will go to Nvidia.

Kress raised Nvidia’s revenue-growth forecast for the next fiscal year to 75% from 70% during her first appearance as interim chief executive. Former CEO and co-founder Jensen Huang was gone. The SEC is investigating whether Nvidia misled investors, and questions swirled about the company’s business with cloud-computing operations in Asia that work with China. Analysts expect Nvidia’s growth to be 33% a year later and 18% the year after that. The Wall Street Journal said those comparisons partly reflect the difficulty of maintaining high growth rates from Nvidia’s much larger base.

The Wall Street Journal said Kress’s reset would have been harder earlier in the AI boom, when Nvidia was an upstart chip player vying for wafer allocation. Some proponents of Huang’s return to Nvidia, including Elon Musk, cited his prowess at striking deals with suppliers. Huang’s deep relationships with companies in the chip and computer-equipment supply chain were instrumental in getting the supplies Nvidia needed to meet a burst of AI demand.

In a 2007 discussion with Morris Chang, founder of Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Co., or TSMC, Huang described a long and close relationship between the companies. One time, Huang said, Chang dropped in for a visit on short notice and asked how many silicon wafers Huang needed. Chang wrote the number down in a black book, which determined how much supply Nvidia got.

The Wall Street Journal said Nvidia was no longer an upstart chip player vying for a wafer allocation. It described the company as a behemoth that controls the lion’s share of the AI-chip market and has major sway over its supply chain. On Tuesday’s analyst call, Kress said TSMC’s leaders were in Nvidia’s offices that week, “fully engaged in helping us.”

Nvidia had accelerated its chip advances to a one-year cycle a couple of years ago and was shipping large amounts of Blackwell-based chips while ramping shipments of Rubin-based chips. The company’s recent results also brought it gross profit margins above 70%, which The Wall Street Journal said could keep Nvidia collecting customers’ cash for some time to come.

Nvidia also reported quarterly revenue and profit Tuesday that were ahead of Wall Street’s expectations. Its revenue outlook for the current quarter came in higher than analysts projected, lifting the stock about 4% in after-hours trading.

The Wall Street Journal wrote that Kress carefully telegraphed that Nvidia might be more generous in returning profit to shareholders and somewhat more prudent about managing financial exposures to the AI boom’s riskier players. The Wall Street Journal characterized both developments as positive for investors. Under Huang, Nvidia entered into many credit-support and guarantee deals to stimulate demand for AI chips among companies that otherwise could not afford them.

Kress said Nvidia would continue those arrangements but would handle them more selectively. “As we advance this work, we remain thoughtful in how we participate and disciplined in how we commit capital,” she said, adding that the company would be “rigorously evaluating future investments, commitments and guarantees.”

Kress described returning cash to shareholders as a “super top priority.” Beyond shareholder returns, Nvidia would help critical suppliers that needed prepayments or other financial assistance, Kress said.

The Wall Street Journal identified China as a major challenge for Kress. It reported that Huang resigned after admitting an affair with an executive at Singapore-based data-center company Megaspeed and that the U.S. believed the executive was funneling intelligence to China.

The U.S. restricts sales of advanced AI chips to China, but Chinese companies have found workarounds including smuggling chips into the country and leasing access to them in other countries. On Monday, the Commerce Department placed an export-licensing requirement on sales of equipment with Nvidia’s chips to countries including Singapore, Malaysia and Indonesia in an effort to address that practice. The Wall Street Journal said the measure could, at least in theory, hurt Nvidia’s future sales.

On Tuesday, Kress suggested that demand for Nvidia’s chips was so overwhelming that such restrictions wouldn’t have much of an impact. “With demand far exceeding supply, we expect our performance to remain strong,” she said.

The Wall Street Journal said Huang’s departure would reverberate for some time, with shareholder lawsuits, congressional inquiries and board-level scrutiny among the consequences. Some of that scrutiny could require Kress’s involvement given her long tenure as CFO. The Wall Street Journal described those issues as potentially serious problems for Kress but said Nvidia’s positive momentum should help Kress navigate the transition.
