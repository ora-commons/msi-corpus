## Administration official says U.S. has found no evidence of Iranian involvement

The United States is investigating Iran’s potential involvement in dozens of cyberattacks against energy tankers, with as many as 50 vessels believed to have been attacked, according to U.S. officials and others familiar with the matter. Iran is the primary suspect in the attacks, while some officials also see Russia as a possible culprit. The targeted ships carry oil, liquefied petroleum, chemical products and other explosive substances, and the attacks are raising concern that Iran is widening the conflict with cyber operations and terror plots against Americans far from the Middle East.

Some of the targeted vessels also received warnings and bomb threats indicating that the ships could be blown up, some of the people said. Ships run on integrated systems that, if compromised, could result in an explosion, a collision or an oil spill. The attacks and the accompanying threats are raising concern that Iran is seeking to sow fear among shippers, disrupt critical maritime routes or cause an explosion at a major port far from the Middle East.

Early reports of some attacks surfaced on Iranian and Russian websites and Telegram channels. Some posts linked the attacks to the U.S.-Iran conflict and cast the United States as an unreliable energy partner. In other cases, ships were not aware that they had been attacked, officials said.

The U.S. Coast Guard deployed cyber protection teams to at least four of the targeted ships as the vessels approached the United States in recent weeks, according to officials. The teams worked to mitigate threats before the ships continued on to American ports. Other targeted ships are operating near Europe and Asia, according to people familiar with the matter.

When asked to comment, a Trump administration official said the U.S. hasn’t found any evidence indicating Iranian involvement. The governments of Iran and Russia didn’t immediately respond to requests for comment.

The Wall Street Journal reported in September on two of the cyberattacks, involving the VL Prosperity and the Kohaku as the ships were heading to Texas. Those vessels were among the ships that received bomb threats, according to a person familiar with the matter, and U.S. Coast Guard cyber teams responded and boarded them to address cybersecurity issues.

A ransom note addressed to the captain of the VL Prosperity and published on a public Russian-language Telegram channel used by shippers described the attack as a “planned execution.” The letter accused the ship of “illegally” carrying Saudi oil to American ports and of being “complicit in the organized crimes” of the United States and Israel. The Wall Street Journal could not determine the letter’s origins or whether it came from the attackers. The VL Prosperity’s manager confirmed that U.S. authorities conducted a cybersecurity screening aboard the ship and said the vessel was cleared for normal operations; the manager did not respond to questions about the ransom note.

Another ship that received a bomb threat, the Vivit Africa, was carrying liquefied natural gas from the United States to Italy when it was attacked, according to ship-tracking data and a person familiar with the matter. News of the breach was pushed on Russian channels, and the vessel exhibited irregular sailing patterns as it approached the Italian coast, ship-tracking data shows. Managers of the Vivit Africa and the Kohaku did not respond to requests for comment.

A European shipowner said two officers aboard one of the shipowner’s vessels received threatening messages on their cellphones last month while delivering natural gas from Louisiana to two Mediterranean ports. The messages said “traitor ships will burn in hell,” “there is no ceasefire” and “we will hunt you down all over the world.” In other cases, ships received threatening messages in Farsi, the language spoken in Iran, warning them not to load American products, according to one person familiar with the matter.

The attackers are exploiting a vulnerability in Fortinet routers that are popular in the maritime industry, according to U.S. officials and other people familiar with the matter. In September, after the first known cyberattacks, the U.S. Coast Guard Cyber Command and the Federal Bureau of Investigation issued an alert saying they had observed a trend of nation-state actors exploiting vulnerabilities in Fortinet FortiGate devices used by shipping companies and other maritime facilities. Fortinet did not respond to requests for comment. The FBI declined to comment.

The Coast Guard declined to discuss the specific attacks but said it is encouraging all shipping companies to report suspected issues and use its free resources to protect their systems. “The Marine Transportation System is vital to America’s economic prosperity and national security, and safeguarding it against potential cyber risks is a standard part of the United States Coast Guard’s safety and security mission,” the Coast Guard said in a statement.

“What we’re seeing in 2026 is that ships are no longer collateral damage in cyber campaigns; they’re deliberate targets,” said Alon Ayalon, co-founder of Cydome, an Israeli-based firm that works to protect vessels against cyberattacks. “The uncomfortable truth is that most vessels sailing today have less cyber protection than a midsize office,” Ayalon said.

Lars Jensen, a shipping analyst and chief executive of Copenhagen-based Vespucci Maritime, which advises leading shipping companies on strategy and maritime risks, said the notion that a ship could be hacked and driven into a bridge or detonated at a port is an unrealistic “Hollywood scenario” because vessels have manual overrides. Cyber attackers can still temporarily disable parts of a ship’s systems and issue threats or demands for ransom, Jensen said. A compromised vessel is also likely to put its journey on hold to address its systems, he said, causing major delays and million-dollar disruptions in the global supply chain.

The cyberattacks come as Iran continues to mount physical attacks against ships in the Strait of Hormuz, and as officials warn that Iran has expanded its threats against American citizens at home and abroad. Director of National Intelligence Jay Clayton told a House committee recently that Iranian threats had “substantially increased.”
