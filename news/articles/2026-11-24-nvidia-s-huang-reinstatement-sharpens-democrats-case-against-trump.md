## Export-control scrutiny anchors the Democratic response to Huang's return

Eleven days after Nvidia ousted CEO Jensen Huang, Democratic contenders were already turning President Donald Trump's successful intervention to bring him back into a national security and corruption issue in the 2028 race for the White House.

Democratic officials with presidential ambitions are treating Nvidia's decision to restore Huang as chief executive as evidence that a president's access to corporate power can override national security rules. The episode has crystallized their concerns about the growing power of artificial intelligence companies and the blending of government and business interests, even as the party has no unified vision for how to handle the technology's proliferation.

Trump said on CNBC that he had convinced the board: "I spoke to them, let me just put it that way. And I convinced them." He also said, "I'm doing it for the country," arguing that Huang's leadership at Nvidia was necessary. Trump said he had been concerned that Huang's ouster was tanking the stock market and risked putting the United States behind Beijing in the race for tech dominance.

Nvidia's own account of the decision pointed the same way. The company said the decision was based on input from "senior levels of the United States government." The Associated Press reported that Huang had lost his job because of extramarital affairs, including one with a Chinese businesswoman suspected of working as a spy for Beijing.

The company's share price dropped sharply during Huang's brief absence, dragging down the broader stock market to one of its worst weeks since the 2008 financial crisis before recovering with Monday's announcement of his reinstatement. Nvidia is under scrutiny over whether it is complying with U.S. restrictions on the export of its most advanced semiconductors to China and their use by Chinese tech firms.

Sen. Ruben Gallego, an Arizona Democrat considering a White House campaign, said Nvidia's decision sent a message "that the national security of this country is not as important as your relationship with President Trump." He said Democrats also had to figure out how corporate America does not take a bad lesson from the episode and come to think "that somehow it's OK for them to evade national security for the purposes of just selling more."

Rep. Ro Khanna of California, writing on social media that "rules should apply to everyone," wrote that "the rich & powerful, like Jensen, do not get to evade export controls or accountability just because it props up the stock price." The comments came as attention turned to Trump's financial stake in the company, which his latest financial disclosure report says was worth at least $12.5 million at the end of 2025, and to the millions of dollars Huang contributed to Trump's ballroom project.

Pennsylvania Gov. Josh Shapiro tied Huang's reinstatement to "the sheer corruption and weakness" of the president, calling it "another example of the dangerous combination governing this country: a rigged system that protects those at the very top no matter what, and a corrupt White House that cannot be trusted to put the economic well-being and safety of American families first."

Sen. Elissa Slotkin of Michigan, a former CIA analyst, said Trump's pressure campaign made clear that, to the president, "making money is more important than America's national security or the rule of law." She added: "If ever there was an issue that deserved national security oversight, it's this one."

Illinois Gov. JB Pritzker compared Huang's return to Democrats' broad wins in this month's midterm elections, calling it an example of "the greed and lack of accountability that the country just rejected." He urged Democrats "to speak with one voice and not be afraid to fight the Trump administration," saying Trump and Vice President JD Vance "will move mountains for tech billionaires."

Sacha Haworth, founder and CEO of The Tech Oversight Project and a former aide to Democrats, said big tech leaders can be a powerful foil. "They are the most unlikeable men in America, and yet it seems like the system is working very well for them," Haworth said. Haworth said the mission for Democrats now is to "continue to prove to voters that they are fighting against this corrupt system, against the influence of tech CEOs and on behalf of Americans."

Many ambitious Democrats, still buoyed by their sweeping midterm victories, are searching for the right message on artificial intelligence, which carries both promise and peril as it becomes increasingly intertwined with the economy, infrastructure and daily life. For now, the episode gives them much the same description of Huang's return: a threat to national security, evidence of presidential corruption and a test of whether export controls can withstand political pressure.
