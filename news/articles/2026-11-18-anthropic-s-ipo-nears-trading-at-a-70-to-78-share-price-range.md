## Founders' special stock class to control 50.1% of voting power

Anthropic's shares are set to begin trading for the first time later this week at $70 to $78 apiece, a range that would likely value the company behind the Claude AI chatbot at more than $1 trillion. The IPO will reverberate far beyond the people who understand what Anthropic's business structure as a “public benefit corporation” means, and it will likely affect even people who make no changes to their 401(k) accounts. The debut is a test for the artificial-intelligence industry and the U.S. stock market, and it arrives with an unusual governance structure: a long-term benefit trust can elect or remove a majority of Anthropic's board, and a special stock class for the company's founders would control 50.1% of the voting power.

The IPO would instantly make Anthropic one of Wall Street's biggest stocks. A valuation above $1 trillion would place Anthropic in the same league as Walmart, which has more than 10,900 stores around the world, and JPMorgan Chase, whose roots go back to 1799. It would also make the company worth more than Coca-Cola, IBM, Starbucks, Delta Air Lines and General Motors combined.

At that size, Anthropic could quickly join indexes that track wide swaths of the stock market, and those indexes could in turn bring in more investment. The company could join the Nasdaq 100 within just a few weeks. The index tracks the 100 largest nonfinancial companies trading on the Nasdaq and is a popular way for investors to get a quick read on how tech stocks are doing generally. It is so popular that an exchange-traded fund mimicking it, Invesco's QQQ ETF, has roughly $457 billion invested in it. If Anthropic joins, QQQ and other funds tracking the Nasdaq 100 would automatically buy Anthropic stock. Investors who make no changes to their 401(k) accounts could still end up holding the company. Joining the more widely followed S&P 500 would take longer because a stock must trade for at least 12 months, among other criteria.

Anthropic is structured as a “public benefit corporation.” Shareholders would have less control of the company than at a typical company. The goal of most companies, above all, is to do well for their owners. Usually, that means making profits and growing, both in the short and long terms. Anthropic says it will consider stockholders too, but it will balance their interests against another fundamental goal: “the responsible development and maintenance of advanced AI for the long-term benefit of humanity.”

Anthropic has set up a long-term benefit trust that will have the ability to elect or remove a majority of the company's board of directors. The trust will not run Anthropic's day-to-day operations, but its influence means the company could opt for strategies that may not make the most money for investors in the long term.

Founders would hold more influence still. As part of the IPO, Anthropic is creating a special class of stock for CEO Dario Amodei, President Daniela Amodei and five other co-founders. That class will control 50.1% of the voting power of all the company's outstanding shares, meaning the founders as a group could overshadow every other shareholder and single-handedly dictate issues put before all shareholders, such as how to vote on a potential sale of the company.

Anthropic expects up to 20% of its Class A shares in the IPO to go to “retail investors” — people buying through a brokerage rather than big institutions receiving shares through connections with Wall Street underwriters. Most IPOs offer only 5% to 10% of the total offering to retail investors, and Anthropic expects to reach them through Charles Schwab, Fidelity and others.

The financial picture behind the offering is complicated. Anthropic reported a net loss of $104 billion for the first nine months of this year, compared with the nearly $53 billion in profit JPMorgan Chase made over the same period. Most of that loss did not reflect cash running out the door; instead, it involved losses accountants said the company had to record because of the shifting value of some of its debt that can be converted into shares.

On an operating basis, Anthropic reported a loss of $1.26 billion for the first nine months of this year on $33.24 billion in revenue. That is an improvement from its operating loss of $5.22 billion on less than $2.55 billion in revenue for the first nine months of 2025.

The IPO comes at a fragile time for AI and for Wall Street generally. The U.S. stock market could use a boost. It has been shaky since the summer because of several worries hitting the AI industry, which has become the market's most influential. The worries have shifted through several stages. At first, there were longstanding concerns that prices for AI-related stocks had shot too high in the frenzy around the technology and inflated into a bubble. Then came worries that high interest rates would mean less borrowed money to fund the industry's growth. Then came this month's sweeping victories for Democrats in the U.S. midterm elections, which raised concerns about more regulation for AI. Most recently, Jensen Huang resigned as CEO of Nvidia, a company that has supplied chips to AI labs and helped fund the growth of the broader industry.

Those worries raised questions about whether Anthropic could delay its IPO to wait for a less turbulent time. Market watchers said such a move could also raise more doubts about AI stocks and drive the industry even lower.

How Anthropic's IPO performs could help clarify Wall Street's questions about whether the recent struggles for AI stocks are a temporary setback or the beginning of the end for a potential bubble.
