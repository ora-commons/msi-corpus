## Analysts say Anthropic can fund spending commitments for a year or more

The debt market expects credit-rating firms to rate Anthropic's bonds as junk, or below investment grade, The Wall Street Journal reported. Several large bond-fund managers said they expect Anthropic to land in the BB range, one or two notches below investment grade. Fitch Ratings and S&P Global Ratings have not made their views public; spokespeople for both agencies and an Anthropic spokeswoman declined to comment. The ratings firms could deliver a split decision, with some granting Anthropic an investment-grade rating, and investors might not get a final decision until after Anthropic decides whether to issue public debt for the first time.

Despite a $1.3 trillion market value in The Wall Street Journal's accompanying summary, Anthropic raised more than $100 billion last week and was worth around $1.18 trillion in the report, above Walmart or JPMorgan Chase. The AI company is unprofitable, less than six years old, and has committed about $900 billion in the coming years to pay for the computing power needed for its leading AI models. Wall Street is not confident it will meet Anthropic's long-term obligations, the report said. Anthropic's ANT stock-market debut was rocky, with shares down 8.50%, and the company lost much of its post-IPO bump last week in a sweeping selloff that also caught Nvidia, Meta Platforms and Oracle.

Craig Manchuck, a bond-fund manager at San Francisco-based Osterweis Capital Management, said, “Any Anthropic debt deal is going to be viewed with a suspicious eye,” and asked, “How do you gauge what the right price is for a company that has committed to spend $900 billion in the next couple of years?” One prominent credit investor said he worried that Anthropic's sheer scale could lead ratings firms to grant an investment-grade rating, potentially allowing its bonds into retirement funds meant to hold ultrasafe assets. “Obviously, at some point in the future, we’re hoping to be investment grade,” Anthropic Chief Executive Dario Amodei said in a CNBC interview on Thursday.

Anthropic reported $17 billion in revenue last quarter but still lost money because of its high spending on computing power, and it has no other business to give bond investors comfort. Analysts said Anthropic can use $96 billion in cash on hand, proceeds from its IPO and a concurrent private-share placement to fund spending commitments for a year or more. The company also started buying its own computing hardware, assets that could ease the way to an investment-grade rating.

Roughly half of Anthropic's spending commitments do not start until 2030. Without an investment-grade rating by then, Anthropic could struggle to pay its bills. “They have about three years of runway to get investors particularly comfortable with the credit story and their plan, so that the capital markets remain open for them,” RBC Global Asset Management bond-fund manager Neil Sun said. SpaceX landed an investment-grade rating thanks to its lucrative satellite business.

An investment-grade rating would give Anthropic access to the $8.6 trillion high-grade corporate bond market. A BB rating would make Anthropic by far the most valuable U.S. high-yield company. Hilton Worldwide currently holds that distinction, according to Dow Jones Market Data, with a market value of around $80 billion.

Fund managers said Anthropic is unlikely to issue many unsecured bonds because the junk-bond market is relatively small at $1.7 trillion and carries high borrowing costs. The fund managers said Anthropic is more likely to continue accessing capital through structured financing, chip financing and similar arrangements, “where there are ways to enhance their own credit quality,” said Brian Barnhurst, head of global credit research at PGIM. Those deals could become more difficult because Wall Street expects Nvidia to pull back on such guarantees following the unexpected exit of former CEO Jensen Huang, who championed the transactions.

The Wall Street Journal said AI has upended the market convention under which equity investors fund unproven technology start-ups while bond investors, who receive interest payments but no upside, steer clear. It said the cost of building digital infrastructure — $10 trillion by some measures — far exceeds what even the stock market can provide, forcing the industry to borrow. The newspaper said AI labs such as Anthropic remain unproven but could become dominant technology companies and outstrip established Silicon Valley companies if their growth continued, while bond investors would have no claim on the upside. The report noted that bond markets were skeptical of Tesla in 2018, just before the company and its share price took off.

An investment-grade rating for Anthropic would also relieve microchip manufacturers. Broadcom and Nvidia have guaranteed tens of billions of dollars in loans to buyers of their chips that are rented to AI labs such as Anthropic and OpenAI. Analysts have grown increasingly concerned about “circular” financing arrangements and the size of the loan guarantees, making chipmakers eager for the labs to borrow on their own.

The Wall Street Journal reported that other large-cap AI companies, including cloud-computing firm CoreWeave, also have junk ratings, highlighting a growing gap between how stock and bond investors view the industry. “Companies on the one hand have $40 billion, $60 billion market caps and are paying 16% to 18% for their debt,” Apollo Global Management Chief Executive Marc Rowan said in an interview with a Wall Street Journal podcast in October. “One of those things is wrong.”

Rising borrowing costs and tightening lending markets have been among the forces battering stocks in recent weeks, with trillions of dollars in market value wiped away. Bond managers are already struggling to absorb the tidal wave of bonds issued this year by large AI companies, or hyperscalers, many of which have performed poorly. Some of the $25 billion in SpaceX bonds issued shortly after its June IPO have since lost more than 10%. Meta and Oracle, which have investment-grade ratings, issued record amounts of bonds and guaranteed jumbo loans used to build data centers. Wall Street banks sold those deals to debt-fund managers at interest rates well above typical investment-grade yields, and the investments later lost value as the market struggled to absorb the supply. “I talked to a lot of my peers…and they don’t really have the appetite to buy into hyperscalers right now,” said Neil Sun, a bond-fund manager at RBC Global Asset Management.

With no public decision from Fitch Ratings or S&P Global Ratings, Anthropic's credit standing remains unsettled. Fund managers said Anthropic was more likely to continue using structured and chip-backed financing than issue many unsecured bonds.
