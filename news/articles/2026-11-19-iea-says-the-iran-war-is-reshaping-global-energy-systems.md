## Solar and nuclear set to gain as coal remains a crisis backup

The International Energy Agency said Thursday that the Iran war and growing climate concerns are reshaping how countries will power homes, cars and industries. The report described the disruption as the world's largest energy shock in history. Its findings came as the United Nations' annual climate talks, known as COP31, entered their second and final week in Turkey, where electrification and moving away from planet-warming fossil fuels were the two leading issues.

Fatih Birol, the IEA's executive director, said the report looks “past the current turmoil to explore the range of energy futures that could emerge from today’s upheaval.” He said the Iran war “delivered a huge shock to the system” and warned that “the longer the crisis lasts, the deeper the effects will be.” Birol also said, “I don’t see any credible pathway for the energy world to return to the way it was before.”

The report said energy-hungry nations will prioritize the security and affordability of their electricity. That approach supports renewable technologies such as solar and alternative technologies such as nuclear power as governments try to expand domestic energy sources. The IEA said renewables, led by solar, and nuclear power are the “biggest beneficiaries,” while fossil fuels still dominate national energy mixes. Energy-security concerns may also lead coal-rich nations to keep coal as a fallback during crises.

The report said coal-rich Asia went into energy triage when the Iran war cut off the Strait of Hormuz. Around one-fifth of the world's traded oil typically flows through the narrow passageway. In March, the IEA coordinated the emergency release of 400 million barrels of crude oil stocks to stabilize global markets.

This year's 560-plus-page World Energy Outlook analyzes government and private data and projects scenarios based on whether countries maintain current policies or make drastic changes in response to continuing crises. The Paris-based IEA, the world's leading intergovernmental energy organization, was founded after the 1973-74 energy crisis, publishes the report annually and has more than 30 member countries. The IEA said conflicts, trade tensions, climate risks and cyberattacks have increased the number of possible energy futures.

Experts said the world's energy sector would clearly have a before and after the Iran war. The IEA expects the Middle East to remain the world's largest oil exporter, but it said countries are likely to reduce their dependence on the Strait of Hormuz. The agency forecasts oil moving through the strait could fall from 20 million barrels per day before the Iran war to 8.5 million barrels in 2030 and under 2 million barrels by 2035. The IEA said the drive to diversify fuel supplies will reshape energy trade.

Mohamed Adow, director of Power Shift Africa, said, “This crisis has exposed the price of fossil fuel dependence.” He also said, “It’s crazy that a conflict thousands of miles away can send energy bills soaring at home.” Dave Jones, co-founder of the think tank Ember, said, “When we look at how energy crises change the world over the long term, governments are only just beginning to get their head around just how much things will change.”

COP31 is taking place after a year marked by the Iran war's energy shock and several climate-induced disasters. Before the negotiations, the United Nations said the world was dangerously close to missing its goal of limiting global warming to 1.5 degrees Celsius, or 2.7 degrees Fahrenheit, above pre-industrial temperatures. Rachel Cleetus, climate program director at the Union of Concerned Scientists, who is at COP31, said, “The future of the energy system and our climate are inextricably intertwined.”

Global warming is adding pressure to rising electricity demand. In what the IEA called a “striking milestone,” energy demand for cooling has overtaken demand for heating for the first time. India, the world's most populous nation, will account for more energy-demand growth over the next decade than any other country or region. China, already one of the world's most electrified economies, could see its electrification rise from 32% last year to 43% by 2035, the highest among major economies.

At COP31, Turkey is pressing countries to increase electricity's share of total global energy consumption from roughly 21% today to 35% by 2035. While the preference would be for the increase to come from renewable energy, United Nations documents on the negotiating table do not spell out that requirement. That means coal and other fossil fuels could be used, at least initially.

Cleetus said, “The IEA’s latest report makes clear that the fossil energy crisis unleashed by the US-Israel war on Iran has irrevocably changed the global energy system, adding to the many reasons why countries would benefit from a rapid transition to clean energy and electrification.”
