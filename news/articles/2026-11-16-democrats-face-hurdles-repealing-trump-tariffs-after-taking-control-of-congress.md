## Trump's veto and uncertain Republican defections block a two-thirds override

Democrats who took control of Congress after the Nov. 3 midterms cannot repeal President Donald Trump's tariffs on their own. The Associated Press reported that legislation repealing the import taxes or curbing his legal authority to impose them would run into the president's veto. It said it was unclear — and probably doubtful — whether enough Republicans would defy Trump and join the vote. Even if Democrats win every congressional race still undecided, they will not have enough votes on their own, leaving the party dependent on Republicans willing to break with Trump.

Trump's leverage runs through the constitutional structure of tariff-setting authority. The U.S. Constitution gives Congress the power to levy taxes, including tariffs, but lawmakers relinquished tariff-setting authority to the president over the years. Trump filled that opening, starting a trade war with China and taxing steel and aluminum imports on national-security grounds in his first term. Although the Supreme Court rejected his most ambitious tariffs in February, he has still managed to build a high protective wall around the U.S. economy, sometimes relying on the expansive legal authority Congress gave up.

The route the administration has used to impose tariffs has also drawn criticism for inconsistency. Last year, Trump suddenly imposed a 50% tariff on Brazil because its government had prosecuted his friend, former Brazilian president Jair Bolsonaro. The Supreme Court decision struck down that tariff, among others. Inu Manak, a senior fellow at the Peterson Institute for International Economics, said, "Trade policy has sort of gone off the rails and has lost a strategic focus." He said, "Now, we're just using tariffs as a blunt cudgel instead of addressing real challenges."

Tariffs became a central campaign issue for Democrats, who argued the levies raise prices for families already struggling with the cost of living. James Talarico, a Democrat running for a U.S. Senate seat in Texas, blamed Trump's tariff on Mexican tomatoes for pushing up the price of a popular appetizer: "Chips and salsa," he said, "are sacred in Texas." Targeting tariffs contributed to the Democrats' gains in the Nov. 3 midterms, and Talarico became the first Democrat elected to the Senate from Texas since 1988.

The AP Voter Poll, a survey of more than 100,000 midterm voters, found that voters who saw a negative impact from the tariffs overwhelmingly supported the Democratic candidate in their local U.S. House race. About two-thirds of U.S. voters said Trump's tariffs had hurt the national economy or the economy in their area, while about 2 in 10 said the tariffs had helped.

The scale of the levies has widened since Trump returned to the White House in January 2025. Yale University's Budget Lab reports the average U.S. tariff rose from around 2.5% then to 11.2% now. Tariffs are paid by importers in the United States, and companies often pass the burden to consumers through higher prices. Yale's Budget Lab estimates that tariffs cost the average U.S. household about $900 a year. Scott Lincicome at the Cato Institute's Center for Trade Policy Studies said, "Americans don't like tariffs." He said, "They connect them to the cost of living."

The strain comes as elevated inflation pinches family budgets, and Trump's war with Iran has driven up gasoline prices. At the same time, the tariffs are delivering significant revenue to the U.S. Treasury — nearly $370 billion in the last two budget years — at a time when the federal government is sitting on $40 trillion in debt. Sally Laing, former chief trade counsel for the Senate Finance Committee and now a partner at the law firm Akin Gump, said Democrats have "just been elected to come in and address concerns about affordability." She said, "It's not as easy as people may think."

Democrats also have not settled on what should replace the current policy. Some want to move back toward the low-tariff trade policies Trump dismantled, while others want the United States to continue using tariffs to fight other countries' unfair trade policies and to sanction U.S. adversaries such as China. Ryan Majerus, a former U.S. trade official during Trump's first term and in the Biden administration, said, "There's going to be a big debate over what should the Democrats' tariff and trade policy be." He said, "It can't just be: We don't like what Trump's doing."

The proposals Democrats made on the campaign trail are specific. One would repeal the tariffs Trump levied in July on 60 U.S. trading partners for allegedly failing to do enough to crack down on trade in products made by forced labor. Another would require Congress to approve his tariffs. Republicans who controlled Congress did nothing to reassert their tariff-setting authority and push back against the president's extensive use of tariffs.

Canada offers Democrats a possible point of alignment with Republicans. Trump imposed 50% tariffs in August on $20 billion worth of Canadian imports, damaging relations with America's neighbor and longstanding ally, drawing retaliatory tariffs from Ottawa and hurting U.S. companies that depend on trade with Canada. Oregon Sen. Ron Wyden, the top Democrat on the Senate Finance Committee who will be Senate Finance chair when Democrats take over, said, "That's what we're focused on, figuring out a way to beat Trump and his veto." Laing said, "The Canadian tariffs, these tariffs on our allies, are wildly unpopular."

The costs are visible in businesses that bought into the Canadian market. Gellert Global Group, a food importer based in Elizabeth, New Jersey, is contending with Trump tariffs on Canadian cheese. Co-CEO Thomas Gellert said, "We are paying millions of dollars in additional costs and tariffs to maintain our distribution and have our products on the shelf." He said, "We're absorbing that today. It's not sustainable for our business." The company, represented by the libertarian advocacy group Liberty Justice Center, has sued the Trump administration over the Canada tariffs.

The Canada tariffs contributed to longtime Republican Sen. Susan Collins' loss in Maine, even after Trump exempted products from the levies to aid her reelection bid. Wyden said Republicans face a choice: They can "help lower costs for American families or they can continue to defend Trump's tariffs and make clear whose side they're on." Majerus, now a partner at King & Spalding, said the midterms result makes "a veto-proof vote more possible," while adding, "Are Republicans willing to break with Trump? I'm still skeptical."

White House spokesman Matthew Middleton said Trump remains committed to the administration's agenda: "President Trump will never waver from putting Americans and America First, whether in trade negotiations or through an economic agenda of tax cuts and deregulation." The votes needed to change the tariff policy sit with Republicans, while the trade policy the new Democratic majority would substitute remains unsettled.
