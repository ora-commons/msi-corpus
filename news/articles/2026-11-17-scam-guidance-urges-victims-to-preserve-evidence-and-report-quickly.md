## Financial sextortion is growing, especially against teenage boys

The Associated Press guidance says scamming is a crime and victims are not to blame. It describes cyberscams as a profitable global criminal industry, with improving tactics and technology making the scams harder to spot. Polling cited in the guidance shows nearly every American adult says they have been targeted by scammers, and research says just about anyone can fall victim. Most victims never report the crime, and few manage to get money back.

Not every scam has every warning sign, but experts cited in the guidance say common tactics should make people wary. Those tactics include pressuring someone to act immediately, insisting they tell no one, and discussing money such as a debt, prize, job opportunity or investment.

The guidance also advises people to remain vigilant because phone and video calls can be faked. People should not engage with unknown calls, texts, messages or emails, nor should they send money or share personal information with someone they know only online.

To verify a contact, the advice is to call back using a phone number from an organization's official website or a number already known and trusted. People can verify a message by searching for the received image, text or email online because others may already have identified it as a scam. Before taking action, they should check with a trusted family member or friend.

For people who believe they have been scammed, the immediate steps are to stop communicating with the scammer, document the communications and download payment records. The information to collect includes social media accounts, cryptocurrency wallet addresses, bank account numbers and transaction details.

The guidance directs scam targets to report the scam to their bank, cryptocurrency exchange and any other company that facilitated it, including social media platforms, as well as to law enforcement. The FBI's hub for reporting cybercrime is IC3.gov.

Financial sextortion involves threatening to post intimate photos unless someone pays. Experts cited in the guidance describe it as a growing crime, especially against teenage boys. They advise targets not to pay a blackmailer, block the offender and stop responding. Targets should not delete images or messages because they can be important evidence. They should report the crime to law enforcement and social media platforms.

The National Center for Missing & Exploited Children, a U.S. nonprofit that works with law enforcement on child-exploitation cases, runs a CyberTipline and a hotline, 1-800-THE-LOST, for reporting. The Internet Crime Complaint Center is the FBI's main portal for reporting cybercrime, and the National Center for Missing & Exploited Children has compiled tips for reporting abuse on different social media platforms.

Project Arachnid and the National Center for Missing & Exploited Children can help get nonconsensual images of minors taken down. In the United States, companies are legally obligated to remove those images within 48 hours of being notified. If a company does not comply, the guidance says to tell the Federal Trade Commission.
