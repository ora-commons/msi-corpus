## Caucus seeks early voting and online voter registration

At Thursday's news conference, members of the Mississippi Legislative Black Caucus requested a public hearing on how credit-reporting data was used to maintain Mississippi voter rolls. They also asked legislative oversight committees to investigate that use, with lawmakers seeking legislation to ensure eligible voters are not mistakenly moved to inactive status.

Democratic state Rep. Kabir Karriem, who chairs the Mississippi Legislative Black Caucus, said members want to know how many voters were marked inactive, what guidance county officials received on using Experian data and why some provisional ballots cast by those who were marked as inactive were counted while others were not.

“Mississippians did their part. They showed up to vote,” Karriem said. “Now election officials owe them an answer to the most basic question in our democracy: Did my vote count?”

Caucus leaders pressed for early voting, the ability to register to vote online and a process allowing voters to fix problems with absentee ballots. They also sought repeal or amendment of laws allowing third-party data to determine voter eligibility. Caucus members said the investigation and legislative changes should be completed quickly, before next year's elections for statewide offices, the Legislature and the state Supreme Court.

Democratic state Rep. Zakiya Summers said, “We need urgency, we need accountability and above all we need to restore public trust in our elections.”

State Rep. Cheikh Taylor, the chair of the Mississippi Democratic Party, said he intends to introduce legislation in the new year that would increase transparency in how credit data is used to verify voter eligibility. His proposal would also give voters whose records are flagged an opportunity to confirm their identities before they are deactivated.

Taylor said he had not seen evidence that data from Interactive Data would be much different from Experian's data. He said he hoped to obtain support from some Republican colleagues. “This is not a partisan issue,” Taylor said. “The right to vote and the freedom to vote belongs to us all.” Taylor also said the Legislature should move quickly because Mississippi has elections next year.

Republican state Sen. Jeremy England said legislation increasing transparency in the use of credit data on voter lists could draw Republican support. “I don't think there was anything nefarious involved with it, but look, Mississippi also does have a dark past when it comes to elections,” England said. “It's something we definitely need to look at more and make sure that we're doing it in a better way, so that if someone's registered to vote, you know, they're not marked as inactive for a reason they shouldn't be.”

Associated Press reported that the number of Mississippi voters listed as inactive before this year's midterms had risen by nearly 60,000 since the 2024 presidential election. Associated Press data showed 185,000 Mississippi voters were listed as inactive during the 2022 midterm elections and 171,000 during the 2024 presidential election. According to the secretary of state, more than 230,000 people were marked inactive before this year's election after Experian data was included in the verification process.

Elizabeth Jonson, a spokesperson for the secretary of state's office, said the 230,000 figure was “accumulated over more than five years of maintenance by local officials.” She also said the state does not classify voters as inactive solely based on Experian data. Secretary of State Michael Watson said the increase was caused by integrating Experian data into the overall verification process.

For years, Mississippi primarily relied on U.S. Postal Service change-of-address data to maintain its voter rolls. In 2025, Republican Secretary of State Michael Watson announced a partnership with Experian, one of the major credit reporting agencies, intended to “strengthen the integrity” of the state's elections. Under the agreement, county election officials received access to credit and Social Security data to use alongside Postal Service data to verify voters' addresses.

Mississippi law requires election officials to move voters whose addresses are questioned during the vetting process to inactive status at the same time they mail a confirmation card, which voters can use to reestablish active status. Policies in Delaware, Maryland, Oklahoma and Rhode Island generally allow voters time to respond to the notice before their status is changed to inactive, according to the MIT Election Data and Science Lab.

The sequence meant Mississippi voters may not have known they had been switched to inactive until they showed up to vote, forcing them to cast a provisional ballot. In another video posted to social media, Watson told voters that any “mistakes” made in the voter-roll maintenance process could be corrected by casting a provisional ballot, called an affidavit ballot in Mississippi. County election commissioners review those ballots and approve or reject them.

In a social media video, Watson said the state had started using data from a new vendor, Interactive Data, which draws on information from all three major credit bureaus to maintain its voter rolls. “I don't apologize for cleaning voter rolls,” Watson said in an interview. “That is my job.”

Scott Anderson, a spokesperson for Experian, said the company had not provided services to Mississippi since early 2025. “Decisions regarding voter registration policies, procedures and record updates are made solely by election officials in accordance with applicable local, state and federal laws,” Anderson said.

Jonson said 21,484 provisional ballots were cast during this year's midterms, nearly identical to the 21,321 cast in 2024, a presidential election year with higher turnout. The secretary of state's office said 75% of provisional ballots were approved statewide for the November election.

Associated Press data showed that approval fell to 34% in Hinds County, which is majority Black and home to Jackson, the state capital; the rate was 60% in 2024. It is not clear whether the use of Experian data played a role in the decline.

The Associated Press described the election as historic for Democrats, who unseated Republican incumbent Cindy Hyde-Smith in the U.S. Senate race. Scott Colom's victory came by just over 8,000 votes with 99% of ballots counted, a fraction of the more than 230,000 people the secretary of state said were marked inactive. Democrats are focusing on the issue as they seek to build on Colom's win and maintain momentum before next year's race for governor.
