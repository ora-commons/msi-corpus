## New York Times reports U.S. Commerce Department investigating MegaSpeed

NPR's midterm analysis found that Democrats held a nearly 50-point lead among voters under 30. NPR called the result a major comeback from 2024, when young voters leaned toward Trump, and said it signaled that the youngest generation was issuing "a strong rebuke of Republicans." Gen Z accounted for 11% of the total 2026 vote. In the Texas Senate race, Democrat James Talarico won by 12 percentage points and by 60 points among Gen Z voters.

Separately, Nvidia's stock slipped around 4.25% after CEO Jensen Huang admitted to an extramarital affair with a woman U.S. intelligence suspects of spying for China. AMD, Nvidia's main AI chip competitor, saw its shares jump more than 13%. The New York Times reported that the U.S. Commerce Department was investigating MegaSpeed and that the woman was giving information to Beijing, but NPR said it had not independently verified those claims.

Americans ages 18-29 have historically voted at lower rates than older voters. NPR said young voters were "ready to fight for an American dream that feels just out of reach." Democrats credited their strong slate of candidates for boosting enthusiasm, while strong opposition to Trump also played a key role. The party gained support from some young voters who backed Trump two years ago, including white and Latino men; both groups voted blue by double digits. Democrats narrowed their margins among young rural voters and young voters without college degrees. Some gains may have been influenced by young Trump voters who chose not to participate, but NPR said it was unclear how many were among them.

## Nvidia stock reaction and unverified MegaSpeed investigation

The woman is an executive at MegaSpeed, a Singapore-based company that Nvidia has collaborated with. The controversy centers on whether MegaSpeed was helping Chinese firms bypass chip export controls. NPR's John Ruwitch said Nvidia's central role in the U.S.-China competition over AI could make Huang's statements and actions of high interest to Beijing. NPR described the reaction as extending from Wall Street to Capitol Hill.

## House Republicans weigh Scalise and Jordan for minority leader

House Republicans were scheduled for a closed-door meeting on Nov. 10 to choose a minority leader for the next Congress. The decision was between current House Majority Leader Steve Scalise, R-La., and Ohio's Jim Jordan, whom NPR described as "a co-founder of the far-right House Freedom Caucus."

## Trump weighs further Iran strikes as talks continue

President Trump has acknowledged that the war he initiated with Iran harmed his party in the midterms. Weeks before the election, he said he would hold off on strikes in Iran. While campaigning, he hinted at potential actions to proceed with the war and teased a significant move to end it. Trump now says there is no need for further strikes if talks are ongoing.

NPR's Deepa Shivaram said Trump might resume military action if he does not get the deal he wants, citing his pattern of shifting rhetoric since the war began. Trump has also said the war was necessary and that he would do it again. Shivaram said Trump was beginning to focus on shaping his legacy during the last two years of his presidency. She said he was trying to frame the war as a success by saying Iran's nuclear stock had been eliminated and that this was his goal, and wanted to establish himself as a dealmaker and peacemaker.

## Massie articles itemize allegations against Hegseth

Rep. Thomas Massie, R-KY, reintroduced eight articles of impeachment against Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth, accusing him of high crimes and misdemeanors. Hegseth has called the articles a sham. Three charges allege that Hegseth illegally waged war against Iran. One alleges that he ignored laws to minimize civilian casualties. Another alleges that he oversaw extrajudicial killings of alleged drug boats in the Caribbean and Eastern Pacific, and one alleges that he captured Venezuelan President Nicolás Maduro without congressional authorization.

NPR's Sam Gringlas said only a few Republicans need to support impeachment in the House for it to be possible. The House may vote this week, and a vote would lead to a Senate trial. Removing Hegseth from the Pentagon requires 67 senators, a threshold Gringlas said would be challenging even once Democrats take control. Massie told reporters he believes removal is possible. He said opposition to the war in fellow Republicans' home states could persuade them and that some members of Congress have come around to the idea that the administration must be held accountable for violating congressional war powers.

## Supreme Court permits west Texas border work as hearings continue

On Nov. 9, 2026, the U.S. Supreme Court permitted the Trump administration to proceed with plans to build hundreds of miles of border barriers and security infrastructure in west Texas. The decision came despite ongoing court hearings. In a five-page decision, the court asserted that the federal judge who issued an injunction against the construction lacked authority to intervene. The plan has faced bipartisan backlash over its involvement with Big Bend National Park, which NPR described as "a cherished area known for its stunning canyons and pristine desert landscape."

## Cumberland Island land-swap proposal could allow new construction and renew preservation dispute

Visitors, ecologists, park rangers and residents all cherish Cumberland Island, Ga., but often disagree about how best to protect it. Feral horses, hogs and deer roam freely across the 18-mile island. Since 1972, Congress has mandated the preservation of thousands of wilderness acres under National Park Service protection.

The island's human history includes Native American artifacts, Spanish missions and grand Gilded Age mansions from the Carnegie era. Descendants of industrialist Thomas Carnegie still reside on private property. A National Park Service land-swap proposal with the family could greenlight new construction. NPR said the resulting development could ignite a clash among stakeholders over what true island preservation actually looks like.
