## Investors pulled money out of US Bitcoin exchange-traded funds

On November 2, former Binance CEO Changpeng Zhao warned that crypto scammers were in “overdrive,” circulating screenshots of fake recompense schemes that promised $150 in free compensation for withdrawal suspensions after the $6.3 billion hack of Binance, the world's largest cryptocurrency exchange. Binance confirmed the information was false and said the screenshots were digitally altered. The Guardian reported that the hack also spooked traditional financial markets, with investors pulling funds out of Bitcoin exchange-traded funds in the US.

## Binance response and user protection

The warning came as Binance worked to contain the damage from a breach late last week that The Guardian described as the world's largest online theft. More than $2.5 billion of the $6.3 billion was taken in one transaction alone, after a Binance engineer's laptop was infiltrated, which in turn allowed attackers to drain wallets. Binance later added that there were “additional points of breach” in its systems. On X, the exchange said it would “cover all losses” and that “user funds are safe”. It also said it had frozen about half of the funds targeted in the hack, stopping the thieves from making off with that tranche of cryptocurrency.

The Guardian reported that the company faced fallout from “angry, scared users” as it worked to contain the damage. On Sunday afternoon, Binance announced it had processed $4.6 billion in withdrawals and $1 billion in deposits — a net outflow of about $3.6 billion, representing 2.1% of its $171 billion in total assets under management.

Most customers would have no legal protection for such a loss. Crypto users in the US, UK and Australia are not covered by formal deposit protections and would not be reimbursed by their governments if an exchange was hacked or went bankrupt. In Australia, users can claim a capital loss on their tax return if they can offer proof that a crypto asset was lost or stolen.

Yi He, Binance's co-founder and longtime partner of Zhao, said the company's top priority was to “ensure the environment is secure, after which users can withdraw their funds and Binance can resume operations.” Alongside a proof of its reserves, Binance tweeted: “Our commitment is unchanged: Binance will absorb the financial impact of the stolen funds, not our users.”

Attribution for the theft remained tentative. Experts cited by The Guardian said the breach was likely the work of North Korean hackers. The Guardian wrote: “The name Binance was already associated with crime before the hack.” Zhao pleaded guilty to violating US anti-money laundering laws in 2023 and stepped down as CEO. The Guardian added: “It’s no FTX, but the company faces an uphill battle to save its brand from $6.3bn worth of permanent damage.”

## Lula's WhatsApp mobilization in Brazil

The same TechScape issue turned to Brazilian president Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva's political mobilization on WhatsApp. Less than two weeks before the newsletter appeared, Lula was accusing Meta of suppressing his posts on Instagram and Facebook and attempting to derail his campaign. His opponent Flávio Bolsonaro, the son of the previous president, had all the momentum after the country's first round of elections, and The Guardian described Lula's supporters as feeling they “would be crushed under the rightwing wave sweeping over Latin America.” Meta denied bias but acknowledged technical failures in response to Lula's accusations.

The Guardian cautioned that the full truth of what Meta did or did not do for Lula may never be known. It said individual algorithmic moderation mistakes — usually a few egregious ones among millions of otherwise unobjectionable choices — seem like bias in times of heightened political tension, and that Meta says this is what happened in Brazil. The Guardian said that raging against those errors makes for engaging posts, which outsizes the glitches' significance. It called the history of accusations against Meta of political interference “arcane and complicated,” said the motives of everyone involved were suspect, and noted that independent studies conducted after the 2020 US election showed that rightwing content traveled further on social media than leftwing posts. The Guardian said that small data point indicates it may not have been “the heavy thumb of Meta on the political scales in Brazil.”

During the second-round runoff, Lula, 81, bought a smartphone for the first time and assembled WhatsApp groups of millions of supporters who mobilized in response to the threat of yet another Bolsonaro. WhatsApp is owned by Meta. At the helm of these channels was André Janones, the captain of “Lula’s army” and a federal deputy from the state of Minas Gerais. Janones issued daily social media challenges to the supporters he called his “soldiers.”

The successful campaigning via WhatsApp bore out a prediction that Meta CEO Mark Zuckerberg made in 2019: that the “digital living room” of private direct messaging and group chats would eclipse the “town square” style of public social media feeds. Lula's supporters gathered in channels full of friendly faces to participate together in on-the-ground canvassing. Janones was not posting to convert the general populace to his side; he was motivating the base with online communication to pursue IRL action. Members of “Lula’s army” explicitly instructed supporters to neither share nor respond to posts from the opposition, displaying an understanding that any engagement boosts algorithmic reach in public social media.

Lula won the Brazilian election by 5 million votes, securing an unprecedented fourth term. The Guardian said that, after Janones's innovative use of WhatsApp, Lula's accusations seemed like “politicking borne of desperation,” and noted that Lula plays into longstanding conflicts between Brazil and tech giants like Meta.
