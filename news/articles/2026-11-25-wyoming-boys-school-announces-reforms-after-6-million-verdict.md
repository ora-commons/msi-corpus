## Advocates say internal policy changes alone will not prevent further abuse

A jury in Casper, Wyoming, returned a unanimous $6 million verdict last week for Blaise Chivers-King, a former resident of the state-run Wyoming Boys’ School, finding that staff subjected him to punitive isolation in the facility’s 7-by-10-foot cinderblock detention rooms and denied him due process to challenge his confinement. The jury did not rule in his favor on his claim of excessive force. On Monday, the Wyoming Department of Family Services, which oversees the 38-acre residential facility near Worland, announced that “sweeping reforms” are underway at the school for delinquent boys ages 12 to 21.

The department said in a statement Monday that it “cannot comment on the recent jury verdict in the U.S. District Court for the District of Wyoming, as litigation is ongoing,” while noting that Chivers-King’s experiences at the boys’ school in 2021 “do not reflect current practices.” The statement said “Current WBS Superintendent Dale Weber instituted sweeping reforms that address the issues raised in the trial.” Those reforms include “eliminating detention rooms and detention status except under extreme circumstances, implementing nonviolent crisis intervention training, and prohibiting the use of the restraint chair,” according to the department. Weber, who was among the defendants in Chivers-King’s lawsuit, also ended the mandatory head-shaving policy and brought the detention facility’s practices into alignment with nationally recognized best practices, the statement said. The department did not immediately provide the specific best practices or the dates the changes took effect, and a spokesperson estimated it would take a week to provide more detailed information.

Juvenile justice reform advocates, including Laramie Democratic Rep. Karlee Provenza, said they hope the verdict spurs much-needed change and that internal policy changes will not by themselves prevent future abuse and mistreatment at the facility. They called for increased oversight and transparency. “I hope this sends a message that legislation needs to be enacted in Wyoming to address solitary confinement,” Allison Mahoney, one of Chivers-King’s attorneys, said after the jury delivered its verdicts.

Four years after Chivers-King was at the school in 2021, the details of what the then-teenager experienced came to light during an eight-day jury trial. Attorneys showed jurors images of the school’s detention facilities, where Chivers-King was held for 15 days at one point. The white cinderblock rooms were described as barren apart from a toilet and a mattress staff provided at night, and the rooms have no windows that look outside; staff at times covered the window in the door with a magnet. Chivers-King testified that his confinement felt arbitrary, describing confusion when staff kept him isolated even after he became calm, and said he was on occasion locked in a detention room for a predetermined amount of time. Images and video presented to the jury showed him self harming while in detention, including banging his head against the wall and, at multiple points, trying to strangle himself.

Ciara Boerman, one of Chivers-King’s attorneys, told jurors during closing arguments that “Even when he did what he was supposed to do, they kept him in there.” Boerman said “The vast majority of that time he spent with his own thoughts, thinking about how to end his life.”

Josh Rovner, a senior research analyst with the Sentencing Project who recently published a report on widespread abuse in juvenile detention facilities nationally, said there is a connection between the public’s lack of information about Wyoming’s juvenile justice system and the abuse Chivers-King experienced at the boys’ school. “We count things we care about in this country,” Rovner said. “We know how many hogs were slaughtered last quarter. We know how many bushels of corn were exported. We know how much a gallon of gasoline costs down to the nearest milli-cent, right?” If Wyoming cares about how it treats children in detention facilities, Rovner said, it should publish juvenile justice information with that same level of detail, and “There are plenty of states that are able to monitor, track, report, and publish what is happening with solitary confinement in their facilities.” The Department of Family Services has fulfilled multiple WyoFile records requests for data on the boys’ school’s use of detention rooms and restraints but does not automatically make the information public. The state makes public education data easily accessible so communities can understand how their public schools perform without jeopardizing student privacy, which Rovner called an apt comparison for what is possible with juvenile justice data. “Why would we give taxpayer-funded agencies the chance to just take their word for it, and to say, ‘Trust me,’” Rovner said. “Like, why on Earth would we do that?”

For decades, Wyoming has recorded the highest juvenile confinement rates in the country, and a report issued by the Sentencing Project found that locking children up does not reduce delinquent behavior and often creates a new set of problems by exacerbating the mental health challenges that led to criminal activity in the first place. In response, many states have been moving toward community-based models that focus on providing struggling kids with support and tools, and using incarceration only when necessary. State lawmakers set out in 2021 to study why Wyoming relies so heavily on locked facilities such as the Boys’ School and the Girls’ School, but hit a wall accessing information. A Wyoming Community Foundation report found the state cannot easily track why young people get in trouble, what local law enforcement does in response or the outcomes of efforts to address delinquent behavior. Because each of Wyoming’s 23 counties handles juvenile cases differently, a child in one community might land in a community-based program while somewhere else a judge might send a kid to a juvenile facility for similar behavior. The decentralized approach also makes it hard to track whether young people graduate from high school or reoffend as teens or adults. Those outcomes matter because they concern the well-being of children and the spending of public dollars. The Joint Judiciary Committee sponsored a bill in 2022 to strengthen information sharing about state-supervised youth, and the measure has yet to pass because many Freedom Caucus members opposed it on privacy grounds.

Laramie Democratic Rep. Karlee Provenza, who helped craft that 2022 bill, said she plans to bring it back during the legislative session that begins in January. She hopes the recent verdict will motivate her colleagues to take a closer look at juvenile justice. “My intention is to create the processes that allow for us to examine if what we are doing is working or not,” Provenza said. “Particularly as I start trying to push for more investment in the things that keep kids out of detention facilities.” She wants the Legislature to clearly define solitary confinement, “so that everybody has the same definition, and creating some strict guardrails around when it can be used, that basically keeps it from ever being used, except for very short stints that protect the safety of the child.” She also wants the state’s use of confinement reported publicly, first and foremost to parents of incarcerated children: “I think that’s an important component of this because parents are the best advocates for their children.” She said: “What happens in the boys’ school feels like a black box to, I think, parents, but also to the state.”

Provenza said she raised concerns about the climate at the boys’ school during a 2021 presentation by DFS Director Korin Schmidt and Superintendent Weber to the Joint Judiciary Committee, when she asked about allegations of abuse or mistreatment and was told that records on the number, nature or resolution of incidents were not made public. Schmidt explained to the committee that abuse allegations at the state-run facility are handled as Child Protective Services cases and are “confidential under the statute.” Child Protective Services is a component of the Department of Family Services, meaning the agency investigates the allegations itself, without external oversight or transparency. The Joint Judiciary Committee was in the midst of its off-season study of juvenile justice in 2021 when Chivers-King was at the boys’ school. Even if Chivers-King had complained at the time, state law barred the release of such records, which meant the allegations could not have become public in 2021.

Wyoming still faces five more lawsuits from former residents who allege civil rights abuses ranging from punitive use of the restraint chair to indifference to medical needs. The six former residents initially sued together, but the court separated them into different trials after a request from the state’s attorneys, who described the case as “deeply complex and full of fact-intensive claims involving multiple plaintiffs and defendants over a four-year time span.”

Gov. Mark Gordon declined to comment on what the state should do in light of the verdict, citing the ongoing litigation. Rep. Art Washut, chair of the House Judiciary Committee, did not respond to questions about whether he plans to take any action in response to the verdict against the Boys’ School.

Provenza said she would like the Legislature to take a stronger oversight role than it has in the past, pointing to the Joint Judiciary Committee’s 2021 study of juvenile justice, which has yet to result in any reforms. Provenza said some might be skeptical about lawmakers considering the issue again. “Like at some point, someone has to say ‘the buck stops with us,’ and so I think we have to keep trying. I also know … we’ve got a bunch of freshmen coming in, but we have to deal with this,” she said, referring to newly elected lawmakers. “I understand the sentiment, and part of me also has some fear that the legislative branch is not going to do the best job of overseeing what needs to happen,” Provenza said. “But I also know that we have to start somewhere, and this is our job. We fund these facilities. We write laws that impact them. We write the laws that send kids into these facilities, and so we have to take responsibility for the impact that they have and actually fix them.”
