## Labour divisions emerge over timing, red lines and a possible referendum

Andy Burnham is preparing to set out options for Britain's long-term relationship with the European Union, reopening a debate that had appeared to recede after the 2016 Brexit vote. BBC News reported that the government now expects the options to be presented around the time Burnham unveils his 10-year plan, shortly before Parliament's Christmas break. Burnham has said that “Hard Brexit we were given not good enough for Britain.”

The government's initial commitment came in Burnham's party-conference speech, when he tied the presentation of the options to the period around Friday's UK-EU summit. After confusion over the timing, the government said Burnham would set them out around his 10-year plan. Burnham's allies say the timing would connect EU policy with his domestic agenda, arguing that a different arrangement with Europe could help with the cost of living and re-industrialisation. Other people in Labour worry that the EU debate will crowd out other parts of the 10-year plan.

The renewed argument has brought figures from both sides of the Brexit divide back into public debate. A new group called Stay Out has been set up with backing from people involved in the official Leave campaign in 2016. Boris Johnson returned to the headlines, saying he still believes Britons would choose to remain outside the European Union. The pro-EU European Movement has also raised its profile, pointing to politicians, diplomats and academics who support its position, while a growing number of Labour figures are calling for a change in Britain's membership.

Former Deputy Prime Minister David Lammy urged Labour to go into the next election backing renegotiated EU membership and then put the question to voters in another referendum. Lammy was joined by former ministers Rachel Reeves, Peter Kyle and Jo Stevens, along with other people who served in Sir Keir Starmer's cabinet and privately support revisiting EU membership. These figures backed Remain in 2016 but later served in a government elected on a manifesto saying it was important to make Brexit work. BBC News reported that some now believe the political picture has changed.

Burnham has opened the door to single-market membership, rejoining the customs union and holding another referendum on full EU membership. Labour's 2024 manifesto continues to rule out rejoining the single market, the customs union or the EU until the next general election, and Burnham returned to those red lines when questioned at Prime Minister's Questions.

Opening negotiations now would not necessarily breach the manifesto commitments if a new arrangement took effect after the next general election, BBC News reported. The government has not decided its final position, and there is no clear timetable for the process that would follow Burnham's presentation of the options.

Burnham's approach has been to discuss possible positions before they are settled. Some people close to the prime minister are also considering whether the prominence of the EU debate should be reduced in the new year so that other government priorities can receive more attention.

Labour MPs are divided over the renewed debate. Some who spoke to BBC News worry that reopening the issue will be “toxic.” Others fear Burnham could disappoint pro-EU voters if he does not go far enough, while still others question whether the arguments have changed enough for voters to make a different choice. One MP said that “Both sides need fresh arguments, not the ones we all got tired of last time.”

Another MP said the case would be “dead on arrival” if it appeared to be driven by politicians trying to rejoin because they had never got over leaving. That MP argued the position would be different if businesses, farmers and other parts of society pressed for renewed membership because of the harm they attributed to Brexit.

The potential destination is also unsettled. Some Labour MPs believe the EU is changing and could have more right-wing leaders at the top, prompting them to question whether they would want to belong to such a union. A long-standing diplomat from an EU member state said political change within the EU, the offer of associate membership to Canada and the possibility of a future arrangement with Ukraine after a peace deal could make Brussels more open to looser forms of membership than it has previously considered.

A renewed government-backed vote on EU membership could also strengthen the case for another Scottish independence referendum, according to some Scottish MPs. Other Labour members welcomed the return of the debate, with one saying that even discussing the issue without a settled destination represented a significant change.

Burnham is due to sign a new UK-EU deal on Friday. Ministers believe the agreement will drive economic growth and reduce red tape for British businesses, but the government has not settled how far the wider reset should go. The unresolved questions are when negotiations might begin, whether Labour's red lines could change and whether another referendum would follow.
