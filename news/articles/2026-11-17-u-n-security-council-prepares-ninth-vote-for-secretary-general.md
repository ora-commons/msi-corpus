## Grynspan leads secret tallies as permanent members maintain opposition

The U.N. Security Council will hold its ninth vote Thursday to choose a successor to Secretary-General António Guterres, who retires Dec. 31 after a decade in the post. Eight candidates remain in the race, and The Wall Street Journal identified Costa Rica’s Rebeca Grynspan Mayufis and the U.A.E.’s Lana Nusseibeh as the two veteran diplomats drawing the most attention. Grynspan says she would reform the U.N. from within, while Nusseibeh describes herself as a transformational leader seeking a “geopolitical secretary-general.”

The Wall Street Journal said the vote comes as some of the most destructive conflicts since World War II ravage the globe, with the U.N. confronting whether a new leader can make it relevant again.

Secret voting has favored Grynspan. She has topped nearly every tally since July and narrowly expanded her advantage, according to the Journal. Her lead has not removed opposition from veto-wielding permanent members, which the report said was believed to include the U.S. Argentina’s Rafael Grossi, the chief of the International Atomic Energy Agency, has polled at No. 2, but his candidacy is believed to face stiff opposition from Russia and the U.K.

Grynspan, 70, has held prominent government positions at home and internationally for the U.N. She said the organization must revive its core mission of peace making. “We are absent on all the scenarios of conflict in the world, and people see that,” she said. “This is an organization that has become very risk averse.”

Nusseibeh, who served as U.A.E. ambassador to the U.N. and later as minister of state of foreign affairs, placed fourth in the last poll despite her prominent profile. The Journal described Thursday as a potential make-or-break moment for her. She said the election represents a turning point for the international system. “This particular race for the secretary-general has gathered so much interest because people see it as a turning-point moment for the international system,” she said in an interview. “What we require is a radical change in culture, in mindset, in approach, and in how we measure delivery.”

The Wall Street Journal reported that Nusseibeh faces intense opposition from some corners of the U.N., particularly because of the U.A.E.’s involvement in the war in Sudan. The report said she is believed to enjoy backing from the U.S. and Russia, while China is on record expressing support for a Latin American secretary-general. Nine days after Nusseibeh entered the race in October, Turkey, which favors the opposing side in Sudan’s war, nominated former foreign minister Feridun Hadi Sinirlioğlu, seemingly to undermine the Emirati candidate.

Asked about Sudan, Nusseibeh said she would be an independent secretary-general if elected, with allegiance to the U.N., its charter and international law. “Dealing with Sudan,” she said, “is going to be the litmus test of success for any incoming secretary-general.”

An election for either woman would be historic because the U.N. has never been led by a woman. Grynspan would be its first leader of Jewish origin. Nusseibeh, the youngest candidate at 48, would be its first Muslim and first person of Palestinian descent to hold the position.

Either woman would contrast with Guterres, a former Portuguese prime minister whom the Journal described as a product of an era of expanding globalization. The report said big powers undermined his leadership: Russia and the U.S. started wars, and two Trump administrations slashed the U.N.’s budget. Candidates in the race pledge to assert the organization’s role more aggressively in major issues involving those powers, though they have no illusions that the lost funding will return.

To be recommended to the full U.N. membership, a candidate needs at least nine “encourage” votes from the Security Council and no votes in the “discourage” category from the five permanent members with veto power. There is no clarity about who would run the U.N. if the succession process extends beyond year-end. The Wall Street Journal described the drawn-out selection process as emblematic of the organization’s recent paralysis.

Both leading candidates have notable diplomatic accomplishments. As head of the U.N. Trade and Development agency in 2022, Grynspan helped lead an initiative that temporarily protected Black Sea grain shipments early in the Russia-Ukraine war. Nusseibeh, while serving as the U.A.E. representative to the U.N., steered a 2023 resolution that allowed food shipments into Gaza during Israel’s war on Hamas.

Former U.K. Foreign Secretary David Miliband told a conference in Paris last week that the next leader must accept the risk of failure. “We need an activist, determined secretary-general of the U.N. who doesn’t mind risking failure in order to try and achieve success,” Miliband said. Grynspan agreed: “The risk appetite has to come from the leadership, and the leadership has to bear the cost of failure.”

The candidates also offer contrasting diplomatic profiles. Grynspan comes from Costa Rica, a small, stable country that has largely avoided the world’s major geopolitical power struggles. The Journal said Grynspan most admires Nobel Peace Prize laureate Kofi Annan of Ghana, who also led the U.N.; like him, Grynspan comes from a small, stable country that isn’t caught up in the world’s great geopolitical power struggles. The report said that country profile may have served her well in the past, but the U.A.E.’s global influence—particularly its ability to speak to competing powers such as the U.S., Russia and China and to serve as neutral ground for peace talks on Ukraine—is part of Nusseibeh’s appeal.

Nusseibeh was raised in the U.A.E. and comes from a prominent Palestinian family in Jerusalem. Her Cambridge-educated father, Zaki Nusseibeh, came to Abu Dhabi after the 1967 war, initially worked as a journalist and later became one of the closest advisers to the U.A.E.’s founder, Sheikh Zayed al Nahyan. Nusseibeh said that history made her appreciate the international system now under threat. “I want to be part of the regeneration of the system because I think it will keep us safe for decades if it is renewed and reoriented towards being credible and impactful on the ground for the people it serves,” she said.
