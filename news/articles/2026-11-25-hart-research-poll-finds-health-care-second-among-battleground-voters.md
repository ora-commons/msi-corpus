## Hart Research poll finds health care second among battleground voters

**Subtype:** fact

- Hart Research found health care the second-most important issue for battleground voters, ranking behind inflation and prices, in a survey of 1,230 voters across 59 congressional battleground districts.

## Congressional action drew backing from 71% of surveyed Republicans

Hart Research surveyed 1,230 voters in 59 congressional battleground districts between Nov. 4 and Nov. 8, and found that health care ranked second among the issues those voters considered most important, behind inflation and prices but ahead of the war in Iran and jobs and wages. Seventy-one percent of respondents said health care was a very important factor in their vote.

Beginning in January, Democrats will hold majorities in both the U.S. House and Senate, taking power back from Republicans. News From The States attributed that shift to voter frustrations over everyday costs that produced what it described as a nationwide rebuke of President Donald Trump and Republican policies.

Hart Research conducted the survey on behalf of the advocacy group Protect Our Care and the Service Employees International Union. Guy Molyneux, a partner at Hart Research, said Democrats held a modest 6% advantage among the voters polled, with 52% backing Democratic candidates and 46% backing Republican candidates. Voters who rated health care as their top issue overwhelmingly voted for Democratic candidates, Molyneux said.

When voters were presented with a list of policy actions Republicans and President Donald Trump had taken in recent years, the three items that voters largely rated as a reason to vote against Republicans in Congress were all tied to health care.

The poll also asked voters which issue was a higher priority, health care or lowering taxes — a major theme in Republican campaigns. Sixty-three percent of voters rated health care as their personal priority, while 34% said cutting taxes was more important. When asked which issue was of higher priority to each party, a broad majority of voters said health care was more of a focus for Democrats, while Republicans were more concerned with cutting taxes.

Looking ahead to what voters want from their elected officials, 83% of voters said it was important for Congress to take action on the cost of health care. While 95% of Democrats surveyed said they'd like to see Congress act on health care costs, Molyneux said the issue was also vital to most Republicans, with 71% saying Congress should take action. The survey data showed voters trusted Democrats far more than Republicans to deliver on affordable health care.

Concern about which party would act did not erase that cross-party support. Forty-eight percent of voters said they were concerned about congressional Republicans or the president impeding efforts to make health care more affordable, while 34% worried that Democrats would go too far in imposing government-run health care.

As for what Congress should do specifically, the survey presented voters with six issues that Democratic candidates and party leadership had identified as possible priorities for the caucus. Each proposal drew support from at least two-thirds of the voters surveyed, with participants showing particularly strong support for capping insulin costs at $35, adopting reforms to bar insurance companies from overriding decisions made between doctor and patient, and restoring funding to local hospitals.

On a press call discussing the survey's findings, U.S. Rep.-elect William Lawrence, a Democrat from Lansing who ousted Republican U.S. Rep. Tom Barrett of Charlotte in Michigan's 7th Congressional District, said the polling data tracked with what he heard on the campaign trail. “We talked to tens of thousands of voters and we heard again and again that healthcare is simply unaffordable, unreliable and it creates this cloud of anxiety over people’s lives,” Lawrence said. “Even those who have high-quality coverage now have no confidence that they’re going to be able to keep that coverage in the future, or that it will remain affordable because of how much volatility there has been in the healthcare markets and how Republicans have made this a political football that they seek to cut at every opportunity.”

Lawrence said health-care costs have put the squeeze on small business owners who provide health care to their employees, placing them in a situation of having to choose whether to cover rising costs or pass them on to their employees. He said 23 Michigan health facilities have announced cuts, closed or are at risk of closure, and that care itself is becoming less available.

Upon joining his Democratic colleagues Jan. 3, Lawrence said he looked forward to undoing Republican cuts to the Affordable Care Act and Medicaid, “and doing so immediately before those cuts begin to bite deeper.” From there, Democrats need to work toward expanding health care access and increasing affordability, working down the road to universal and guaranteed health care, Lawrence said.

Gabriella Jones-Casey, an executive vice president for SEIU Healthcare Michigan, said the voters gave members of Congress a mandate to put people first and deliver affordable health care. “We are not asking. We are not begging. We are not pleading,” Jones-Casey said. “We voted for those who committed to improving care for our families in our communities, and we will not back down. Lawmakers must put care ahead of corporate tax breaks and strengthen the care that allows Michiganders to be healthy and to thrive.”

Hart Research's results and Lawrence's account both linked health-care frustrations to Democratic wins in battleground districts. The party breakdown also showed that the concern extended beyond Democratic voters: majorities of Republicans backed congressional action, while concern about Republican obstruction exceeded concern about Democratic overreach.
