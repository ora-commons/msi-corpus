## Scottish Trans reports hospital avoidance and conflicting legal advice

At a Holyrood session of the Scottish Parliament’s equalities, human rights and civil justice committee, Mary-Ann Stephenson urged people to “take the temperature down” over reactions to Equality and Human Rights Commission guidance on single-sex spaces. Stephenson, the EHRC chair, said “misinformation” about the guidance had created “more anxiety among people.”

The EHRC released the guidance earlier this year after a Supreme Court ruling that “sex” in the Equality Act 2010 referred to biological sex. The ruling prompted concern among transgender people in Scotland, according to organizations that also appeared before the committee.

Stephenson said significant numbers of transgender people, including younger people, were anxious because they had believed the law entitled them to protections that the ruling showed were not available. She said some of that anxiety reflected descriptions of the code in “really inflated language,” including claims that it excluded all transgender people from public life and required genital checks to verify access to services.

Stephenson said neither the law nor the code stated that the guidance excluded all transgender people from public life or required genital checks for service access. She said the principle at the heart of the Equality Act was to build a society in which everyone could live with dignity, obtain needed services and avoid discrimination.

Organizations supporting transgender people told the committee that the guidance had affected various areas of life, including access to leisure facilities and healthcare settings.

Florence Oulds, Scottish Trans’s policy and public affairs coordinator, gave the committee a more immediate account of the effect on hospital care. Oulds told of cases in which people had refused to go to a hospital.

Oulds said Scottish Trans was aware of at least three transgender people it had spoken to, along with other evidence it had heard, who had not gone to hospital against medical advice because they were concerned about where they would be placed on a ward. Oulds described the concern as “a very common sentiment.”

Oulds also said the code of practice was “just fundamentally a very confusing and difficult document to follow.” She said the EHRC often instructs organizations to seek legal advice, but that organizations receive a very wide range of advice that is often completely conflicting.

Oulds said trans-inclusive organizations that wanted to comply with the law while remaining inclusive had spent lots of money on legal advice without getting anywhere. Stephenson rejected that interpretation and said, “I think the code is actually quite straightforward.”
