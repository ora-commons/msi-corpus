## Legislation would expand year-round H-2A access and poultry eligibility

More than 30 Pennsylvania farmers, agriculture businesses and organizations endorsed a letter to U.S. Agriculture Secretary Brooke Rollins and U.S. Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin asking them to reform worker visa programs and federal immigration enforcement practices. The letter described ICE agents’ tactics as aggressive and said they targeted immigrant workers. Leaders of some of the businesses and organizations involved in the effort met Thursday in Lancaster County with Gov. Josh Shapiro and state Agriculture Secretary Russell Redding to discuss the struggles workers had faced and the path to reform.

The letter, authored by PennAg Industries Association President Christian Herr, read: “We are concerned that employees with legal status have been detained in recent actions and aggressive enforcement has caused workers with status to miss work due to fears of being caught in farm-targeted actions.” It also said: “Due to an aging and declining population, and a lack of interest on behalf of many nativeborn Americans, it is essential that our industry be able to utilize an immigrant workforce.” Herr was also at the roundtable meeting.

The letter explicitly called for federal immigration agents to prioritize targeting criminals instead of workers and to act in a “lawful, orderly, and transparent manner.”

Herr and other farm leaders at the roundtable specifically advocated passage of the Securing Agriculture’s Workforce Act, a bill sponsored by Pennsylvania Republican Rep. Glenn Thompson, who is wrapping up his time as chair of the House Agriculture Committee. The bill would allow workers with H-2A visas to remain in the United States year-round instead of only seasonally. It would also expand the program to workers in agriculture-related businesses like Melhorn’s that are currently ineligible and permit some experienced, unauthorized agricultural workers to apply for and become eligible for H-2A visas.

The proposal currently has 61 co-sponsors, including 10 Democrats. Rep. Chrissy Houlahan is the only Pennsylvania Democrat currently signed on.

Shapiro said it was important for him to hear directly from the business and organization leaders who met with him. “It was important for me to hear directly how these immigration challenges — immigration enforcement challenges — were affecting you,” Shapiro said. “At the same time, we have a secure border, we can also have compassion for the people who are working here in our communities, whether it’s on our farms, in our factories or in our small businesses.” He said many requested reforms would have to be enacted by the federal government, though his “loud voice” could help sway federal officials and Pennsylvania’s congressional delegation.

State officials visited Cherry Hill Orchards in Lancaster County on May 8, 2026, in a photograph from PACast. Sarah Zost, president of the State Horticultural Association of Pennsylvania and an Adams County fruit grower, said ICE agents detained three workers at her farm during traffic stops in commute hours. The workers made up a quarter of her winter crew. “Two of them had worked for us for over a decade, and one has a wife and kids who go to my local school district,” Zost said.

Zost said other farms around her had also had workers detained under similar circumstances, including workers who were legally in the United States. “In our case, they had a warrant, but not for any of our employees,” she said. “They were looking for a neighbor of one of them and just happened to see [workers] carpooling to work in the morning of the target demographic and pulled them over.” Zost said the workers ended up agreeing to be deported. Workers who self-deport are often barred from entering the United States for three or more years.

“Being here legally does not get you a get-out-of-jail-free card for harassment from ICE,” Zost said. “There’s a culture of fear and concern because you get targeted because of what you look like.” Zost said ICE’s tactics affected not only the workers detained and the businesses they worked for but also foreign-born employees at those businesses and nearby. “There are worker chats and farmer chats of any sightings of ICE in the area, and everyone will stay home to avoid those kinds of interactions,” she said. “It causes lost time on all farms because workers, regardless of visa status, stay home.”

Astin Melhorn, president of Paragon Poultry Management Group, said immigration-enforcement actions and sudden changes in policy had hurt his business and workers. Poultry workers do not qualify for the seasonal H-2A visa program used by traditional agriculture businesses, so Paragon had sponsored dozens of employees through the EB-3 visa program since 2015. Workers who obtain that visa are granted permanent residency. The process can take years, but Melhorn said it had been relatively predictable.

This year, eight employees finishing the six-year process of obtaining their visas learned that President Donald Trump had signed an executive order stopping all visas from Guatemala. Melhorn separately called the development frustrating. “That’s a very frustrating thing when you’re an employer trying to do things correctly,” Melhorn said.

Melhorn also said his business had been affected by aggressive ICE tactics. Earlier this year, 17 workers were detained, including some with work permits. “[They] were shipped as far as Oklahoma,” Melhorn said. “When they were released, they had to find their own way back to Pennsylvania … Some self-deported, some have cases and so they’re working through those, and some were released.” “That very much rocked our world and rocked our industry,” he added.

Herr said he had heard from many in the agriculture business who considered themselves conservative, including some who had voted for Trump, but were uncomfortable with how federal immigration enforcement had played out. He disputed the argument that Americans had voted to deport 20 million people. “We look at the talking heads throughout the country who, from time to time, state that Americans voted for deporting 20 million people. I think that’s a misread,” Herr said. “Because I can tell you that there are many, many folks in this community, from this sector, from agriculture and agribusiness, who don’t feel that way. I would say a large majority of them don’t.” An accompanying photograph by Nicole Neri for Minnesota Reformer showed federal agents on patrol in Minneapolis in January 2026.

Agriculture business owners at the roundtable called on federal elected officials from Pennsylvania, especially Republicans in Congress, to work with them. News From The States reported that Democrats would hold majorities in the U.S. House and Senate next year. It described the election as “seen broadly as a rebuke to Trump.” It said it remained unclear whether President Donald Trump would work with Democrats on immigration reform. Following the election, Trump appointed Stephen Miller as his chief of staff. Miller had advocated for family separation, the end of birthright citizenship and the suspension of habeas corpus for detained immigrants.

Herr said he had conversations with local congressmen and believed there had been a reassessment of the issue and constituents’ views. “This idea with congressmen who are afraid of the White House and pander to — I think that changed,” he said. “I’ve had some conversations with local congressmen about this and I do think there’s a reassessment of this issue and where the constituents are. When you have a room full of folks from construction, and agriculture and healthcare, it’s a meaningful point to say, ‘Look this really needs to be maturely addressed,’”

Herr said the issue had become “personal” for him, especially after immigrant workers helped his businesses through Pennsylvania’s avian influenza outbreak. “They’re looking over their shoulders. They’re uncomfortable. They’re always wondering when their time is gonna be up here,” he said. “There’s people who have been here for a quarter century, who have raised their families here. It’s sort of Congress and whoever is in the White House to say, ‘Alright, let’s do a reset here.’”
