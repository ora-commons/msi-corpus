## Local-seed project targets 50,000 trees over two years

Volunteers have started planting spindly cypress saplings along the Guadalupe River in Kerr County, Texas, where two floods left many mature cypress trees bent, broken or missing. The San Antonio Botanical Garden-led effort uses trees grown from regional seeds and aims to put 50,000 of them in the ground over the next two years.

The damage spans two floods. Last year's July 4 flood killed more than 100 people and tore up the riverbanks. Machinery scraped away plants and earth as workers searched for bodies and hauled off debris. The river rose again to disastrous levels this July. Roughly half the vegetation along a 30-mile stretch of the river was lost, according to an analysis by the Bandera County River Authority and Groundwater District.

Many of the new saplings stand only a foot or two tall. Plant experts said the local cypresses are so unique they might even be their own species. Project leaders say the river could recover on its own, but they see the planting effort as a way to jump-start regrowth, restore habitat and stabilize banks while keeping invasive plants from taking over. They also seek to rehabilitate an ecological landscape they say was already out of whack before the floods and to educate residents about the role of native plants.

Erin Davis, program coordinator for Texas Resilient Landscapes at the Texas A&M Forest Service, which supports the project, said the river can recover. “Will the river recover? Yes,” Davis said. “But I think we do have an important role to play where we can direct how that restoration goes.”

The effort began with more than 850,000 seeds collected by the San Antonio Botanical Garden and sorted by hand. Only a third of the cypress seeds germinated. The young trees were raised with periodic water, wind and nutrient stress in hopes of making them heartier, though hungry deer or future droughts could kill them after planting. Ryan McGillicuddy, watershed and conservation team leader for the Texas Parks and Wildlife Department, tied the work to the community's grief: “We’re grieving,” McGillicuddy said. “Just like there are these steps that nature has to go through to heal, we’re going through it in tandem, and so we can look to nature as a source of healing for ourselves.”

The cypresses have long defined the river and the surrounding community. Early settlers arrived in the mid-1800s and cut the trees down to make shingles. By the 1920s, children spent summer weeks at camps along the water. Some trees lost in the flood were hundreds of years old. Artist Carol Arnold, who grew up in Dallas and later raised her family in Kerrville, used pre-flood pastels to preserve what she had seen. “It’s like the roots of the cypress trees were a foundation for us all,” Arnold said. “It was emotional, spiritual — more than just roots to a tree, they meant something to everyone that lives in the community.”

The trees also supported the river's ecology. Their shade cooled water for aquatic life, their roots held soil in place, and they provided habitat for insects, birds and mammals. By slowing the water, the cypresses caused soil to drop around them and form additional habitat. The pristine river sustained a tourism and health economy built around family reunions, fishing trips, vacations, hunting, weddings and autumn views. Jeremy Walther, 47, who owns a local brewery and started the Kerr County River Foundation after the July 4 flood, said: “The river system is not just a pretty thing to look at,” Walther said. “Becoming an economic hub … all of that is built on the river.”

The July 4 flood ripped up homes, carried away cars and forced an untold number of people to fight a debris-filled current. Some survived by clinging to trees. In many places, people could suddenly see clear across the water that had been hidden by a thick canopy. Mindy Wendele, president and CEO of the Kerrville Area Chamber of Commerce and a fourth-generation Kerr County resident, said: “That part is sad, because it’s different,” Wendele said. “It’s kind of an assault on what we’ve grown up with.”

Michael Eason, vice president of conservation for the San Antonio Botanical Garden, was riding with other garden leaders shortly after the July 4 flood when he proposed growing trees from local seeds. “My landmarks were trees, and those were just gone,” Eason said. “It was just wiped clean.” He contacted Becky Etzler, executive director of Riverside Nature Center in Kerrville, an education center for plant-focused groups whose property and native-plant gardens also flooded. “It’s understandable: They’re looking for loss of life. They’re trying to recover bodies,” Etzler said. “We’re trying to recover banks, so there was a priority there.” Heavy machinery had scraped away beneficial plant matter and compacted the soil while workers searched for victims.

Rather than buy cypress trees grown in Louisiana or Florida, the botanical garden began collecting cones from multiple local properties where plants were adapted to the Hill Country climate and limestone soils. The effort sought to preserve local tree genetics. One collection site was Camp Stewart on the North Fork of the Guadalupe River, where the water did not reach the same devastating heights it did on the South Fork before pushing through Kerrville. “It just makes me happy that we’re helping reforest our neighbors on the South Fork,” said camp matriarch Kathy Ragsdale. Her daughter, Jane, grew up at Camp Stewart, dreamed of becoming a camp director and died on July 4 at the Heart O’ the Hills camp, which she ran on the South Fork not far from Camp Mystic.

San Antonio Botanical Garden staff dried the cones in trays and bags in a walk-in refrigerator before volunteers broke them apart and sorted the seeds. The garden shipped some to about a dozen nurseries across Texas that volunteered to grow plants for the effort. The project included willows, pecans, oaks and sycamores as well as cypresses. While the trees grew, the Hill Country Alliance met with landowners, distributed seeds for grasses and forbs, handed out plants such as sedges and demonstrated where to plant them. The alliance and its partners told property owners to leave downed trees and branches in place because they could stabilize the banks until new plants grew. “It all takes time,” said Daniel Oppenheimer, land program director for the alliance. “Time and grace and hope.” The second July flood tore through the area and ripped away some plantings, although others survived. “Throwing your hands up in frustration is not an option,” said Matt Welch, the garden's director of conservation and collections.

Oil painter and master naturalist Nancy Huffman of Hunt noticed debris lodged in trees and trunks stripped of bark, but she also found flowers emerging among the rubble. Huffman recorded signs of recovery in her journal and included it in a small exhibition of river paintings at the Heart of the Hills Heritage Center in Kerrville. Most of the paintings depicted the river before the floods. “Life returns,” she remarked. “It may not be the cypresses all at once, but life is returning.”

Other landowners pursued their own restoration efforts. Don and Carolyn White, both 77, planted a cypress sapling from a local nursery on their family land in Center Point. They reached it by navigating down their neighbors' steps with walking sticks, then protected it with metal wiring and covered its base with mulch, sticks and leaves. The Whites watered the tree, scattered seeds that grew into grass on their scraped-away banks and righted the sapling after the second July flood knocked it over without uprooting it. “It’s growing,” Carolyn White said. “That’s to us the best sign you could have.” Northern Hendricks, 50, left immense fallen trees on her property as she restored a former hay farm; some of the trees sprouted green leaves even while lying on their sides. Hendricks adopted the philosophy: “Give it time.” Jonathan Letz, a rancher, landscaper and former Kerr County commissioner helping coordinate restoration through the area's long-term recovery organization, said: “One person can’t fix the whole river,” Letz said, “but you can fix that little piece of the river.”

The San Antonio Botanical Garden hosted its first planting on Nov. 5, matching the natural time when seeds drop into soil. Staff have continued plantings every week since, relying on volunteers. Some helpers live in Kerr County, including one whose home flooded twice, while others drove from Austin and San Antonio. Several have family connections to the area or have camped there. Hollis Uecker, a 64-year-old retired elementary school teacher who lives in Kerrville, worked alongside her husband Wayne, who ran a railing company. “It’s going to make a difference, maybe not to us but to our grandkids or our great-grandkids,” Uecker said. “But that’s our responsibility: to make it better.”

The botanical garden could expand the work with more funding. In addition to its own fundraising, it received a $2 million grant from the Community Foundation of the Texas Hill Country, which pledged to match every additional dollar donated by April 15, up to $1 million. Volunteer Lisa Andrews, 56, who moved to Kerrville from Austin after her children left for college, patted the wet, rocky earth around her sapling. “Grow, little tree,” she told it. Cypress trees take decades to reach full height, so adults planting today may never sit in the shade of the trees they help establish. The saplings nevertheless provide a tangible step toward recovery for the river and the community.
