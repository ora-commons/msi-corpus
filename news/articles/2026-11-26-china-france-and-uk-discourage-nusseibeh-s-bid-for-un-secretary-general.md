## China, France and UK discourage Nusseibeh's bid for UN secretary-general

**Subtype:** fact

- Lana Nusseibeh is campaigning in Britain for the UN secretary-general post after China, France and the UK voted last Friday to discourage her candidacy.
- The UN requires nine positive Security Council votes from its 15 members to recommend a candidate to the General Assembly, and no discouragement from the permanent five.
- A UN panel of experts suggested last month that the UAE supplied crucial support to Sudan's Rapid Support Forces, a charge Nusseibeh has repeatedly denied.
- Nusseibeh says she has taken a leave of absence from her role in the UAE government and that she would fully back the findings of all UN panel of experts reports.
- Many British NGOs and others have labelled Nusseibeh an apologist for war crimes, The Guardian reported.
- The next Security Council vote is on Monday.

## UN panel of experts suggested UAE supplied crucial support to Sudan's RSF

Lana Nusseibeh spent this week in Britain trying to present herself as a candidate for the next secretary general of the United Nations, meeting ministers, MPs and academics and holding a public event with the UN Association. The Guardian described the campaign as a high-risk strategy and said Nusseibeh was trying to shift attention from her past as a UAE diplomat who denied the UAE's role in arming a militia accused of war crimes and genocide in Sudan's four-year civil war. Three permanent members of the Security Council — China, France and the UK — voted last Friday to discourage her candidacy, and the next vote falls on Monday.

Nusseibeh says she has taken a leave of absence from her role in the UAE government so that she can campaign independently, and she has the backing of the US and Russia. She thinks her cause is not lost. The UN's electoral system requires nine positive votes from the 15-strong Security Council for a candidate to be recommended to the General Assembly, and no discouragement from the permanent five may stand in the way of that recommendation. China, France and the UK have now delivered that signal.

Photographs published with The Guardian's interview show Nusseibeh in March, when she was the UAE's minister of state, and arriving in New York in September 2025 to address the 80th General Assembly as UAE ambassador to the UN.

She framed the contest in geopolitical terms. "These are very geopolitical times," she said in an interview. "You need a geopolitical secretary general, because the fact is these geopolitical ruptures are not going away. These fissures in the international system are not going to dissolve any time soon."

On the use of the veto, she said: "Sometimes, the use of the veto is tactical. Sometimes, it's a holding position, sometimes it's a leveraging position. There are a number of different theories".

The Guardian assessed that the centre of the resistance to Nusseibeh's candidacy is probably London and specifically the Foreign Office, the penholder on Sudan at the UN. The newspaper said this may explain the UK's political blitz against her candidacy. Asked about her record in Sudan, she asked that her candidacy be seen in the round, highlighting her role in forcing humanitarian aid into Gaza, her support for Afghan women and her promotion of interfaith tolerance.

"Like every candidate, I come from somewhere, but the independence of the incoming secretary general is the key to their credibility," she said. She added: "I've taken a leave of absence from my role in [the] UAE government in order to independently campaign for this role. But I think that if I was secretary general, Sudan would be the litmus test of success for any incoming secretary because it's such a horrific humanitarian crisis."

The war in Sudan erupted in April 2023 after a power struggle between the military and the Rapid Support Forces, displacing more than 14 million people and creating one of the world's worst humanitarian crises. The Guardian describes the RSF as a militia accused of war crimes and genocide in Sudan's four-year civil war. Multiple regional powers, including the UAE, have backed different local proxies since the beginning of the conflict in an attempt to win influence and strategic resources. The UAE has long been accused of supporting the RSF, a charge Nusseibeh has repeatedly denied; she has publicly disputed the findings of UN investigators. A UN panel of experts suggested last month that the UAE supplied crucial support to the RSF.

Asked if she regretted dissembling during her time as a diplomat, by denying the UAE had armed the RSF or her personal role in challenging UN reports critical of the UAE, Nusseibeh said she regretted that "the Sudanese civil war had continued for so long".

"But I don't think saying that is enough for the Sudanese people and the people in the surrounding countries where they've also felt the impact of this war, or for the victims of sexual violence and in this conflict, the atrocities committed in El Fasher without accountability, and the use of chemical weapons against the civilian population; all of these are a litany of suffering that has been inflicted on the Sudanese people."

Asked if the UAE had been supplying weapons to the RSF in breach of a UN embargo, she replied: "I'm aware of the allegations. I haven't read the last UN panel of experts report, but I'm not going to comment as I'm no longer a UAE official. In this campaign, I am a secretary general candidate."

She added: "As secretary general, I would fully back the findings of all UN panel of experts reports and I'm aware that I think several countries have been mentioned in various reports." And: "I would call out countries that are supplying arms to any of the parties, including those in the RSF … this needs a process that requires accountability for atrocities that have been committed. That it needs to be a process primarily led by the Sudanese people."

The Guardian's assessment was that Nusseibeh's intelligence and skills as a diplomat are not in question, while her track record over Sudan is. Many British NGOs and others have labelled her an apologist for war crimes, The Guardian reported. One critic said: "It is a very smart strategic play by the UAE that seeks autocratic capture, and then the asset-stripping of the UN and its standards. The UAE would end the UN's role on climate. It would be a catastrophe."

On what she would do in office, Nusseibeh said she was willing to take risks to mediate and to put UN values, expertise and its development toolkit at the centre of disputes. The Guardian said Nusseibeh was presenting herself as a skilled diplomat with the network to put the UN back at the centre of mediation, development and human rights. Her reading of contemporary diplomacy was that the world had descended into a default setting of non-cooperation, and she argued that the UN needed to restate the case for collaboration between nations as being in the self-interest of everyone. She also argued that the UN had been missing from too many conflicts and would set up a mediation board drawing on the full UN toolkit, from confidence-building measures to development to secure peace. "What's missing is this idea of a secretary general that is a strategist and a mediator-in-chief that is looking at the sort of the blips on the dashboard of conflict and saying, 'How do we deploy to have maximum impact for most effective use of resource and cost?'" She also said, "Because resources are not going up, they're going down." She cited a 40% increase in military spending since 2010 to argue that hard power is not working. The Guardian said this would require some prioritisation at the UN, which has 4,000 different open mandates.

The next Security Council vote is on Monday. Some of the backmarkers may have dropped out of the race by then, and The Guardian wrote that the diplomats of the permanent five may also be clearer on the flexibility of their mandates, in which case "the papal conclave may finally produce white smoke."
