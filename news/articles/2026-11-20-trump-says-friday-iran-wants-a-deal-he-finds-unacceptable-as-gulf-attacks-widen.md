## Gulf Arab states pursue partial solution that could remove all shipping constraints

President Donald Trump said Friday at a White House news conference that Iran wants a deal to end hostilities, but not one acceptable to him. Trump said, “We want to make a real deal, not a fake deal,” and said the Iranian government had been “bull——ing people for 51 years.” He renewed his warning that “Iran will never have a nuclear weapon, ever.” His comments came after Iran widened attacks on Gulf neighbors and as Gulf Arab states worked through existing communications channels to seek a sustainable, if only partial, solution nearly nine months into the war.

Trump has oscillated between favoring an agreement that would end the fighting and saying the United States does not need to make any deal. Diplomats and analysts described uncertainty about next steps, alongside the uneven back-and-forth hostilities, as reflecting the untenable position both sides occupied nearly nine months into the war. Mona Yacoubian, director of the Middle East program at the Center for Strategic and International Studies, said, “The level of kinetic activity, the open threats both sides are leveling against the other, underscores that this is an incredibly volatile moment.” She added, “And yet, neither side, I don’t think, seems to be willing to just jump headlong back into renewed, all-out conflict.”

A Middle Eastern diplomat who spoke on condition of anonymity because the conversations were sensitive said before Trump’s news conference that Gulf Arab states were concerned about the potential resumption of full-scale hostilities. Gulf Arab states were working through existing communications channels to seek a sustainable, if only partial, solution, the diplomat said.

The proposed solution could remove all constraints from shipping in the Strait of Hormuz, a crucial thoroughfare for the global energy trade, in exchange for limited sanctions relief for Iran, the Middle Eastern diplomat said. The arrangement could also pave the way for a resumption of broader negotiations to include Tehran’s nuclear program. Countries seeking to mediate placed little confidence in public posturing from both the United States and Iran and took seriously only statements made in closed-door negotiations, the diplomat said.

Iran widened attacks on its Gulf neighbors last week, targeting a U.S. air base in Kuwait and launching several attacks on Bahrain following a U.S. fighter jet’s strike on a Revolutionary Guard missile launch site on Tuesday on Qeshm Island in the Strait of Hormuz. It had been about two months since Tehran had ordered such attacks targeting U.S. facilities in neighboring countries, and Iran said it was responding to U.S. drone surveillance operations and an attack on Iranian communications towers.

Analysts described the tit-for-tat but somewhat measured attacks as part of a larger signaling from both sides that, despite external pressure to reengage, there was little appetite or capability from either side to return to the height of the war. Yacoubian said Iran’s recent attempts to widen its attacks to some of its Gulf neighbors could be a signal of reluctance. “Who are they hitting? They’re hitting Bahrain and Kuwait. The two weakest of the (Gulf) countries,” she added. “I mean, they’re not hitting the UAE. They’re not hitting Saudi. They’re not hitting Qatar.”

Michael Singh, a former senior Mideast director at the National Security Council during President George W. Bush’s tenure, assessed the Trump administration’s current approach, despite its shortcomings, as likely the most sustainable option. Singh, now managing director of the Washington Institute for Near East Policy, said, “It is the most sustainable of the options the president has to choose from and holds the best hope for getting a diplomatic deal more favorable to the United States.” He said the risks of escalation were “pretty significant” while the “returns” were uncertain, citing potential casualties, damage to military hardware and steep increases in energy prices that were just beginning to stabilize. “Strategic patience really looks like the most attractive approach — the surest course,” Singh said.

The Associated Press reported that Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu has pushed the United States to restart the war or allow Israel to launch airstrikes on Iran’s nuclear facilities and missile capabilities.

Iran’s strikes kicked off days after Trump said the conflict was “militarily is effectively over,” following the Republican Party’s loss of control of Congress. Voters cited the unpopularity of the war and the hit to their pocketbooks from its economic effects. Thousands of U.S. troops remained off Iran’s waters, maintaining a naval blockade to stem the flow of Iranian oil into the global market.

The Joint Maritime Information Center, a coalition overseen by the U.S. Navy, issued an updated warning this week that ships may be violating the blockade if they transferred cargo from another vessel under restrictions or carried goods loaded in Iran even if the ships never entered an Iranian port. The Trump administration also maintained an economic-pressure campaign targeting Iran’s few remaining international financial ties. Ali Vaez, Iran director at the International Crisis Group, said, “Trump may have declared the war over, but Tehran is determined to show that Washington cannot declare victory while keeping Iran under economic siege.”

While Iran’s actions had intensified lately, the Trump administration appeared to show more restraint than the U.S. military had during its retaliation over the summer. Earlier in the month, Trump rejected the idea that the United States even needed a deal with Iran. Yacoubian said the situation could reflect Iran hardening its bargain ahead of the election and demanding so much more “in ways that are simply untenable for this administration.” She added, “They basically have deprived President Trump of that face-saving off-ramp.”
