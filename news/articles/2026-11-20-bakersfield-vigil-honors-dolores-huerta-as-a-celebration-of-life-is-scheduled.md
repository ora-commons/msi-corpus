## Friday’s scheduled interfaith service features music, civic speakers and remembrance

The Guardian reported that hundreds of people honored and celebrated the life and legacy of California civil rights activist Dolores Huerta during a week of public events, including a Thursday vigil in Bakersfield and a celebration of life scheduled for Friday. The Guardian described the events as dedicated to Huerta’s memory, courage, resilience and impact on the community. Huerta co-founded the United Farm Workers in 1962 and died on October 28 at age 96.

A two-day tribute began Thursday evening with a public rosary and vigil in Bakersfield, where Huerta had lived since 1990.

Friday’s interfaith memorial service and celebration of life were scheduled to begin with an opening blessing by Aztec dancers, followed by the memorial service and celebration of life program. Music groups Los Lobos, Ozomatli, Las Cafeteras, Nancy Sanchez and Trio los Dados were expected to perform.

Scheduled speakers included California governor-elect Xavier Becerra, California Attorney General Rob Bonta, United Farm Workers president Teresa Romero, actor Eva Longoria and comedian Cristela Alonzo, among others. Both public events were to be live streamed on the Dolores Huerta Foundation’s YouTube channel.

Huerta’s family said in a Thursday afternoon statement, “Our family has been deeply touched by the condolences, remembrances, and tributes we’ve received from people whose lives our mother, Dolores, impacted. Countless people have shared how she changed the course of their lives, and each story has reminded us of how fully she committed her life to helping and supporting others.”

The family also said, “Our mother devoted her entire life to fighting injustice and lifting up communities too often left out and treated unfairly.” It added, “As a lifelong organizer, her activism has been a source of inspiration and empowerment for people around the world.”

The Guardian said Huerta’s legacy would continue through the work and empowering mission of the Dolores Huerta Foundation. She founded the foundation in 2003 as a grassroots community organization intended to create leadership opportunities for community members, organize underserved communities and pursue social justice. Huerta continued organizing into her 90s.

For more than six decades, Huerta spearheaded historic boycotts and fought for farmworkers’ rights, women’s equality and environmental justice, The Guardian reported. She coined the enduring national rallying cry, “¡Sí, se puede!” Her honors included the Eleanor Roosevelt Human Rights Awards. In 2012, Barack Obama awarded her the Presidential Medal of Freedom, which The Guardian described as the nation’s highest civilian honor.

In the Central Valley, Huerta supported minorities running for office at the local, state and federal levels to make a difference. In 2022, Huerta spoke at a USCIS Special Children’s Ceremony in Delano, encouraging new US citizens to fulfill their civic responsibilities, especially voting.

For her 94th birthday, Huerta asked people to mark the occasion by getting others involved in elections through conversations about voting and registering to vote. She also encouraged legal residents to become US citizens so they could exercise their right to vote.

Huerta partnered with Covered California to urge uninsured Latinos and Californians to enroll in health coverage following the passage of the Affordable Care Act. Several schools throughout California were named after her. Last year, Huerta joined Central Valley educators in urging US Representative David Valadao of California to defend funding for schools.

**Capitol ceremony context:** State government officials, community leaders and members of the public honored Huerta during a separate Monday ceremony in Sacramento. California Senate President pro tempore Monique Limón, Assembly Speaker Robert Rivas and California governor-elect Xavier Becerra were among the officials who paid respects. The Guardian described the ceremony as historic.

The ceremony made Huerta the first female, the first Latino and the first non-elected leader to lie in honor at the state Capitol. Her casket was escorted into the building after a march organized by local unions.
