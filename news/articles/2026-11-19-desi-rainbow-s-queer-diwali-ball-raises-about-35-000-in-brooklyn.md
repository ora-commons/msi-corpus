## Aruna Rao says policy targeting LGBTQ+ rights marked this Diwali season

More than 200 people in festive Indian formalwear - glittering saris, lehengas and kurtas - packed a Brooklyn community center last weekend for the fourth annual Queer Diwali Ball. The evening featured drag queens and classic Bollywood songs, and by design it gave LGBTQ+ South Asians a place to celebrate Diwali without choosing between their identities and their faith and culture. The event raised, in total, about $35,000 for the nonprofit that ran it, Desi Rainbow.

Desi Rainbow began with a search that came up empty. Aruna Rao said her son came out to her as transgender nearly 10 years ago, that she looked for support for South Asian families like hers and found almost nothing. She started a small support group to help parents of LGBTQ+ South Asians and their children navigate persistent cultural shame and stigma, and that group grew into Desi Rainbow, a nonprofit that says it has served over 2,000 people across the United States.

The room was decorated with tea lights, orange and pink sequins and traditional strings of marigolds, and people of all ages, from toddlers to the elderly, celebrated Diwali, the major Hindu festival also celebrated by Jains and some Buddhists, which symbolizes the victory of light over darkness. Guests came from many different faiths, speaking a mix of languages, and dressed to embody the theme "Rang Mahal," which translates to "Palace of Colors."

The ball welcomes people of all ages, sexualities and faiths, and was designed, in contrast to traditional religious settings, as a place where everyone could show up exactly as they are. Same-sex couples danced together, non-binary attendees wore colorful, androgynous South Asian formalwear, and the crowd cheered as drag queens, in lehengas and golden jewelry, twirled in their stilettos. The scent of fresh jasmine perfumed the space, and as people entered, drag queens strung and clipped it into their hair.

Rao said the Diwali festival held a special significance for the LGBTQ+ South Asian community this year, as lawmakers across the country introduced a slew of bills targeting same-sex marriage and transgender rights. The reporting on the ball also noted that the Trump administration restricted H1B visa access, engendering fear among many in Desi immigrant communities. Rao tied that politics to the festival's central symbol: "So much of what has happened in terms of policy and legislation has come out of ignorance," she said. "We're hoping the light of knowledge burns out the darkness of ignorance."

Rao also said Hindu temples do not always announce that they welcome LGBTQ+ people, and that celebrating Diwali in traditional religious spaces where queerness is stigmatized can be isolating for LGBTQ+ Hindus and their families. "It's usually a hush, hush affair," she said.

A self-proclaimed "7 foot tall Bollywood Barbie," drag queen Abhijeet made a grand entrance to an Indian classical rendition of Adele's "When We Were Young." An immigrant from Mumbai, Abhijeet said she traveled from her home in Chicago to perform at the ball in New York City. "When you are queer and South Asian, it's also all of the extremely racist and xenophobic laws affecting you twofold," she said. "That's why communities like this are so important."

Fahmida Rashid said she immediately accepted her two teenage children's identities when they came out to her, but also wondered how to share the news with her Bangladeshi Muslim parents and how to explain the importance of correct gender pronouns in Bengali, which only has gender-neutral pronouns. She found answers through Desi Rainbow support groups. Now she tries to pay it forward, acting as a sister or auntie to queer South Asians whose families have cut them off. "It breaks my heart that what I'm doing for my kids is unusual," she said.

Vendors at the event sold ayurvedic candles, Indian tribal jewelry and even back massages, and one stand advertised healthcare specifically for LGBTQ+ youth. A spread of samosas, mattar paneer, butter chicken and mithai was provided by TAGMO, a caterer owned by Michelin-starred pastry chef Surbhi Sahni, who identifies as a queer woman on her website.

Tina Jacob Sandhu attended the ball with her mom, Gita Jacob, who was visiting from India. The mother-daughter duo said they attended to demonstrate support for their LGBTQ+ friends and family members as straight allies. As they danced together, Tina urged her mother to show off her moves on stage, and Gita crossed herself when one of the drag queens stripped to her lingerie. "It was something I've never seen in my life, and I really enjoyed it," Gita, a Malayali Christian, said of the event. "At the end of the day, all are God's children."

The event raised, in total, about $35,000 for Desi Rainbow, an organization that began when one parent's search for support turned up almost nothing. For the families in the room, the point of the celebration was that no one had to choose between a child's identity and a community's traditions, a distinction that, by Rao's account, Hindu temples do not always make openly.
