## Federal records show CSI taking manifest duties as flight-tracking limits expand

Federal contracting records show that Immigration and Customs Enforcement expanded private contractor CSI Aviation’s role in deportation flights, agreeing to pay up to $305.7 million for new staffing work without competitive bidding. ICE also signed an order worth up to $1.28 billion on 31 October to keep CSI running charter flights for another year, with CSI staff preparing lists of people assigned to removal and transfer flights. The Guardian said the continued private-company role provided a windfall despite protests and political reversals in the Trump administration’s deportation program.

The September contract change added LADD — the Federal Aviation Administration’s Limiting Aircraft Data Displayed program — to the contract-management section of the agreement. The program lets aircraft owners and operators keep planes off some public tracking websites. The planes continue broadcasting their positions, but the block changes what the websites display. The change may further inhibit monitoring of ICE flights, including flights to third countries, by immigrant and civil-rights advocates. The Guardian said it contacted the Department of Homeland Security, ICE and CSI Aviation for comment.

A procurement justification signed on 16 July by ICE head of contracting Albert Dainton says the staffing arrangement covers up to 140 dedicated flight coordinators at field offices and air-operations hubs, working 18 hours a day, seven days a week. Dainton’s justification calls CSI the only source capable of the work, citing its proprietary systems and integrated flight-tracking and manifest-management systems that interoperate with ICE’s operational platforms. It says increased operational demands and ICE staffing challenges make the service necessary and that the coordinators would reduce administrative burdens on ICE personnel. With options, the agreement runs through March 2029, just after Donald Trump’s scheduled term ends. Dainton approved the deal both as ICE’s advocate for competition and as its head of contracting.

Under the original agreement DHS published in 2025, the government was required to give CSI a manifest containing the names of all passengers before each flight departed. ICE’s 2023 air-operations handbook assigned that work to agency staff: air-removal coordinators reviewed and allocated seat requests and prepared flight manifests in the T-8 charter application. The new order instead calls for CSI staff to draw up the lists, reversing the division of work described in the earlier contract and handbook.

CSI already had broad access to ICE data systems, according to legal documents. In a sworn declaration filed in January in the U.S. Court of Federal Claims, where CSI unsuccessfully challenged a separate DHS contract, the company’s senior vice-president for government services, Richard DeLucia, said its personnel “have full access to ICE’s data systems to input data and verify manifests on every mission.” He said the program had evolved to capture more administrative functions for ICE ERO and unburden officers so they could continue field operations.

The same documents say 19 of CSI’s more than 300 employees came from immigration agencies, mostly recent retirees from ICE and Customs and Border Protection. Those employees handled immigration-paperwork verification for migrant domestic transfers and international removal missions. “We pride ourselves on hiring the best ICE personnel after they have left active duty to continue to bring a high level of continuity to our deportation system,” DeLucia wrote.

The September federal award record places LADD in the agreement’s contract-management section. In March 2025, FlightRadar24 reportedly received blocking notices for more than a dozen aircraft, including planes often used for ICE deportations and transfers. The 2024 law behind the program authorizes blocking upon request of a private aircraft owner or operator for the owner’s or operator’s noncommercial flights. In May, 40 House Democrats wrote to the FAA citing reports that ICE had misused the program to shield deportation flights from public and congressional scrutiny, saying the use was outside the program’s scope.

Rep. Rob Menendez, a New Jersey Democrat and signatory to the letter, told The Guardian that “ICE operates in near-secrecy, evading congressional oversight and making it almost impossible for families, their lawyers, and our casework teams to locate their loved ones.” Menendez said Democrats had “known for months that the Trump Administration has been abusing its ability to hide flights from public view. It was never intended to let ICE hide deportation flights from public scrutiny. That’s why we led over 30 of our colleagues in demanding answers from the FAA back in May, but the Administration continues to stonewall us.” He warned: “When Democrats take back the Majority next year, we will launch aggressive oversight into these secret flights and end ICE’s private flight secrecy once and for all.”

Fetaine Seddighzadeh of Human Rights First’s ICE Flight Monitor said “since late September, all ICE Air aircraft that previously showed visible tails became blocked on FlightAware and Flightradar24.” She said that as of 5 November, “the vast majority” of ICE flights could no longer be followed by their callsigns. Without posted flight plans, she said, monitors did not know where removal and domestic transfer flights were headed until the flight path was complete.

LADD affects commercial trackers such as FlightRadar24 and FlightAware that use FAA data, but Martin Strohmeier, a board member of the OpenSky Network who researched aviation privacy at the University of Oxford, said it “does nothing even against a casual observer, since many other aircraft trackers, including OpenSky or ADS-B Exchange, do not use FAA data and rely purely on crowdsourced ADS-B data which shows the aircraft.” Strohmeier said many charter aircraft operators put their aircraft on the list for various reasons.

Seddighzadeh said tracking the flights had become “an incredibly laborious process” at more than 70 ICE flights a day. The monitor’s work increased threefold since the previous year, reaching more than 2,000 ICE flights per month. She said real-time tracking may provide an early, and sometimes the only publicly visible, clue that a transfer or deportation is underway. “LADD blocks are the Trump administration’s latest attempt to further obscure its cruel deportation campaign,” Seddighzadeh said, calling it “this opaque and lawless system that has systematically violated due process, separated families, and uprooted thousands of people’s lives from communities across the country.”

The $1.28 billion order was issued under a 2024 framework agreement that ICE awarded to CSI after receiving seven bids. The staffing deal was not competitive, and a $562 million order placed with CSI in February 2025 was also reportedly noncompetitive. Rival bidders contested the framework agreement. In February 2025, Classic Air Charter challenged CSI’s award at the U.S. Court of Federal Claims after what its complaint called five rounds of bid-protest litigation, arguing that ICE had evaluated CSI’s bid wrongly and allowed the company to cure defects in its quotation.

ICE staff recommended terminating CSI’s award and cancelling the solicitation, and government lawyers told the court that the decision belonged to the deputy homeland security secretary. ICE terminated the agreement in April 2025 because of the protest at the court, according to the federal award record.

ICE moved to reinstate the agreement in May. Internal ICE emails released to the ACLU of Colorado under the Freedom of Information Act show the request awaiting approval at the top of DHS. An ICE contract specialist wrote on 14 May 2025: “The ICE Air reinstatement was added back to the list as awaiting S2 approval (though I hear it’s actually S1 now for this one).” S1 was DHS shorthand for the secretary, then Kristi Noem, whose office had to approve contracts worth more than $100,000.

A tracker of items awaiting the secretary’s approval still listed the reinstatement as “In Review” on 5 June. On 12 June, ICE told the court it was working to implement a proposal that would result in the voluntary dismissal of the protest. Classic Air Charter dropped its challenge on 24 June, and the federal record dates the reinstatement to 25 June. The Guardian contacted the ACLU of Colorado for comment. Noem’s successor, Markwayne Mullin, rescinded the requirement for the secretary’s sign-off on contracts worth more than $100,000 in April.

The expanding DHS relationship with CSI comes as the contractor faces a lawsuit over its role in flights that took Venezuelan men to a prison in El Salvador in March 2025. The Guardian reported on the suit’s commencement in July. The complaint, filed in Washington DC federal court, alleges that CSI “remains the contractor responsible for brokering nearly all ICE Air flights for the Administration.”

On 12 November, CSI asked the court to dismiss the case and move it to Texas. The company argued that ICE, not CSI, retained responsibility for the manifest identifying passengers scheduled for removal, citing the same contract clause. In a sworn declaration, CSI vice-president for government services Andrew Talbott said “ICE, not CSI, determined which individuals would board the planes.” CSI also argued that the complaint did not allege the company knew of the J.G.G. proceedings or that court orders had been communicated to CSI.

Angelina Godoy, director of the University of Washington’s Center for Human Rights, told The Guardian she was “alarmed” by the contract, including its apparent intention to remove ICE flights from tracking websites. “There is no legitimate reason to remove such flights from tracking systems; the only reason to do so is to seek further secrecy for the agency’s operations,” Godoy said. She added: “It’s an affront to democracy and the rule of law for any government to be secretly shuttling people across the globe with no effective oversight or accountability for such operations.”

Godoy also said ICE already had considerable problems putting people onto flights despite federal court orders prohibiting their transfer or deportation. “This would almost undoubtedly get worse if there were also private companies involved in the decisionmaking process, who would have to be notified by ICE of court rulings,” she said. Contractors, she added, “typically argue that Foia does not apply to them.”
