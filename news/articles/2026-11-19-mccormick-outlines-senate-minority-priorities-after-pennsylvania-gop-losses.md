## Gas prices and permitting anchor McCormick's lame-duck agenda

U.S. Sen. Dave McCormick said Thursday that he intends to work with Senate Democrats after their party clinched 56 of the chamber’s 100 seats, even as Republicans absorb an election rout in Pennsylvania and lose congressional power. The result leaves the senator elected in 2024 in the minority for the first time. McCormick said voters told Republicans to focus more on affordability and raised concerns about elected officials’ financial self-interest.

McCormick described the result bluntly: “We got thumped,” Pennsylvania’s junior U.S. senator said. “As a collegiate athlete, when you get beat badly or fail, you need to step back and say, ‘What happened? What did we learn?’” He said the message from voters was that Republicans had not focused enough on the high cost of gas, healthcare and housing.

His first lesson was greater attention to those concerns. “I think lesson one is more focus, more attention … that we heard you and we have a plan to deal with that,” he said. McCormick also said concerns about public corruption or selfishness among elected leaders who benefited from their roles, including through stock trading, had eroded voter confidence in the party.

McCormick said his day-to-day approach would not change much in the minority. “I look for opportunities to get things done with my colleagues in the Democratic Party,” he said. “Even if I disagree with them on 80% of the stuff, I look for the 20%.” He said voters had elected him with an expectation that he would work with whoever was needed to get things done, which was what he had promised. McCormick said he was 100% sure of that expectation.

McCormick pointed to his conservative voting record and his reputation for working in a bipartisan fashion. He said that combination positioned him to pursue common-ground issues intended to make Pennsylvania a place for innovation. He also said, “I think I’m going to be able to continue to do that, and I think that’s a big part of the job.”

Democrats at the state and national levels have identified raising the minimum wage from $7.25 as an affordability priority. McCormick said employers had to offer higher wages to compete and that he was “open-minded” about an increase. McCormick said he thought a minimum-wage increase was not the main thing that would help Pennsylvanians. “I think the thing that we really need to do that’s going to change the landscape is bring down gas prices,” he continued. “We got that message loud and clear in the election.”

During the lame-duck session, when Republicans still held majorities in both congressional chambers until Democrats take power in early January, McCormick identified three priorities. He said he hoped a permitting reform bill would be on President Donald Trump’s desk before the new year, with the measure intended to streamline infrastructure and energy development. Senators also would work to confirm Trump’s judicial nominees and finalize a reconciliation bill funding the military.

McCormick said he was very worried that disagreements in Congress over the Iran War could lead Democrats in particular to use military funding to show their lack of support for operations there. Whether or not people supported those operations, he said, the military needed “the financial support, the resources for our men and women in the military to protect them … to make sure we remain the most lethal military in the world.”

McCormick said he would “likely” be the ranking minority member of the Senate Energy Committee. He had recently dined with the committee’s new chair and said they discussed accelerating nuclear power, small modular reactors and other power sources. “I think Pennsylvania is really uniquely positioned to be a leader in this,” he added, pointing to natural gas and nuclear resources. “We’ve got a lot of assets, a lot of expertise, and I expect we’ll be at the forefront of many of the things that happen.”

McCormick also highlighted Eli Lilly’s $3.5 billion investment in Lehigh County to build an advanced pharmaceutical manufacturing facility. “They could have put this anywhere in 50 states. They chose Pennsylvania,” he said. He called the project an example of Pennsylvania’s focus on advanced manufacturing and reindustrialization, a direction he said he championed, and said attracting investments of that kind required bipartisan support.

McCormick said support from Gov. Josh Shapiro and U.S. Sen. John Fetterman would strengthen Pennsylvania’s application to host the new U.S. Space Academy. A West Point graduate, McCormick said, “We think … southwestern Pennsylvania has the talent, technology and industrial strength to help America win the race for space dominance.” He added, “We have the strength to really have a great facility, or really ecosystem, to train the next generation of leaders.”

The proposed school would be the nation’s sixth service academy and was expected eventually to enroll up to 4,000 students. The site, adjacent to Pittsburgh International Airport, is paired with a $285 million investment from the commonwealth. McCormick said Pennsylvania’s proximity to other major cities and higher-education opportunities made it an attractive location.

McCormick described Pennsylvania as a purple state and said its political balance was a benefit to the Space Academy application. “It’s not a political decision, but it’s certainly a benefit,” he said. He added that the proposal was being advanced with representation from both Democrats and Republicans.
