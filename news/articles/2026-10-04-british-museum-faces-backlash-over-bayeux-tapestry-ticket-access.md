## One Aldwych guests and Palantir's Thiel entered without joining public queue

The 11th-century Bayeux Tapestry, a 230-foot embroidered cloth depicting the Norman conquest of England in 1066, is on display at the British Museum in London in its first return to England in about 1,000 years. The exhibition has drawn record demand — and widespread criticism over how that demand is being allocated.

When tickets went on sale ahead of the September opening, the museum's online queue grew to over 80,000 people, the longest in the institution's history, according to the Wall Street Journal. Sasha Mills, a 25-year-old postgraduate art history student, set her alarm for 8 a.m. to log on early. "I was like, 'OK, so there's no way I'm ever going to get these tickets,'" she told the Journal.

The scramble drew comparisons to a Taylor Swift concert ticket sale. It also drew a sharper criticism once it became clear who was getting in without waiting.

One Aldwych, a luxury hotel near the museum that is among its sponsors, offered Bayeux Tapestry viewing packages to guests staying at the property, where rates reach roughly $1,100 per night. The package promised viewing at any date or time of the guest's choosing.

"That's not what this is all about. The Bayeux Tapestry should be for everyone," Mills said.

Helena Dollimore, the parliamentary representative for Hastings, told the Guardian: "How can it be right that some are able to pay their way in while others have to scramble for a chance to see this piece of our heritage?"

The British Museum defended the hotel allocation, noting it had also reserved tickets for children and rolled out tapestry-related school programs.

Controversy intensified in August when the Guardian reported that Palantir co-founder Peter Thiel had received a private viewing of the tapestry before it opened to the public. Thiel had no public record of donations to the museum at the time of the report, according to the Journal. Author Frank Cottrell-Boyce wrote on X that "history will record that the most significant moment in its visit to the British Museum is that an american oligarch was allowed to see if before the King and Queen or President [Macron] were." Thiel did not respond to requests for comment from the Journal.

The anger spread online. Rebecca Dale, a 28-year-old government worker from Leicestershire, posted her thoughts on Instagram. On a lunch break at work shortly after her Instagram post, she opened her phone to 200 new comments. "I didn't expect people to be so outraged," she told the Journal, adding that the scramble felt like "the Eras tour for history fans." She said the experience highlighted "this uncomfortable feeling of it being a two-tiered system."

The dispute reached Parliament in the first appearance by the new prime minister at Prime Minister's Questions. Andy Burnham was asked about access to the tapestry amid questions about soaring debt, taxes and Russian threats to NATO. "We want the maximum number of kids to have access, to stand in front of that tapestry while it's here in the U.K. It's a major moment in our island's history," Burnham told lawmakers.

Some in the public remain unconvinced the museum is delivering on that commitment. Laura Roberts traveled down from Blackpool with her daughter for a tapestry-less visit to the museum. "It's just not fair is it," she told the Journal, challenging the sponsor argument. "That's not the point, is it? They should be doing it for altruistic purposes."

Standard tickets cost £33 — more than three times the price of viewing the tapestry at its regular home in Bayeux, France. The museum will release a fresh batch of tickets on October 21 for viewings later in the tapestry's 10-month London stay. Competition for the 40-minute, one-way procession past the embroidery is expected to remain intense: a separate showing of a full-scale replica in nearby Reading is sold out through the end of January 2027.

For many Brits, seeing it is akin to a birthright. The tapestry holds a particular position in British public consciousness. It tells, over 230 feet of finely embroidered cloth, the story of the last time England was conquered — the victory of William of Normandy at the Battle of Hastings in 1066. Since its creation by nuns in England, the tapestry has lived mostly in Bayeux, a medieval town in northern France. It has survived French revolutionaries who wanted to use it to cover a wagon and Nazi soldiers who tried to move it to Berlin, according to the Journal. After seven years of diplomatic negotiations, Britain and France reached an agreement to bring it to the British Museum on temporary loan.

"It's an integral story to British identity. That's why people feel an emotional attachment to it," Daisy Dixon, a philosophy of art lecturer at the University of Cardiff, told the Journal.

For Mills, the controversy carries a final twist. "It's not like 'yay, England!' It's literally a record of how we got conquered."
