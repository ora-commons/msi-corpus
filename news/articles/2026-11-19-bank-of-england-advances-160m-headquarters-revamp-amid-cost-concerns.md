## The project pairs estimated £100m annual savings with staff relocations and redundancies

The Bank of England is preparing a £160m renovation of its Grade I-listed headquarters on Threadneedle Street in the heart of London's Square Mile, with work due to begin this month. The project would modernize the neoclassical complex while 3,500 staff continued working on the site. By summer 2028, the plan gives 1,400 staff from the Bank's nearby office at 20 Moorgate three options: relocation to Threadneedle Street, voluntary redundancy or alternative jobs at a new Leeds outpost that is also being refurbished.

The Guardian described the secretive overhaul as a major test of Governor Andrew Bailey's project-management skills and said the Bank's reputation for carefully counting the nation's pennies was at stake. The report said the central bank was keen to avoid delays, budget overruns and echoes of the expensive US Federal Reserve renovation, which left Bailey's former peer Jerome Powell clashing with Donald Trump on camera. It also noted that Bailey may spend his days watching developments in the Strait of Hormuz and US stock markets for signs of shocks to the global economy, while the headquarters project presented a closer management challenge.

Bailey and his senior managers believe the Bank can modernize the Grade I-listed structure and interiors while 3,500 staff remain busy on the site. Some staff hope the neoclassical building will become a Google-like workplace, with friendly office spaces, break-out rooms and in-house cafes. It stands opposite the Royal Exchange and beside Mansion House, and work on its 1.4-hectare, or 3.5-acre, site must proceed while the institution remains in use.

The staffing changes are also intended to finance the overhaul. The Bank says an estimated £100m will be saved each year for three years, with those savings accruing from 450 redundancies and other cost reductions. One staff member told The Guardian: “It’s like there is a leaving party every week.”

The Guardian said the job appeared unusual for Britain's struggling building trade: an overhaul of a globally recognized exterior that would preserve Roman mosaic floors, stone columns and vaulted, ornate ceilings. It has been described as an “incredibly complex project.” Exact floorplans were being kept under wraps because of fear of terror attacks. The building includes a Roman mosaic found during 1930s works beneath a cantilever staircase, and the golden statue of Ariel on the dome was designed by Charles Wheeler and unveiled in 1937. The building also contains an ornate courtroom.

First announced in July 2025, the project was due to begin work this month, but the Bank awarded the building contract to construction company Sir Robert McAlpine only in October, with one month to spare. The Bank says there will be no delays, citing the appointment of architects last November. A project manager, a cost consultant and a firm of engineers also have begun evaluating budgets and timescales. A Bank of England spokesperson said: “As part of our plans to preserve our historic Threadneedle Street office we are considering how best to renovate the site and optimise our running costs. All our procurement processes aim to deliver value for money.”

Bailey's team, based in the Parlours, must assess the cost implications of new fire-safety, accessibility and electrical-testing rules, new plumbing and a more environmentally friendly building with heating based on renewable sources. The Guardian noted that the fire at Notre Dame mid-renovation remained fresh in the memory as the safety requirements were considered. Security is another concern: the source said Bailey would be focused on protecting 400,000 gold bars worth more than £200bn and £100m in bank notes in the vaults from terrorists and thieves while work proceeds.

The Bank's museum, open to the public and tucked into the side of the building facing Bartholomew Lane, adds another design question. Some senior staff wanted it moved into the central quad so the museum's 100,000 annual visitors, mainly schoolchildren, would enter through the main entrance. Others wanted cafe space near the entrance to make the Bank appear more modern and appealing. Vivienne Grafton, the executive director of central operations, told the Architects Journal: “This is an opportunity to breathe new life into a legacy building, not through radical reinvention, but through thoughtful, intelligent transformation.”

The wider architectural plans have not yet been agreed with the City of London Corporation, the local authority, or Historic England, which must sanction the proposed work on a building the report describes as a cornerstone of Britain's financial district. Anna Joynt, a senior architect and heritage expert at the London practice of Allies and Morrison, said it was common for large, complex historic-building projects to begin preparatory work before full consent. She said: “There may be bones of the future scheme in place but the wider scheme may not be developed enough to submit for listed building consent – which needs a lot of detail.” She added: “It’s fairly common to split out simpler early enabling works and get permission for them in advance of a main scheme. It means work can start earlier and save money as the longer you wait the more things cost.”

There has been tangible progress on the scheme. The roof of the main portico is clad in scaffolding to allow repairs, and last year the Bank secured planning permission to overhaul a small section known as the former governor's secretaries offices, where plasterboard walls will be removed and the space opened and modernised. For the rest of the 1.4-hectare site, staff still must wait and see.

The building's history stretches back to the Bank's beginnings in rented premises in 1694 and its move to Threadneedle Street in 1734. A satirical cartoon of William Pitt the Younger gave it the nickname Old Lady of Threadneedle Street. Sir John Soane remodelled it from 1788 to 1833, and the interior was largely demolished and rebuilt in the 1920s. The Bank owns the building. Gordon Brown gave the institution independence to set interest rates in 1997, and George Osborne later assigned it responsibility for regulating the banking sector after the 2008 financial crash.

The Federal Reserve's Washington renovation illustrates the risk the Bank wants to avoid. Delays and cost overruns led Trump to threaten the US central bank with court action. The initial construction budget was $921m, or £696m, in February 2020 and was revised several times before reaching $2bn in December 2024. Work was supposed to be completed in mid-2024 but is now expected to run until December 2027; the final cost is likely to be nearer $2.5bn.

The Guardian compared the Bank scheme with the possible £60bn cost of renovating the Palace of Westminster and said it was more akin to the £80m restoration of Elizabeth Tower, which houses Big Ben. It said Bailey would want to avoid joining Elizabeth Tower as a subject of parliamentary scrutiny after its budget doubled and then rose by a further 20%. The report noted that critics have long accused the Bank of mishandling its core purpose of helping to manage the economy by setting interest rates at an appropriate level.

At present, the project has tangible early progress, an awarded construction contract and an assembled project team, but no final agreement with the City of London Corporation and Historic England on the wider scheme. The Bank's position is that procurement will deliver value for money and there will be no delay. The remaining work depends on consent, budgets and schedules that the project team is still evaluating, leaving staff at the 1.4-hectare site to wait for the full shape of the transformation.
