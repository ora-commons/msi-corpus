## Hegseth says Venezuelan oil deal makes Hormuz a non-factor

Hegseth addressed diplomats and military officials from both countries on Wednesday in Caracas during his visit to Venezuela's interim leaders, and Alejandro Betancourt attended the speech. Betancourt owns North American Blue Energy Partners, the second-largest oil operator in Venezuela, and the company and the U.S. government entered into an agreement to tap the country's vast oil reserves. Trump said in August that Hegseth helped broker a sweeping deal for the United States to take control of some Venezuelan oil reserves.

Hegseth praised the energy deal at the U.S. embassy in Caracas, saying it "makes the Strait of Hormuz a non-factor for the Western Hemisphere." He referenced the Iran war in making that case. The conflict has disrupted global energy supplies through a critical waterway in the Persian Gulf, raising gasoline prices and contributing to the Republican Party's severe losses in this month's midterm elections.

Acting President Delcy Rodríguez, who attended a meeting with Hegseth, said the agreement enables "the energy development of our nations" and "benefits both the people of the United States and the people of Venezuela." Hegseth also praised the "close cooperation" with Rodríguez, saying it allowed officials "to stabilize Venezuela and rid it of narco-terrorists once and for all," a reference to drug traffickers.

Hegseth used the speech to praise the January operation in which U.S. forces captured then-President Nicolás Maduro, telling the audience that Venezuela "has gone from our most aggressive regional threat to a compliant security partner." He called the operation "the single most sophisticated military operation in the history of Latin America" and said it "lasted just 48 minutes."

Adding that "America's best and bravest warfighters solved a 27-year problem for this hemisphere in a matter of hours," Hegseth turned a Maduro catchphrase from last year back on the ousted leader. Referring to Maduro's prediction, Hegseth said the operation ended with "no war, yes, peace."

Hegseth said the United States and Venezuela had signed a deal to reestablish military ties. Few details were provided on what the agreement would entail beyond Hegseth's description that the two militaries would be "training together, partnering together, sharing intelligence, more defense equipment and capabilities together." Speaking separately, Rodríguez said she hoped the countries' military cooperation "can be brought to its highest possible level" to allow them to "address common threats." Hegseth pointed to Venezuela partnering with the United States "to recover its territory from narco-terrorist networks." The Pentagon did not immediately offer further details on the new partnership.

Hegseth's trip was the latest in a series of stops in Venezuela by members of President Donald Trump's Cabinet, a sequence that has underscored U.S. efforts to deepen Washington's influence in the country. Both the Interior and Energy secretaries visited Venezuela earlier this year and focused their efforts on tapping oil reserves and critical minerals. Gen. Francis Donovan, the head of U.S. military operations in Latin America for Southern Command, met interim authorities at least twice this year.

The visit also came as the U.S. military expanded its attention on the region since Trump's return to the White House last year, with the Republican president promising to focus more on the Western Hemisphere and battle Latin American drug cartels. To try to stem the flow of narcotics into the United States and the scourge of fatal overdoses, American forces have attacked boats that the administration says are smuggling drugs in the Caribbean Sea and the eastern Pacific Ocean. At least 240 people have been killed since the administration began targeting those it calls "narco-terrorists" in September 2025, though U.S. officials have released little evidence publicly to support claims that the vessels carried drugs or made an impact on drug availability in the country.

Venezuela's ruling party, which has held onto power since 1999, still leads the country, with Rodríguez in charge. Rodríguez, a Maduro ally, took over after Maduro's capture and, though her court-allowed term expired in July, has continued to function as Venezuela's head of state. Hegseth praised her leadership and thanked her for "working closely and pragmatically and diligently with the United States to stabilize Venezuela."

Opposition leader María Corina Machado recently returned to Venezuela from exile after seven failed attempts that opposition activists claimed were thwarted by the government. U.S. Secretary of State Marco Rubio said the "safe" return was the result of "reconciliation talks" supported by the United States, which he considered the "best opportunity" Venezuela has had in three decades to achieve free and fair elections and a lasting democracy.

The lack of a date for Venezuela's next presidential election has left many Venezuelans conflicted in their view of Trump. Many are relieved that Maduro is no longer president, but many also believe the White House's decision to prioritize reviving Venezuela's oil industry over pro-democracy steps was the wrong approach for meaningful changes in their country.
