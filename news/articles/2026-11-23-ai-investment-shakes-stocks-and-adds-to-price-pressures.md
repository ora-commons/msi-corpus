## Survey finds AI productivity gains remain uneven

All major stock indexes fell 8% or more last week as tech firms led the decline amid investor uncertainty about huge sums plowed into AI. Futures markets pointed to a possible rebound in the shortened holiday week as investors might go bargain hunting, but uncertainty over whether those bets would pay off remained.

NPR’s Scott Horsley said investors were selling stocks even as Anthropic, the company behind the AI chatbot Claude, was raising substantial capital by selling shares to the public for the first time. Horsley said the episode highlighted the uncertainty surrounding the enormous sums being invested in AI.

The National Association for Business Economics released a survey that morning asking firms about their experience with AI. About 1 in 5 businesses said the technology was producing measurable, widespread productivity gains. About half reported some gains, but they were not widespread. About 30% said either they were not using AI or it was too soon to tell what the results would be. Horsley said the findings showed that businesses remained in the early stages of the technology’s effects.

Austan Goolsbee, who heads the Federal Reserve Bank of Chicago, said the sheer size of AI investment was already affecting the broader economy. “It’s fabulous that we are coming up with innovations that might raise the productivity growth in the country and might make us rich,” Goolsbee said. He paired that optimism with caution about major trillion-plus-dollar IPOs, asking what they imply for inflation.

Goolsbee said demand from the AI boom was adding to other price pressures and making inflation “a little bit scarier.” He said anything construction-related costs more because it is competing with AI construction, while products with electronic components, including computer chips, also face higher costs.

Over the longer term, boosters argue AI will help to curb inflation by helping businesses produce more goods and services at lower prices. Goolsbee said that outcome would be welcome, but the Federal Reserve has to address current inflation. He compared the AI build-out with a freeway expansion project in Chicago: “You can dream about how great it’s going to be once the highway is widened. But in the here and now, they shrunk the number of lanes, and so the traffic is worse.”

Goolsbee suggested the Fed may have to raise interest rates to tamp down the resulting inflation. He acknowledged that higher rates might not put the brakes on the AI boom but instead could hit more sensitive parts of the economy, including housing, furniture and appliances.

For consumers, the effects remain uneven. NPR reported that overall spending has remained resilient, although some shoppers have become more cautious and are spending less in the grocery aisle. Other categories have shown less movement. Gasoline and airline tickets are more expensive than they were a year earlier, but NPR reported those increases probably will not stop many people from driving or flying to see friends and family for Thanksgiving.
