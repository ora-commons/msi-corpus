## FBI interview account links a waterboarding scene to Salt Pit

Declassified documents obtained by The New York Times under a freedom of information lawsuit appear to support the possibility that the CIA used waterboarding beyond the two sites and three detainees it has acknowledged. A former CIA contract translator who worked at Salt Pit told an FBI agent in 2012 that he saw a man called “Bahir” strapped to a board tilted downward into water. The Guardian said the account suggests waterboarding may have been more common and may have occurred beyond the two acknowledged “special rendition” sites in Thailand and Poland.

The Guardian reported that the CIA has acknowledged waterboarding three men: Abu Zubaydah, believed to be a high-ranking member of al-Qaida; Khalid Shaikh Mohammed, the suspected architect of the September 11, 2001 attacks; and Abd al-Rahim al-Nashiri, accused of planning the 2000 bombing of a Navy destroyer. The acknowledged sites were in Thailand and Poland. The new account points toward Salt Pit, also known as Detention Site Cobalt, a secret prison north of Kabul.

The documents contain the translator’s account to an FBI agent during 2012. The linguist was a former CIA contract translator who worked at Salt Pit, described as a black site. He said the man he knew as Bahir was strapped to a board tilted downward into water. The session, as described, occurred in a hallway.

The agent’s account stated, “There were approximately three to four people ‘doing this,’” and said the witness “saw the incident in passing but did not participate himself. The waterboard session was in the hallway and there was not a lot of room.” The estimate concerned the people carrying out the session, not the number of people waterboarded.

In the same interview, the translator told the FBI that Bahir was “a relative of Hekmatyar.” The Guardian said the wording seemingly referred to Gulbuddin Hekmatyar, the former Afghan prime minister, rather than establishing a definitive identity.

The account may correspond to Ghairat Bahir, The Guardian reported. He was apprehended in a raid in Islamabad in 2002, appears in CIA records and was subjected to “enhanced” interrogation techniques. The source did not present the correspondence as confirmed.

The translator’s account came from the investigation by John Durham, then a special prosecutor, into the CIA’s use of torture and destruction of waterboarding videotapes from 2008 to 2012. No charges were ever brought. The account thus appears in records generated by a criminal inquiry that ended without prosecution.

The Salt Pit account points to a location north of Kabul and to a man called Bahir, both outside the CIA’s acknowledged list of three men and two sites. The witness was a contractor who said he saw the incident while passing and did not participate. The source described the occurrence as a possibility, and Durham’s inquiry produced no charges.

A Reuters satellite image published with The Guardian’s report depicts a facility known as Salt Pit near Kabul, Afghanistan, and is dated 17 July 2003. The image shows the facility referenced in the interview account, while the transcript provides the reported description of the waterboarding scene.

The documents add a possible location and identity to the CIA’s acknowledged waterboarding record without confirming the reported session. Their principal evidence is the 2012 FBI interview summary, whose account says the translator was passing, the hallway left little room and no charges were brought in the broader investigation.
