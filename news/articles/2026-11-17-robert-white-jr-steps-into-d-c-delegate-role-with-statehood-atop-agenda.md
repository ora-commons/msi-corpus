## Two additional senators would be “a game changer,” White says

Robert White Jr. is stepping into the District of Columbia’s congressional delegate seat as Eleanor Holmes Norton retires after 36 years. The former at-large city council member, 44, told The Associated Press between orientation events on Capitol Hill that his priorities are statehood and improving the district’s economic situation. With Democrats holding unexpectedly large advantages in both chambers, White said the prospect of two additional senators would be “a game changer.”

White said he ran for the seat because of his concern about the need to defend D.C. He was eight when Norton took office and later worked for her before serving on the city council. He is the third delegate since Congress passed the District of Columbia Delegate Act in 1970. Walter Fauntroy was sworn in on April 19, 1971, and Norton was sworn in on Jan. 3, 1991. The last time D.C. had a freshman delegate going through orientation, the Soviet Union still existed, George H.W. Bush was president and the first Gulf War in Iraq had not started.

Congress established limited home rule with congressional oversight in 1973, leaving the District of Columbia semi-autonomous. D.C. delegates can introduce legislation but cannot vote on final passage, a limitation that leaves White dependent on other members of Congress to advance his legislative priorities.

White said statehood was his first priority, a long-elusive goal. “Serious support for D.C. statehood should be a baseline requirement for any Democratic presidential nominee in 2028,” he said. “I think it would be malpractice for Democrats to not get statehood the next time we have the House, the Senate and the White House.”

The House voted in 2021 to grant the city statehood while Democrats controlled Congress and President Joe Biden was in the White House. Republicans used the threat of a filibuster to stall the bill in the Senate. White said he has begun conversations with his new colleagues about how two additional senators — who would almost certainly be Democrats because of the city’s deep-blue politics — would alter the political calculation. He said recent developments would help convince people around the country why statehood should matter. He also said, “This is a time in history because of the redistricting battles, because of what the Supreme Court has done to the Voting Rights Act that people can understand D.C. statehood.”

The city’s economic losses have become a second focus for White. The D.C. Office of Revenue Analysis estimated that D.C. had a net loss of 22,000 jobs in 2025, costing residents $3 billion in annual income. City officials attributed at least part of the losses to the Department of Government Efficiency, or DOGE, which President Donald Trump empowered to slash the bureaucracy.

“We’ve lost some 30,000 federal jobs. Because of those, families are struggling to stay in D.C. Many have left already,” White said. White cited the cost of utilities, gas and groceries as pressures on residents and said, “These are the things that have to be top priorities of Congress, but some specific to D.C.” White also said job losses reverberated through the local economy as former federal workers stopped going out for lunch or drinks after work.

White said he is speaking with incoming committee chairs who will have financial and oversight roles over the city, particularly regarding President Trump’s proposed projects. He wants Democratic leaders to understand why he considers D.C. pivotal to their national strategy. To build relationships with incoming lawmakers, White invited them to attend a Wizards basketball game with him on Monday evening and described a more informal approach to finding common ground.

“I’m asking, who were former staffers like me? Who smoke cigars like me? You know, who goes to church on Sundays with their family like me? That’s the way we build a relationship,” White said.

The proposed projects have personal stakes for White. He started golfing in college and often played at East Potomac Golf Links, an affordable option that became one of his favorite courses. His children have performed at the John F. Kennedy Center for the Performing Arts, and his grandparents are buried at Arlington National Cemetery.

Trump has shut down the Kennedy Center after failing to add his name to the building. His plans include turning East Potomac into a world-class course and ending its reputation as an affordable golfing destination, and constructing a 250-foot triumphal arch in the middle of a traffic circle near the cemetery. The plans are embroiled in litigation. “These are local and national treasures,” White said, adding that they should not be altered.

White said he has not spoken with Trump but is trying to make inroads by talking with D.C. residents who have ties to the White House. He is working especially to arrange a meeting with Stephen Miller, who is now going to be chief of staff.

The relationship between the city and the federal government has long been tense and has been exacerbated since President Donald Trump returned to office two years ago. With the backing of a Republican-controlled Congress, Trump deployed National Guard troops, laid off thousands of federal workers and started reshaping local institutions such as the Kennedy Center.

White criticized the ongoing National Guard deployment, which Trump has said is necessary for the city’s safety, as a waste of money. He also expressed concern about how the troops could be used in the future, particularly after the Jan. 6, 2021 attack on the U.S. Capitol. “We cannot have a little army under the control of the president two years from now, when there’s supposed to be another peaceful transfer of power,” White said.

D.C. also has a new mayor, Janeese Lewis George, who is taking over from Muriel Bowser, who served three terms. White said he talks with Lewis George regularly and that they are aligned about their next steps. He said, “We’re seizing every opportunity for D.C.” White said Democratic control of the House and Senate would make it harder for the president to have impact nationally and internationally. He said that could mean Trump turns his attention to D.C., and that he and Lewis George have to be ready.

White also addressed the significance of the transition for incoming lawmakers. “It is all I can do for my eyes not to well up,” he said. “What I’ve said to other incoming members of Congress, both Democrats and Republicans, if we keep that sense of awe and privilege, we will do the right thing here.”
