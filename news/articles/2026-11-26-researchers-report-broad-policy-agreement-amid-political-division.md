## Immigration, AI regulation and health care draw broad support

In a PBS News interview published Nov. 26, 2026, Lisa Desjardins spoke with Steven Kull and Tatishe Nteta about the gap between the image of a deeply divided public and Americans' policy preferences. Kull, who directs the Program for Public Consultation at the University of Maryland, and Nteta, a professor at the University of Massachusetts Amherst and director of the UMass Poll, said data show Americans often agree on policy even as they disagree over candidates and parties. Nteta focuses on public opinion, race and political behavior.

Kull said many Americans share another point of agreement: "they're not being listened to." Kull also said Americans believe that if Congress listened to the people more, it would find more common ground. He said his research found "there's a tremendous amount of bipartisan common ground among the American people on a wide range of issues." Americans may disagree about which candidates and parties to support, he said, but that conflict does not extend to many policy questions.

Desjardins said the data show agreement on many difficult topics spanning more than 100 policies. Nteta said UMass polling data also showed shared views on AI, corruption and a pessimistic view of the economy. On those issues, he said, "Americans really do speak in one voice."

Immigration was among the areas where Kull said there is bipartisan agreement on specific action. The policy he described would deport people who commit crimes. Kull said most undocumented immigrants have been in the country a while and are not criminals. For that group, he said, there is agreement on a path to legal status if they pay back taxes and a penalty and can remain. He also cited support for employers checking legal status and for more work visas so employers can meet worker demand.

Nteta said "there's clear consensus on Americans' distaste for the construction of AI data centers in their local communities." He said majorities across Republicans, Democrats and independents, as well as racial, class and generational groups, oppose local data-center construction. Those majorities see few material benefits for their communities or themselves and anticipate high costs, whether environmental or in the form of higher utility bills. Nteta described the opposition as a reflection of growing distaste for the AI industry more generally.

Kull also found support for a federal agency to regulate artificial intelligence, particularly when the technology is used in critical decisions such as determining who receives health insurance or gets hired. He said there is very strong support for regulating the use of deepfakes, particularly in political campaigns.

Health care produced another area of agreement, Nteta said, as Americans viewed the amount they pay for insurance and the precarity of their coverage as continuing problems in what he called a post-Obamacare America. Nteta said people want the federal government, and even state governments, to act on insurance costs and coverage.

Kull identified several policy responses with support in his surveys. They included providing access to Medicare for people ages 55 to 64, offering a public option and having the government regulate drug pricing more. He said respondents opposed reducing Medicaid spending, and some supported increasing it. Kull said those options would make a difference in health care costs.

Nteta said the narrative that Americans are hopelessly divided begins with institutions such as the U.S. Congress, where partisan conflict is high, and with social media, which emphasizes conflict. Kull said Americans believe there is more common ground among the public than exists in the political system, and he said "they're annoyed by it." He acknowledged that people still get pulled into partisan conflict and said candidates actively work them up by focusing on areas of division.

Kull and Nteta both said Americans want the incoming Congress to work together across partisan and ideological lines and find areas of agreement. Desjardins asked whether politicians could do so under the divided government expected in January.

Kull said he could not be terribly optimistic, citing substantial investment in polarized positions and special interests that prod politicians to take them. He said elected officials nevertheless have the option of orienting themselves more closely to the public, which he believes would produce a positive response. If political leaders would "calm down a bit and really consult the people," he said, they could overcome many conflicts and solve some of the country's problems.

Nteta said he was more optimistic. He argued that the recent midterm election showed Americans want real solutions to the economic challenges they face. He pointed to support for the Democratic Party over Republican incumbents across different electoral offices as evidence that officeholders needed to act, or voters could remove them in favor of candidates willing to address those problems.
