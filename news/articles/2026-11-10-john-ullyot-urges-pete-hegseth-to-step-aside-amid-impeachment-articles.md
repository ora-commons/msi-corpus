## Ullyot raises Signal sharing and a reported double strike as possible crimes

Former Pentagon spokesman John Ullyot urged Pete Hegseth to lower the temperature or step out of the way as articles of impeachment were filed against the defense secretary. In an NPR interview with Steve Inskeep published November 10, 2026, Ullyot said a cabinet secretary who is truly loyal to Trump and becomes a liability should step out of the way, because he is becoming baggage for the president. He argued Trump deserved better than a bombastic defense secretary heading into the final two years of his presidency, when, unlike the previous two years, robust oversight of the secretary and the Department of Defense was expected.

Inskeep introduced Ullyot as a longtime government spokesman who served in the White House during Trump's first administration and briefly as a senior Pentagon spokesman under Hegseth, and who has since been a critic of the administration.

In the news conference recording Inskeep aired, Hegseth said: "For the first time in American history, a cabinet secretary overseeing America's military is being attacked not for losing conflicts with our enemies, but for winning them." Ullyot rejected the argument. Troops, he said, follow lawful orders or what they believe are lawful orders, so they are not the people investigators would pursue. The focus, Ullyot said, should remain on Hegseth.

Ullyot said Hegseth, once again a very divisive figure in his view, hides behind the troops and portrays criticism of him as criticism of the military and Trump. He also said the defense secretary was playing to an audience of one, looking into the camera and talking over reporters to impress the one person who controls his fate, Trump.

Ullyot said Hegseth brought the tone of a Fox News "fire-breathing weekend cohost" to the Pentagon, an approach that served him well as he moved up from correspondent to cohost, and said the news conference was a carbon copy of what Hegseth did whenever he came out publicly. Hegseth fenced off the press from the Pentagon — the first defense secretary to do that, in Ullyot's account. Hegseth was confirmed by the slimmest of margins, 50-50, with the vice president breaking the tie, a point Ullyot noted NPR's Tom Bowman had made. Someone emerging from that "near-death experience" of a confirmation should have pivoted, Ullyot said, asking how — with all his personal baggage — to lower the temperature and make it not about him.

Inskeep noted Trump's praise of Hegseth as an "A-plus-plus-plus" and said Ullyot's book, *The President Deserves Better*, was out that day. Ullyot said the president was not being well served and that his aides needed to tell Trump how to advance his agenda and get things done in the final two years — probably now the job of his new chief of staff, Stephen Miller. Miller, Ullyot said, had a reputation as a fire-breather too, though not quite as much as Hegseth. The question, Ullyot said, was whether the agenda could advance with somebody who would be "investigated top to bottom" if he was not impeached, and somebody who took that bombastic approach.

Ullyot connected Hegseth's situation to Trump's first term, saying — as he wrote in an op-ed — that the president was very much about accountability then, dismissed Tillerson and carried out numerous firings after just one year. Hegseth, Ullyot said, had been a real liability — a lot of baggage and too bombastic — for a military used to very low-profile people like Robert Gates, whom Ullyot called one of the most successful secretaries. Inskeep offered a parallel: President George W. Bush, facing a controversial war, got rid of Defense Secretary Rumsfeld, who resigned, and replaced him with Gates, a more bipartisan figure.

Inskeep relayed Hegseth's argument that if Hegseth broke the law, the troops broke the law, and if he committed war crimes, the troops committed war crimes, and asked whether it was accurate — whether, if Thomas Massie was right that the war was illegal because it lacked proper authority from Congress, everybody in the military was guilty of a crime. Ullyot said no and called the premise bogus. He said the troops were following instructions from their commanders and distinguished the potentially criminal matters involving Hegseth from broader policy disagreements.

Ullyot identified two actions by Hegseth that he said could possibly be criminal. The first was what he said was Hegseth's sharing of classified information with his wife over Signal, even though she had no clearance whatsoever. Ullyot said that was exactly the conduct for which former national security adviser John Bolton was being prosecuted by the Justice Department. He also noted that David Petraeus resigned as CIA director once it came to light that he had shared classified information with somebody who was not authorized.

The second was a reported double-tap strike. Ullyot said Hegseth reportedly ordered the operation personally and quoted the direction as "take them out, take them out right now." Inskeep described the target as a boat that had already been disabled or sunk, while Ullyot framed the operation as a second strike on a boat that had surrendered. Ullyot called the reported conduct absolutely a war crime, said it violated the laws of war and said it could be prosecuted.

Ullyot said responsibility for the reported strike lay at Hegseth's level and that the people who pulled the trigger were not expected to face prosecution because they had reportedly followed the secretary's instructions. Asked by Inskeep whether the practical move was to get out of the way even if Hegseth believed he was innocent, Ullyot said, "Yes, absolutely." He tied his recommendation to loyalty, noting that he had served Trump as a senior adviser during the 2016 campaign, all the way through the first term and a little bit of the second term. Ullyot's conclusion was that a genuinely loyal secretary should lower the temperature and, if necessary, step out of the way.
