## Black turnout reached historic levels across the South

## Measured office results show gains and limits

Democrats made significant inroads in Alabama, Louisiana, Mississippi and Tennessee during the 2026 midterms, changes that would have seemed unthinkable a year earlier, but their gains in the four states were more limited than Democratic gains elsewhere in the country. Republicans retained power in most of the four states. The results included a narrow U.S. Senate win in Mississippi, state legislative flips in places Donald Trump had carried by wide margins two years earlier, and close Republican wins in the Tennessee and Florida gubernatorial races.

Scott Colom defeated Republican incumbent Sen. Cindy Hyde-Smith by a razor-thin margin to become Mississippi's first Black senator since Reconstruction. U.S. Rep. Shomari Figures defeated his Republican opponent in an Alabama congressional district that state lawmakers redrawn earlier in 2026 to favor Republicans. Republicans held the closely contested Tennessee and Florida gubernatorial contests by one or two percentage points. The wins expanded Democratic opportunities without displacing Republican control in most of the four states.

Democrats also improved their performance among white voters. Nationally, the party had its best performance with white voters in years, tying Republicans after not winning white voters in a presidential election since 1964. In the growing urban center of Madison County, Alabama, a majority-white electorate flipped three state House seats and defeated down-ballot Republican incumbents, including the sheriff. In suburban, majority-white Rutherford County south of Nashville, Tennessee, Democrats flipped three state House seats. Kent Syler, a political science professor at Middle Tennessee State University, said many of the campaigns “weren’t on the radar” of the national Democratic party and “won anyway, with little resources.” He expected more recruitment of top-quality candidates and more resources because the election demonstrated an opportunity to win.

Tennessee state Rep. Justin Pearson continued visiting churches and community centers across the congressional district he narrowly lost, thanking voters who supported him and trying to preserve relationships built during his long-shot campaign. Pearson came closer than most people expected. His Republican legislative colleagues had redrawn the U.S. House district earlier in 2026 to break up a longtime Democratic stronghold around the majority-Black city of Memphis and dilute it with Republican-heavy territory, delivering the state another GOP seat.

Pearson lost to Republican Brent Taylor by about 1 percentage point in the newly drawn district. The nonpartisan Cook Political Report said President Donald Trump would have carried it by 21 points. Pearson, who is Black, said, “I was campaigning in communities that were over 60% rural, white, Southern, and we came within one point.” He added, “It is possible to win here.” Pearson also said voters told him they were casting ballots for a Democrat for the first time or registering to vote because they wanted something different. “You knock enough doors, you talk to enough people, you go to enough places, and you’re hearing the same stories,” he said.

## Turnout evidence separates organizing from redistricting

Midterm turnout is typically much lower than in presidential elections, but 2026 reached historic levels even in the South. In states such as Alabama, Democrats increased their vote share in every county; in Alabama, Democratic turnout nearly doubled from 2022 levels while Republican votes declined slightly. Nearly 1 million Mississippians voted, rivaling presidential-election years. Black voters cast one-third of the ballots in Mississippi and Louisiana. In Georgia, turnout among Black voters increased more than 11% over the 2022 midterms while white turnout shrank. Stateline reported that young, diverse voters showed up for the midterms as older Trump voters stayed home.

Activists said the high turnout was built on decades of voter outreach and community organizing, particularly in Black communities. Separately, activists and experts told Stateline that recent congressional redistricting helped prompt unusually large turnout among Black voters. In the activists' account, Republicans' gerrymandering push, begun in earnest earlier in 2026, supplied the catalyst.

Anneshia Hardy, an Alabama voting rights activist who studies Southern politics, said the newly drawn maps gave casual voters a tangible example of how state and national leaders could wield political power and take it away from constituents. Louisiana political consultant Mary-Patricia Wray, who has worked on statewide campaigns for Republicans and Democrats, said turnout surged especially in Louisiana parishes, the state's equivalent of counties, where congressional lines had been redrawn. Louisiana was the state at the center of the Supreme Court's Callais case. “Voters are very much aware that when we elect our legislature, it will be in charge of redrawing our maps again,” Wray said. “I think that awareness truly did drive Black turnout in Louisiana specifically, and I think it did in any state where there was a potential to have an impact on congressional maps.”

## Court ruling and redistricting changed the electoral context

The redistricting debate unfolded in a region that had been a center of the voting-rights fight 60 years earlier. The U.S. Supreme Court's decision in Louisiana v. Callais earlier in 2026 all but nullified a provision in the federal 1965 Voting Rights Act that required states to draw electoral maps giving racial minority voters an opportunity to elect their chosen candidates. Republican state legislators redrew congressional maps to favor GOP candidates in Alabama, Florida, Louisiana, North Carolina, Tennessee and Texas, while Democrats tried gerrymandering in California and Virginia. Pearson said the redistricting decision was pivotal for Southern voters, and for Black voters in particular. “I think if that had not happened, we would not have seen a number of the victories we saw,” he said. “To have such an ignited and excited Black Democratic voting base, I think the attack on voting rights was the key issue that really awakened people’s attentiveness and conscientiousness.”

## Competing assessments weigh the durability of gains

Black voting advocates in the South said the midterm surge was not just a ‘blue wave.’ One Black voting advocate said, “It’s interesting, but it’s largely a fluke and there will be a reversion to the mean.” Southern Democrats saw opportunities for lasting, even generational change in Colom's victory, Figures's win and the close gubernatorial races in Tennessee and Florida. The election put Black politicians in state and federal offices, which those Democrats viewed as a possible foundation for broader political change.

The results came in a region full of Republican legislative supermajorities, where Trump remained popular and where few Democrats had held statewide offices in more than a decade. Many experts were skeptical that Alabama or Mississippi were on a path to resemble neighboring Georgia, now a solid battleground state where Democrats won every statewide seat on the ballot in 2026. The experts said the results reflected opposition to Trump more than an embrace of Democrats. University of Louisiana at Lafayette political science associate professor David Hughes, who studies the politics of the American South, said, “I know Democrats get excited in places like Alabama and Louisiana and Mississippi when Democrats win elections.” He said voters were primarily motivated by anger over issues including high costs, the Iran war and congressional redistricting.

Hardy said the results did not mean the Deep South had suddenly become a different place politically. “But it does mean that we need to pay closer attention to what’s happening beneath the statewide results,” she said. “The political landscape is not nearly as static as our electoral map suggests.” She said national Democrats could learn from the South about building coalitions and creating political opportunities where they lack power. Quoting Atlanta rapper Andre 3000, half of hip-hop duo Outkast, she said, “‘The South got something to say,’ and I hope folks are listening.”

## Louisiana and Mississippi test the gains in 2027

Louisiana and Mississippi are two of just three states — Kentucky is the third — that will elect state offices and legislative seats in 2027. Democratic near-misses in those states are already fueling speculation about the next election cycle. Democrat Jamie Davis lost a Louisiana Senate race to Republican U.S. Rep. Julia Letlow by about 25,000 votes, a margin below two points, and is reportedly considering a run for governor.

The political climate remains unsettled. Hughes said, “Neither party can take anything for granted. The electorate is just ticked off.” He added, “If Democrats don’t find a way to deliver (on their promises), I have confidence that the voters will turn them out of office down the line.” Several experts told Stateline that more moderate candidates who avoided national disagreements over wedge issues tended to do better. Wray said, “I saw a preference for moderate incumbents over extreme incumbents at every level of the ballot,” for both parties.

Hughes said states such as Alabama would need a greater demographic shift — voters moving in from other states and bringing their politics with them, plus continued growth in turnout among Black voters and other minority groups — to create the conditions that propelled Georgia or North Carolina to swing-state status. Southern Democrats said they were seeking more investment from national groups, pointing to the tens of millions poured into races in Texas and Georgia and arguing that even a fraction could make a difference in state houses and governors' mansions across the region and, from there, influence control of the nation's highest offices. Pearson said he was working to create a political action committee to continue building on the Democratic successes and near-successes of the 2026 midterms. “I believe the litmus test for America’s democracy is what happens in the South,” he said. “We can perfect this American experiment if we invest in the South and I’m more hopeful and optimistic about us doing it than ever before.”
