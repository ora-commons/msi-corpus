## ReFED says offering different portion sizes could cut 2.35 million tons annually

Restaurants are converting food waste into savings and saleable products as operating costs rise and margins tighten, according to a Wall Street Journal review of creative waste-reduction strategies. ReFED, a nonprofit that works to prevent food waste, estimates that U.S. restaurants waste 12.5 million tons of food worth $156 billion every year.

The financial impact extends beyond the cost of ingredients. “For restaurants, it isn’t just about the actual ingredient that’s being wasted,” ReFED Executive Director Sara Burnett said. “They also need to think about the labor costs and the time it took to prepare and serve that product, not to mention the hard costs of the whole facility.” Burnett said food waste has a significant financial impact on restaurant operators.

Plant City X pairs a vegan drive-through in Warwick, R.I., with a 20-foot-long black shipping-container dining room where microbes process discarded food. The microbes turn discarded chickpea wraps and shiitake-bacon BLTs into a liquid fertilizer called “Soil Sauce,” which customers can buy from the restaurant to spread on their gardens. Plant City founder Kim Anderson said kitchen and front-of-house scraps, leftover food and compostable packaging all enter the process, which produces fertilizer in a few days. “We’ve saved 450 tons of trash from the local landfills, and we’ve saved money,” Anderson said.

Donating excess food to food banks has been a standard waste-reduction practice for chefs and restaurant owners for years. As costs rise and margins tighten, some businesses are adopting more specialized approaches, including repurposing ingredients, preserving food, changing portion sizes and using AI to forecast demand.

At Providence, a fine-dining restaurant in Los Angeles, bar director Kim Stodel turns kitchen scraps into cocktail ingredients. He made a cordial from passion-fruit rinds left over from sorbet, then put the cordial in his $100 “electric margarita,” which he mixes for diners at their tables. “Sustainability does require more labor,” Stodel said, “but if you’re reusing the ingredients from something you’re already making, it’s a no-brainer. You can make more money at less cost, and waste less resources.”

Blue Square Pizza, a Massachusetts chain based in Hopkinton, is using an AI agent called Claude Cowork to forecast demand. CEO and founder Troy Sproul said the agent analyzes restaurant data to predict how much dough to prepare, how many meatballs to order and whether the chain will run out of cheese on busy Friday nights. It took a few weeks for the predictions to improve as more data arrived and Sproul tweaked the prompts.

Sproul now schedules automatic Monday updates showing staff what to order and how much to prepare. He also uses Claude Cowork to draw up daily schedules that tell staff when to take dough out of storage. Sproul said the system has reduced Blue Square Pizza's dough waste by 40% and its expired-meatball waste by 25% compared with the chain's level before using AI.

ReFED says 70% of restaurant food waste comes from diners leaving food on their plates. The nonprofit estimates that offering different portion sizes could reduce waste by 2.35 million tons annually while saving restaurants $547 million. Shake Shack's Good Fit Menu and Olive Garden's Lighter Portions Menu offer size choices, while Denver breakfast chain Snooze Eatery offers cottage cheese or salad as an alternative to hash browns.

Snooze co-founder Adam Schlegel said customers are happy to take extra food home to eat later, making him wary of reducing portions across the board. “We definitely are seeing the trend of people eating smaller portions,” he said. “But I’d still say the majority of diners want value for what they spend.”

FiNO, a Denver restaurant that opened in March and originally served dinner until 11 p.m., found another approach in smaller late-night meals and leftover wine. Owner Steven Waters said late-night customers wanted smaller meals, while unsold food and wine opened for by-the-glass service were going to waste; opened wine also loses flavor over time. The restaurant's late-night happy hour offers lower prices for smaller portions and wine that would otherwise go unused that night. A half-sized pasta portion and a glass of leftover wine cost $20, about a 50% discount from regular prices. “We’re seeing less waste now, and the people that come in are grateful to have another option, something that’s a bit more on the healthy side,” Waters said.

At Hestia in Austin, Texas, chefs dry and cure fish bodies for months, then combine them with old bread, soybeans and koji, a traditional Japanese mold culture, to make a seasoning similar to soy sauce. The process takes about a year and produces a condiment that chefs use regularly at minimal cost.

Hestia also repurposes flavorful mushroom stubs. Keith Rzepecki, vice president of culinary at Hestia's parent company, Emmer & Rye Hospitality Group, said the restaurants have about 300 pounds of mushroom miso at any given point. Chefs clarify the miso, set it with gelatin and serve it alongside scallops. “Preservation definitely saves you money,” Rzepecki said, “but more importantly, it gives you a better pantry to make your food stand out.”

Under a “nose to tail” approach at Emmer & Rye Hospitality Group's restaurants, pork skins become chicharrones or add a sticky richness to carnitas, bones become stock, and fats are turned into mayonnaise or soap. Rzepecki said the group makes the soap in bulk and distributes it to all its restaurants for internal use. He said the company keeps it out of guest restrooms so vegan or vegetarian diners are not uncomfortable.

Across the examples, purchasing forecasts, menu changes, preservation and by-product sales reduce the amount of food restaurants throw away. Operators also report lower costs. Soil Sauce and tableside cocktails turn discarded ingredients into goods customers can buy. Hestia's chefs use the fish-and-bread seasoning in dishes, while Emmer & Rye makes soap for internal use across its restaurants.
