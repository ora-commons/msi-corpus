## NGOs warn Afghanistan could run out of groundwater by 2030

A PBS News report published Nov. 23, 2026, documents severe drought leaving families in Afghanistan's northern Bamyan province without clean drinking water and reliable farm income. Special correspondent Leila Molana-Allen and videographer Eric O'Connor traveled to Bamyan, one of the country's poorest regions, where dry fields and contaminated water have left residents struggling to farm, wash and cook.

The drought is compounding a broader economic crisis. By the end of last year, nearly three-quarters of Afghanistan's population were living in poverty, according to the United Nations. Millions of Afghans also faced drought and had no access to clean drinking water.

Bamyan was once Afghanistan's agricultural heartland, but swathes of the province are now bone-dry. Yasin, an Afghan farmer speaking through an interpreter, said each of the past five years brought the loss of another field as its soil became dry, brittle and unplantable. The family now has one field left. Yasin said: “We work very hard, but we get no yield. This year we had no harvest at all. Everything we have depends on agriculture.”

Farming methods there are basic, and each field depends on its closest water source, Molana-Allen said. A rudimentary piped irrigation system could distribute water more evenly, but communities have received no support from the government or aid agencies and cannot afford to build one. Neighbors have already left for the city, and Yasin said families may be forced to move if water cannot reach their land another way. U.N. documentation says half of Yakawlang district's residents have left this year, while tens of thousands are leaving central Bamyan.

Further up the hill, Mah Gol still has water for her fields, while other residents are running short. Mah Gol, an Afghan farmer speaking through an interpreter, said other fields are drying and crops are being damaged. She also said she fears what will happen when the drought reaches her area. Women have traditionally farmed alongside men in the region, and the PBS report said work for women has become increasingly scarce under Taliban rule. Mah Gol said: “Things have only recently become like this. Under the previous government, there were job opportunities for men and women. There were schools, and girls could go to school and work.” She also said the lack of money puts pressure on families and causes problems and violence within families.

A park that once gathered Bamyan's women and hosted a women's market is now dying, according to the report. Benazir Ebrahimi, who founded the park, said women are now focused on making sure they and their children do not go hungry rather than finding places for recreation.

More than half of Afghanistan's provinces have faced severe drought in the past year, PBS News reported. Aquifer stores have fallen by more than a quarter, and NGOs warned that the country may run out of groundwater altogether by 2030.

The United Nations says 22 million people in Afghanistan currently need humanitarian assistance. As of mid-2026, only 14% of the $1.7 billion needed to support them this year had been funded. The United Nations also says 3.7 million children are facing acute malnutrition because of poverty and water scarcity.

PBS News reported that some residents spend as much as 30% of their income on clean drinking water, while the average Afghan salary is around $200 a month. Benazir Ebrahimi, speaking through an interpreter, said one bottle of drinking water costs around $2. She said: “Some people can't afford to buy bottled water, so they're forced to use water from the streams, which may be full of germs, dust, and waste.”

The PBS report said clean water was essential for Ebrahimi and her newborn because contaminated water would make her breast milk make the baby sick. Ebrahimi said she has been sick for the past two months because of the water quality. She now tries to use only bottled water, which her family struggles to afford, and said contaminated, untreated water adds financial pressure because medicine and private clinics require money.

The river residents rely on has a pungent smell and dark brown color as its level falls, and its water quality is becoming dangerous, according to PBS News. Many now use it not only to wash clothes but also to drink and cook food. Fatima brings her daughters to the source every day; it takes them an hour to reach it because their closest springs have dried up.

Feces and other pollutants are dumped into the river and flow downstream to people using it, the report said. Fatima, a Bamyan resident speaking through an interpreter, said: “It makes people sick, causing diarrhea and vomiting. People cannot even afford to take their children to a doctor. The water is contaminated and dirty, but we are forced to use it because we have no other choice.” She also said residents regularly catch eye infections.

The PBS report described endemic poverty in Bamyan as among the country's most extreme. It said the long-neglected region has struggled because of discrimination against its largely ethnic minority Hazara population. With the drought cutting off farm work for vulnerable day laborers, the report described the loss of livelihoods as catastrophic.

Mohammed previously worked on a farm and received lodging from the landowner, but the work dried up two years ago. He and his wife, Gulchereh, now live in a small natural cave in the mountains. The report said the damp air gave them lung problems, worsened by black smoke from a rudimentary gas heater during the freezing winter. Mohammed, an Afghan farmer speaking through an interpreter, said he has difficulty breathing, high blood pressure and frequent illness, and no money for treatment. Gulchereh, also speaking through an interpreter, said the moisture made her dizzy and worsened his breathing; she described large scorpions coming out at night, his inability to sleep, side pain and his blood pressure rising before he coughs.

Mohammed walks hours to fetch water and has no earnings, relying on scraps and what neighbors can provide. He said: “Some days, we can find something to eat, and some days we can't. Sometimes, I go to other people's houses and ask them for food.” Gulchereh said the couple had received three pieces of bread from people at the market the previous day and had only tea and a piece of dried bread at home. Their three children, who are trying to care for their own families, cannot help them, the report said. Gulchereh said: “Even if we die here, our children would not know about it.” The PBS account ended with Gulchereh saying she did not know which she feared more, dying alone or continuing to live in those conditions.
