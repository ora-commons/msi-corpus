## EPA reports no lead release as independent monitoring continues

The U.S. Army carried out two controlled detonations in Iowa on November 25, 2026, destroying roughly 10,000 pounds of lead azide after a federal court cleared the disposal. The Army said the operation eliminated the nation's entire stockpile of the bomb-making chemical. It had become unstable after decades of storage and, according to the Army, posed a risk of a spontaneous accidental explosion as temperatures dropped.

The Army said controlled blasts were the only safe way to remove the material. The central concern was whether the detonations could propel toxic lead dust into the air and threaten health and the environment. The explosions could be seen and felt for miles across the Iowa countryside.

Officials called the operation a success. Maj. Gen. Eric Shirley said, "Every indication is that all of the munitions that were employed absolutely reduced the lead azide threat." The Army said all five tons were destroyed, but officials did not take questions from reporters.

Before the blast, the Army predicted that a lead plume could drift as far as 100 miles southwest into Missouri. The blast was delayed several times while officials waited for the right weather conditions. At the site, the Army said it had built mounds of earth, applied layers of spray foam and prepared canisters filled with 100,000 gallons of water to trap potentially toxic particles. Robert Cekada said the mitigation efforts and the explosive ordnance disposal design plan drove the blasts into the ground and along a planned channel to minimize dispersion. Missouri's governor said the mitigation efforts appeared successful.

After the operation, FEMA Administrator Cameron Hamilton said, "We're finding a 0.0 detection level of lead in the atmosphere." Federal officials said the dust risk was under control, and the EPA concluded that no lead was released.

The official findings did not settle the concerns raised after the operation. The University of Iowa and the University of Illinois were collecting independent readings, which Eliza Billingham of Iowa Public Radio said would take a few days to return. Environmental groups that had tried to block the detonation in court remained unconvinced and demanded a full accounting of the blast's effects.

For days before the detonation, residents had been urged either to evacuate or remain indoors. The Army recommended that people within a five-mile radius might want to leave, but some residents left their homes while others stayed and found the detonation less loud than expected. Billingham was about 10 miles away in Burlington, where she saw people running errands and someone walking. She said someone passing through probably would not have known the detonation was taking place, and residents who had left began returning around late morning.

The response varied with proximity and experience. Billingham said Middletown residents had been in communication with the Army since at least October and were accustomed to occasional explosions and houses shaking from the nearby ammunition plant. People farther away tended to be more concerned because they felt they had not been brought into discussions about the detonation and possible public-health effects. The potential impact zone had also widened over the preceding few weeks, adding to the alarm.

Residents also differed over cleanup. Lucy Giehl of Burlington said the ammunition plant spokesperson had said people could hose everything down or wipe it down with a baby wipe, without mentioning PPE. "So, yes, people are completely unprepared," she said. Daren Morris of Middletown said he believed much of the material had been contained, though he said there was probably some lead dust to clean up.

The report said inhaling airborne lead can harm the brain, nervous system and other organs, and can cause developmental damage in young children. The extent of any lead outside the controlled blast channel remained unresolved while the universities completed their monitoring.

Billingham said the back-and-forth over whether the detonation would occur had hurt trust between Eastern Iowa and the military. She said many people felt excluded from conversations about public-health issues that might affect them, producing angst and frustration. Three states said they were monitoring the effects of the blast.
