## Three storm days accounted for about 20% of annual microplastic deposition

A study by Virginia Tech biologist Austin Gray and his team suggests that Hurricane Helene contributed to unusually high microplastic deposition at the Highlands Biological Station in North Carolina during three storm days. Jason Love, the station's associate director, said, "During those days of the storm, we were getting a total over 10,000 microplastics per square meter." He said the three days accounted for about 20% of the microplastics the team usually collects over a full year.

NPR host Sacha Pfeiffer introduced the report by presenting meteorologist Nick Gilmore with member station Radio IQ. Communities in western North Carolina were still picking up the pieces more than two years after Helene brought huge amounts of rain. Gray's team had collection buckets in place at Highlands before the storm hit in 2024, allowing researchers to gather data before, during and after the Category 4 hurricane.

Before Helene hit in 2024, Gray's team was already looking into how microplastics end up in streams and waterways in parts of western North Carolina. Gray said, "Literally, it was a perfect storm of, well, we got our samplers already out."

Gray said the opportunity to study microplastic deposition from a tropical system in an understudied area was practically unheard of in that part of the world.

Gray said the only earlier study in the United States or, more broadly, the Western Hemisphere had been of 2021's Hurricane Larry, a Category 1 storm. Much of the work on microplastics from hurricanes and typhoons has been done in Asia and other eastern parts of the world, he said. Helene was a Category 4 and a much larger storm event.

At the station, researchers poured samples through a filter that caught all the microplastics and other debris from the samples. They then put the collected material under a petri dish and examined it under a microscope, Love said.

Nick Gilmore, a meteorologist with NPR member station Radio IQ, reported for NPR that it was estimated trillions of gallons of water fell on Georgia, Tennessee, Florida and the Carolinas during Helene. At Highlands, Love said the rain from the storm caused unusually high deposition.

Gray said microplastics can come from water bottles, shopping bags and tires as larger pieces of plastic break down because of sunlight, wind and ocean waves. He said clashing waves can project fragments back into the atmosphere, and the same phenomenon occurs in streams and river networks.

Microplastics are tiny, microscopic fragments of plastic that end up in the air, water and people as larger pieces break down. Gray said more research is needed to understand the effect of microplastics on human health.

Love described the problem as human-caused. He said, "We can't blame this on the bears." Love also said, "And until we have serious discussions about plastic production and single-use plastics and fast fashion, the problem is not going to go away. It's actually probably going to get worse."

Love said plastic production increases each year and human-caused climate change is making storms like Helene more intense. He said the combination means future storms will likely continue to be major depositors of tiny plastic material.
