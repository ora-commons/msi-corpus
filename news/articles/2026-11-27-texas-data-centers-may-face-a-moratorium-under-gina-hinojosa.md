## Texas lawmakers will address data centers when their session begins in January

Democratic Gov.-elect Gina Hinojosa is set to take a different approach to Texas data centers after defeating Republican Gov. Greg Abbott, who had made the state one of the country's most welcoming states for new data centers. NPR's Leigh Walden reported from Austin that new state lawmakers will be sworn in in January and that data centers will be on the legislative session's agenda.

Any change to Texas data-center rules could affect developers and rural communities in a state that has welcomed the industry. The immediate questions include whether new projects should face a pause and whether operators must supply their own water and power and offset emissions.

Under Abbott, Texas declared itself the epicenter of AI development in November of last year. The governor also signed laws exempting large data centers from sales taxes on the equipment they buy.

During his summer campaign for a fourth term, Abbott said it was time for Texas to start pushing back against AI data centers trying to build in neighborhoods. As public backlash grew, he imposed a pause on all new data-center projects while the state studied their impact; that pause has already expired.

Right after her victory, Hinojosa said on CNN, "Right now, what has happened is data centers have moved into mostly rural communities, and there are no rules and laws to protect Texans, and Texans are being taken advantage of."

Kate Goodrich, a lobbyist for the tech industry at K&L Gates, an Austin law firm, said in the days after the election that most people she spoke with on both sides were still absorbing the change. Her clients range from individual data-center developers to multibillion-dollar companies such as Microsoft.

John Hall, CEO and president of Houston Advanced Research Center, which focuses on environmental policy, expects Hinojosa to impose a moratorium and then new regulations. Hall said, "Establish it in policy that data centers should have to bring their own water and bring their own power. But in addition to that, offset their emissions."

Austin McCarty, also a tech lobbyist at K&L Gates, said some clients are comfortable with more regulation because it could build trust and improve the industry's reputation. He said, "It's being dragged down by some folks that are not as prepared as the good actors who have really put in a lot of work to show that they're here to be a part of a really important ecosystem here in Texas."

Torrie Martin, a horse rancher outside San Marcos in Central Texas, has attended numerous local-government meetings to oppose a proposed data-center site near her ranch. Martin said, "I am optimistic, but, you know, we have to hold people accountable and pay attention to what is happening."

Martin also believes the data centers' unpopularity could lead to laws and bipartisan agreements. She said people across the political spectrum are "fighting the same fight," which gave her confidence in speaking for ranch lands and agriculture in Texas.

New state lawmakers will be sworn in January and then begin a legislative session with data centers on the agenda. The issue is also playing out after the midterms in Kansas and Georgia, where incoming governors have promised to pause data centers. Walden noted that the size of the Texas economy often makes the state a precedent for how other states do business.
