## ICE later finalized a $16.7 million contract for 6,000 pairs

Associated Press reporter Ryan J. Foley said ICE has finalized a $16.7 million contract to purchase 6,000 pairs of electric-shock gloves and plans to complete the rollout in 2027. In a letter to AP readers, Foley described how he found the procurement while reviewing government spending and purchasing databases in 2026.

Foley said he began covering ICE in 2026 to track how the agency was spending the unprecedented $45 billion Congress had approved for its expansion. He spent time every day digging through government spending and purchasing databases for potentially newsworthy contracts and programs.

In August, Foley found a notice saying ICE was planning to spend up to $20 million to buy “conductive distraction and de-escalation devices” known as G.L.O.V.E., which stands for Generated Low Output Voltage Emitter.

Foley had already encountered the devices during a 2023 reporting trip to a police training and technology conference in Las Vegas. He attended the conference as part of an investigative project on police custody deaths and saw the products demonstrated. Foley described the gloves as delivering a powerful electric shock when touched to a suspect’s skin to gain compliance.

Some observers saw the gloves as promising alternatives to Tasers, while others questioned whether they would be effective or acceptable to the public, Foley wrote. He said he remembered the products while reviewing the ICE notice and knew that a plan to arm thousands of ICE officers would be newsworthy. The proposal came while ICE faced allegations of excessive force and misconduct across the country.

Foley based his story on the notice, sources familiar with the gloves and groups that monitor ICE’s use of force. He said the reaction to that reporting was significant. Democratic lawmakers demanded that ICE cancel the plan or at least explain how the devices would be managed to prevent abuses. The White House border czar defended the gloves as a way to prevent fatal shootings.

Nebraska’s largest school district also acknowledged that school police officers had been armed with the gloves and had used them on a handful of students. The district ended the practice amid what Foley described as a firestorm of criticism.

A previously secret Army study also surfaced, Foley wrote. The study showed that the shocks caused intense pain and fear among participants.

Foley said the reporting would continue. “It’s a story we’ll keep following,” he wrote.
