## Kalshi has produced more than 35 TV commercials in six months

Kalshi recruited Martha Stewart to star in a humorous television commercial warning users of the prediction market against insider trading, according to the Wall Street Journal's WSJ Leadership Institute. In the spot, Stewart encounters a jar on a kitchen counter labeled “confidential information” and exclaims, “We won’t be using that ingredient!” Stewart was imprisoned in 2004 for lying about a stock sale.

The ad is one of more than 35 television commercials Kalshi has run in the last six months, with stars from Timothée Chalamet to Shane Gillis. That output comes from Es Muy Good, a four-person agency founded only last December. After successfully rushing out a “Trade on Anything” commercial starring NFL great Terrell Owens in two weeks early this year, the agency quickly produced a “Grease” movie spoof featuring Greece-born NBA star and Kalshi investor Giannis Antetokounmpo.

Co-founder Steve Gonzalez said, “Then, after that, they just kept giving us more work.” He said, “At Kalshi, they don’t have time to sit on an idea or overthink things.” The newsletter described Kalshi and Es Muy Good as churning out television commercials at a relentless pace, operating with shared Slack channels and 1 a.m. phone calls.

When the newsletter author asked WSJ Leadership Institute’s Katie Deighton whether that pace was peculiar to Kalshi, she said: “Kalshi may be an extreme example, but practically every marketer I speak to lately realizes they need to move faster. Nothing’s cringier than when a brand jumps on a trend weeks after everybody stopped talking about it.” Deighton said avoiding that fate means removing as many middle-management decisions as possible.

In a separate passage, Wall Street Journal newsletter author Nat Ives wrote, “Earlier this year, I wrote about how smaller agencies appear to be doing well, in part because they lack a bloated middle.” Ives also wrote, “Yesterday, we heard that Verizon’s Leslie Berland is bringing more work in-house.” Ives said other brands are trying hybrid models that “might see them call up consulting firms to help with strategy, and deal directly with production studios for execution, but skip the typical creative agency bit in the middle.” The author added, “I’m sure 1 a.m. phone calls aren’t totally uncommon for the account managers of some particular clients.”

Kalshi is running its celebrity ad blitz while prediction markets face serious pushback from some lawmakers and gambling watchdogs. The newsletter asked how Kalshi was balancing aggressive customer acquisition with the need to avoid giving critics any ammo. Deighton said Kalshi had done some policy-forward and educational advertising and that it could be argued the Martha Stewart spot fell under those categories because it aims to communicate Kalshi’s ban on insider trading. She said Kalshi said it was done with the criticized advertising style of a few years ago, which sold the platform as a tool to maybe get rich quick. Deighton said Kalshi had become skilled at blanketing social feeds and television with ads that are “very weird and watchable” but do not really say much about the product, thereby alleviating the risk of getting in trouble for making claims. “Basically, it’s gotten really good at good old-fashioned brand marketing,” she said.

Kalshi also plans to launch equity trading within the next two months, according to people familiar with the matter.

Burger King, meanwhile, is asking customers to identify the restaurants that most need a refresh, the WSJ Leadership Institute’s Megan Graham reported. The chain has introduced a website for guests to point out locations that still need improvements under its “Rebuild My BK” initiative, which runs through Jan. 5. Joel Yashinsky, the chain’s chief marketing officer for the U.S. and Canada, said about 60% of the chain’s restaurants have been remodeled so far. The website feedback will help guide where to focus its remodeling efforts next and what the work should entail. The company’s ongoing “Reclaim the Flame” turnaround plan involves upgraded menu items, brand collaborations and store remodels.

Yashinsky said Burger King leaders “recognized that our restaurants were old and tired.” He said: “It only made sense to tell the story that ‘Yes, we understand that our restaurants are not as relevant and as comfortable as you would like them to be, acknowledge that we’re hearing you as the guest, and that we’re taking action.’”

The chain earlier this year solicited consumer feedback by posting the phone number of Tom Curtis, its president for the U.S. and Canada business, as part of a larger “listening initiative.” The 120,000 customer calls that followed included critiques of the chain’s chicken nuggets, app, staff and stores. One customer in Great Falls, Mont., called Curtis to report a sign whose paneling had been stripped and that “looked like freakin’ crap.” Burger King featured the customer and the sign’s repair in a social-media video. Yashinsky said the company hopes to address similar complaints through the new initiative. “We’re always looking for new ways so people can give us that feedback and not feel like a number,” he said.
