## Seven incoming freshmen cite policy progress as conversations continue

Democrats are set to hold about 260 seats in the House next year, giving the party its largest majority since the 1990s. The Wall Street Journal reported that the wide margin would require defections of more than 40 House Democrats to block Hakeem Jeffries from becoming speaker in the January vote. Different blocs, including the socialists, will have less leverage in the next Congress within the House because of the majority’s size, giving Jeffries a better chance of avoiding the intraparty standoffs that bedeviled current Speaker Mike Johnson (R., La.).

Seven of the holdouts in Wednesday’s vote were incoming House freshmen. In a statement, they said there had been “meaningful progress” on several policy issues, including Medicare for All, but that the “conversations are ongoing.”

The Wall Street Journal said sweeping victories in the midterm elections swelled both left-wing and centrist ranks of the House Democratic caucus. The elections also swept in freshmen with ties to the Democratic Socialists of America and dozens of incoming members who represent districts that previously backed President Trump.

Rep. Jim Clyburn (D., S.C.) told reporters ahead of the vote that it was “a very surreal moment for me,” as he prepared to help deliver a speech supporting the first Black speaker. He said he was thinking of the late civil-rights leader Rep. John Lewis (D., Ga.), along with Lewis’s late wife, Lillian, and Clyburn’s late wife, Emily.

Jeffries, the New York Democrat, won his party’s nomination in a closed-door meeting on Wednesday on a vote of 251-11, according to people familiar with the vote, and had no declared opponent. The nomination vote precedes a contest in early January when Jeffries is expected to become the nation’s first Black speaker in a vote by the full House. Jeffries has been meeting with members across the caucus.

The House election was more low-key than the brewing Senate competition for leader between Sens. Chuck Schumer (D., N.Y.) and Chris Van Hollen (D., Md.). No House Democrat stepped forward to challenge Jeffries, despite complaints from activist groups that he was an establishment figure, out of touch with the party base and too close to Israel.

Jeffries has made tackling high costs his main focus. He has outlined plans to move quickly on legislation to reverse cuts to Medicaid and Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program benefits and extend the enhanced Affordable Care Act tax credits that lapsed last year. Jeffries has not endorsed Medicare for All and has opposed the proposal, but he has floated ideas to expand coverage to more Americans, helping his relationship with his left flank.

At a recent appearance in Denver, Jeffries expressed support for lowering the eligibility age to qualify for the current Medicare program, although he did not specify an age. He said Democrats should add dental, vision and hearing coverage to Medicare and expand children’s coverage. Those steps have been supported by progressives within the party.
