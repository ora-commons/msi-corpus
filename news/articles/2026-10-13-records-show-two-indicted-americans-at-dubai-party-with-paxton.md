## Records show two indicted Americans at Dubai party with Paxton

**Subtype:** fact

- The Wall Street Journal reported a previously unreported New Year's Eve 2024 celebration in Dubai where Texas Attorney General Ken Paxton was among the guests with two Americans who had been indicted months earlier on federal healthcare-fraud charges.
- One of the two men, Anosh Ahmed, donated $190,000 to Paxton's political campaigns, including $25,000 days after he was indicted, campaign finance records show.
- Democratic nominee James Talarico consistently holds a narrow lead in polls over Paxton, raising a once unthinkable possibility that a Democrat could win statewide office in Texas for the first time since 1994.
- President Trump endorsed Paxton in the May GOP primary runoff despite being warned that damaging revelations were likely to surface, and months later the Journal wrote that the decision is looking like Trump's biggest gamble of the midterms.

## Ahmed donated $190,000 to Paxton, including $25,000 days after he was indicted

As fireworks exploded over the Burj Khalifa skyscraper to welcome 2025, Paxton and his girlfriend Tracy Duhon watched from a luxury high-rise balcony full of people, according to a video of the gathering reviewed by the Journal, details of which were contained in court filings and confirmed by people familiar with the matter.

The video of the event was taken by another guest at the party and was cited in a filing in a Texas civil court case in which that guest is a defendant.

Among those at the event with Texas' top law-enforcement official were Anosh Ahmed and Sameer Suhail, two Americans who had been indicted months before but failed to return to the U.S., according to the court records and people familiar with the matter. Both men were indicted in federal court in Illinois in 2024 on charges that they embezzled $15 million from a Chicago hospital where Ahmed was chief financial officer.

Ahmed, a healthcare executive with business interests in Texas, was later charged in a much larger scheme involving at least $195 million in alleged payments from the government for fraudulent Covid-19 tests. He has pleaded not guilty. Campaign finance records show Ahmed donated $190,000 to Paxton, including $25,000 days after he was indicted.

Both men are now back in the U.S. to face the charges. Ahmed is in custody; his attorneys have said in court filings that he was abroad at the time of the first indictment and that his return to the U.S. was delayed by family health issues. Suhail's attorney, Adam Sheppard, said civil matters in Dubai affected Suhail's ability to travel after the indictment and that he returned voluntarily when they were resolved.

In a statement, Paxton senior adviser Nick Maddux didn't directly address the Dubai trip. "Like President Trump, Ken Paxton has been politically persecuted for years, and every time, voters have seen through it and sent him back to office because they know he has always fought for Texas," Maddux said.

The disclosure lands three weeks before the Nov. 3 election. Paxton, 63, has long been a survivor of controversy, and Trump's allies maintain the MAGA faithful will power him to a win. None of Paxton's political baggage has sunk him so far, the Journal reported; prosecutors who have taken aim at him said they have learned that people who doubt Paxton do so at their peril.

"It's just amazing to me that he's never been held accountable," said Kent Schaffer, a special prosecutor who filed charges against Paxton in 2015.

An Air Force brat born in North Dakota, Paxton first moved to Texas in 1981 to attend Baylor University and later worked at a Dallas law firm. His involvement in politics first came via a North Texas community of Christians pushing religious issues. He served on the boards of an antiabortion crisis pregnancy center and an organization aiming to make the Bible easier to study.

When Kelly Shackleford, a friend who now heads the First Liberty Institute, a Christian conservative legal organization, suggested that redistricting could make 2002 a good year to consider running for office, Paxton responded "What's redistricting?" Shackleford recalled. Still, he ran and won a seat in the Texas House of Representatives. Shackleford recalled that when Paxton received political threats from fellow Republicans for taking an unpopular position on a tax bill early in his career, he remained unflappable and asked friends to pray for him.

"The thing that makes him not necessarily a very charismatic campaign person is what makes him steady in office," Shackleford said.

By the time Paxton had ascended through the state House and Senate to become attorney general in 2015, he was beginning to weather a series of controversies. Another lawyer accused him of stealing a $1,000 Montblanc pen at a courthouse; Paxton later returned it and called it a misunderstanding. He was facing simultaneous state and federal securities-fraud investigations. An effort to pay his legal bills resulted in a bribery investigation when he accepted $100,000 from a former client under investigation by his office; the case was closed with no finding of wrongdoing. Paxton denied wrongdoing in all the cases.

He was re-elected twice as attorney general while securities-fraud charges against him were pending. Civil charges filed against him by the Securities and Exchange Commission alleging securities fraud were dismissed in 2017, and in 2024 he resolved state felony-securities fraud charges with a deal that involved paying $300,000 in restitution.

His finances grew over the same period. Before he first ran for public office in 2002, Paxton's only assets were a handful of stocks and a house with a mortgage, with a combined value of less than $175,000, according to a state disclosure. By 2018, he told a lender he had amassed a net worth of some $5.5 million, not including millions of dollars in assets he and his wife had moved into a blind trust. They now own at least a dozen residential properties.

In 2020, eight of his deputies reported him to law enforcement, accusing him of using the attorney general's office to help a top donor and spurring a Federal Bureau of Investigation probe. Nothing has brought Paxton closer to political ruin than his relationship with Austin real-estate developer Nate Paul, a friend and campaign donor. Paxton's former aides accused him of abusing the attorney general's office to interfere with an FBI investigation into Paul, sparking a federal probe into Paxton, the 2023 impeachment trial and a whistleblower lawsuit that the former employees won. Paxton denied their claims. The accusations led members of his own party to impeach Paxton in 2023 on abuse-of-office charges, which he denied. He was acquitted by the state Senate and returned to office.

At one point, Paxton requested a meeting with Justice Department officials in Washington, D.C., to raise objections to the FBI agent investigating Paul, which wasn't within those officials' jurisdiction, according to people with knowledge of the situation. As a result, at least two top officials later had to recuse themselves when their office took charge of the separate investigation into Paxton, the people said. The Justice Department ultimately dropped its probe into Paxton.

In attack ads, Talarico has highlighted the abuse-of-office claims and the $6.6 million awarded to the whistleblowers who alleged they were fired for reporting Paxton's actions to law enforcement. Talarico has also hammered Paxton over his jet-setting travels, accumulation of wealth in office and his business associations, and has criticized what he calls Paxton's "jet-setting lifestyle."

From 2022 through 2025, state-funded security accompanied Paxton on 19 foreign trips to countries including Greece, Italy, South Africa and Mexico, according to records, more than either the governor or lieutenant governor during that time. The records aren't comprehensive and don't include the Dubai trip.

Paxton's personal life has also drawn public attention. His wife of 38 years, Texas state Sen. Angela Paxton, filed for divorce last year, citing adultery, after public reports of two affairs, including with Duhon. Paxton met Duhon, a Christian influencer who became his girlfriend, at the Kentucky Derby in 2024, people with knowledge of the relationship said. She divorced her husband the following year, and Paxton's divorce is pending. Tabloid photos captured the pair as they traveled through Europe over this past summer, while Talarico's campaign ramped up.

Earlier this month, a PAC funded by Senate Republicans released an ad featuring Angela Paxton. In the ad, she tells people to look past individual candidates and support the entire GOP ticket. She never mentions Paxton by name. "It's not as much about the individual players on the team, it's about getting the whole team across the finish line," she said.

As President Trump weighed endorsing Paxton over incumbent Texas Sen. John Cornyn this spring, he was told that damaging revelations were likely to surface about Paxton, according to people familiar with the conversations. Trump was dismissive and said there would be more negative material about Democratic nominee James Talarico. Trump "plowed ahead and endorsed the scandal-plagued Texas attorney general," helping him win the Republican-primary runoff in May, the Journal reported. Months later, that decision is looking like "Trump's biggest gamble of the midterms," the Journal wrote.

Paxton is hoping an influx of Republican money can head off Talarico. He has held a series of fundraisers around Washington in recent weeks, say people who attended, but has struggled to attract the high-profile crowds.

Despite efforts by Paxton to call his opponent a "far-left zealot", Talarico consistently holds a narrow lead in polls, raising the once unthinkable possibility of a Democrat winning statewide office in Texas for the first time since 1994.
