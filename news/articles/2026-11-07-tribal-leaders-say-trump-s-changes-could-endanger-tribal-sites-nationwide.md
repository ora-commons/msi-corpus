## Federal agency and Congress propose narrowing what counts as damage to sites

Section 106 of the National Historic Preservation Act, which has been around for 60 years, allows states, tribes or local governments to consult with federal agencies on development proposals and redesign projects to mitigate harm to sites. The Trump administration has proposed changes to the act. Separately, the Advisory Council on Historic Preservation, a federal agency, and Congress are proposing revisions that would narrow what counts as damaging to a site and make consultation optional.

Democratic Senator Martin Heinrich of New Mexico said the current rules cause too much delay when it comes to approving energy projects. "America needs to be able to build things in years, not in decades," Heinrich said. Advocates said the proposed changes should streamline the permitting process.

Oklahoma tribal officials warned that the administration's proposed changes could weaken protections for historic sites significant to tribal nations across the country. The officials said the proposal puts ancestral lands in Ohio at risk and could undermine the federal government's responsibility to cooperate with tribal nations.

Ohio is home to hundreds of earthworks sacred to Native Americans, including the Miamisburg Mound. The mound is one of the largest conical mounds in the eastern United States, rises 65 feet and was built by the Indigenous Adena Culture. Miamisburg Mayor Michelle Collins said, "You're talking something that's a couple thousand years old."

Oklahoma is home to 38 federally recognized tribal nations. For some Oklahoma tribal nations, Ohio was once home, but they were forcibly displaced to Oklahoma because of broken treaties and federal policies in the 18th and 19th centuries.

Chief Ben Barnes of the Shawnee Tribe said the National Historic Preservation Act should remain as it is. "The Section 106 consultation process allows the Shawnee Tribe and other tribes to make sure that sacred sites, such as the earthworks in Ohio or ancestral burial grounds or places of sacred memory, are preserved," Barnes said.

Barnes said tribes also want to protect the places where they now live and said it is key that Native Americans retain a say in the process, with no optional provision for their involvement. He cited a large gas line running across Oklahoma to Springfield, Missouri: "We have a very large gas line that runs across Oklahoma to Springfield, Missouri. And one day, that gas line is going to need to be disturbed or going to need to be enlarged or need to be repaired. And without having Section 106 processes, they can do that without ever consulting the people that gather in that place to worship."

Blake Norton, an official with the Citizen Potawatomi Nation, said the sites are much more to tribes than places needing development. He likened treating them that way to stripping away the humanity of his ancestors and disregarding the relationship Native Americans have to those spaces. "And for what? So a project can be completed? So that capitalism can continue?" Norton said.

In a statement, Heinrich said he was speaking with tribal leaders about possible compromises on the bill.
