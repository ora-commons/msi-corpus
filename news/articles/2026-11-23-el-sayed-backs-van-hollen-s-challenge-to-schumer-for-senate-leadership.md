## Healthcare, military aid and willingness to work with Republican critics

Elissa Slotkin has become the latest of about half a dozen Democratic senators to endorse Chris Van Hollen’s long-shot challenge to Chuck Schumer for Senate Democratic leader. The Wall Street Journal described Slotkin as El-Sayed’s more centrist Democratic counterpart in Michigan. Democrats will choose between Van Hollen and Schumer by secret ballot on Dec. 1, and the contest remains unresolved; Schumer says he has locked down the votes he needs to retain the post.

Incoming Michigan Democratic Sen.-elect Abdul El-Sayed was among the first to publicly call on Maryland Sen. Chris Van Hollen to run against New York Sen. Chuck Schumer for the party’s top leadership post when he did so in April. El-Sayed’s backing has helped the challenge gain steam.

## Policy priorities emerge before El-Sayed takes office

El-Sayed’s victory in battleground Michigan, a state President Donald Trump carried twice, gave him a national platform for changes to healthcare, campaign finance and U.S. foreign aid. El-Sayed is the first Muslim elected to the U.S. Senate. During a recent interview while visiting Washington for Senate orientation, the 42-year-old epidemiologist and former public health official sat at his desk in a temporary ninth-floor office space overlooking the U.S. Capitol. He argued that voters wanted more than a return to the status quo. El-Sayed said, “What voters told us is, they hate Donald Trump and Trumpism.” He added, “They didn’t tell us they love us. Our job is to go earn our mandate.”

Republican opponents have criticized the direction of El-Sayed’s politics, The Wall Street Journal reported. North Dakota Sen. John Hoeven said, “They’re going hard left, even to the point of socialism, and that’s a real problem, because socialism has wrecked every country [where] it’s been tried.”

Over the past two weeks, El-Sayed has described his priorities during meetings across his state and the nation’s capital. At a retirement event hosted by a local United Auto Workers chapter in Dearborn, Mich., he reaffirmed support for property-tax freezes for seniors. Addressing a congregation at a Black church in Detroit, he pledged to overhaul the healthcare system by creating a Medicare-for-All insurance plan. At a surprise appearance at a gathering of Muslim leaders in Washington, he reinforced the need to rein in U.S. military aid.

Last week, El-Sayed launched a political-action committee that will help him build his network and pursue a more influential role in the Democratic Party ahead of the 2028 presidential election. His political activity is part of a broader effort to consolidate support and stand out in a Congress with an unusually large incoming freshman class. El-Sayed tapped Brenda Abdelall, a former aide to former Vice President Kamala Harris’s presidential campaign who also served in the Biden administration, to lead his transition team.

## Republicans respond to El-Sayed and his challenge

As Republicans prepared to take control of the House and Senate, the Republican National Committee used Van Hollen’s challenge to highlight tensions within the Democratic Party. The committee cited Van Hollen’s backing from El-Sayed and other progressives as evidence of his connections to progressive Democrats.

El-Sayed said he was willing to work with Texas Sen. Ted Cruz if that was needed to get things done. El-Sayed said of Cruz, “He’s an a—hole.” He continued, “But if he’s the a—hole from Texas with whom I have to work, I’ll work with the a—hole from Texas. I’ll still call him an a—hole. But if we’re going to get some things done, let’s get some things done.”

During the campaign, Cruz called El-Sayed “a jihadist” and warned that “Americans will die” if El-Sayed were elected. Asked about working with El-Sayed, Cruz said he would “work with anyone who’s doing positive things for America” while reiterating his campaign claim that El-Sayed supported the Muslim Brotherhood, a charge El-Sayed has denied. Cruz declined to comment on El-Sayed’s characterization of him.

Other Senate Republicans were divided about whether they could find common ground with the incoming Michigan senator. Florida Sen. Rick Scott said El-Sayed’s push for Medicare-for-All would “destroy the Medicare program.” Ohio Sen. Bernie Moreno, the chair of the National Republican Senatorial Committee, said Friday that he had called to congratulate the Democrat on his victory after saying during the campaign that El-Sayed “should be nowhere near Washington.” The pair traded barbs about the college football rivalry between Michigan and Ohio State. Moreno said he could envision working with El-Sayed on policies supporting U.S. automakers and other efforts to lower costs. “We’ll see what kind of senator he wants to be,” Moreno said.

Louisiana Sen. John Kennedy, who campaigned alongside El-Sayed’s opponent Mike Rogers and suggested El-Sayed “sleeps in a bin Laden jersey,” said he had no problem working with the incoming Democrat.

## AIPAC spending and Stevens’s reversal

El-Sayed’s race brought the debate over U.S. support for Israel into view and served as a test of the American Israel Public Affairs Committee’s political influence, The Wall Street Journal said. The group and its affiliates spent nearly $30 million backing El-Sayed’s primary opponent, Rep. Haley Stevens, and close to $10 million supporting Rogers in the general election. Disagreements over El-Sayed’s position on Israel continued to draw scrutiny from more moderate Democrats. Those differences also contributed to Stevens withdrawing her endorsement of El-Sayed and backing Rogers just two days before the election.

## El-Sayed ties military aid to international law

During his interview, El-Sayed said his opposition to unconditional military aid was not specific to Israel and also applied to countries including Egypt, Pakistan and Saudi Arabia. He said strategic partnerships should be conditioned on other nations upholding international law.

“We do not pay our taxes in this country to buy bombs and tanks for other countries,” he said. “We should not be subsidizing other countries’ militaries while our kids’ schools crumble.”

## El-Sayed and Vance discuss a possible meeting

Schumer has said he has locked down the votes needed to retain the leadership post. El-Sayed’s support for Van Hollen did not prevent him and Schumer from sharing a friendly interaction when the Democratic leader posed with incoming freshmen at the U.S. Capitol last week. “I’m very friendly with everyone,” El-Sayed said. “Unless it’s JD Vance.”

El-Sayed and Vance, political rivals who clashed on the campaign trail, were discussing the possibility of sitting down together, according to representatives in both of their offices.
