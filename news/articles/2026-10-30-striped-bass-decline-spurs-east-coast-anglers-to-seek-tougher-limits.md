## Chesapeake Bay sees eighth straight year of weak striper spawning

MONTAUK, N.Y. — Striped bass fishing has been Bill Jakob’s passion for more than 60 years, and he remembers an era when he would spend all night casting his line into the crashing waves off Montauk and get eight or nine fish ranging up to 40 pounds (18 kilograms), a big catch by anyone’s standards. Then, after years of heavy fishing, the big runs of stripers stopped.

“I’m part of the problem, back in the years. I ran party boats, we slayed fish with reckless abandon,” said Jakob, president of the Montauk Surfcasters Association on the easternmost tip of Long Island.

Increasingly, fishermen like Jakob fear the good days of catching striped bass are coming to an end. The fish aren’t showing up like they used to, and young stripers are becoming harder to find, they say.

Scientists who study striped bass say anglers on the East Coast are right to be concerned, and they say years of overfishing are just part of the problem. The fish have experienced eight consecutive years of poor reproduction in their most critical habitat in the Chesapeake Bay, regulators said last week, and the population has declined along much of the Atlantic coast.

Factors like years of warming waters — a prime concern cited by many scientists — poor river flow, drought, pollution and possibly increased predation from seals and sharks have left experts and fishermen asking whether tighter fishing limits would even be enough for striped bass to recover. Striped bass are higher-level predators that help regulate smaller fish, and losing them would threaten an industry that depends on the fish surviving. Fishing guides, boat trips, gear, bait and hotel rooms all add up to what members of the industry say is about $8 billion in economic impact and as many as 100,000 jobs in the U.S. alone.

Striped bass fishing is a long-standing East Coast tradition. “Stripers” are prized in part because they put up a fight and can be fished right from shore. From the slippery rocks of Maine’s coast to the warmer waters of the St. Johns River in Florida, anglers wearing headlamps can be seen casting into the predawn darkness, hoping for a bite.

Interstate regulators have made changes to the way the migratory fish are managed in recent years, including standardizing the way they’re measured against size requirements. But regulators have balked at reducing the level of recreational catch. Some fishermen say the current moves might not go far enough, and stricter action — including possibly a fishing moratorium, a strategy used in the 1980s — is needed to ensure that future generations of anglers can still catch striped bass.

“They are not replacing themselves at a sufficient level. That obviously has everybody concerned,” said Beth Versak, the striped bass program manager for the Maryland Department of Natural Resources. “People all up and down the coast are passionate about striped bass.”

“Striped bass as we know it is going to collapse,” said Patrick Paquette, government affairs coordinator for the Massachusetts Striped Bass Association and a member of an interstate advisory panel about the regulations. “Sometime around 2029, 2030 it’s going to go off a cliff,” he said, “and it’s going to be ugly.”

Scientists say a variety of factors, including warming waters, have contributed to poor spawning. Striped bass hatch in fresh or brackish water and then spend the early years of their lives in estuaries where the rivers meet the sea. Their ability to reproduce and bring new fish into the population is predicated on environmental conditions that favor them — specifically, cool, wet springs.

Scientists identify four major spawning stocks along the Atlantic. The Chesapeake Bay is where up to 90% of stripers spawn and grow. When freshwater flows are too low, such as in times of drought, or when spring or summer temperatures are too high, the fish suffer and populations can fall, said Toni Kerns, fisheries policy director with the Atlantic States Marine Fisheries Commission, which regulates striped bass.

Conditions have been poor in recent years, and fewer fish have entered the population going back to 2019, said Chris Batsavage, chair of the commission’s striped bass management board. “Low precipitation and waters that warm quickly result in weak year classes,” Batsavage said. “It seems like it’s environmentally driven.”

The conditions have led to a “slump of bad years” that have made it especially difficult for the fish to maintain their population in the Chesapeake, Versak said.

John Skinner, who wrote a book about striped bass fishing and runs a YouTube channel on the subject that has more than 100,000 followers, has been fishing off Long Island for more than 50 years. He said once-reliable spots have completely dried up. “I’m not saying the fishing is bad. I’m saying when you go there in perfect conditions, you catch nothing,” Skinner said. “Absolutely nothing.”

Many other striped bass fishermen across the Northeast shared a recurring story this summer and fall — the fish’s once-plentiful schools are dried up, and the individuals that remain are clearly older, meaning years of reproduction failure are starting to take their toll. Some fishermen said stricter rules or a fishing moratorium could be the only ways to prevent the fish’s population from becoming dangerously low. Some see years of heavy fishing on striped bass becoming apparent today in the form of a lower population; others blame the resurgence of sharks buoyed by years of conservation efforts as a reintroduction of competition for the fish.

The Atlantic States commission considers the stock to be overfished, although it says overfishing is not occurring at the moment. The fish are caught commercially as well as recreationally, but recreational fishing is much larger. Most of the recreational catch is tossed back into the ocean alive. Still, some fish don’t survive the encounter: regulators have historically expected 9% to die after release, while newer data shows the number could be less than 5%.

Limits on how large a striped bass can be caught and kept are typically in the range of 28 to 31 inches (71 to 79 centimeters), with rules varying by state and location. Striped bass are popular with catch-and-release fishermen, and these days it’s more common to catch larger bass, many well over 31 inches.

Paquette, a longtime angler himself, said that without drastic action he fears the end of striped bass fishing. “If you’re a guy who thought he was going to retire and striped bass fish, and you’re planning on retiring soon, get a different plan,” Paquette said.
