## Talarico's call for Trump cooperation draws a mixed reaction

More than 2,000 people attended the two-day "What's Next: investigating strategies and priorities for the future of the Democratic Party" conference in Denver. Activists and political organizers were energized and celebratory in the week after a "blue tsunami" saw Democrats win in Republican heartlands in the midterm elections. The party took control of both chambers of Congress, winning a House majority by over 80 seats and gaining nine Senate seats. The gathering was both a celebration and an assessment of how the party had won, which issues had failed to resonate and what Democrats would need to continue their momentum.

James Talarico was among the rising stars who took the stage after becoming the first Democrat to win election to the US Senate for Texas since 1988. He will be 37 when he takes the oath of office in January and become the youngest member of the Senate. His entrance drew cheers and a standing ovation, but the room's enthusiasm dimmed when he proposed working with the Republican Trump administration.

"We should make the final two years of his presidency the most productive of any presidency in American history," Talarico said. "Because this moment is so much bigger and so much more important than the red team or the blue team." He argued that Democrats should work alongside Republicans on an economic agenda that would resonate with Americans, including extending health coverage and reducing inflation.

Majority Democrats, a political action committee launched the previous year, staged the event to address tensions between the party's left and centrist factions. The sharpest divisions included how Democrats should deliver health care and what the party should do about the war in Gaza. Prominent left-wing members Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez and Senator-elect Abdul El Sayed were not in attendance. Protesters waving Palestinian flags shouted as people walked past, while armed police looked on.

Alabama Congressman Shomari Figures, who surprised many pollsters when he held his seat despite Republicans redrawing his district months before the election, cautioned against going after Trump when there were more pressing concerns for Americans. Previous Democratic-led efforts to impeach Trump both ended in Senate acquittals. "We impeached him twice and got him back - with a Republican majority," Figures said.

Maryland Congresswoman Angela Alsobrooks took a different position and insisted that voters wanted to see accountability. She said voters "realised the smash-and-grab that occurred, and they want people to account for it" and pushed for transparent investigations into the Trump administration. Her position contrasted with Figures's call for Democrats to concentrate on other concerns.

Some attendees directed criticism at Chuck Schumer, the long-time Democratic Senate minority leader who is seen as part of the party's centrist establishment. Chuck Schumer has been challenged for the Senate majority leader role by Chris Van Hollen, a Maryland senator considered a left-wing populist. Protester Vicki Saxe, holding a sign criticising Schumer, said Democrats had not been "strong enough in their displeasure with what's been happening with Trump, and I think that's one of the reasons that people are asking for new leadership."

Tami Butters, 65, said, "I'm frustrated with the lack of urgency that I feel coming from the party leadership." Standing beside a "Vote Yes for Chris Van Hollen" sign, Butters cited the absence of 11 Democrats the previous week when the House voted on whether to consider impeaching Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth. The effort failed.

New York Congressman Hakeem Jeffries, a centrist Democrat poised to become the country's first black speaker of the House, faced similar pushback, and protesters heckled his speech. The war in Gaza remained one of the conference's biggest fault lines. Schumer and Jeffries backed continued military assistance for Israel, while some new members of Congress, including El-Sayed, supported ending unconditional US military aid to Israel.

Non-profit founder Shiva Saram, who also chairs the Iranian American Legal Defense Fund, traveled from Connecticut to the conference despite having "almost left my party over Gaza and the Iran War." Saram said the party's incoming members had renewed her hope. She cited the "real depth in our bench of new and young leaders" and said, "What my party has done wrong for too long is we did not listen."

Many speakers and guests said Democrats won because they focused on policies that affected constituents' daily lives, and that failing to do so in earlier years might explain previous losses. Delaware Senator Chris Coons told BBC News he had advised newly elected members to "under-promise and over-deliver." He said, "We got hired because the average American wants us to help make their lives better - lower their costs, end the cruelty and the corruption and give them more reason for hope." He added, "That's going to require hard work, and it isn't going to be easy to get done."

Michigan Representative Kristen McDonald Rivet agreed and said she did not care "if we have to put Donald Trump's name" on legislation to gain the president's support for efforts to help her district.

Tahra Hoops, a 28-year-old senior director of economic analysis at the Chamber of Progress, a left-of-centre tech think tank, argued that Democrats should embrace their divisions rather than pave over them. She said, "I think Democrats can look different wherever they are." Hoops noted that very progressive candidates can win in her home town of New York City, while Talarico's more centrist views made sense because running in Texas "is not going to be the same." She added, "America is a very diverse country; our politicians and their views should also be diverse."

Campaign consultant Carlos Repetto-Ayala, a 25-year-old Texan, made a similar argument. He traveled with two friends to the "What's Next?" event, wearing a cowboy hat and a red-white-and-blue pin on his lapel in the shape of the Lone Star State. He said, "It is a big tent party, but I think, as long as everyone that's in the party is laser-focused on addressing the issues that people would like for them to fix, I think Democrats will be successful." The conference ended without settling how Democrats should govern after their victories or how they would bridge their internal differences.
