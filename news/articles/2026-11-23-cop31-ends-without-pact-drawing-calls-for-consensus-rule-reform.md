## Final draft omitted fossil fuels and drew formal objections

Delegates to the Cop31 climate summit in Antalya, Turkey, ended the meeting late Sunday without approving a final pact after formal objections blocked the consensus decision. Turkey and Australia co-chaired the summit, which had been expected to produce a sweeping agreement intended to add urgency to global climate action and support countries exposed to the crisis.

Countries became entrenched before the final plenary, and the proposed “Antalya pact” stalled. Many were angry that the final draft’s cover decision neither met the moment of the climate crisis nor mentioned the words “fossil fuels.”

Countries including the EU bloc, Panama, Belize, Vanuatu, the UK, the Marshall Islands and Norway formally objected to the cover decision, preventing it from being gavelled through. Some viewed the resulting refusal to proceed as a positive decision to walk away — a “sign of courage,” as former UN climate chief Christiana Figueres called it.

Former US vice-president and climate activist Al Gore called the failure “an outrage” and said significant reform was needed. Gore said: “World leaders no longer want to settle for the lowest common denominator. They want real action to implement and finance a just transition away from fossil fuels. It has been clear for years that the Cop process is in need of significant reform.” He said most countries had come to Antalya to move forward “in good faith” and said it was “time that we empower those leaders with a reinvigorated multilateral process that reflects the latest science and represents the will of the majority of the world.”

Australian climate minister Chris Bowen, one of the Cop31 co-chairs, said: “The fundamental root problem is that when Cops were created, for reasons I understand, it was made consensus only. That’s a very tough ask when you also need consensus to change consensus, which is a particular challenge.” He said there had been an attempt at Cop31 to introduce efficiency reforms.

UN chief climate negotiator Simon Stiell defended the consensus approach, saying it had succeeded with the 2015 Paris agreement. He said it had helped lower expected global heating at the end of the century from almost 5C to 2.6C, but conceded that the system had fallen short in Antalya. Stiell said he was championing a review process within the UN climate convention to seek external advice on “areas of potential reform.”

Johan Rockström, director of the Potsdam Institute for Climate Impact Research, said the COP process needed to move from “negotiating promises and commitments that we never deliver on” to accountability for promises made and their implementation. Tobias Holle, co-founder of the NGO group Shifting Advocacy, said Cop31 and previous failures showed that “the process is not fit for purpose any more.” He said: “Despite the political will of the many, the few blocking the progress end up winning in a process whose rules and decision-making structures repeatedly allow the lowest common denominator to dictate the level of ambition.” He called for “a structural reform that delivers on climate justice.”

During the talks, Panama released an open letter to the Cop31 co-chairs listing areas on which it would refuse to compromise, including opening a path to changing from consensus to simple majority voting. Matt McDonald, a professor of international relations at the University of Queensland in Australia who attended the talks, said: “The legacy of Cop31 might be recognition of the limits of this consensus-driven approach to climate negotiations and the development of alternative sites for climate action.” He said the world was “a different world now” than in 2015, when the Paris agreement was forged, raising questions about “the future of this type of consensus-based multilateralism going forward.”

Cop31 overran its official end-time of Friday evening and ended late on Sunday, making it the longest climate summit so far. It was also the largest, according to many estimates.

China, India and a large number of developing countries did not formally object to the text and subsequently expressed disappointment at the pact’s failure. The Ghana delegation, which accepted the text, told the plenary: “Africa has always chosen to build multilateralism rather than walk away from it.”
