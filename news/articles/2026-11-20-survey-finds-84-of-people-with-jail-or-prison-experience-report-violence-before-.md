## Just Safe calls for trauma funding, referral training and compensation access

Researchers with Just Safe, an Oakland, California-based victims' advocacy nonprofit, found that 84% of people who had spent time in US prisons or jails reported being assaulted, shot or stabbed before their first time in jail or prison. The Guardian published its account of the findings Friday. The report drew on a nationally representative survey of 20,000 Americans.

Among survey respondents who had been incarcerated, half had been hurt by a caregiver as a child and one-third had been victims of sexual or intimate partner violence.

The report described how some people who had been harmed but had not received trauma recovery services turn to drugs and alcohol, while others start hurting people in the same ways they had been hurt. It said both paths can lead people to cycle in and out of the justice system and put them at higher risk of future violence.

Lenore Anderson, president of Just Safe, said the research could push the public and elected officials to reframe the politics of crime. “It’s meant to share this very different viewpoint that most people don’t think about: the collateral consequences of victimization,” she said. “We can focus all of our time on how long the sentences are and how challenging the prison environment should be or we can flip the script to ask: ‘What happened and did people get help?’” Anderson said the findings show the necessity of bolstering trauma recovery resources instead of simply focusing on punishment.

The report's authors recommended training first responders, nurses and teachers to refer people to assistance services. The authors called on municipalities to invest more money in trauma recovery and to remove bureaucratic barriers to victims' compensation. “If we’re committed to public safety we have to ask what stops the cycle and that looks different from what our current criminal justice system does,” Anderson said. “It means ensuring that what happened never happens again through accountability measures that allow people to address underlying drivers.”

The findings were no surprise to Jemaine Hunter. He grew up in Fresno, California, where as a child and young teenager he faced violence from some of his family members. By 13, he was getting into fights with young men from his neighborhood. In addition to being harmed at home, Hunter watched people being shot and killed when conflicts went sideways. He decided he would do what he could to avoid the same fate.

“Hearing those stories made me say, ‘I don’t wanna be a victim.’ It was the homeboys being killed that made me feel like I needed to keep a gun and be ready to use it all the time,” Hunter said.

“I was probably a victim in every situation possible. Things have been stolen from me, I got into fights that I didn’t win,” he said. “But it wasn’t my fault that things happened to me.”

At 24, after being in and out of juvenile detention as a teenager, Hunter was convicted of attempted murder and sentenced to 34 years to life in prison. He spent 23 years in California prisons, including San Quentin in northern California, before being released in December 2025. He now works as a counselor for Sunset Youth Services, a San Francisco-based nonprofit that supports young people who have been repeatedly arrested or are on probation.

While in San Quentin, Hunter co-founded Arms Down, described as a first-in-the-nation support group for people convicted of gun offenses. He heard stories of men who were jumped and abused, then joined a gang and carried firearms to feel safe. He said that, like him, the men he served time with often lacked protection from violence, a space to help process what happened and mentorship that could steer them away from repeating the same violence they faced.

“As a kid I needed that superhero and someone who would stand between me and that fear. Someone to say: ‘Hey man, back up. Don’t touch him’ and bring that ear and shoulder to lean on,” Hunter said.

To help prevent future violence, Hunter has been teaching the Arms Down curriculum to young people in San Francisco who carry guns. Many have been jumped before or have lost a friend to violence, he said. Hunter said he was working to give the young people what he needed when he was a teenager and hoped the model developed by Sunset Youth Services would be duplicated in cities across the country.

Anderson summarized the report's message with the line “Hurt people hurt people,” she said. She also said the data showed people who had been harmed first harmed themselves and were showing up in the justice system for matters related to substance abuse. Just Safe's recommendations included referral training, municipal trauma recovery spending, easier compensation access and accountability measures, while Hunter's Arms Down curriculum focused on support and mentorship for young people affected by gun violence.
