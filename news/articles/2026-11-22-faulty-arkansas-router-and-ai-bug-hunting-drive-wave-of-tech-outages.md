## Costco generator sales rise as outage conspiracy theories spread

The failure that delayed Reindorf's takeoff originated with a faulty router in the city of El Dorado, Arkansas, population 16,609. That router was installed in the midst of a maintenance procedure that has been under way for more than two decades. In 2002, the US government contracted a small company, now known as L3Harris, to modernise the communications system of the Federal Aviation Administration, which oversees America's airports. As part of that work, L3Harris has been tearing up communication lines made of copper wire across the country and replacing them with fibre optic cables.

The newly replaced router misfired and caused air traffic communications across the US to crash, bringing all departing flights nationwide to a grinding halt. L3Harris's contracts have now run into the billions of dollars.

The incident began on Thursday 12 November. By Tuesday, big US banks, Apple and the National Security Agency had also been affected by technical glitches. Researchers also flagged a cyber-attack that targeted 178 organisations worldwide, although the financial losses appeared to be small. On closer examination, the chaos of those weeks — grounded planes, frozen email servers, iPhone outages — appears to be more a string of coincidences than anything else, in part driven by fears over advancing artificial intelligence.

The narratives that have sprung up around the outages are a measure of an unstable and frenetic global mood dealing with two powerful trends: the rot of decades-old infrastructure from chronic underinvestment, and the genesis of some of the most powerful technology humanity has made.

Governments are not equipped to keep pace. In the US, oversight reports have urgently called for upgrades to the FAA for years. The agency only announced last year that it would phase out the use of decades-old floppy discs containing vital weather and other critical information.

Other incidents were more subtle. An upgrade at Microsoft in July left in place a software bug that lurked quietly for months before a string of coincidences triggered it, overwhelming the company's servers and halting the use of most of Microsoft's systems worldwide. The outage halted everyday procedures such as the release of detainees in American jails and access to some patient records at UK hospitals.

It was accompanied by more chaos. A software bug at JPMorgan Chase, the largest bank in the US, delayed transactions, a disruption that rippled throughout the country's banks and their customers. An internal update at Apple caused outages of some of its iPhone apps. Routine maintenance went awry at the NSA, temporarily disrupting the internal network the agency uses to send and receive classified information. The NSA told Congress that its communication outage was a result of quick fixes to systems identified as vulnerable by AI.

Microsoft, JPMorgan Chase and Apple have moved far beyond copper wire and floppy discs, mostly, but they — and software companies everywhere — have been forced to run at double the pace in recent months because of a deluge of bugs being discovered by AI. This spring, Anthropic and OpenAI both released powerful AI models capable of finding bugs in lines of code. They immediately spun this to the media, suggesting that all software everywhere is extraordinarily vulnerable to AI-driven hacking, and offering their tools to banks and spying agencies. Some have argued that this hype is not entirely justified, suggesting that many vulnerabilities these models discover are not particularly useful to hackers. But the threat of AI-driven hacking has changed the game.

AI helped to uncover a bug in the PHP programming language in October. That was followed by the discovery of a vulnerability in the Java programming language on Tuesday, and then another bug in NGINX, a web server. Java and PHP are both part of the essential backbone of the internet, vital infrastructure that keeps most of the world online.

One senior cybersecurity professional told the Guardian the relentless exposure of IT flaws had left their team "exhausted." "When NGINX happened I thought, 'Oh come on – another one?'" the professional said. Jamieson O'Reilly, a cybersecurity specialist, said: "AI is accelerating vulnerability discovery so rapidly that organisations are applying patches at a volume and pace their systems were never built to absorb." He added: "The volume and speed of AI-driven discovery makes their problems far more acute."

The backdrop was a cybersecurity narrative already running at a fraught pitch because of the hacks of supermarket fridges across Europe, global scares such as the discovery of the flaw in PHP, and the multibillion-dollar hack of the Binance cryptocurrency exchange.

Social media filled the gaps where organisations could not immediately supply information. Laura Loomer, a prominent conspiracy peddler, posted on X that "we are clearly being attacked by foreign adversaries via cyber-attacks," adding "go get cash." On TikTok, there are posts about a #90daypoweroutage, a conspiracy theory sparked by a nationwide test of the US emergency alert system — and a Simpsons clip.

The consequences of conspiratorial thinking can be seen at Costco, the bulk-buying retailer, where there has been a rise in emergency prep sales including purchases of generators, and general "pantry-loading" in the UK. Analysts at Mizuho Bank said this was a result of "consumer anxiety" following the outages and "apocalyptic concerns flaring up across social media." The concerns have not been confined to the fringes: the UK government recently told its citizens to stock up on three days' worth of cash, among other basic items, as part of a readiness-for-crisis campaign.

Stephan Lewandowsky, a professor of cognitive psychology at the University of Bristol whose research centres on the spread of myths and misinformation, said the "clustering" of events such as multiple outages "can be perceived as being indicative of purposeful coordination rather than chance." Feeding the anxiety, state and criminal actors have also caused disruption, from Russia targeting undersea internet cables to last year's crippling of Britain's biggest carmaker, Jaguar Land Rover. A solution, Lewandowsky said, was "for responsible governments to be transparent and accurate in their communications" in order to suppress wilder explanations.

Experts say the string of outages highlights the fragility of US and global infrastructure, where a single mistake or malfunction can bring entire transportation, communication and security systems to a standstill. "We've built a world in which essential systems are so interconnected that a failure in one can just cascade through those systems really quickly," said Aarathi Krishnan, the founder of Raksha Intelligence Futures, a global intelligence firm. "All of our systems, whether it's comms, finance, capital, all of it, are so interconnected and concentrated, it creates a dependence. And that dependence creates fragility."

Mike Chapple, the faculty director of Notre Dame University's business computer science programme, said it was "unfortunate" that one faulty router could have such an effect on a nation's aviation communication system. "That just points to a design that isn't resilient – it shouldn't be designed in a way that one failure at one location can cause the whole nationwide system to come tumbling down."

The senior cybersecurity professional said heightened public awareness of cyber disruption had led to hypersensitivity about any incident. "It's like the tide has gone out on cybersecurity in terms of public perception, and there's the same amount of wrecks out there as there have been for years. Any time you change a complex system, stuff happens." The professional is concerned that AI, with its capacity to identify flaws and help plan and execute attacks, will be harnessed by "already very competent" adversaries. "Thank goodness we haven't had an AI-sourced vulnerability that is easy to exploit. Because that would be carnage."

For many, recent events have been bad enough. When Josephine's prescription request for lithium for her bipolar disorder was lost "in the ether" during the global Microsoft outage, she admits she was "freaking out." "My medication is life-changing and keeps me stable," said the 26-year-old, a student living in the east of England. After an agonising wait she was finally able to collect her prescription, but the experience left her deeply troubled. "For myself and many others, we struggled to access life-saving medication," she said. "It is alarming there was no contingency plan."
