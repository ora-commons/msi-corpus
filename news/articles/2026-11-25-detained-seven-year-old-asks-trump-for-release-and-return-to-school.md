## Dilley detention exceeds federal 20-day limit for children; DHS offers no explanation

Seven-year-old Charlotte Ortiz has been held at the Dilley detention center in Texas since late October, after federal immigration agents arrested her and her mother in suburban New Orleans. In a letter written in Spanish inside the facility, Charlotte asked President Donald Trump to release her so she could visit her father and go back to school. A photograph of the letter was shared with the Guardian by the independent reporting outlet Perdido Street News.

The letter included hand-drawn figures of Charlotte, her "mama", Karen, and her "papa", Bruno. "Mr President Trump, I would like to visit my father, and study at my school," the letter reads. Charlotte said in a statement that she has been sad to miss out on learning, that she misses her art class and teacher, and that she misses her father and wants to get out so she can give him a hug and play with him.

Her parents, Bruno, 27, and Karen, 25, arrived from Venezuela in April 2024 seeking asylum through a humanitarian parole program that the Trump administration has since eliminated. Bruno, who spoke to the Guardian and Perdido Street News in Spanish, said the couple were hoping to be granted asylum after government corruption, widespread criminal extortions and a general lack of freedom in Venezuela prompted them to try their luck in the United States, and said the family did not arrive illegally. "We never came to this country illegally," he said. "We came here looking for a better opportunity, for our family to grow, to fulfill our dreams – and little by little we were [getting] it." The couple were childhood sweethearts, began living together in 2018 and married in 2022, and supported themselves as delivery drivers for the retailer Walmart after arriving in Miami and settling in the New Orleans suburb of Gretna, where Charlotte enrolled at William Hart elementary school, a public school, and gained a proficiency in English that Bruno was hopeful would serve her well. Bruno said he and Karen became active in a local Pentecostal church, regularly attending services and retreats. He said the couple had no criminal record and paid their share of taxes, and that his family does not fit the profile of "dangerous criminals" whom the administration once pledged its mass deportation efforts would focus on.

The administration discarded that parole policy, and the Supreme Court's subsequent decision to uphold that termination removed the temporary protected statuses of more than half a million people and exposed them to removal, the Guardian reported. A Department of Homeland Security spokesperson said an asylum application "does NOT confer legal status in our country", and said an immigration judge issued a final order of removal for Karen on 14 October for "failing to show up for her mandatory immigration proceedings". Bruno blamed his wife's absence from those proceedings on a noticing error. The spokesperson suggested Karen could secure the release of herself and her daughter by arranging "to self-deport now".

The arrests came on 26 October, after Karen dropped Charlotte at school and went to a store. In a statement provided by the family's attorney, Kristin Etter of the Texas Immigration Law Council, Karen said Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE) agents boxed her in with vehicles: "I thought they were going to crash into me," she said. The agents shouted at her to open the car, pulled her out of it and handcuffed her, Karen said. "I thought it was a nightmare," Karen's statement said. She recalled screaming "my daughter, my daughter" and unsuccessfully begging to be taken to Charlotte's school.

Karen said agents took her to a garage for initial holding and allowed her a three-minute call to Bruno at about noon, and her abrupt silence until then had made Bruno worry that she had perhaps been abducted by kidnappers wanting a ransom – not that learning what had actually happened was a comfort, he said. She said officers told her she would suffer if she were detained alone and that they would bring Charlotte to her if she "signed a paper", which she did. A family friend brought Charlotte to Karen at about 6pm that day, and an officer told her that "if she did anything wrong" she might not see Charlotte again until she was 21.

Karen and Charlotte were put on a plane and did not arrive at Dilley until 3am the following day, going 10 hours without food, Karen said. Bruno said he has been in as much contact as possible with Karen and Charlotte throughout their detentions at Dilley. Dilley is the only family detention center in the United States and is operated by CoreCivic, the country's largest private prison contractor, and Bruno said his family's accounts of life inside it are consistent with earlier reporting of conditions there. Demonstrators left butterfly drawings on a fence during a protest in support of families detained inside Dilley on 3 October.

On Wednesday, Charlotte's detention at Dilley reached 29 days, exceeding the 20-day limit for detaining immigrant children set by a federal court in 1985 under a class-action lawsuit that accused the US government of mistreating children in immigration detention. The DHS spokesperson did not provide an explanation for keeping Charlotte beyond that limit. A Texas Tribune analysis published in August found at least 60 children had been detained at Dilley for 100 days or longer. The number of people detained at Dilley has fluctuated over time, and Rep. Joaquin Castro, a Democratic congressman from Texas, said in late August that 576 people were detained there, including 92 children.

Bruno said Charlotte loathes the processed foods served at Dilley, and that she had dropped about 7lbs (3kg) halfway through her first week there and about 15lbs more recently. He said Karen once hid cereal in their quarters and that staff found it and threw it away. He described what he had heard about the meals: "That was the most harrowing thing to hear," he said. "Over the course of a day, she might eat five spoonfuls of rice."

Dianne Garcia, a Texas pastor who leads the San Antonio-based church Roca de Refugio, has visited Dilley weekly since November 2025 and has had 41 members of her congregation detained there. She said she has found children losing weight and not eating to be "super common" at the family detention facility, and said the problem runs deeper than taste: "It's not just that they don't like the food," Garcia said, citing American Academy of Pediatrics research. "Any time in detention is harmful for children. It's also an indicator of the stress that kids are going through."

Etter, the council's director of policy and legal services, said Charlotte's letter is something no child should ever have to write. "We are calling on every leader at every level of government and in both parties to demand the release of Karen and Charlotte, close Dilley and end family detention for good," she said.

Bruno said he and Karen are pursuing reviews of her immigration case, and that members of their church and community have asked ICE to release them while those efforts are pending.

Asked what he wanted to tell those in charge of the country's immigration policy, Bruno said: "give us an opportunity". "We've always had the intention of doing things correctly in this country," he said, adding: "Ultimately, we are supporting this country – buying things, paying taxes. We are supporting the country and ourselves. So give us a chance to do things right."

Gina Hinojosa, the Texas governor-elect, the first Democrat elected to the office since 1990 and the only Hispanic person to win it, told the New York Times she would use the state's licensing power to halt private prison companies such as the one contracted to run Dilley from holding children in detention. "Such detentions should not be happening", she said in a social media video published on 19 November by Ms Rachel, the children's YouTube star and activist. "It needs to stop."

Rep. Troy Carter, a Democratic congressman from New Orleans who sits on a House committee providing oversight of DHS leadership, management and policy, said he could not discuss the mother and daughter's case for privacy reasons. But he acknowledged being aware of what the pair were confronting amid reports from people in immigration detention conveying grueling conditions: a lack of access to phone calls, temperatures that are too cold, a lack of menstrual care products, not recognizing people's religious dietary needs and a lack of access to doctors. Carter, favored to then lead the Congressional Black Caucus, said Karen and Charlotte's case provided a close-to-home example of why ICE needs to be dismantled and replaced with an agency focused on providing a clear path to citizenship for immigrants. "ICE, as we know it, cannot exist," Carter said. He said he was optimistic that House Democrats would be able to implement substantial oversight of the administration's mass deportation campaign once the majority their party won in both congressional chambers takes effect in January.

The political stakes have widened beyond one family. The Guardian reported that tactics associated with the administration's mass deportation campaign, which much of the US public has come to see as cruel or overly aggressive, were widely considered a decisive factor in the 3 November midterm elections that cost the Republican Party control of Congress for the last two years of the president's term.
