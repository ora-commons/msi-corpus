## Noncollege adults and white and Hispanic men show the sharpest under-30 shifts

The 2026 movement among young voters ran through both party switching and abstention. The AP Voter Poll by SSRS found that 16% of young 2024 Trump voters flipped directly to a Democrat, while many others stayed home. Circle at Tufts University, a center that studies youth voting, preliminarily estimated youth turnout at 27%, matching the 2022 midterms. “I cannot imagine a future election in which campaign managers and politicians don’t have young people in mind,” said Circle Executive Director Leela Strong.

Within the under-30 electorate, the AP poll showed the change was not uniform. Americans ages 18 to 29 swung toward House Democrats by more than 20 percentage points compared with 2024, the sharpest political realignment of any age group. The shift was sharpest among young adults without a college degree and among white and Hispanic men under 30, groups that had helped return Trump to power in 2024. Young voters cited economic pressures in turning on Trump's party, and their frustration over traditional milestones of adulthood moving out of reach helped drive the change. Along the way, they positioned themselves as a potential new force in American politics.

Half of voters under 30 said they were financially worse off than two years earlier, according to the AP Voter Poll, a higher share than any other age group. They were also more likely than their elders to say they had suffered hardship because of inflation and to blame rising prices on Trump's policies. Young voters stocked shelves despite having a college degree, paid $90 to fill a pickup's gas tank — vastly more than a year earlier — and could not afford a plane ticket home for Thanksgiving or even a meal at the fast-food chain where they worked.

Unemployment for Americans ages 18 to 29 averaged 7% so far in 2026 and remained roughly 1 percentage point above prepandemic levels. Separately, wage growth for early-career workers ages 16 to 24 had decelerated to its slowest pace in over a decade, according to Federal Reserve Bank of Atlanta data. For broader context, [FRED](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/FRBATLWGT3MMAUMHWGO), the Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis's data service, listed the Atlanta Fed Wage Growth Tracker at 3.9% as of Nov. 7, 2026. “Young people are feeling that the ground has shifted out from under them,” said University of Michigan economics and public-policy professor Betsey Stevenson. “The economy has not been trustworthy in delivering a predictable path.”

Gavin Jacobsen, 23, cast his first ballot this year, voting for Democrats across the board by mail in rural Jackson, Minn., where he grew up. Until this year, he said, no candidate or party represented him. “The Democrats are finally hearing our voices and starting to cater and campaign on issues that actually affect young people and working-class Americans,” Jacobsen said.

Jacobsen's vote was motivated by roughly $120,000 in student loans and by his family's struggle to get by. His father, a high-school teacher who coaches three sports for extra income, and his stepmother, a dental hygienist, could not rationalize the $500 ticket to fly him home from school in Los Angeles for Thanksgiving. “In the richest country in the world, my parents are working multiple jobs and can’t afford to eat with their son,” he said. “That’s a huge problem.” Jacobsen worries about finding a job when he completes a sports business graduate program in June, especially after watching his roommate struggle to land a full-time job in the five months since graduating. Many of his friends back in Minnesota are Trump supporters who told him they sat out the midterms because of the president's unmet promises.

Joshua Evans, a 26-year-old in Kannapolis, N.C., voted for Trump in 2020 and 2024 but skipped Tuesday's election because he felt let down by Republicans' failure to use their control of the White House and Congress to make good on campaign promises. “The Republican party took for granted their base of young Gen Z men,” he said. Evans took on more than $40,000 in student loans for a journalism and communications degree but has not found work in his chosen field. He now stocks shelves at a big-box retailer for $21 an hour and puts close to $1,000 a month toward his debt. He rents a house with his sister and views eventual homeownership as out of reach.

Evans said he did not expect to become a millionaire under Trump, but he did expect the president to work to bring down gas and grocery prices. Instead, Evans watched the war in Iran push them higher. Evans still considers himself a Republican but has not decided whether to vote for one in the next election.

Young men's economic prospects have trailed women's in recent years amid declining male college enrollment, the automation of blue-collar work and job growth largely concentrated in female-dominated fields such as healthcare. Republicans tapped that anxiety to pull young men rightward in 2024, while Democrats reversed that momentum on Tuesday with an economic pitch of their own. “Young men are the new swing voters in American politics,” said Richard Reeves, president of the American Institute for Boys and Men. Democrats focused on the group's stalled economic progress rather than cultural debates that cast men as the problem in American society. “They focused on the issues young men care about,” Reeves said.

Just 42% of under-30 voters who supported Trump in 2024 now approve of his job performance, according to the AP Voter Poll. Nearly one in five young Trump voters said their vote on Tuesday was to express opposition to the president.

Beyond inflation, Democrats tapped into concerns about the physical and financial footprint of the artificial-intelligence boom. Some young people see data-center build-outs driving up energy costs, competing for land where houses could be built and signaling diminishing prospects for high-paying jobs. Xavier Sepesi, an 18-year-old college freshman and bank teller in Kansas City, Kan., cast his first vote ever for Democrats after seeing his local race polarize around data centers. “I don’t see them benefiting us as a community or the planet at all,” Sepesi said.

Sepesi, who is Hispanic and lives in a diverse working-class neighborhood, watched friends and bank customers grapple with the deportation of loved ones under stepped-up immigration enforcement. While his own family was not directly targeted, seeing families separated reinforced his decision to back Democrats. “I just don’t think that was right,” he said.

In the suburbs north of Columbus, Ohio, Grayson McCartney, 19, and Abby Brookover, 18, also voted for the first time on Tuesday, for the Democratic ticket. McCartney, who works at Arby's, would like to attend college but cannot imagine how to afford it, and he is trying to save to rent his own apartment but cannot afford current prices. “I can barely even get fuel for my truck to make it to work there and back,” he said. McCartney would like elected officials to provide more financial support for young people to attend college and to offer more loan forgiveness. Brookover is paying her own way through Ohio Wesleyan with a scholarship and a loan. She remembers better times under the Obama administration, when her parents were not struggling as much. “The spike in gas prices, the spike in food. It’s so expensive. We can’t eat at Arby’s anymore,” Brookover said.

If Democrats fail to make improvements on the economic issues young people care about before the next presidential cycle, the youth electorate — especially its men — could desert them just as quickly. “The very swinginess of this group should make everyone cautious,” Reeves said. “You don’t win them permanently. You have to keep winning them.”
