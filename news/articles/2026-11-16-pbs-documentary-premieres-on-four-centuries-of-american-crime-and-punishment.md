## Filmmaker connects colonial penalties to present-day incarceration debate

"Crime and Punishment in America," a four-part PBS documentary scheduled to premiere Nov. 16, traces the evolution of policing, prisons and the criminal justice system across four centuries of American history. Directed by Lynn Novick and executive produced by Ken Burns, the series examines how Americans have defined crime, determined punishment and wrestled with justice and fairness. It combines historical inquiry with testimony from people who have been incarcerated and people who have worked within the justice system. The premiere was set for 8 p.m. Eastern and 7 p.m. Central, with local listings available.

Novick said the history is intended to illuminate current debates, including how the United States became the world's leading incarcerator. She traced the system's roots to laws and punishment inherited from England and said Americans gradually tinkered with the inherited approach without changing its central focus. The country, she said, has tended to concentrate on what to do after someone commits a crime rather than addressing the root causes of crime. "And I think our approach has been proven to be deeply flawed."

Novick said the United States has the highest incarceration rate in the world, while the documentary says the country once held roughly 20 percent of the world's incarcerated population. The system she described is spread across 50 states, the federal government, 3,000 counties and more than 18,000 police departments. Novick said each entity operates somewhat independently and contributes to how justice is administered, what prisons look like, how people arrive there and what happens while they are confined.

Novick said that, as law professor and legal scholar James Forman tells viewers in the film, "there's no one reason that we got here." Novick said undoing overincarceration will require many small decisions. She said the United States is "out of whack with every other peer nation by orders of magnitude" and that it arrived at that point over about 20 to 25 years rather than overnight.

Novick said there were no prisons or penitentiaries in the form known today before the American Revolution. People accused of crimes were held in jail until their cases were settled, then subjected to punishment and released if they survived. The penalties she listed included execution, branding, whipping, torture, and confinement in stocks or a pillory.

Around the Revolution, Novick said, many of the founding fathers wanted a different response for citizens of the new republic. The penitentiary they proposed was meant to hold people convicted of crimes so they could repent, serve a period of time and return to society reformed. In Novick's account, the idea that incarceration could serve as both punishment and rehabilitation was new, and Americans invented it.

The film supplements archives and statistics with what Novick called the experience of people who have borne witness. Novick said those accounts include men, women and children who have been incarcerated, along with people who worked in prisons and other justice institutions and tried to improve them. "What does this really mean if you were convicted of a crime in 1870 and sent to a chain gang, sent to a prison?" Novick asked, describing the question she and the filmmakers use to connect institutional history with individual experience.

She asked viewers to consider both people harmed by serious crime and loved ones who committed it. "Do we believe in redemption for everybody? Do we believe in second chances? Do we believe in hope? Do we believe in human dignity?" Novick asked. She also tied those questions to equal justice in the national creed and asked whether the criminal justice system is operating as it should.

Novick said filmmakers took a highlight reel from the series into prisons and jails around the country. The excerpts covered correction officers, victims and what they experience and do or do not receive from the system, and the history of incarceration. Incarcerated viewers told the filmmakers the material was hard to watch and painful, made them angry at a system they felt had treated them unfairly, and brought regret for mistakes that led to confinement. They also said they had not known the history presented in the film, as Novick had not known it when she began working on the project. "So I think knowledge is power, and knowing where we came from and how we got here is helpful to go forward," Novick said.
