## Bill would broaden project exemptions and shorten feedback and appeal timelines

Vermont’s business, clean-energy and conservation groups are divided over the Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act as it moves to the U.S. House of Representatives after the U.S. Senate passed the legislation Nov. 19. Introduced Sept. 30, the nearly 500-page measure is sponsored by Sens. Shelley Moore Capito (R-W.Va.) and Sheldon Whitehouse (D-R.I.), with Sens. Mike Lee (R-Utah), the Energy and Natural Resources chair, and Martin Heinrich (D-N.M.) also sponsoring it. Its stated aims are to modernize project permitting and advance energy and transmission-line development.

Vermont’s congressional delegation is split. U.S. Sens. Bernie Sanders (I-Vt.) and Peter Welch (D-Vt.) voted against the legislation in the Senate. U.S. Rep. Becca Balint (D-Vt.) released a statement shortly after Senate passage urging House colleagues to reject the legislation in favor of more “meaningful” permit reform protecting tribal sovereignty, historic preservation and the environment. “This bill sacrifices environmental protections in the name of accelerating energy deployment and advancing infrastructure projects,” Balint wrote. “What’s more, there are too many giveaways to oil and gas development, undermining our efforts to transition to clean domestic energy deployment.”

Some of the legislation’s controversial provisions would broaden exemptions for projects from review under the National Environmental Protection Act, the Endangered Species Act, the Clean Water Act and the National Historic Preservation Act of 1966. The bill would also truncate timelines for public and tribal feedback and legal appeals against federally funded projects. Conservation, historic-preservation and tribal groups say the rollback of bedrock laws should not come at the expense of development.

Developers, the business community and energy-transition advocates say the legislation would expedite energy, housing and other infrastructure projects delayed by prolonged permitting and make those projects more affordable. “A strong permitting process should provide meaningful opportunities while also ensuring decisions are made within a reasonable timeline,” said Amy Spear, the president of the Vermont Chamber of Commerce. “Those objectives shouldn’t be mutually exclusive.” The chamber signed on to a letter of support for the bill spearheaded by the U.S. Chamber of Commerce.

Spear said the reforms would create a predictable and efficient regulatory environment, prevent costly project delays and allow developers to build more housing, affordable energy and modern infrastructure. She said public participation is part of responsible development and that the legislation can be amended to improve the balance between participation and deadlines.

Some environmentalists think streamlining federally funded projects is necessary to hasten a green-energy transition. “It’s a compromise bill,” said Jennifer Durgin, Vermont state coordinator for Citizens’ Climate Lobby, who said the legislation had received bipartisan support. Durgin said the trade-offs were worth expanded green-energy projects and reduced greenhouse gas emissions. “It’s also important to remember that no action is an action,” Durgin said. “The consequences of not taking any action mean that the planet gets warmer every year. It continues to drive erratic and extreme weather, such as the catastrophic floods, you know, that we have experienced in Vermont.”

A consistent, timely and predictable process that caps legal challenges to energy projects at 150 days, compared with a window of several years, would benefit Vermont’s green-energy developers and bring affordability for energy consumers, said Norwich Solar CEO Jim Merriam. Merriam said the bill had gained bipartisan support because developers had experienced a “misuse of the system” by opponents and appeals that otherwise would have allowed projects to proceed, delaying construction and adding unforeseen costs.

Jonathan Dowds, deputy director of Renewable Energy Vermont, said the organization supports the legislation conceptually but that federal permitting reform is “not likely to have an immediate impact on the Vermont energy mix.” Dowds said state-level permitting reform is needed to roll out more clean-energy projects in Vermont.

Britta Tonn, the Vermont director of cultural resources for the engineering firm Vanasse Hangen Brustlin, Inc., called the bill “pretty devastating” for environmental assets and historic sites. Tonn said it would “gut” the National Historic Preservation Act and other federal regulations established decades earlier, allowing federal agencies to take a “top-down approach” regardless of the implications for local communities.

Zack Porter, executive director of the public lands advocacy organization Standing Trees, said the legislation presents a false dilemma by asking the public to choose modest emission reductions in exchange for weaker environmental laws. “We are being asked to give up, you know, decades and decades and decades of hard-fought measures to protect clean air, clean water, endangered species,” as well as “the public’s right to know what the government is doing and to weigh in,” Porter said.

The federal permitting process for energy projects is complex and could be improved, said Jenna Lapachinski, a field service representative with the Preservation Trust of Vermont, but the bill falls short of creating predictable development outcomes. Lapachinski favored investing in personnel to facilitate reviews with stakeholders at federal agencies that have been understaffed and underfunded, especially after massive job cuts under the Trump administration. The bill “puts a lot of power into the hands of the federal agencies to make unilateral decisions about what’s an adverse impact,” Lapachinski said. “We’re thrusting even more responsibility onto agencies that are just, you know, despite their best efforts, probably not well positioned to take that on.”

The bill was “rushed through the lame duck session” in the Senate, said Brad Campbell, president of the Conservation Law Foundation. Campbell said the sweeping changes should be considered by the newly elected Congress after lawmakers are sworn in. If enacted in its current form, legislation barring legal challenges during project development would likely spawn litigation against the federal government, Campbell said.

The amendment the Senate approved earlier this month included provisions allowing tribal groups and states to serve as lead agencies on projects and requiring data-center operators to pay their share of electricity costs. Tonn said data centers would also benefit from streamlined permitting. Tribal advocacy groups said the amendment did not address concerns about meaningful consultation; the bill would shorten the timeline for consulting tribes and reduce protections for sacred sites.

Vermont is not currently home to a federally recognized tribe, but southern parts of the state are the ancestral homelands of the Mohican tribe, and the tribe is consulted by federal agencies on projects in that area, said Vermont State Historic Preservation Officer Laura Trieschmann. Welch wrote in a statement to VTDigger that the nation needs to build clean-energy infrastructure more efficiently to mitigate climate change and reduce Vermonters’ utility bills. His statement also said the package undercuts the National Environmental Protection Act and contains “provisions that could open the government up to unprecedented financial liability.” Welch introduced amendments protecting judicial power to stop projects when environmental violations are found and excluding data centers from permitting reforms. The Senate did not adopt those amendments before its final vote.

Giovanna Peebles, the former longtime Vermont state archeologist and state historic preservation officer, warned of the potential loss of historical resources. “I think that there would be an incredible loss of resources if this bill were passed,” Peebles said. “It would be an existential threat to historic preservation, and certainly to archeology.” Peebles cited the proposed 16-mile limited-access Chittenden County Circumferential Highway, which was scrapped after historic-preservation review led to the archaeological discovery of dozens of indigenous sites. Peebles said similar discoveries could yet be made in Vermont.

Trieschmann said she expects reviews of federally funded projects to be minimal if the bill becomes law in its current form because of limitations on what counts as an adverse impact. She said even the most obvious historic resources could be lost because the federal government would not be looking.

Historic preservationists previously found success after weighing in on a plan to demolish a “cornerstone” historic former hotel in St. Johnsbury through the review process established by the National Historic Preservation Act. The building instead was restored and converted into apartments for $17 million in 2022. “We historic preservationists know the significance of climate change and weatherization, and how our buildings live and breathe,” Trieschmann said. “We want to be part of those conversations, and I think there’s ways to make it happen where there’s everyone at the table.”

The legislation remained before the U.S. House as supporters emphasized predictable deadlines and development while opponents focused on public participation and federal safeguards for environmental, historic and sacred resources.
