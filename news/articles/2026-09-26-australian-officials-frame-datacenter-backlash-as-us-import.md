## Australia plans renewable-energy mandate for new datacenters

Australian politicians and datacenter industry leaders are pushing back against a growing wave of community opposition to AI datacenter construction, framing the concerns as an import from a US debate where opposition has run deeper and earlier. Australian state governments have welcomed datacenter investment and in some cases overridden local planning approvals, given the scale of the projects. Legislation for the planned national framework is not expected to reach Parliament before 2027.

Like the United States, Australia is seeing community resentment grow in response to a surge in the construction of massive AI datacenters. Pro-datacentre officials and executives argue the scale of Australia's buildout is smaller, more regulated and therefore less damaging than that in the US, and they say the burgeoning negative reaction is being tainted and inflamed by a heated US debate that has arrived down under via social media feeds. The backlash that began in earnest in the United States in 2025 reached Australia slowly at first, then exploded into national conversation during 2026, producing calls for a national moratorium, community protests, three government inquiries, state frameworks under development and federal moves to set the rules for how datacenters are built and how they consume energy and water.

In July, Victoria's then-minister for economic growth, Steve Dimopoulos, said his state was second only to New South Wales in the number of datacenters and was outstripping Australia's most populous state in growth for new centres. Dimopoulos attributed the growing resentment to US social media influence. "We're seeing some US states move to ban new large datacentres. And those campaigns and mega-developments in the US shape public opinion in Australia," Dimopoulos said. "Because when it's on your phone, you think it's right here, or you think it might just come here." Dimopoulos declared: "We are not the United States. Our planning, energy and water systems are much more carefully monitored, managed and regulated."

Industry leaders have also pushed back against the backlash, calling it misleading and disingenuous.

Belinda Dennett, head of Data Centres Australia, said in a March speech that "Australia's datacentre debate is being fought on America's facts." Dennett said everyday reactions to datacenters range from curiosity to confusion and fear, and that the news around the sector was "alarming." "The industry is depicted as energy vampires, water hogs and job killers," she said. "The problem is that most of those headlines originate in the United States, where the industry is operating at a completely different scale, under completely different market rules. They are being transplanted wholesale into Australian debates as though the contexts were interchangeable." Dennett said the US has 5,400 datacenters with 53.7 gigawatts of total capacity, compared with 252 in Australia with 1.4 gigawatts of capacity; she noted that measurements of the number of US datacenters vary.

"When people read about datacentres straining the grid, driving up bills and consuming vast amounts of water, they are reading about America's challenges, applied uncritically to an Australian market that bears almost no resemblance to it," Dennett said.

Per capita, Australia — with a population of 28 million — has just under one datacenter per 100,000 people, compared with about 1.5 per 100,000 people in the US, which has a population of 342.7 million. The pace of datacenter construction is increasing rapidly in Australia, with several large facilities of one gigawatt or more of capacity currently in planning.

Trending videos on TikTok and Instagram highlight major local community concerns, including a 2.4 gigawatt centre planned for outer Melbourne and a 2 gigawatt gas-powered centre in the Northern Territory. Rob Nicholls, a professor and senior research associate in digital policy at the University of Sydney, said the Northern Territory project's electricity demand is "the equivalent of 2m households' annual electricity consumption." The Northern Territory's population is just over 265,000.

Some experts find the backlash justified in following the US example. "The social media input from the US is very vocal and rationally based: 'It's going to increase the cost of electricity and it's going to increase the cost of water and I don't get a benefit from it.' And that's a rational position," Nicholls said. Nicholls said the hurdle facing the Australian government is explaining to constituents how the issues in the United States differ from local ones.

The Australian government has planned to override the states and ban the use of gas to power datacenters, with new rules requiring operators to bring as much new renewable energy into the grid as the centre would otherwise consume. The legislative delay has prompted calls for Australia to follow the lead of New York and implement a moratorium on new development until proper rules are in place. Until then, two state inquiries and one federal inquiry are examining the sector, alongside a steady stream of new development applications that have generated local pushback.

Many of Australia's datacenters are US-owned. Federal assistant minister for technology Andrew Charlton said this week that Australians may be cynical about datacenters in their communities because many of the operating companies are not Australian. Microsoft and Amazon operate several facilities, and OpenAI and Anthropic are pursuing their own developments.

"Simply building datacentres in Australia does not necessarily mean that Australia captures the economics of AI," Charlton said. "We can supply the land, we can supply the electricity, we can host the machines, and still find that much of the value — and many of the economic rents — flow offshore." Charlton called for datacenter providers wanting to build in Australia to enable access to compute for Australian start-ups, researchers and not-for-profits on favourable terms, and flagged that Australia could develop its own AI capability based on open-weight models. "We risk becoming dependent on imported intelligence itself," Charlton said.
