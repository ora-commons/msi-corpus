## Postelection interviews identify party splits, economic concerns and cultural rhetoric

Democrats swept every statewide seat in Texas in the 2026 election. James Talarico defeated Ken Paxton in the Senate race, Gov. Greg Abbott lost to state representative Gina Hinojosa, and Lt. Gov. Dan Patrick lost to state representative Vikki Goodwin. Talarico won the Senate race by 12 points, a nearly 26-point swing from Trump’s 2024 victory and the largest shift of any Senate race in the country. Democrats now control a state with the world’s eighth-largest economy and 40 presidential electoral votes, the largest bloc after California. Without Texas, a Republican path to the White House would be precarious.

Every Texas county swung to the left, some by more than 70 points. Trump had won the state in 2024 by nearly 14 points, with 56.1% of the vote, and had carried almost every county. The Associated Press county-by-county Senate comparison, with data as of Nov. 16 at 5:00 p.m. ET, showed Talarico winning a larger share than Harris did in 2024 and Paxton winning a smaller share than Trump. Counties along the Mexico border turned much more blue, while margins in Republican strongholds narrowed.

The shift extended across demographic and regional groups. Latino voters, who had moved to the right in recent cycles, swung 37 points leftward. White voters with a college degree moved 15 points in that direction, while those without a degree moved 13 points. Young voters moved 54 points left, seniors 12 points, and voters in small towns and rural areas 22 points. The economy was the top issue for 41% of Texas voters in the last two elections; 65% of those voters backed Trump in 2024, compared with 57% for Talarico this year. Hundreds of thousands of voters with a Republican primary history stayed home.

“I’m 65 and have never not voted Republican, and this was the one that broke me,” said Tom Giovanetti, president of the Institute for Policy Innovation, a Dallas-based conservative think tank. “The party has asked too much of me.”

Texas had once been a Democratic state before shifting during the 1990s. Rick Perry, first elected to the state House as a Democrat, changed his party affiliation in 1989 before eventually becoming governor. George W. Bush defeated Ann Richards, the state’s last Democratic governor, in 1994. By 2003, Republicans controlled both legislative chambers and all statewide offices.

Postelection interviews with Texas voters and political observers — both Republican and Democrat — said right-wing interests, intraparty fighting and the rise of MAGA had made the state GOP more extreme and alienated former supporters. The interviewees said the party had rejected the conservative stalwarts who built it, ousted moderates, ignored key rural issues, turned off women and maligned Jews, Hindus, Muslims and other minorities. In their assessment, that combination gave Democrats a once-a-generation opportunity to win with moderate, disciplined messaging.

New forces had pushed the party to the right. Christian oil billionaires Tim Dunn and the brothers Dan and Farris Wilks pumped tens of millions into efforts to rid the party of Republicans they considered moderate or insufficiently loyal. Dunn has said the country was founded as a Christian nation and advocated for leaders to be Christian. Candidates he supported pushed to eliminate property taxes, restrict morning-after pills, eliminate vaccine requirements and make it a felony for doctors to provide gender-affirming care to young adults.

U.S. Rep. Dan Crenshaw, who lost his primary re-election bid to a candidate Dunn supported, called Dunn an agent of chaos dedicated to tearing down pillars of the GOP. “The name Bush is toxic here in Republican circles,” Crenshaw said of the right-wing voices. “All they want to do is attack the people who made Texas red in the first place.” Crenshaw and Tom Giovanetti, president of the Dallas-based Institute for Policy Innovation, said the GOP organization in Texas had increasingly been shaped by far-right forces and had become less focused on traditional fundraising as it targeted certain Republicans.

Abbott and Patrick alienated some Republicans, particularly in rural areas. After rural Republican legislators declined to support a statewide school vouchers plan in 2023, Abbott supported primary challengers against GOP incumbents, many of whom were ousted. Their successors helped pass the vouchers last year. Texas Agriculture Commissioner Sid Miller, who lost his primary re-election bid to an Abbott-endorsed challenger, said school vouchers threatened to gut rural public schools and infuriated many rural Texans. He also criticized Abbott’s prior support for data centers, which Abbott reversed after political backlash, and Patrick’s push last year to ban THC products, many of which the state health department banned this year. Miller said the THC ban alienated some veterans’ groups and other Republicans who used THC. “It was death by a thousand cuts,” Miller said.

Top Abbott adviser Dave Carney rejected the idea that school vouchers or other Texas-specific issues drove the losses. He said Texans were voting — or not voting — based on the national economy.

Party rhetoric in recent years had also become increasingly hostile toward some religious and racial groups. Bo French, a former Tarrant County GOP chairman who ran for the Railroad Commission, which regulates the oil-and-gas industry, posted on social media a poll asking whether Jews or Muslims posed a bigger threat to America. In September, he posted a photo of South Asian University of Texas students celebrating a football win with the message, “the problem is now obviously far worse than anyone imagined.” Republicans including Abbott, Paxton and Patrick continued to support French, who lost.

For years, Giovanetti had let a local Republican women’s club use his think tank’s offices for its monthly meetings. He became disturbed, he said, when the meetings began focusing on what the group called the threat of Islam, and he eventually told them they could no longer hold meetings there. Giovanetti also noticed that rank-and-file conservatives had stopped attending Republican precinct meetings and conventions.

Texas had already deployed troops to the border and built segments of a state border wall. When dozens of Texas GOP lawmakers visited Washington, D.C., last year, Trump adviser Stephen Miller berated them for not being aggressive enough on immigration and complained about “RINOs,” according to two people who were there. A White House official denied that Miller had berated the lawmakers and called the meeting positive and focused on “eliminating obstacles” to Trump administration policies. The official said, “There was a general frustration among those in the room because several Republicans had been joining forces with Democrats to obstruct policies.”

Fifty-eight percent of Texas voters disapproved of aggressive enforcement strategies, which they ranked as their No. 3 issue in exit polling. Planned construction of border barriers through Big Bend National Park was also unpopular.

Democrats had long claimed Texas was ripe to flip, but previous attempts failed. This time, the campaigns of Talarico and Hinojosa emphasized the divide between working people and the ultrawealthy. “It’s economic populism,” said Amy Friedman, Hinojosa’s campaign manager. “We talked a lot about it being a post-partisan campaign.”

Hinojosa entered the governor’s race after more prominent candidates declined to challenge Abbott. Her campaign stretched limited funds with advertisements against data centers and Flock cameras, which were proliferating across Texas. Talarico’s campaign sent targeted mail to voters living within several miles of proposed data centers. Morris Katz, a Democratic strategist, said during a panel at a recent Crooked Media event that Talarico supporters paid so people searching online with queries such as “I think my husband is cheating on me” would receive sponsored results about Paxton’s alleged infidelity.

Talarico’s campaign manager Seth Krasne said the candidate’s focus on the cost of living and corruption mattered more than those “minor tactics.” Talarico capitalized on the divided Republican base. He also reached out to Texans who had previously voted for outgoing Sen. John Cornyn, whom Trump did not support in the primary, and highlighted a string of Republican endorsements. “We were setting out to break the longest losing streak of any political party in American politics, and we never had the luxury to write anyone off,” Krasne said.

Much Republican messaging focused on cultural concerns and other topics that had resonated in 2024, including transgender issues and assertions that Shariah law threatened Texas. On Election Day, that messaging failed to resonate. “The noncrazy people not only stopped showing up to the precinct meetings and the state convention, they stopped showing up at the ballot box,” Giovanetti said.

Cameron Cushman, a conservative evangelical Christian and the 46-year-old head of a North Texas economic development group, said he was moved by Talarico’s talk of the Gospel, turned off by Paxton’s mounting ethical controversies and fed up with Texas Republicans. In May, when Cushman and his wife walked into the school across the street from their house to vote in the Texas Republican primary runoff, a poll worker reminded him that he had already voted in the Democratic primary in March. “You did what?” Cushman recalled, his wife nearly shouting. He said he sheepishly told her he had voted in March for Democratic Senate candidate James Talarico, which under state rules made him ineligible to vote in the Republican runoff. This month, he cast his vote for Talarico again — the first time he had ever supported a Democrat in a statewide general election. “People switched from their normal, ‘I’m going to vote red every time, all the way down the ticket,’ to ‘I can’t do this anymore,’” Cushman explained. “These guys have changed. Their policies have changed. And some of them are just terrible people.”

Cushman was among the hundreds of thousands of Texas voters responsible for the state’s political flip.

Mujeeb Kazi, who runs an umbrella group representing 124 mosques and Islamic groups in North Texas, said he felt personally betrayed by Texas Republicans. A Kashmiri immigrant who came to Texas nearly 30 years ago, Kazi had supported Trump twice and Abbott, had raised money for Republicans and said he felt closer to the Republican Party on economic and social issues. He said some of the Republicans he supported began targeting Muslims as the South Asian population in North Texas suburbs increased.

Texas Republicans in Congress launched a “Sharia-Free America” caucus. One local mayor whom Kazi considered a personal friend issued a proclamation rejecting Shariah law in his city. Abbott and Paxton also launched investigations into a planned development by a popular Plano mosque, accusing it of discriminating against non-Muslims and committing other regulatory violations. Kazi was stunned. He voted for Democrats this year because of what he considered hatred against his community. “Now, all of a sudden, we’re being used as a punching bag community for their election gains,” he said. “Look at the level of betrayal they have done to us.”

Andrea Coker, who works for a North Texas advocacy group and previously worked for elected Republicans, said Texas conservatives like herself were disappointed that candidates overlooked fuel prices, the Iran war and housing affordability. She backed Talarico. “I don’t have a clue what Ken Paxton wanted to do on infrastructure when he got to Washington,” Coker said. “But I know he’s against trans kids, and that just doesn’t affect my life. And I don’t think it affects 99.9% of Texans’ lives.”

Along the border, Starr County, which was 97% Hispanic, went for Trump in 2024. Key to that Republican success were locals such as Ross Barrera, who chaired the local GOP; Pat Saenz, who organized weekly Trump trains; and Cornelio Garza, who swayed friends and neighbors to support Trump. This year, Barrera cast a ballot for Talarico because he considered Paxton too flawed to support. Garza voted for Democrats across the ballot after hearing Talarico’s message on education and becoming angry about people killed by immigration agents.

The Texas GOP had built a mid-decade redistricting effort around an assumption that its gains with Hispanic voters in the Rio Grande Valley would hold, but it ended up losing a seat. The economy was a prime concern in the low-income region dependent on trade. Local building groups had spoken out about aggressive immigration enforcement snagging workers and halting growth.

Though he continued to support Republicans, Saenz was unsurprised when his largely rural county of 66,000 people swung 76 points leftward from Trump to Talarico. “The betrayal was everything—the war and the gas and the fajitas,” Saenz said about rising costs. “I still voted red, but in my heart I knew other people would vote blue.”

The Texas GOP announced last week that a committee would spend six weeks reviewing grassroots outreach, candidates and fundraising. The review comes as postelection accounts point to economic pressures, party tensions and cultural messaging as factors in the result, while Carney has said voters were responding primarily to the national economy.
