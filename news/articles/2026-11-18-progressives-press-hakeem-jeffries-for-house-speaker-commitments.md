## Medicare for All, ICE abolition and military spending test Jeffries

House Democrats nominated Hakeem Jeffries for speaker on Wednesday, placing the House minority leader at the center of a debate over what the incoming majority will prioritize. Norman Solomon wrote in The Guardian that dozens of progressives who will be in office, including at least eight democratic socialists, will need to figure out how to deal with Jeffries after campaigning against the Democratic establishment that he exemplifies. Solomon urged House progressives to insist that Jeffries make an ironclad commitment to allow a floor vote on Medicare for All, which he said is now favored by two-thirds of the US public as a whole, and urged activists nationwide to pressure congressional Democrats to insist that Jeffries get behind efforts to abolish Immigration and Customs Enforcement. Solomon wrote that the contest will test whether Jeffries can be pushed to the left by fellow Democrats.

Jeffries, 56, is set to become the first Black speaker of the House. He worked as a corporate lawyer and as a state legislator before representing a largely working-class district in New York City, and he was in only his third term in the House when he became chair of the House Democratic Caucus in 2018. The Economist profile quoted by Solomon called him "nearly as moderate as a safe-seat Democrat gets." Five years ago, Jeffries said: "There will never be a moment where I bend the knee to hard-left democratic socialism."

His early congressional record included work challenging police brutality and racism in the criminal justice system. After an unsuccessful 2015 bill for a national ban on police use of chokeholds, Jeffries co-authored the First Step Act, a reform measure aimed at improving federal prison conditions and reducing sentencing inequities for drug offenses that became law in 2018. As he climbed through congressional ranks, Solomon wrote, Jeffries earned a reputation as a fast-rising corporate Democrat.

Fundraising has been a persistent source of tension between Jeffries and the left. Solomon wrote that over the years, Jeffries had become even more oriented to defending the interests of wealthy Americans and large corporations. The Intercept, as cited by Solomon, reported that "Jeffries is heavily backed by big money and corporate PACs" and that "Less than 2% of his fundraising comes from small donors, who contribute less than $200." During his eighth year in the House, American Prospect reported that Jeffries was "the leading congressional recipient of hedge fund money." The outlet said he banked "$1.1 million from the financial sector, real estate interests, and insurance industry in the 2019–2020 cycle" and wrote, "From the outset, he has governed with those interests at heart."

His importance to donors increased after Democrats lost control of the House in November 2022 and Nancy Pelosi left the speakership, with Jeffries becoming minority leader. He was paired with the party's longtime Senate leader Chuck Schumer, whom the column described as a record-setting recipient of Wall Street donations. Solomon wrote that the duo had been a mismatch with most Democratic voters. Hours after Jeffries delivered his midterm victory speech two weeks ago, Sludge reported that since early 2025 he had received "financial backing from executives at Anduril and Palantir, cryptocurrency industry interests, and major Wall Street investors — business interests with a stake in how aggressively the new majority pursues corporate beneficiaries of Trump's presidency." Federal Election Commission filings showed that Anduril's chief executive and chief operating officer gave $144,300 to Jeffries' fundraising committees, while a manager at Palantir contributed another $44,300.

In 2022, Jeffries teamed with Representative Josh Gottheimer — whom Solomon describes as one of the most zealous anti-progressive lawmakers in the Democratic caucus — to form Team Blue Pac, a group whose avowed goal was to defeat leftwing primary challengers to incumbents. Solomon cited a Rolling Stone headline describing the effort: "Top House Democrat Unveils Plan to Beat Back Progressive Rebellion."

On health care, Jeffries supported Medicare for All through 2021, and this summer told NBC's Meet the Press: "It's not legislation that I currently am cosponsoring or that I support." He did not answer a direct question, "Will you put Medicare for All up for a vote if you become speaker?" Solomon said Medicare for All is now favored by two-thirds of the US public as a whole and urged House progressives to demand an ironclad commitment allowing a floor vote. On immigration, Solomon said Jeffries had refused to support abolishing ICE and had instead declared, "We're committed to dramatically reforming ICE." The Democratic National Committee approved a resolution three months ago calling for the agency's abolition.

Solomon also said Jeffries is a longtime supporter of large military spending, that his opposition to the Iran war is narrowly tactical and that his backing of Israel has remained unequivocal. During Jeffries' first dozen years in Congress, from 2013 through 2024, pro-Israel groups gave him $1,741,230, according to the column, making him the fifth-biggest Democratic recipient of such funds in the House since 1990. Aipac's political action committee gave upward of $427,000 to his campaign and fundraising committees over the last two years. Solomon wrote that rank-and-file Democrats overwhelmingly favor an arms embargo on Israel. Four months ago, Jeffries sent a "dear colleague" letter opposing an amendment to block $3.3bn in military shipments and other aid to Israel; the next day, a record 103 House Democrats voted for the measure and 98 voted against.

Support from the left has not been uniform. Greg Casar, chair of the Congressional Progressive Caucus, said in May that he would support Jeffries for speaker, and Representative Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez did likewise in June. Solomon also wrote that many of the leftists about to become congressmembers have remained publicly noncommittal about the speaker choice. He urged progressives around the country to support them.

Melat Kiros, a 29-year-old attorney, is a democratic socialist on her way to the House from a Denver district. "I want to see an agenda, an actual plan for how we get things done," she said, adding: "What's your plan to actually get Medicare done? What's your plan to get these data centers under control and rein in these big tech companies, right? That's what the voters want to see. And if that's not a priority of yours, then I don't think you should be speaker."

A former Capitol Hill staffer familiar with the thinking of current and soon-to-be House members on the left told Solomon days ago: "Many progressives and incoming socialists are far from sold on Hakeem Jeffries. They're making clear that it's not personal or hostile, but they want real evidence that he's willing to fight for their priorities, rather than defaulting to the traditional Democratic leadership approach." Solomon described the dispute as a political culture clash on the left, saying the party's base had a very different view of Jeffries' fitness to be speaker than the outlook prevailing among Democrats at the Capitol.

Public opinion data cited by Solomon pointed in the same direction. A Reuters-Ipsos poll conducted midway through 2025 found that 62% of self-identified Democrats agreed that "the leadership of the Democratic party should be replaced with new people." A large majority of Democrats wanted elected officials to reduce "corporate influence," and 86% of Democrats said changing the federal tax code so wealthy Americans and large corporations pay more in taxes should be a priority. In the first two days after the election on Tuesday, more than 750,000 people signed a petition stating that "The speaker should represent the Democratic voters, not just the Democratic establishment" and that "Hakeem Jeffries is simply not the right choice."

The speakership carries substantial procedural authority. Solomon wrote that the speaker can largely determine committee assignments and, except in rare instances, prevent specific bills from ever reaching the House floor for a vote. A senior Democratic aide recently told him that Jeffries "has had a relatively light touch" compared with Pelosi and "could be amenable to significant and valuable concessions." Solomon argued that pressure and systematic organizing from grassroots progressives would be essential to stop Congress from continuing to be a stronghold of corporate power and militarism.
