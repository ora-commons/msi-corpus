## Capital Economics warns AI goods account for up to 80% of recent export growth

U.S. data-center investment has served as a major engine of growth for the global economy this year, particularly as the Iran war has created an energy crunch. The boom has driven strong exports of computer chips, server racks and electronic equipment from China, Taiwan and South Korea to meet computing demand in the United States. That leaves economies with few other signs of growth vulnerable to a pullback in AI spending.

The gains have spread through supply chains beyond China, Taiwan and South Korea. Assembly hubs in Vietnam and Mexico are seeing exports take off, while Malaysia, Thailand and Australia are building data centers of their own. European industrial giants are supplying the power and cooling systems that support the build-out. A slowdown in U.S. spending would therefore reach beyond American technology companies into hardware production, assembly, construction and equipment supply chains.

Market anxiety intensified after the Democrats' victory in the U.S. midterm elections, which raised concern that Congress would place limits on data-center development. The latest industry upheaval cited was an alleged spying scandal engulfing Nvidia, described as a linchpin of the AI ecosystem because of its deep pockets and advanced chips. Stocks have recovered some losses in recent days, moves that suggested many investors think the selloff was overdone, but key shares and indexes remain well below recent peaks.

The Nasdaq Composite closed Tuesday 10% below its year-to-date high. Japan's Nikkei index was down 16%, and South Korea's Kospi index, home to major memory-chip maker Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix, was down 42%. For now, the stock-market gyrations have not spilled into real economic activity, and global data continue to point to a strong AI boom.

Asian economies are still posting bumper export growth, with many gains driven by surging prices for chips and other hardware. CEIC data showed China's exports up 35% year over year in October. South Korea's were up 93%, while Japan, Taiwan, Singapore and other economies also posted double-digit gains. Capital Economics said global trade was on track for its best year of annual growth since the early 1990s era of hyperglobalization, excluding the large rebounds after Covid-19 and the 2008-09 financial crisis.

That performance comes with a concentration warning. Capital Economics said as much as 80% of recent export growth has been concentrated in AI-related goods, leaving global trade vulnerable to any change in U.S. data-center spending. China, South Korea and other major economies also have weak consumption, meaning a drop in trade would carry an outsize hit on growth. Marcel Thieliant, head of Asia-Pacific at Capital Economics, said in a report, “AI capex will turn from a major prop to a drag on world trade.”

Political and regulatory hurdles could drag on data-center investment. Rising interest rates or disappointing earnings could also restrain spending if AI firms' revenues fail to keep up with their outlays. The AI spending surge has already eclipsed past infrastructure booms such as railways and might struggle to maintain its breakneck pace even without external pressure.

Economists said the central uncertainty is the severity of any global fallout. “There is very little that can be done to prevent a global recession should the AI boom collapse,” said Frederic Neumann, chief Asia economist at HSBC in Hong Kong. A sustained stock-market decline could also crush smaller investors worldwide who bought technology stocks in anticipation of bumper returns. Overstretched budgets, meanwhile, mean governments have limited room to respond with more spending.

Some economists have looked to the dot-com collapse of the early 2000s as a yardstick for the possible costs of an AI blowup. U.S. business investment sank and stocks crashed, hurting consumer spending and crushing imports. According to Oxford Economics, U.S. imports of IT-related equipment fell 55% as a share of gross domestic product in two years from their mid-2000 peak. The drop hit Asian and other economies that supplied the goods.

Global trade plummeted in that episode, and world growth in 2001 collapsed to just 2%, a pace consistent with a global recession. The historical comparison was presented as a measure of possible costs rather than a settled forecast. Economists said the question for the current cycle is how severe the global consequences would be if AI investment slowed sharply or the boom became a full-blown bust.

The globalized financing of the AI boom could spread financial pain around the world in a bust, adding to the damage from a steep fall in trade. Gaurav Ganguly, head of international economics at Moody's Analytics in London, said the world would struggle to absorb a sharp AI stock slide on top of an oil shock, tariffs and rising interest rates. “The result could be a severe economic shock with global recessionary consequences,” he said.

For now, export data still show AI spending supporting growth rather than weighing on it. The same concentration that has lifted trade leaves China, South Korea and other economies exposed to a change in U.S. data-center budgets. Whether that support becomes a drag will depend on how quickly investment slows and whether stock-market turmoil reaches real economic activity.
