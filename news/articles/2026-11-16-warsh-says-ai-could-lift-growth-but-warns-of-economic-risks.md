## Fed chair skips inflation and rate outlook at AI conference

Kevin Warsh opened a one-day conference on AI and productivity at the Federal Reserve's San Francisco branch on Monday with an argument that the technology could reshape how fast economies innovate — and a warning that the same force could turn sharply against growth.

"What might set SI apart, as an economic influence, is that it could supercharge idea generation," Warsh said. "It appears to be changing the method — and, at least as important, the speed — of innovation. If idea generation broadens, growth could be sustained at a level substantially higher than the long-run average."

The Fed refers to AI as "super intelligence" following an executive order issued by President Donald Trump requiring federal agencies to use the term.

The chairman paired the upside case with a longer passage on downsides than he has delivered in previous speeches. "As policymakers, we have to be mindful that great opportunities rarely come risk free," he said. "And the risks in this case, if overlooked, can be highly disruptive and economically costly."

The specific risk Warsh named was a repricing. "After significant capital investment and stock market gains, expected returns could turn down and equity valuations could fall, leading to an economic slowdown and a more notable rise in unemployment," he said. Warsh added that Fed staff has begun evaluating different scenarios for how AI's impact may play out.

The conference is part of a task force Warsh set up to evaluate AI and its impact on the economy's efficiency. Warsh appointed five different task forces soon after taking the helm at the Fed.

Warsh did not address AI's impact on inflation, a top concern of many of his Fed colleagues, and he made no comment on what the Fed's next steps would be on interest rates. He also did not speak to recent government data showing inflation accelerated last month. That data led many of Warsh's colleagues on the rate-setting committee to signal support for additional interest rate hikes, including at the Fed's next meeting in December.

Several policymakers expressed concern last week that AI is fanning inflation because the buildout of AI infrastructure has sharply raised demand for computer chips, electronic components, electricity and copper. Those increases, in turn, have lifted the cost of smartphones, computers and other consumer electronics. Smartphone prices rose last month at the fastest pace on record, the government said last week.

Fed Vice Chair Philip Jefferson said Friday that AI's effect on rates could run through a channel other than prices. "If the new technology leads to permanently higher levels of productivity growth, it may increase firms' desire to invest and, hence, their demand for funding, raising the level of interest rates," Jefferson said. He added that if AI does accelerate economic growth, it could lead to higher interest rates over time, even if inflation declines.

Warsh's posture has shifted before. He has previously touted AI as a technology that could significantly boost the economy's efficiency, which would enable it to grow without worsening inflation — a scenario under which the Fed could potentially lower interest rates.
