## Nebraska Appleseed data show 1,000-plus denied care or lost coverage since May

Nebraska will not take part in the six-month "hold harmless" period the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services announced Friday, keeping in place the Medicaid work requirements the state began enforcing in May. The Nebraska Department of Health and Human Services, which oversees Medicaid implementation in the state, said it managed the transition effectively for eligible individuals and sees no need for the additional time. Nebraska Appleseed, a justice-focused advocacy organization, reports that the most recent available data show more than 1,000 Nebraskans have been denied care or have lost coverage since May.

The extension is intended to give state staff time to assess whether Medicaid recipients meet certain requirements or exclusions before the work requirements take effect, a cushion Nebraska says it does not need. Nebraska opted in on May 1 and became the first state to adopt the changes, leaving the state to enforce them without the federal buffer ahead of the original Jan. 1 implementation deadline.

The Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services announced the six-month "hold harmless" period on Friday, extending the deadline for states to roll out changes to Medicaid work requirements. The additional six months is intended to give state staff time to assess whether patients meet certain requirements or exclusions and how they need to proceed to get coverage as a result. It applies to people who may be required to work a certain number of hours each month while receiving Medicaid, and the original deadline to implement the changes was Jan. 1.

Nebraska is not among the states taking the extension. The state adopted the changes to Medicaid early, opting in on May 1 and becoming the first state to do so.

Jeffrey Powell, director of communications for the Nebraska Department of Health and Human Services, said in a Tuesday statement that the state managed the transition to the new requirements effectively for eligible individuals. "Our implementation has been successful and serves as a model for other states," Powell wrote. "Given Nebraska's strong implementation and the progress we have made, there is no need for the state to participate in the hold-harmless period," he wrote.

Advocates in the state dispute that assessment. Nebraska Appleseed reports that the latest data available, though two months old, shows more than 1,000 Nebraskans have been denied care or have lost coverage since May. Sarah Maresh, the organization's Healthcare Access Program Director, said the changes are confusing and deter eligible patients from even applying for coverage. "It's going to cause people to be sicker and more disabled," Maresh said at a Nov. 9 Omaha town hall revisiting the changes.

Most of the changes to Medicaid, passed under the One Big Beautiful Bill last year, apply to the Medicaid expansion group. New work requirements apply to able-bodied enrollees, who now have to log at least 80 hours a month of employment, work program attendance, volunteering or schoolwork.

The restrictions apply to Medicaid members between the ages of 19 and 64 who are not pregnant, do not have a disability, are U.S. citizens or meet immigration rules, and make less than 138% of the federal poverty level. In Nebraska, that is about $22,025 per year for one person and $45,540 for a family of four, according to the Department of Health and Human Services.

Critics of the program argue that cancer patients, pregnant people and other patients who should not have to work are falling through the cracks because of Nebraska's expedited adoption of the requirements.

People who spoke at the town hall said some Medicaid recipients have spent hours on the phone with the Department of Health and Human Services and often are disconnected before they can reach the help they need to explore what they are eligible for. Maresh said this is because the state does not have enough staff to handle the changes.

Nebraska's decision leaves the state enforcing the work requirements on the schedule it set, without the six months of review the federal pause would have provided. The state says it is confident in its approach and remains committed to ensuring the federal requirements are implemented effectively; advocates say the changes are costing Nebraskans coverage, and the most recent data available to them are two months old.
