## A six-day West Virginia shoot produced a film tracked near $1 million

The Asylum’s “The Odyssey” was filmed over six days in West Virginia with a cast of 10. Its budget represented 0.1% of the $250 million Christopher Nolan production, which was filmed over 91 days in six countries, from Iceland to Morocco. The Asylum version was led by Myrom Kingery, a former GE employee, rather than Matt Damon. It followed a journey home from the Trojan War that lasted days rather than the decade endured by Matt Damon’s Odysseus and pitted the hero against A.I.-generated monsters. It ran about 90 minutes, featured A.I.-generated monsters and was available on digital platforms for around $5.

In one scene, Kingery’s character tells three companions, “Today our journey begins with a cyclops or it ends before it begins. Now who’s with me?” before one of the men has his head bitten off.

The Asylum’s film had the home-video market to itself until Nov. 17, when Nolan’s three-hour “The Odyssey” left theaters for rental or purchase after its July opening. The timing put an around-$5 option in front of viewers while the larger production remained in theatrical release. The Asylum version used artwork showing a tentacle curling out of the sea. It found an audience even though some viewers did not realize they had paid for a mockbuster. Hundreds of comments on the film’s YouTube rental page ranged from “I should have watched the trailer first” to “This the Temu version” and “They got me yall.”

The Asylum’s lineup draws on demand for movies that major studios spend millions to market. That niche has helped the Burbank, Calif., studio survive near the bottom of the Hollywood market for decades. David Rimawi, one of the studio’s three partners, described the approach: “We’ve been hiding in the back alleys, making sure we don’t get trampled on, and jumping on the scraps as we see them,” he said.

Besides “The Odyssey,” which is based on an ancient tale in the public domain, the Asylum released numerous films this year with titles resembling major releases. They include “The Last Hail M.A.R.Y.,” a “Project Hail Mary” mockbuster; “Another Toy Story,” a “Toy Story 5” mockbuster; “Alien Disclosure Day,” a “Disclosure Day” mockbuster; and the coming “Doomsday Avengers,” an “Avengers: Doomsday” mockbuster.

The Asylum’s owners said the company has generally steered clear of legal trouble apart from occasional letters from studios’ lawyers. They said they often resolve complaints by tweaking a title and its font. The partners said they are not trying to trick people; they are capitalizing on existing interest and offering entertainment in the vicinity of the real thing. They said they did not feel bad for viewers who mistook Nolan’s three-hour film for the Asylum’s 90-minute, star-free film.

“The Odyssey” is on track to make close to $1 million, Rimawi said, including rental fees and revenue from distributors. He said the figure does not match what the studio earned from “Titanic II,” but it “really helps pay for some of the duds.”

The Asylum produces up to three movies a month. Mockbusters are one category in a slate heavy on aliens, sharks and disasters. That separate, reliable lane was recently hit by “Meteor Extinction” and “Arctic Annihilation.” The company only produces concepts its distributors ask for or approve, and each movie’s budget sits slightly below what the Asylum expects it to earn. “You can go out of business really quickly by spending more than you have and hoping that you’re going to find an audience,” co-owner Paul Bales said.

The Asylum started with DVDs in 1997, when eye-grabbing covers mattered at Blockbuster. It then supplied basic-cable movies to buyers including Syfy, whose broadcast of the 2013 “Sharknado” became a breakout hit for the studio. That film, about storm-tossed predators raining down on L.A., launched a seven-movie franchise that includes the coming “Sharknado: Origins.”

Netflix and other platforms initially stocked Asylum movies as cheap inventory, but streamers’ shift toward premium originals reduced demand. Demand surged again around the pandemic, when Pluto and Tubi offered channels of free, ad-supported movies, before cooling. The Asylum still puts out around 30 movies a year.

“It’s not because business is so good,” Bales said. “It’s really because the margins have become so slim.” To make the economics work, he said, the company relies on a certain kind of filmmaker: “People who don’t realize that it’s impossible to make a movie in six days.”

Director Marcel Walz, a German-born filmmaker who has made nine Asylum movies and specializes in low-budget horror such as “Ebenezer Scrooge: A Christmas Carol Horror,” a mockbuster for the new Johnny Depp release, was dispatched to West Virginia for a period saga, where a tax credit offset costs. Walz and a crew of 20 set up on the shores of a 55-acre lake that would stand in for the Mediterranean Sea. A 20-foot wooden boat stood in for Odysseus’s sailing vessel. “My first question was, can it swim?” Walz said. The boat cost $5,000. Icy temperatures and a looming storm limited its time on the water, where it was tied off to a dock at the Mountain Lake Club.

Lead actor Myrom Kingery, who has also been a mockbuster Thor, completed 44 script pages in one day to finish the boat scenes before the storm hit. One challenge was pretending to survive sea monsters sent by Poseidon. Another was: “Trying to get my lines out without chattering my teeth.”

After filming wrapped, staffers in California used A.I. to add creatures, a fleet of ships and stormy seas intended to blend Odysseus’s boat into the action. David Michael Latt, the Asylum partner in charge of production, said he was proud of how the technology had helped the team improve. “Look, we’ve made some crappy films, there’s no doubt about it, but for the most part, everyone goes in there with the highest hopes.”

Walz said the six-day production demonstrated what the model could accomplish: “If we can pull off a movie like this in six days, give us 20 days, give us a million. That would show what we can do.” The Asylum’s “The Odyssey” put that proposition to digital viewers while Nolan’s film was still in theaters, before its Nov. 17 move to rental or purchase.
