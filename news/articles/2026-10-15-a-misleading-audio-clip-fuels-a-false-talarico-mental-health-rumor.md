## A misleading audio clip fuels a false Talarico mental-health rumor

**Subtype:** fact

- A false claim that Democratic Texas Senate candidate James Talarico had been discharged from an Ohio mental-health facility after a breakdown spread through right-wing media and conservative influencers in early October before collapsing.
- The Guardian traces the conspiracy to a 47-second audio clip posted on X by conservative influencer Nick Sortor on October 11, derived from a phone call placed by self-described "right-wing provocateur" Rhein Amacher.
- The Bulwark obtained the full phone call and reported the purported employee repeatedly denied any record of Talarico at the facility; the Lindner Center of Hope later publicly stated he "has never received treatment" there.
- Talarico, who has type 1 diabetes, said he had been recovering from the flu at his Austin home; his campaign called Sortor's claim "a lie designed to promote a pathetic conspiracy theory."

## Talarico case shows both reach and limits of midterm disinformation

A conspiracy theory claiming that Democratic US Senate candidate James Talarico had suffered a mental health crisis and been discharged from a treatment center metastasized through right-wing social media and conservative outlets — with President Donald Trump offering his own claims at a San Antonio rally on October 7 — over roughly two weeks in early October before collapsing when the named facility publicly stated he had never been a patient there, the Guardian reported in an examination of the disinformation chain.

The Guardian's account traces the episode to a nine-day public absence by the Democratic nominee challenging Republican Ken Paxton. Talarico was not seen in public from late September, and his campaign was slow to explain why. On September 30, speaking by video at a Hispanic Business Council event, Talarico said he had been diagnosed with the flu at an urgent care facility in Dallas after "starting to feel kind of under the weather a few days ago" and would not appear in person because he had a fever and was still contagious. Talarico has type 1 diabetes, which the Guardian noted places him at higher risk for flu-related complications.

The vacuum filled quickly. On October 7, President Trump, speaking at a San Antonio rally supporting Paxton, called Talarico "Tala-freako" and claimed without evidence that the Democrat is vegan and "sick because he's been forced to eat meat for political reasons and he's throwing up all over the place." That same day, an X account under the username "Dan The Man, Always with a Plan" and a profile photo of a "Trump 2028" cap tweeted without evidence that "He had a mental health crisis. That is what I heard from someone who would know inside information."

On October 8, former Trump fundraiser Caroline Wren told Steve Bannon's War Room podcast, the Guardian reported, that "I'm hearing that he checked himself into a mental institution – that he had a full-blown, like, mental breakdown." On the same day, Paxton posted a "Wanted"-style image of Talarico and wrote that "Jimmy appears to be having a full-blown mental Tala-freak-out on the national stage," demanding "proof of life." On October 9, former Los Angeles mayoral candidate Spencer Pratt added his own claim: "My sources are telling me Talarico is in a mental health facility. His campaign has stopped actively planning events in the vital homestretch of the campaign."

Conservative sites — RedState, the Daily Wire, the Right Scoop, Twitchy and PJ Media — amplified the claims, the Guardian reported. Elon Musk added "more toxins in the bloodstream," the Guardian wrote, with posts including one that read: "The Talarico campaign is hiding something big that voters ought to know about."

The supposed proof arrived on October 11, when Sortor posted a 47-second audio clip on X that he said demonstrated Talarico had been discharged earlier in the week from the Lindner Center of Hope, a mental health treatment center in Mason, Ohio. The call, placed by Amacher, included a woman Sortor identified as a center employee saying it looked like Talarico "was just discharged this evening."

The episode fizzled out once the reality became clear, the Guardian reported. The Bulwark obtained the full phone call, in which the purported employee repeatedly denied any record of Talarico at the center. The timeline also did not fit: Amacher said the call was placed Saturday evening, but Talarico had been seen at the Austin City Limits music festival on Friday and at a Kacey Musgraves concert in Dallas on Saturday. On Sunday, the Lindner Center of Hope wrote on X that Talarico "has never received treatment" from the facility. Talarico replied to the post, writing "I've never even been to Ohio" and including a tears-of-joy emoji. Even Maga followers began to acknowledge that the claim had collapsed, the Guardian reported.

JT Ennis, a spokesperson for Talarico, called Sortor's claim "a lie designed to promote a pathetic conspiracy theory" and said Talarico had been recovering from the flu at his home in Austin. To establish his presence at home during the period in question, the Guardian reported, Talarico shared home security camera footage with the New York Times.

Reed Galen, a former Republican strategist and co-founder of the anti-Trump Lincoln Project, told the Guardian: "It was a bit wild but a good reminder to all of us how sophisticated, effective and efficient the rightwing/ bot/ Russian generated misinformation-disinformation machine is." Galen said the story "came from some weirdos in Ohio or whatever" but was amplified "to a point where – despite no evidence of reality – it became something that was an issue in the Texas Senate campaign." He added: "Even if Talarico wins, that in and of itself was a win for the tactic. If you have the ability and the willingness to do this stuff, it's not gonna work every time, but if you do it a lot, sometimes you're gonna have success, and sometimes those successes might be strategic in nature, not just tactical."

The episode sits against a wider pattern in the 2026 cycle. NewsGuard, a company that tracks online misinformation, told the Guardian it had identified and debunked 25 false claims about the midterms that had gone viral on social media platforms. The list, the Guardian reported, includes claims that Abdul El-Sayed, the Democratic candidate for US Senate in Michigan, had called for sharia law in a 2022 speech; a Dominion Voting Systems whistleblower accusing US politicians of taking millions in bribes to rig the 2026 midterms; and a Texas billboard attributed to Paxton's campaign reading "Pedos Ain't That Bad." The list also includes an AI-generated image purportedly showing centrist Iowa Democratic gubernatorial candidate Rob Sand campaigning with progressives Ilhan Omar and Tim Walz.

The defenses against such content have weakened, the Guardian reported. Last year, US Secretary of State Marco Rubio shut down the State Department's Counter Foreign Information Manipulation and Interference office, accusing it of censorship and wasting US taxpayer money. Major platforms — Musk's X and Mark Zuckerberg's Meta — have reduced their trust, safety and fact-checking teams, the Guardian said. Musk has spent a record $366 million in the election cycle to help Republicans retain control of Congress.

Richard Hasen, a UCLA School of Law professor and election law expert, told the Guardian: "When you've got Elon Musk owning one of the important social media platforms and being a vector for spreading the misinformation, and also when you've got the companies like Meta no longer doing the same kind of trust and safety work, it's just an atmosphere where this kind of stuff can spread."

Yet Hasen and other analysts pointed to limits on the tactic's reach. "The Talarico case shows the limits of being able to do this," Hasen said. "This was something that was easily disproven and could have potentially even backfired, showing that the anti-Talarico forces are so desperate that they have to make up a false claim to try to boost a weak candidate." He added that candidates are now better equipped to dismantle digital lies, citing Talarico's release of home security footage, because "we're leaving digital footprints everywhere, that sometimes could be another way to debunk a rumour." Voters, Hasen said, are "on their guard. We all know that we can't necessarily believe our eyes or our ears."

Tara Setmayer, a former Republican communications director on Capitol Hill, told the Guardian that "the voters have become hip to this game" and that "there are too many other negative things going on in everyday American lives, so I don't think these types of cheap attacks are going to resonate at this point." Mainstream media, she added, "isn't platforming the crazy as often" as in past cycles. "They're actually reporting it in a way with the healthy amount of scepticism that it deserves, so it's dismissed off the top whereas in the past it was so novel," Setmayer said. She added: "At this point now it feels like Donald Trump and Maga's greatest hits: we're really in the fat Elvis period of the Maga election campaign tactics and it's obvious to everyone. The absurdity is finally breaking through even with some Republican voters, evidenced by how terrible Donald Trump's numbers are and Republicans writ large."
