## New USDA guidance may ease Florida's SNAP cost exposure as error rates rise

**Subtype:** fact

- The U.S. Department of Agriculture issued guidance allowing states to recalculate SNAP payment error rates, potentially lowering Florida's future cost share.
- Recent reports obtained by The Phoenix show Florida's preliminary SNAP payment error rates rising.
- The Florida Legislature directed $4 million in 2026 toward hiring a vendor to use AI and machine learning for fraud detection and eligibility review.
- An October Department of Children and Families report said the agency contracted with Executive Information Solutions on Sept. 11.
- The U.S. Department of Agriculture reported Florida's official 2025 payment error rate at 12.97%, potentially exposing the state to 15% of SNAP food costs.
- Department of Children and Families data showed Florida's July 2026 SNAP enrollment fell 22% from a year earlier to 2,219,639 residents.

## Final 2026 rate exceeding 13.3% could delay Florida's SNAP cost-sharing until 2030

New USDA guidance allowing states to recalculate SNAP payment error rates may lower Florida's future cost exposure even as recent reports obtained by The Phoenix show preliminary error rates rising in the state. Florida's official 2025 rate was 12.97%, which could expose the state to 15% of SNAP food costs and, based on previous spending, as much as $1 billion. The final 2026 rate will not be finalized until June.

The Florida Legislature in 2026 agreed to direct $4 million to the Department of Children and Families to hire a vendor and use AI to identify fraud in the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program. The Legislature also required monthly reports to the House and Senate disclosing the latest error rates and directed DCF by Sept. 1 to hire a vendor “capable of providing large-scale analyses of eligibility determinations using machine learning, identifying and correcting erroneous determinations, identifying root causes of erroneous determinations, and recommending operational improvements to avoid future errors.”

DCF, which is charged with determining eligibility for SNAP, Medicaid and Temporary Assistance for Needy Families, did not respond to The Phoenix's question about whether it had hired a contractor. An October DCF report said the agency had reached a deal with Executive Information Solutions on Sept. 11.

Payment error rates measure the accuracy of a state's eligibility and benefit determinations and include both overpayments and underpayments. SNAP is the nation's largest nutrition assistance program, accounting for about 69% of federal nutrition assistance spending in fiscal year 2025.

The state also reported a shrinking SNAP caseload. Data updated last month showed 2,219,639 Florida residents on SNAP in July 2026, a near 6% drop from June and a 22% drop from July 2025, when 2,849,239 residents received food benefits. The data also showed 1,256,484 households receiving benefits, down more than 7% from June and nearly 22% from July 2025.

Gov.-elect Byron Donalds said Florida was doing the work necessary to remove waste and fraud from the system, a mandate under the One Big Beautiful Bill Act that he supported in Congress. Donalds called it the “prudent, common-sense thing that not just Florida but every state in the nation should be doing,” speaking to reporters in South Florida earlier this month. Donalds said his mother received food assistance for a time when he was a child, distinguishing the old food coupons from the card used today. “We want to make sure that people who do need those benefits get them,” he said. Eliminating fraud, he said, would help ensure that “people who do need resources get those resources and they get them efficiently and in the time that they need them.”

The One Big Beautiful Bill Act, President Donald Trump's signature tax reduction bill, reduced federal funding for Medicaid by about $1 trillion over the next decade and reduced SNAP by about $187 billion. It also required states to cover 75% of SNAP administrative costs, a $50 million annual increase for Florida.

Florida's Legislature put $38 million in recurring general revenue into the current-year budget to cover the higher administrative costs from Oct. 1, the start of the federal fiscal year, through June 30, 2027, the end of the state's fiscal year. DCF has requested another $12.6 million for state fiscal year 2027-28 for those administrative costs.

States with high payment error rates must begin covering part of the cost of SNAP food benefits on Oct. 1, 2027, the start of federal fiscal year 2028. Any funding for the food benefits would need to be included in the budget the Legislature works on next year when it meets in regular session. States with error rates below 6% would not contribute to food costs, while states with higher rates would share those costs; they may use either 2025 or 2026 rates. Preliminary error rates are calculated monthly but are not finalized until June.

Florida's official 2025 rate of 12.97%, according to USDA, could place the state on the hook for 15% of food costs, potentially as much as $1 billion based on previous spending. DCF's legislative budget request for 2027-28 includes a nominal $1 placeholder for that possibility.

The federal law temporarily delays cost-sharing for states with error rates greater than 13.3%. If Florida's final 2026 rate exceeds 13.3%, the state would not have to contribute to food costs until fiscal year 2030, according to the state's most recent Long Term Financial Outlook. Legislative leaders did not discuss the potential obligation during the just-completed organizational session. Sky Beard, Florida director of No Kid Hungry, told The Phoenix, “This is something we’re going to have to continually talk about.” Failure to qualify for the delayed implementation, Beard said, would make it an issue for the coming session and budget.

USDA issued revised guidance on Friday allowing states to recalculate their SNAP error rates, a move that may help Florida lower a potential future contribution. The change followed a September federal court ruling that states had not been given enough time to comply with changes made by the 2025 tax and spending law, H.R. 1. The ruling did not undo the law's requirement that states with higher payment error rates cover part of SNAP benefit costs beginning Oct. 1, 2027.

The case concerned a 120-day grace period meant to give states time to put new SNAP rules in place without implementation mistakes counting against payment error rates, the Virginia Mercury reported. USDA treated the period as starting July 4, 2025, when the law was enacted, although the agency did not issue its first implementation guidance until Aug. 29, according to the ruling. USDA waited another two months to explain how states should handle noncitizen eligibility changes, leaving states one day under the agency's timeline to make those changes. A Nov. 14 memo then told states the grace period had expired, the Virginia Mercury reported.

U.S. District Judge Mustafa Kasubhai said states could not reasonably be expected to implement federal changes before the government told them how to do it. He vacated the deadline in USDA's guidance and ordered the agency to provide 120 days after issuing an implementation memo, along with adequate notice of when changes must be made. For Florida, the recalculation guidance may lower a future cost share, but the final 2026 error rate remains unresolved.
