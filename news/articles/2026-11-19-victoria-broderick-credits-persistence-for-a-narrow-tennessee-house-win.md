## Fewer than 400 votes separated Broderick from defeat

Victoria Broderick, a tech support worker from rural Tennessee, unseated eight-term Republican U.S. Rep. Scott DesJarlais. PBS News reported that Broderick raised less than $100,000 and received zero investment from the national party. In an interview from Capitol Hill, PBS News correspondent Liz Landers said Broderick won by fewer than 400 votes in a district President Donald Trump carried by 24 points. Landers said Broderick's district was not expected to be competitive; Broderick had lost her first campaign there by 40 points, and DesJarlais conceded the rematch earlier in the week.

Broderick said the goal of running for office is a two-year term. She said: "So if I'm not doing my job, then, yes, get me out." Landers noted that another large Democratic victory was unlikely in 2028 and asked whether Broderick would govern as though limited to one term.

Broderick said she planned to pass legislation protecting rural hospitals and housing. She said Congress could address both on the first day of her term and described the measures as already bipartisan. Landers noted that Broderick believed rural hospitals could bring together the conservatives and Democrats she represents. Broderick said every politician should run as though the time were up because the work had to get done.

Landers asked the working mother of two what was stretching her budget, noting that the average member of Congress is far wealthier than the average American. Broderick answered, "Groceries." She said she drove across state lines to take advantage of Alabama's grocery-tax treatment and that groceries were cheaper there, saving an average of $50 to $100 on groceries.

Broderick said her two young children think chocolate milk comes from chocolate cows. She said she wanted every person to understand that making things affordable meant focusing on "groceries, eggs" rather than the economy "when it comes to like Wall Street." She contrasted that concern with that of richer people: "Rich people don't have to think about that. They don't have to think about their grocery prices when they go in the store. I do." Broderick said most people in her district shared the concern and wanted affordability to be easier for every person.

Broderick said she campaigned on Medicare for All. Landers said Senator Bernie Sanders wants to phase it in over four years, beginning with an eligibility age of 55 or older. Landers also said incoming leader Hakeem Jeffries, probably Speaker Jeffries, had endorsed lowering the age. Broderick called lowering the age a good starting point but not enough: "It's not enough for me, but it's a complicated system that we have to work towards." She said the incoming Congress could possibly push broader health coverage forward and that she wanted to see better.

Broderick also said she was not opposed to making capitalism work for everyone and to requiring companies to provide better health care and benefits to their employees. If capitalism is the goal, she said, companies need to make it more competitive.

Asked whether Jeffries was the best person to lead Democrats in the new Congress as speaker, Broderick said, "As far as my opinion on the Speaker Jeffries, he has agreed to a bold progressive agenda." She said all members were on notice that work needed to get done and that her job was to hold whoever became speaker accountable and make sure that person was doing the job.

Asked whether she would support Jeffries when the speaker vote comes to the floor in January, Broderick said: "I put a lot of conditions in place, and so far, everything he's been amenable to, even the bigger pushbacks." She added, "So, as far as that goes, yes, I would support him." She said her job would not stop with the vote for speaker; she would work to make sure Jeffries delivered. "And if he doesn't deliver, then we're going to -- we're going to talk about that," she said. Broderick said whoever emerged did not matter and hoped her colleagues and Jeffries would stick to the work.

Broderick credited her persistence: "I won because I continued running since May of 2024. I never stopped running for Congress." She said the work would begin speaking for itself and that she had seen it happen after being outspent 12.5 to one in the primary and again in the general election. Broderick credited relationships developed over 2.5 years of campaigning and said running in Tennessee takes time to build those relationships. "And time is the only thing you can't get more of," she said, adding that she had used her 2.5 years wisely.

Landers said, "Tennessee Republicans redrew the state's congressional maps in an attempt to sweep all nine of those House districts." She said Broderick's district was not expected to be competitive, that Broderick did not have much money and that she did not have the name recognition other Democrats had. Broderick said the district stayed the same after redistricting and actually became more competitive, adding that the work she had already done would speak for itself.

Landers asked, "You have said that you would work with President Trump if he is willing to play ball. What are areas where you see alignment with him?" Broderick said legislation had to go across the desk and receive his signature. "He has to sign it and go across the desk. So it's really down to him," she said. She added that "Congress needs to take its power back" after it failed to do so in the previous couple of years. Broderick said the new Congress would represent more of the district and what its residents were experiencing.

Broderick rejected the description of Tennessee as conservative or red: "So I would push back and say that it's Tennessee's not a conservative state. It's not even a red state. It's a nonvoting, voter-suppressed state." She said residents were not being properly represented, adding, "which means that a 16-year incumbent who neglected the district gave up his seat because I worked -- outworked him."

When Democrats take control of the House in January, Broderick will join them from Tennessee. Her stated agenda centers on rural hospitals, housing, broader health coverage and household costs, while her support for party leadership remains tied to conditions and legislative delivery.
