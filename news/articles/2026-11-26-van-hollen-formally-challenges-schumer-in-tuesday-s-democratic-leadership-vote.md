## Van Hollen formally challenges Schumer in Tuesday's Democratic leadership vote

**Subtype:** fact

- Sen. Chris Van Hollen of Maryland is challenging Sen. Chuck Schumer of New York in a secret-ballot Senate Democratic leadership election on Tuesday.
- Van Hollen needs 28 votes to become majority leader and says more than a dozen Democrats support his challenge.
- Sen.-elect Troy Jackson of Maine endorsed Van Hollen on Wednesday.
- Sen.-elect Abdul El-Sayed of Michigan backed Van Hollen as more willing to fight for progressive priorities, including a Medicare-like system for everyone.
- Sen. Mark Warner of Virginia publicly backed Schumer on Wednesday, putting Schumer's reported total at 17.
- The Tuesday ballot is the first competitive contest for Senate Democratic leader since 1994.

## Schumer's public count reaches 17; Van Hollen claims more than a dozen

Sen. Chris Van Hollen of Maryland has formally challenged Sen. Chuck Schumer of New York for Senate Democratic leader, setting up a secret-ballot election Tuesday that will be the first competitive contest for the post since 1994. Van Hollen says he has more than a dozen votes and needs 28 to become majority leader; Schumer says his support is locked down. Publicly, neither side has assembled the votes needed to make the outcome certain.

The election will determine who sets the agenda and floor strategy for the Senate Democratic majority, and the candidates differ on how to do it. Van Hollen argues that Senate Democrats need more energetic leadership in confronting President Trump and greater responsiveness to party activists, including on Israel funding. Sen. Jacky Rosen of Nevada, a centrist voting for Schumer, said, "We need to hit the ground running," and, "There's ways we can improve the caucus and bring everyone in," while adding that "experience matters."

Van Hollen's decision came after a private conversation with Schumer shortly before he launched the campaign. Schumer asked to talk after learning that the Maryland senator, who had been exploring a 2028 presidential bid, had shifted to what Van Hollen called "listening mode" about taking on the incumbent. During a discussion of the party's future, Van Hollen said he told Schumer directly, "I don't want to bulls— you," and "I am seriously considering doing this." The exchange signaled his challenge before he announced the bid days later on Jen Psaki's MS Now show. Schumer's argument was that he had helped deliver the party's midterm wins and deserved another term, after a year in which much of the party's base was demanding his departure.

Van Hollen, 67, is the candidate of forces in the Democratic Party who say the party needs change at the top: more energy in confronting President Trump, more responsiveness to activists on issues including Israel funding, and a leader other than Schumer. He has described the 2024 election and its aftermath as a turning point for the party.

The vote count remains the central obstacle. Van Hollen needs 28 votes and has seven Democrats on record supporting him; he says the total is more than a dozen and that the momentum is in his favor. Sen.-elect Troy Jackson of Maine endorsed him Wednesday, and other progressives, including Sen.-elect Abdul El-Sayed of Michigan, have backed him as more willing to fight for their priorities, including moving toward a Medicare-like system for everyone.

Schumer's side has 17 publicly reported votes after Sen. Mark Warner of Virginia backed him late Wednesday, and the incumbent says he has the votes locked down when 55 Democratic senators and senators-elect hold the secret ballot. Sen. Amy Klobuchar, who was elected Minnesota governor, will not vote. The public endorsements have stayed short of the majority Schumer needs, and some senators are keeping their options open in part to avoid protests from progressive activists outside the offices of his backers.

Some Democratic senators have privately said it would be easier to vote for Schumer, 76, if he agreed this was his last term as leader, and one senator familiar with the matter said some of them have told Schumer directly. Sen. Kirsten Gillibrand of New York, an ally, disputed that the leader needs to make such a promise: "Chuck doesn't need to make promises about his future to earn votes. He already has strong support in the caucus and is going to win."

The differences between the candidates go beyond the ballot. Van Hollen has said he would roll back Schumer's top-down approach, revive antitrust legislation that some Democrats suspect Schumer blocked in 2022 by refusing to put it on the floor, and schedule a vote on a bill making it easier for unions to organize. Labor leaders have said Schumer reneged on moving the organizing bill forward. A Schumer aide said there were not the votes to pass either bill, in part because there were not enough votes to end the 60-vote filibuster threshold.

Van Hollen's argument is aimed at public proof of alignment with the party base: "A press conference is not enough," he said in a recent interview, a veiled criticism of Schumer. "You really do need to show the public that you stand with them." At a Thanksgiving parade in Silver Spring, Md., Van Hollen rode in an old-fashioned Buick driven by a longtime constituent, and people shouted "future majority leader" at him. In past parades, he joked, "I'd wave, and they'd go, 'Hey! Nice car!'" Van Hollen has taken steps to show that he understands the frustration with Schumer among members of the Democratic caucus, a group that includes progressives but also centrists such as Sen. Elissa Slotkin of Michigan.

Critics of the bid say it has divided the party, has little chance of succeeding and was designed to raise Van Hollen's profile ahead of a possible presidential bid, though Van Hollen has said recently that he is not running in 2028. The critics also worry that his proposals to weaken the role of super PACs, which can accept unlimited campaign donations, could disadvantage Democratic candidates in future contests.

Van Hollen's challenge marks a change in a senator who once worked within the system. He ran the House and Senate Democratic campaign arms and served as a top lieutenant to then-House Speaker Nancy Pelosi of California. In the budget battles of the tea party era, he was deployed to combat GOP efforts to curb Medicare. He opted to run for the Senate in 2016 instead of waiting to try to succeed Pelosi, as she once floated.

He described the 2024 election and its aftermath as a turning point. After the Trump administration began unilaterally shrinking government programs and firing workers in February 2025, Van Hollen was among the lawmakers who protested at the U.S. Agency for International Development, which the White House targeted for closure. One month later, after the Trump administration deported Kilmar Abrego Garcia, a migrant living in Maryland, Van Hollen flew to El Salvador to try to verify that he was still alive. A judge later found that the government had no authority to arrest Abrego Garcia, and the Supreme Court ordered him returned to the United States. Abrego Garcia left the country this week under an agreement with the U.S. government and hopes to return to Maryland under the next administration, his spokesman said. Van Hollen said, "I had no idea it’d be such big news," then added, "I just sort of did it out of instinct."

The episode made an impression on colleagues as Democrats struggled to find their footing in Trump's second term. Retiring Sen. Tina Smith of Minnesota said, "He had the courage to go after an issue that maybe the polls say don't go there; that's dangerous for Democrats, when actually what Democrats need to do is to dive into it more."

He also came around to the point of view of Sen. Bernie Sanders of Vermont, who argued the economy was rigged in favor of the wealthy and was an early proponent of Medicare for All. "The election was a moment of real reflection," said Van Hollen, who endorsed Medicare for All in late 2025. "The conclusion I came to was that this is not about progressives or moderates. It is about an economic populism." He said that argument, combined with foreign-policy concerns including his growing concern about the use of U.S. taxpayer funds to support Israel's attacks on Gaza, prompted him to consider a presidential run that later became the leadership bid.

Abdul El-Sayed said he has been having conversations with other Democrats about their votes, including some who do not want to cross Schumer but see a "real opportunity" with Van Hollen. "We're picking a quarterback here, and we want a quarterback to play to win the game always, rather than to consolidate power," El-Sayed said. "That in and of itself is part of what frustrates people about the way the Senate works."
