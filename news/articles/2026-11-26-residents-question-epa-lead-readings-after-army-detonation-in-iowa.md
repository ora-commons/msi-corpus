## The EPA says about 300 people are sampling air, soil and water

Federal scientists are testing the air, soil and nearby surface water across eastern Iowa for lead contamination after the Army detonated the country's stockpile of thousands of pounds of lead azide on Wednesday at the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant in Middletown. The agency says it has found no lead in the air and no lead dust on surfaces near the detonation zone so far, but some residents nearby say they want independent measurements before they trust those results.

The Army said the stockpile was unstable and that a controlled explosion was the only way to get rid of it, according to Eliza Billingham of Iowa Public Radio. Billingham said the barrels were already underground and that the bunkers were covered with tarps, water and particle-collecting foam, steps the Army took to mitigate the explosion. The main concern around the detonation, she said, was how much lead would be released into the air.

Shortly after the detonation, Major General Eric Shirley said the results exceeded his expectations. "Every indication is that all of the munitions that were employed absolutely reduced the lead azide threat," he said.

The EPA's survey extends beyond the air. The agency and the Army are watching lead levels on surfaces near the detonation zone against EPA legal limits for how much lead can be on windowsills and floors in houses, Billingham said. So far, the EPA's measurements have not shown any lead dust.

Lead is dangerous because it is a neurotoxin, Billingham said, and it is especially dangerous to children and people who are pregnant because of how it can inhibit childhood development. That focus has made the early measurements the practical test of whether the Army's disposal method kept contamination out of the surrounding environment.

Reaction among residents has been mixed. The Army told some people living within 5 miles of the detonation that it might be best to evacuate, and some people in that radius left their homes, though not all did.

Alan Kleinkopf, who lives around the corner from the plant's headquarters, said he and his wife left their house yesterday morning and came back around noon. He said his home did not appear to have any damage and that this made him feel better. "I think the U.S. government has done the best that they could with their mitigating efforts that they put into place," he said.

After a week and a half of delays and mixed messaging about the detonation, other residents have lost trust in the Army, Billingham said, and they are hesitant to trust the EPA's initial readings. They want independent monitoring. The University of Iowa and the University of Illinois are sampling around the region, and Billingham said the results in a few days would show whether their measurements line up with the EPA's.

Iowa Gov. Kim Reynolds declared a disaster emergency for three counties in southeastern Iowa. The declaration opens the path to FEMA funding, and FEMA said it is ready to assist anyone who thinks they need relief.

The public library in Burlington, next to Middletown, said it has service navigators every week to help people navigate state and federal aid. The Army's claims office is also in contact with residents who want to file personal injury or property damage claims with the Army.

EPA sampling of air, soil and water is continuing, and the University of Iowa and University of Illinois results expected in a few days are expected to show whether the universities' measurements align with the agency's. So far, the EPA has reported no lead in the air and no lead dust on surfaces near the detonation zone.
