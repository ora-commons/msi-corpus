## Gas volatility and depleted reserves leave Europe poorly resourced for winter

Ofgem, the energy regulator, announced Wednesday that the price cap applied to domestic gas and electricity bills will rise in January. In an editorial, The Guardian reported that about 22 million UK households will pay an average equivalent of £2,166 a year for gas and electricity under the new cap, up from £1,723 under the current cap.

The newspaper's editorial linked the increase to international conflict and the climate emergency. It said uncertainty in the Strait of Hormuz has made gas supply volatile, while exceptional energy usage to mitigate the effects of the summer's prolonged heatwave depleted European reserves. The Guardian also said Europe is poorly resourced for what might be an unusually cold winter caused by disruption of the polar vortex, which it said was plausibly accelerated by anthropogenic climate change.

The rise comes against relief the Guardian described as the cost-of-living "breathing space" already offered by Prime Minister Andy Burnham: a VAT cut to bills and a one-off £100 payment to some households. The editorial said the increase would exhaust that breathing space, leaving many British households to choose between heating their homes and paying for other essentials.

Burnham has made the cost of living the focus of his political argument. Responding to the results of the US midterm elections, he said the Democratic party's triumph meant there was "an opportunity now to unite around the issue that is affecting us all, and that's the cost of living."

The Guardian said Republicans lost control of Congress in large part because Donald Trump failed to address concerns about the cost of basic goods, and that Trump's war against Iran aggravated the affordability problem by causing a spike in commodity prices. Burnham tied domestic price pressures to the conflicts themselves, saying: "I can't get people's cost of living pressures down until the conflict in the Middle East is resolved, the conflict in Ukraine started by Russia."

The Conservatives and Reform UK claim that high bills are a function of environmental policies and are looking for solutions in Britain's abandonment of its net zero commitments. The Guardian rejected that approach, saying it "combines economic illiteracy with international irresponsibility" and that a dash to put dwindling North Sea oil on to a global market would be no shortcut to cheaper British bills. The editorial said the price on that global market is determined more by Donald Trump or Vladimir Putin.

The Guardian argued instead that hastening the transition away from fossil fuels is the strategic route to energy security and to the stability that permits other interventions to help with the cost of living. It said that argument must be central to the analysis Burnham offers when he moves on from "breathing space" policies to the more ambitious vision promised in the 10-year plan.

Burnham has advertised that document, due next month, as a blueprint for fixing "broken" markets and restoring public control over utilities and other services on which people depend. The Guardian said what the plan means in practice will decide the fate of the Labour government.

The editorial said voters are quick to punish incumbents who fail to appreciate the scale of the cost-of-living problem or are perceived to lack credible solutions. Burnham, it said, has passed the first of those tests but cannot afford to fail the second.
