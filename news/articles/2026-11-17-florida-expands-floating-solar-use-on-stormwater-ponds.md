## Solar costs fell as scientists study how panels alter pond ecosystems

Some of Florida’s tens of thousands of retention ponds, designed to collect runoff during the state’s frequent downpours, now host floating photovoltaic arrays. The technology mounts solar panels on buoyant structures anchored in reservoirs and ponds, allowing electricity generation without competing for scarce land. The water’s cooling effect may increase output, although scientists caution that the technology carries high upfront costs and its environmental effects remain incompletely understood.

The Orlando Utilities Commission began with a 32-kilowatt system, expanded it and later developed a separate 120-kilowatt project. In November 2025, the commission launched the 2-megawatt Conway Road array, comprising more than 3,400 panels, which it said was Florida’s largest floating solar installation.

Justin Kramer, the commission’s director of emerging technologies and data analytics, said available land is limited and expensive. “Floating solar provides locations that are within the city limits, but they’re not being used for anything else,” Kramer said.

Florida explicitly supports floating solar through a state law enacted in 2022. University of Florida researchers, using 2012 land-use data, estimated that the state’s towns and cities have 76,000 stormwater ponds, accounting for about 2.7% of its urban land area. AJ Reisinger, an associate professor at the university who specializes in urban soil and water quality, said Florida has so many stormwater ponds because of its heavy rainfall, flat terrain, sandy soil and development history.

Florida’s geography, climate and development history make it particularly well suited for floating solar projects as electricity demand grows. Utilities, municipalities and other public agencies may already control bodies of water in places with high power demands. Gandhi said floating systems have been installed on water treatment ponds and sewer facilities across the United States, as well as on a winery irrigation pond in Napa Valley. Experts said site suitability depends on proximity to the electrical grid, water levels, anchoring conditions, size, competing uses, economics and environmental considerations.

D3Energy said it holds exclusive rights to develop floating solar systems on eligible Florida Department of Transportation stormwater ponds within state highway rights-of-way. The company estimates those systems could generate more than 1 gigawatt of energy. D3Energy has more than 15 floating solar projects in Florida.

Floating solar has become less expensive since the technology emerged two decades ago, but it still carries an upfront premium over comparable land-based projects. Gandhi said the median reported cost fell from $2.41 per watt in 2015 to $1.25 per watt in 2024, a decline of about 48%. Published comparisons suggest a premium of roughly 10% to 25%, he said.

The technology’s first pilot was installed in Japan in 2007, Gandhi said. By the end of 2024, more than 9.6 gigawatts of floating solar generating capacity had been installed in 66 countries and regions. Floating solar still accounted for less than 1% of global solar capacity. After about 20 years, “people have started to believe, have started to see that it works, that it’s fairly reliable, and that lenders and investors have also gained confidence in the technology,” said Oktoviano Gandhi, a scientist at the Qatar Environment and Energy Research Institute.

Rafael Almeida, a freshwater ecosystem scientist and assistant professor at Indiana University Bloomington, said floating solar’s growth is part of the broader expansion of solar development, driven by demand for renewable and lower-carbon electricity. “It’s not like only floating solar is increasing,” Almeida said. “All kinds of solar energy, ground-mounted, in particular, has been accelerating over the past decade.”

About 90% of global floating solar power-generating capacity is in Asia, and about half of that is in mainland China, Gandhi said. Almeida said the technology is well established in China, Japan, Taiwan and South Korea, where land is at a premium, while the United States is lagging mainly because it has more land available for energy development.

Almeida’s research group found about 95% of floating solar sites are on managed waters, including reservoirs in Japan, quarry lakes in Europe and aquaculture systems in China.

Kramer said Florida’s floating systems generate about 5% to 8% more power than a typical rooftop array because the water cools the panels. Gandhi cautioned that the benefit is sometimes overstated and varies considerably with wind and ambient temperatures. Floating panels can also reduce evaporation, which Gandhi said is especially useful for irrigation reservoirs in dry regions.

Kramer said Orlando’s arrays were designed for changing pond levels and Florida wind speeds and have operated through multiple hurricanes. They are “designed to go up and down as the stormwater pond levels increase or decrease,” he said. “And they’re designed for the wind speeds that we have to deal with.” D3Energy said none of its systems suffered structural damage when Hurricane Milton hit the Gulf Coast in 2024.

Stormwater ponds are engineered infrastructure, but they also support algae, plants, bacteria, insects, fish and birds and can help manage nutrients, pollutants and sediments. “They definitely do conduct and perform a lot of the same natural ecological processes and ecosystem functions that we would expect from a natural pond or a natural wetland,” Reisinger said.

Floating solar can limit the amount of sunlight entering the water. Reisinger said shading can alter photosynthesis and primary production, potentially changing algae, submerged plants, nutrient removal, habitat and aquatic food webs.

Almeida said covering part of a water surface can cool it and reduce evaporation, but it can also change the amount of oxygen available to the aquatic ecosystem. The Orlando Utilities Commission tries to limit coverage to 70% to 80% of a pond’s surface, with the exact amount depending on maintenance needs and the pond’s ecosystem, Kramer said.

Scientists do not yet know how much coverage is too much. “Are there tipping points after which you start changing the system in ways that the nature of the system is dramatically changed?” Almeida said. “At what levels should we stop covering?”
