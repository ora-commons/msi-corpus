## Resigned CEO accused Novatek of controlling Alaska gas project

ProPublica's investigation found that Tech Wave Industrial Project Management, an Abu Dhabi company, paid for offshore surveys for the Polar Train LNG project while the work was steered by Novatek, a Russian liquefied natural gas company under partial U.S. sanctions. ProPublica said the proposal had already put detailed scientific information about the region within reach of Novatek leader Leonid Mikhelson, who is close to President Vladimir Putin. The reporting drew on interviews with a dozen people and documents including corporate records, correspondence, memos, survey presentations, budgets, a wire-transfer receipt and loan agreements. The Financial Times and The New York Times had reported Russian ties to the venture, but ProPublica said the extent of the sensitive information gathered had not been previously disclosed.

The surveyors began work in the second half of 2025, paused during harsh weather and restarted in mid-2026, documents and sources say. The research vessels operated in the Beaufort Sea off Alaska, where oil and gas companies have regularly explored for decades. The contractors performing the work were American, but the Abu Dhabi company paid for the surveys. By late August, according to a person familiar with the situation, dozens of contractors were stationed on three large vessels — the Annika Marie, the Ukpik and the Uŋalaq — carrying equipment to map the seabed, collect shallow marine sediment, scan for obstructions such as shipwrecks, monitor waves and currents, test tides and drill core samples in mud.

The 2025 survey work focused on an area just west of Prudhoe Bay, near where the Trans-Alaska Pipeline System originates. Documents showed the work sat not far, as the crow flies, from the start of the pipeline — a cornerstone of U.S. energy production. An Air Force long-range radar station sat just to the west. More than 100 miles offshore, the Navy's fast-attack nuclear submarines sometimes practiced surfacing through thick Arctic ice.

Riddle said Gundersen wanted to locate the LNG plant just off the coast near Prudhoe Bay, an area that includes the largest oil field in North America and operations by companies that include Exxon, ConocoPhillips and Hilcorp. The site, Riddle said, is also near the Oliktok Long Range Radar Site. Installations like it watch for inbound missiles and aircraft and play a critical role in national defense because satellites do not have good visibility over the poles. Prudhoe Bay is also a key location for communications infrastructure supporting the military and residents of northern Alaska, through a subsea fiber-optic cable that originates there and stretches to Nome.

Such information is highly valuable in the ever-changing and unpredictable Arctic, with its potential routes for both naval ships and missiles. Norwegian security services and U.S. lawmakers have expressed concerns this year about the danger of increased spy and sabotage operations by Russia in the Arctic. Heather Conley, a former State Department official and senior fellow with the American Enterprise Institute, said Russia-backed information gathering so close to the U.S. shoreline could be seen as "a direct challenge to sovereignty and security." Conley called the information "super, super sensitive" and said, "Any scientific data, it immediately is dual use."

Troy Bouffard, director of the Center for Arctic Security and Resilience at the University of Alaska, Fairbanks, said information about changing sea ice and other Arctic conditions could help Russia plan activities that undermine U.S. security. He said the fact that the surveys received permits and proceeded without interference could be seen as an intelligence failure. Bouffard called it "highly unlikely" that intelligence personnel did not learn of the work because projects in the remote, militarily sensitive North Slope area stand out. "All this is really surprising," Bouffard said. "Should not have happened."

Tech Wave was 95% owned by Norwegian Bjorn Gundersen, a former Novatek deputy director who played a key role in developing Arctic LNG 2 on Russia's Gydan Peninsula. The Alaska project began in 2025 when half a dozen ExxonMobil executives met Tech Wave representatives, according to a person familiar with the project. Gundersen and Tech Wave CEO Rajeev Lehar assured Exxon and others that no Russian money was involved, the person said. Tech Wave said Novatek "offers the only proven technology suited to Arctic conditions" that also meets the project's timeline, and that its expertise was "informed in part" by Gundersen's experience with Arctic LNG 2. ExxonMobil did not respond to a request for comment, and Gundersen declined to comment.

Tech Wave planned to seek permission to buy state-of-the-art natural gas equipment intended for Arctic LNG 2 before U.S. economic sanctions prevented the project from being completed. The unfinished equipment sat atop a concrete platform nearly the size of 10 football fields in a Murmansk shipyard. Tech Wave wanted to move it by sea to Alaska's North Slope, where it could process and sell millions of metric tons of liquefied natural gas trapped underground for decades. Because the gas was a byproduct of existing oil and gas extraction, the venture would need agreements with companies already operating in the area. Its American-ownership proposal also promised jobs, tax revenue and more affordable, reliable energy for Alaskans. Tech Wave had been exploring possible sites for the plant in the summer of 2025, before its leaders met with the Exxon executives, according to documents obtained by ProPublica and interviews, and it dispatched the first group of surveyors who worked off the coast near Prudhoe Bay collecting detailed information about the seabed.

Joel Riddle was hired as Polar Train LNG's CEO in March 2026. Documents show his employment agreement was signed by Gentry Beach, a Texas investor and college friend of Donald Trump Jr. who helped raise money for President Trump's 2016 campaign. Beach, who took part in meetings about the Alaska project, later told The Financial Times that he had an "incredible relationship" with Mikhelson, the Novatek founder and CEO. Beach did not respond to requests for comment, and Novatek said he had no connection to the company. Riddle previously led the Australian fracking operation Tamboran Resources, where he developed a business relationship with Chris Wright, who was confirmed as Trump's energy secretary in February 2025. Wright had been CEO of Liberty Energy, which partnered with Tamboran in 2023. In meetings throughout the summer, Riddle and other Polar Train executives met with Novatek executives, including Mikhelson, according to documents reviewed by ProPublica and interviews with Riddle. Tech Wave also employed several others who had worked for Novatek besides Gundersen, according to social media profiles and documents. Riddle said the actions of the venture's UAE leaders made him suspicious about who was behind the company, and he was becoming concerned that the operation was controlled not by Abu Dhabi investors but by Novatek and Mikhelson, who has close ties to Putin.

After the survey area thawed in 2026, Riddle and his team told their financiers that the location near Prudhoe Bay was unsuitable because dredging would be expensive and could interfere with whaling by local residents. They urged Tech Wave to consider a site near Wainwright, more than 250 miles farther west, and Riddle hired another survey team for about $10 million, records and interviews show. Tech Wave pushed to return to Prudhoe Bay, saying it would be quicker and cheaper. On Aug. 20, Lehar sent Polar Train orders telling contractors to "cease and suspend works and services related to the Wainwright site." That day, Tech Wave also asserted control over Polar Train's spending, directed Riddle to follow its budget and barred him from obtaining financing without its approval. The budget allocated $13 million for summer field surveys near Prudhoe Bay, including studies of the seabed, weather and oceanographic conditions. At an Aug. 31 meeting in a private suite at the Four Seasons Hotel in Abu Dhabi, Riddle said he tried again to convince Mikhelson and Tech Wave that Wainwright was the only location that made sense; Mikhelson told him a Prudhoe Bay site was non-negotiable. Riddle told him he would consider the position and do what was in the best interest of the company.

Riddle said he had sought information about how Tech Wave spent tens of millions before he joined the company and that its backers were hesitant to disclose the cost or origin of that money. Records show Polar Train's funding, on paper, came in the form of loans from Tech Wave and Gundersen. Riddle said his efforts to secure U.S. backing for the project met resistance from his funders. "For months now, there has been no engagement on productively achieving substantial US shareholding," he told Gundersen in a September letter.

In late August, Riddle asked Gundersen and Lehar for more funding. An employee connected him through WhatsApp with Denis Solovyov, a deputy of Mikhelson's who lobbied in Brussels and Washington against tougher Russian sanctions. Solovyov promised money but did not respond to a request for comment. Messages show Solovyov and Riddle discussing unpaid bills, payroll, budgets and loan proceeds sent to Polar Train through as many as three other companies. In the days after the Abu Dhabi meeting, Riddle again became concerned about making payroll; on Sept. 6 he wrote to Solovyov asking him to confirm that new funding "agreed last Monday" would be transferred "no later than Monday 7th," and Solovyov's next message said "the first 5 million were supposed to arrive by today." Abu Dhabi Commercial Bank wired $5 million from Tech Wave to Polar Eagle, Polar Train's U.S. parent, on Sept. 7. Tech Wave's funding came via a loan from a UAE financial services company, records show.

Tech Wave and Polar Train said the company was funded exclusively through Tech Wave, including personal investments by Gundersen and borrowed funds. All three companies said Novatek had agreed to buy a minority stake and had paid Tech Wave an undisclosed amount, with the arrangement contingent on attracting more than 50% American investment. Novatek simultaneously said it "did not provide any funding to the Project," while noting that "provision of debt financing, including to U.S. persons, is not prohibited." Novatek did not answer whether it provided debt financing. Polar Train denied that Novatek was secretly financing the venture.

Riddle and his team consulted lawyers about sanctions concerns, including Treasury personnel's belief that Gundersen "may be acting on behalf" of Novatek. In an Aug. 3 letter, Baker Botts told Riddle those concerns "could result in heightened regulatory scrutiny of the Project even absent a direct sanctions prohibition." On Aug. 14, Polar Train formally requested Treasury permission to import parts into Russia to finish constructing the LNG processor and then buy and transport the plant to Alaska. The six-page application did not identify who was steering the financing and decision-making. Its metadata listed the application's author in Cyrillic as Evgeniya Kononova, whose LinkedIn profile identifies her as deputy head of legal for Novatek in Moscow. Novatek said it had provided technical information and comments on the draft application but did not answer questions about Kononova's role. The application said the proposal would "ensure this cutting-edge equipment is deployed primarily to the benefit of the United States rather than Russia."

The Graham Act, which expanded sanctions on Russia and was named for the late South Carolina Sen. Lindsay Graham, was signed into law on Sept. 18. Riddle resigned the next day, and several other executives followed. He had warned that Polar Train's "investments" were "very likely sooner or later to trigger" a federal inquiry. When he resigned, Riddle questioned the operation's legality, accused Novatek of "financing and ultimately controlling the strategic direction" of the supposedly American-led Polar Train and asserted that the venture was a Russian ploy to gain control of Alaska's natural gas reserves. "They wanted to have Russian technology controlling the resource and the North Slope," Riddle said. "I just felt like that was not in the U.S. interest." Riddle said he told energy and state officials what he had learned, and that the officials said they would alert the FBI. Polar Train called Riddle "a disgruntled employee" and said he had been terminated for cause. Riddle replied, "You can't legally terminate someone who's already resigned." Tech Wave's termination notice, dated six days after his resignation and accusing him of planning a competing LNG project, was signed by new acting CEO Tim Talkington.

ProPublica said it was unclear whether the Russian natural gas executives behind the project wanted to operate an LNG plant in Alaska, finish the incomplete plant stranded in Russia or gather intelligence for the Kremlin. Novatek denied the intelligence allegation, stating, "We state clearly and categorically: any such claims or implications are false." The company said it was focused on using its technology in Alaska and considered the opportunity a standalone, self-sufficient project. Polar Train said the research "involved a standard set of surveys routinely conducted by companies worldwide," that the data was not classified and that "all activities complied with applicable laws," and said the purpose of the work was to find an appropriate site for the LNG project. Talkington, formerly CEO of Goldman Sachs Bank Russia and a board member of Rusal, one of the world's largest aluminum producers, which was founded by oligarch Oleg Deripaska, who is under U.S. sanctions, said the United States had "a generational opportunity to advance U.S. energy dominance, unlock profound benefits for the state of Alaska, and strengthen American Arctic navigation." Lehar said the project was ready to work with the Trump administration and Alaska and would "deliver jobs, tax revenue, and more affordable, reliable energy for Alaskans."

The disclosures came as the Trump administration scaled back rules requiring U.S. companies to report their true owners, reduced FBI counterintelligence staffing and disbanded a Department of Justice task force aimed at seizing Russian oligarchs' assets. Emily Harding, a former CIA analyst and former deputy director of the Senate intelligence committee under Republicans, said, "It's not just that you need people knowledgeable about the counterintelligence mission, but you need people knowledgeable about highly technical topical areas. That's a big problem in normal times, but especially hard when staffing is down." The FBI did not respond to a request for comment.

The status of Polar Train's Treasury application was unclear. A Treasury spokesperson declined to comment on specific licenses or applications, including whether one existed. Polar Train's parent company had also applied to import LNG into Alaska directly from Russia, according to a person familiar with the request. Polar Train said on its website that LNG deliveries were expected in 2030. The White House has sought to use energy deals involving Russia as an incentive to negotiate an end to the war in Ukraine, and Putin has spoken publicly about his desire to cooperate with the United States in the Arctic, potentially through Novatek. Putin said in August 2025, days after meeting Trump at a summit in Anchorage, "We are discussing with our American partners the possibility of working together in this field, not only in our Arctic zone but also in Alaska." The New York Times reported that Putin discussed the Polar Train project in September with Trump's peace envoys, Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner. In October, Trump signed a waiver exempting Russia from the new sanctions established by the Graham Act.

In June, Republican Sen. Mike Lee of Utah and Democratic Sen. Jeanne Shaheen of New Hampshire introduced a bipartisan bill intended to stop vessels from countries including China and Russia from conducting research and espionage operations in U.S. waters. Lee said the legislation "shuts the door to foreign adversaries attempting to commit espionage and exercise influence at our doorstep in the Arctic, affirming the sole right of the American people to determine who may approach their shores, from the Aleutians to the Beaufort Sea." The Senate had not acted on the bill. In January, Russian authorities arrested an Arctic public health and environmental researcher and accused him of treason, saying his work could help Norwegian intelligence; he denied the charges through a family member. Key questions remained unsettled: the status of Polar Train's waiver application was unclear, the FBI had not responded to a request for comment, and Novatek continued to deny gathering intelligence while Polar Train said its surveys were unclassified and lawful.
