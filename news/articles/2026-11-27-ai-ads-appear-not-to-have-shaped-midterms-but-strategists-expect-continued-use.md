## Strategists cite lower costs, faster production and detection challenges

Campaigns spent at least $187 million on political advertisements generated or enhanced by artificial intelligence in the midterm election, according to the nonpartisan Wesleyan Media Project. NPR political reporter Maham Javaid reported that the ads did not appear to affect the overall outcomes, even as campaign strategists said lower costs and more precise targeting would keep the technology in use for the next cycle.

Republican candidates received more than 90% of the spending on AI ads, NPR reported, and many of those candidates lost. The overall spending included both advertisements created with AI and live-action campaign material enhanced by the technology.

Many New York voters had seen a deepfake featuring an AI-generated avatar of Kathy Hochul earlier in the year. The spot placed the avatar in settings including a windmill and a hotel lobby. Its AI-generated voice, speaking as Hochul, said, "We provided luxury accommodations for illegal migrants in beautiful hotels just like this."

The ad included an AI disclaimer and was paid for by Bruce Blakeman, Hochul's Republican opponent in the gubernatorial race. The AI-generated campaign material also included this message: "This November, don't let Bruce Blakeman lower your taxes or make New York safe. Keep it unsafe and unaffordable with Kathy Hochul as your governor." Hochul went on to win the race by roughly 20 points.

Basil Smikle, a Democratic strategist, said the ads often did not connect with voters. He nevertheless expected campaigns to continue using AI because of the technology's production speed and potential for greater shock value.

"You could create an ad that has a lot more shock value than a typical live-action video and produce them more quickly," Smikle said.

Brian Ruddle, a GOP strategist, said the ads were much cheaper than live-action alternatives. He described AI as a way for candidates with limited budgets to communicate a message without hiring a television production company.

"If you're a candidate who does not have a big budget, can't go out and hire a television production company, but you've got a good message and something that can break through," Ruddle said. He said AI could help such candidates, while warning that improving technology would make it harder to determine what was real.

"It is not just about convincing some people that fake things are real. Sometimes it's about people thinking that real things are now fake," Ruddle said.

Jason Riley, a retired Navy pilot from Austin, Texas, said the AI ads he saw did not fool him or change his vote. That reaction was personal rather than representative of voters as a whole.

The UC San Diego Center for Transparent and Trusted Elections reported that about 61% of Americans were concerned that audio or video deepfakes affected the election's outcome. Steve Geller, a book editor from Sacramento, California, said the technology's effectiveness for politicians did not outweigh his concern about allowing its use.

"I think it's not worth having a society which allows this, even though I do think it can be effective for a politician to use it," Geller said.

Brendan Steinhauser, another GOP strategist, said AI collected a lot of voter data during the election cycle that campaigns could use to deliver AI-generated ads to specific people based on their behavior online. He said the approach would make ads highly targeted at scale and more effective over time.

NPR also reported that the data AI companies have been accumulating in this cycle will help serve future ads to hyper-specific audiences. The strategists' case for continued use therefore rested on lower production costs, faster output and increasingly precise voter targeting. Smikle said the ads often did not connect with voters.
