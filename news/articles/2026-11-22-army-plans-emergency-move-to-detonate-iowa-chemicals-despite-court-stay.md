## Army plans emergency move to detonate Iowa chemicals despite court stay

**Subtype:** fact

- The U.S. Army plans to invoke emergency provisions to avoid a court order that put on hold its plan to detonate several tons of bomb-making chemicals in Iowa, government lawyers told the 8th U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals in a filing late Saturday.
- Government lawyers wrote that "Protection of human health and the environment demand action now, not delay," and that the Army "needs to take swift action" to protect personnel and surrounding communities.

## Government lawyers cite falling temperatures in appeals court filing

The U.S. Army asked the 8th U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals to let the operation proceed, arguing that the chemicals cannot safely wait. "To protect its personnel and the surrounding communities, the Army needs to take swift action," government lawyers wrote in the filing late Saturday. "Protection of human health and the environment demand action now, not delay."

Environmental groups described the move as an attempt to escape the court's authority. The appeals court on Thursday had granted their request to put on hold a permit that cleared the way for the detonation in Middletown, a small community about 160 miles (257 kilometers) southeast of Des Moines.

The operation was initially planned for Friday. The Army later announced it would push the detonation to Monday following an outpouring of public concern about potential health and environmental effects. Environmentalists have called for a review of the emergency permit approved earlier this month by the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency.

In a statement Sunday, the Iowa Environmental Council, the Sierra Club and Earthjustice questioned claims that all alternatives were given appropriate consideration. "The Army seemed more concerned over the weekend with finding a way to sidestep the judicial process than on meeting the public's demands for greater transparency, notice to affected communities, and far stronger protection of people, property, and the environment from serious harm," said Earthjustice Attorney Thien Chau.

Officials contend the chemicals are unstable and that the risk of an uncontrolled explosion is growing, particularly as temperatures drop. They said the chemicals may explode as temperatures are expected to fall below 30 degrees Fahrenheit (-1 Celsius) in the coming days.

The material was discovered in March, when staff at the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant found several dozen barrels of degraded lead azide, which is used in munitions production. Crystallization of the material created an unstable and dangerous situation, prompting the Army to assemble experts in munitions chemistry, engineering and environmental safety to come up with a plan.

According to EPA documents, alternatives such as neutralizing and decomposing the drums' contents with other chemicals were considered. Officials said handling the drums would increase the probability of an accidental, fatal detonation. The environmental groups disagreed, arguing in their petition that other alternatives were not fully considered, and criticized officials for not sharing more information with the public.

The Army shared details during a public meeting in Middletown in October, but a public advisory a week ago was the first time other communities — some as far away as 100 miles (160 kilometers) — became aware of the potential impact. Nearly 400 people live in Middletown, but thousands more in surrounding areas could be affected by detonated material that could disperse in the air and land on the ground.

Vibrations from the two planned blasts were expected to be quick, but they could break old windows. That led officials to recommend that some residents in Middletown vacate the area.

Iowans in the region were told to stay inside; keep windows and doors closed; bring pets and livestock indoors; and clean cars, windowsills and outdoor surfaces afterward. "The detonation will result in an air plume with some lead-containing particles which will disperse in the air and deposit on the ground," an advisory states. "Lead is toxic to humans and animals."


The Saturday filing follows the 8th Circuit's Thursday order pausing the EPA permit, which MSI reported in [Appeals court stays Army lead-azide detonation permit in Iowa](/articles/2026-11-20-appeals-court-stays-army-lead-azide-detonation-permit-in-iowa/), and the Army's move of the operation from Friday to Monday after public concern, covered in [U.S. Army delays Iowa detonation of degraded lead azide after lawsuit](/articles/2026-11-20-u-s-army-delays-iowa-detonation-of-degraded-lead-azide-after-lawsuit/).
