## Utility, property and fuel bills add pressure beyond holiday groceries

Long previewed a Navy Federal report scheduled for the next day and said it found people across the income spectrum are actively cutting back on grocery spending. Shoppers know prices are up and are trying to free up cash for other necessities or fun purchases, she said, adding that consumers have an intense focus on getting their grocery bills down.

Long said her data show middle-class and moderate-income consumers shopping more and more at Walmart, Sam's Club, Costco and Aldi as discount shopping increases. She described the holiday as "It's definitely brutal, and this is very much the bargain holiday." People will spend more overall, she said, but the increase reflects higher prices. The top 20% to 30% of consumers are not particularly deterred by higher turkey prices and continue spending, while middle-class and moderate-income households are strained.

NPR host Rob Schmitz said consumers may have noticed that turkey and gravy were more expensive, and the American Farm Bureau Federation said the cost of a 10-person Thanksgiving meal was 6% higher than a year ago. Long described the food-price mix as uneven. Consumers were already familiar with rising meat prices, but vegetable prices were rising rapidly and various condiments had soared, making it harder for households to adjust their recipes.

Beyond groceries, Long called the combined pressure a "silent squeeze." She said electricity and utility costs had become a major driver of inflation and weighed on household budgets. Her data showed people spending more on household-related bills, while property taxes and property insurance also continued to rise.

Long said people were spending close to a hundred dollars more than they were a year ago on a lot of those bills. Added to high gasoline prices, the increase was particularly squeezing moderate- and middle-income households.

For Thanksgiving travel, Long offered a little silver lining in gasoline prices, though she said the improvement was limited. She put the national average at about $3.70 a gallon, compared with almost $4.50 at the end of September. Schmitz said the price remained "far worse than a year ago," and Long agreed that prices were still way up. Before the war, the average was under $3, she said, leaving fuel costs a continuing squeeze and every dollar important for many families.

Warm weather provided another source of savings. Long said October was one of the warmest on record and November had been warm across many parts of the country, meaning many people had not needed to turn on their heat. She said that had provided another big saving and that every dollar matters.

Long's account presented holiday pressure as cumulative rather than confined to turkey, with grocery bills alongside utility, property and fuel costs while lower gasoline prices and warm weather offered limited offsets.
