## Lockheed targets about 2,000 PAC-3 MSE interceptors a year by 2030

The Pentagon is trying to accelerate production of the PAC-3 MSE, the latest Patriot interceptor, but one critical material remains tied to a narrow supply chain. Only one carbonized rayon product is approved for the most advanced Patriot missiles, and the sole approved U.S. supplier, Park Aerospace, obtains its material from France's ArianeGroup. The Pentagon has signed multiyear deals with Lockheed Martin and L3Harris to increase production and qualify new rayon sources as U.S. and allied demand rises.

Earlier this year, a senior Pentagon official met with a vendor far down the supply chain of a top U.S. missile maker for an urgent conversation about rayon, then pressed Park for more material to help ramp up missile production. ArianeGroup's limited supply constrained Park's ability to provide it. Rayon used in the PAC-3 MSE begins as strands of fiber at a Czech Republic factory owned by a Thailand-based chemicals conglomerate. The yarn moves to France to be woven; ArianeGroup, a joint venture between Airbus and Safran, carbonizes the fabric near Bordeaux. ArianeGroup sells the blackened rolls it calls Raycarb to Park, its lone North American distributor. Park coats the material with a custom-designed resin at a temperature-controlled facility in Newton, Kan., then sends it to L3Harris, which layers it inside solid rocket motors bound for Lockheed's Patriot factory.

The carbonized material forms an ablative barrier inside the rocket nozzle. It burns away gradually while protecting the motor as fuel burns at temperatures above 5,000 degrees Fahrenheit. Qualifying a replacement is not like building a car engine, where a manufacturer can buy pistons from a second supplier if the first supplier runs out: even a small structural flaw can send a Patriot interceptor off course. Each component therefore must undergo a rigorous selection process to be qualified. "There's a lot of testing that's involved," Shore said. "You could go down the path for two years, everything's looking good—then at the last test it fails, and now you're back to the drawing board."

By the end of July, the United States had rapidly burned through its already-stretched PAC-3 inventory, leaving it roughly 65% smaller than before the Iran war began in February, according to estimates from the Center for Strategic and International Studies. The PAC-3 is a hit-to-kill interceptor that slams into an incoming target, such as a ballistic missile, to destroy it. Each interceptor of the latest model, PAC-3 MSE, costs about $4 million and takes roughly two years and many hundreds of components to make. Demand extends beyond U.S. forces to allies including Ukraine, and interceptor shortages left Ukraine exposed. Shore said a physician recently asked him, "How many do they have left, Brian? Because I've heard a lot of stuff."

While Lockheed builds the interceptor, it relies on Boeing for the seekers lodged in each missile's nose that help it home in on incoming projectiles. L3Harris's dual-pulse solid rocket motor extends its range. Energetics and electronics, castings and chemical precursors, micro-thrusters and motion controllers, and a wide range of other inputs each have their own supply chains.

The rayon problem reflects broader contraction in the U.S. defense supplier base. Hundreds of subcontractors must ramp up together to support a surge, often by expanding factories or building new ones, while alternative materials undergo long, costly testing. Many critical inputs come from outside the United States, with few or no ready American alternatives. After the Soviet Union collapsed, U.S. policymakers sought a "peace dividend." At a 1993 meeting known in national-security circles as the Last Supper, Clinton administration officials told defense-industry leaders they planned to slash military spending. Over the following quarter-century, top-tier aerospace and defense companies consolidated from 51 primary contractors to five. Specialized manufacturers and machine shops left defense work or closed, and the closures were exacerbated by a broader manufacturing shift overseas, from mineral processing and chemical mixing to cables, coatings, casings and chips. Workers with niche skills lost jobs and decades of know-how. In the decade preceding a 2022 Pentagon report, the number of small businesses in the U.S. defense industrial base shrank by 40%.

March data from defense software company Air, covering PAC-3, Thaad, SM-2, SM-3 and SM-6, showed how high-stakes missile programs rely on largely the same cluster of vendors. More than 70% of second-tier suppliers for the newest generation of Patriot missiles, PAC-3, also supply Thaad, with even greater overlap among third-tier suppliers. A natural disaster, workers' strike or bankruptcy affecting one company could therefore have cascading effects across several weapons.

The latest urgency followed Russia's 2022 invasion of Ukraine. Russian barrages pushed Kyiv to ask the United States for a Patriot missile battery. The battery quickly proved effective against Russian ballistic missiles and more-maneuverable hypersonic projectiles.

Some in the Pentagon foresaw a production problem. To determine how many missiles of each type to buy annually, U.S. defense planners had long relied on a formula based on post-Cold War assumptions about likely levels of conflict around the globe, and those assumptions were eroding. The prospect they found most frightening was prolonged wars or conflict on multiple fronts between well-equipped adversaries, such as the United States and China. Responding required abandoning old calculations and overhauling an aging arms-acquisition system. Major defense companies were initially wary because, in preceding decades, the government had surged orders during spasms of conflict and then cut back sharply afterward.

The Pentagon shifted toward longer, multiyear contracts. It inked a seven-year deal with Lockheed Martin, which makes the most advanced Patriot missile, and a seven-year agreement with L3Harris, which supplies solid rocket motors. The deal awarded to Lockheed Martin calls for more than tripling PAC-3 MSE production to about 2,000 a year by the end of 2030. One defense executive said companies needed to have launched a new munitions plan five or more years earlier to be ready for the current surge; another compared the effort to pushing a basketball through a garden hose.

Park Aerospace's production complex in south-central Kansas operates under tightly controlled conditions. Sixty air-conditioning units on the factory complex keep the temperature inside below 80 degrees Fahrenheit and humidity at 20% during summer heat that can reach triple digits. Rolls of ArianeGroup's Raycarb are coated with phenolic resin and passed through a custom-built, 40-foot treater combining ovens and cooling systems before being rerolled and shipped to L3Harris. Park described the demand as a "new world order" that was "hyper and frenetic" and said in a July investor presentation, "In all our years, we have never seen anything like this." On frequent calls with L3Harris, Shore brainstorms ideas on how to bridge the supply gap. Shore said, "We see these numbers all the time. They're not good numbers."

Beyond its existing contracts, the Pentagon is contacting third- and fourth-tier suppliers directly to try to ease bottlenecks hidden beneath the surface. The Pentagon said it is "continually working on supply chain resilience and qualifying new suppliers." L3Harris said it was confident it could "maintain the materials needed to produce at quantities required, through at least the end of our 7-year framework agreement." The company said it was on target to qualify an additional rayon source but did not provide a timeline.

Park has provided a $5 million advance to help fund a new ArianeGroup production line in France. The line is expected to begin rolling out rayon in 2028, although its output would fall far short of U.S. demand. After extensive talks, Shore said ArianeGroup has signed a preliminary agreement to build a U.S. factory, backed by a promised $25 million advance from Park. If all goes to plan, the facility could open around 2030. Shore said, "The big problem is that nobody wants to wait 3½ years." Park is separately building a $65 million plant in Oklahoma while using spare capacity at its Kansas factory.

For ArianeGroup, funneling more of its current output to the United States would mean taking away allocations from top-priority clients at home, including the French military. Shore said he knew direct pressure would not work: "They're a French company. They can't be bullied. They'll just shut down on you." Earlier this year, Shore brokered a call between the Pentagon and ArianeGroup executives, who reluctantly agreed to speak after several requests. A Pentagon official pressed the executives for more supplies and an accelerated timeline for the factory. The executives said they could not do much more, according to Shore, who was briefed on the call. ArianeGroup did not answer specific questions for this story. It said it was ready to expand production capacity, including the possibility of adding manufacturing facilities outside its core countries of France and Germany.

Enka, based in Obernburg, Germany, is the U.S. defense industry's other major rayon source, but its product is not qualified for the PAC-3 MSE. Enka's main business supplies rayon for men's suits and women's high fashion, a market severely affected after the Covid pandemic popularized working from home. On Jan. 9 last year, Enka executives called their sole U.S. importer, New Jersey-based ICF Mercantile, to say they planned to shut down production. "For me, it's a day that lives in infamy," ICF Chief Executive Dave Ronner said. Ronner flew to Europe the following week to seek a solution.

U.S. defense contractors and the Pentagon eventually committed to buying enough fabric to keep Enka operating, with the Pentagon promising to purchase more than 470,000 pounds over two years. The bulk purchase postponed Enka's plant closure in Germany, but not for long, Ronner said. Ronner said he had told parties interested in the rayon, "If you need more, you need to let me know as soon as you possibly can." He also said, "As it sits now, there's an ending sometime in '28."

Meanwhile, defense officials are encouraging domestic production of aerospace-grade rayon that meets environmental standards. Both Ronner and aerospace engineer William Kaukler said they are working on green alternatives, but their efforts remain nascent. Park and L3Harris are testing Enka and other rayon sources as possible "gap fillers" for PAC-3 MSE production until ArianeGroup's U.S. factory opens, Shore said. The companies are putting contenders through suites of tests, but none has crossed the finish line. Shore said, "Even with these gap fillers, it's going to be a challenge."

Rayon's brushed form is intensely flammable. Knockoff "torch sweaters," made from easily ignited rayon masquerading as wool, sparked consumer panic and a Federal Trade Commission investigation in the 1950s. Researchers at Union Carbide discovered in 1958 that slowly heating rayon in a vacuum transforms it into heat-resistant carbon fiber. For rocket use, fibers must be long and continuous and washed free of impurities, a process that produces toxic runoff. For most of the Cold War, the sole producer of aerospace-grade rayon in North America was a textile factory on the banks of the Shenandoah River in Front Royal, Va. After the factory fell on hard times in the 1980s, NASA and the Department of Defense pooled $38 million to keep it running, but state authorities shut it down in 1989 for discharging dangerous chemicals into the river. North American Rayon Corp. filled the void, producing rocket-ready rayon in Elizabethton, Tenn., but it struggled financially in the mid-1990s. NASA and the Pentagon stockpiled NARC rayon until the company wound down production in 1998. With that stockpile dwindling in the mid-2000s and no major U.S. options left, the Pentagon had to look abroad for new supplies.

One promising candidate was the French aerospace engine maker Snecma Propulsion Solide, which had experimented with ablative materials made from high-tenacity rayon fibers used in car tires. NASA previously tested tire-cord rayon and found that it lacked strength, but Snecma developed a proprietary treatment that solved the problem. Snecma's C2 product matched or beat NARC rayon in tests. The company also expected European enthusiasm for high-performance car tires to ensure steady fiber supplies. Snecma joined with a U.S. materials company, later folded into Park Aerospace, to get a resin-coated version of C2 qualified for Patriot rocket nozzles in 2005. Snecma later became part of ArianeGroup.

Kaukler, an aerospace engineer at the University of Alabama, Huntsville, said the rayon is "so strong and so resistant to temperature" that "You can throw the highest-energy fuel through there." Park and L3Harris have candidate materials, but none had cleared the qualification process for PAC-3 MSE use, while ArianeGroup's added capacity was not due before 2028. Shore said, "Even with these gap fillers, it's going to be a challenge."
