## Seven-page draft goes on display in Atlanta before January sale

Words scrawled in blue pen — and others scratched out — give an intimate look into the writing of what became the Rev. Martin Luther King Jr.'s most famous speech. That draft goes on display in King's hometown Monday, ahead of its sale to the highest bidder at auction on Jan. 22 at Christie's in New York.

Delivered Aug. 28, 1963, from the steps of the Lincoln Memorial, the "I Have a Dream" speech reflects on the legacy of slavery, segregation and discrimination and looks forward to future equality for the country's Black citizens. The manuscript runs to seven pages from a pad of yellow, lined paper, and the words that became most famous do not appear on those pages: the "I have a dream" refrain was added by King on the spot as he stood before a crowd of tens of thousands of people gathered on the National Mall for the March on Washington. Other key parts of the speech are there in King's own handwriting, including the opening lines and the finish invoking an African American spiritual.

The public will see the draft first at the National Center for Civil and Human Rights in Atlanta, where it will be displayed from Monday through Sunday. It will then be on view in Chicago at a date to be determined, and it will hang at Christie's in New York from Jan. 15, King's birthday, through Jan. 21 before being offered for auction on Jan. 22.

According to Christie's, which will handle the sale of the manuscript, King completed the handwritten draft in his hotel room in the wee hours of the morning, less than 12 hours before it was delivered. It was then given to assistants to be typed and sent to the media, the auction house said in a news release.

The draft passed through the hands of King's speechwriting circle before it reached Xernona Clayton. Speechwriter Edward Clayton was among the multiple advisers who helped King hash out the content of the speech, and his wife, who worked alongside him in the civil rights movement, found the pages in the late 1990s, three decades after her husband's death, in a drawer of his desk, tucked inside an envelope labeled "speech."

"The two of them gelled with writing and words," Xernona Clayton said in the Christie's news release. "They would go back and forth and there was such respect. Dr. King, who was a great thinker, said Ed was so good at getting his words on paper and putting them in the right place at the right time."

Clayton recognized the document as important at the time but didn't realize it was the only remaining handwritten draft of the speech, so she stuck it back in the drawer — where she came across it again just two years ago.

Clayton established the Still Marching Irrevocable Charitable Unitrust to advance causes important to her, and she decided, after consultation with trusted advisers, to sell the draft to support charities that advance her own legacy and that of King. Christie's expects the draft to fetch more than $10 million, and the organizations that will benefit include the Xernona Clayton Foundation and the King Center in Atlanta.

"I am so grateful for the opportunity to give this amazing speech new life, and to give new life to people who are in need," Clayton said. "And we cannot wait to give that first gift."

King's two remaining living children, the Rev. Bernice King and Martin Luther King III, said in the Christie's release that they are grateful the sale will support the legacy of the movement their father helped build.

"It is poetic that the past will protect the future," Martin Luther King III said in the Christie's release. "We are grateful that this treasure was preserved over decades by the Clayton family. It is a remarkable piece of history that shines a light on our father's process as he prepared to make a speech that so profoundly affected the course of the Civil Rights movement and our nation. Now, for the first time, the public will have the opportunity to be inspired by this document."
