## European Union officials rejected the proposed Cop31 agreement

The Hunter Valley Operations coalmine expansion in New South Wales awaits a decision from federal environment minister Murray Watt, and climate campaigners are urging the Albanese government to refuse the project. The campaigners’ case leans on a warning issued at the United Nations’ Cop31 climate summit, where dozens of countries said they would not support a deal without an explicit statement that the world needs to transition away from fossil fuels — a warning they describe as a “line in the sand” for the Australian government as it considers the coal expansion.

The proposal would extend open-cut mining at the site by 19 years to 2045 and allow an additional 430m tonnes of coal to be extracted and burned.

The New South Wales planning commission approved the expansion in September after hearing from workers, unions, climate organisations, scientists and people around the state who had experienced harm as a result of the climate crisis. The commission ruled that the climate damage caused by the expansion was “outweighed by its benefits to the Hunter region and NSW.”

The companies behind the development, Glencore and Yancoal, estimated the project could lead to 800m tonnes of greenhouse gas emissions, mostly when the coal is burned overseas.

A final decision under federal nature laws now rests with Watt. The national environmental protection agency completed a report making a recommendation about the development earlier this month, and the report has not been released. A federal government spokesperson said “the decision-making process is still under way and it would be inappropriate to make further comment.” Hunter Valley Operations declined to comment.

Activists said the decision would test the Albanese government’s position on fossil fuels in a changing world. Their case rests on the outcome of the Cop31 negotiations in Turkey, where a proposed agreement failed after a list of nations — including European Union members, Panama, the Marshall Islands, Vanuatu, Norway and Belize — said they would not support it unless it included an explicit statement that the world needed to transition away from fossil fuels.

European Union officials rejected the proposed Cop31 agreement, saying it 'doesn't speak to reality of the climate crisis.'

Climate change minister Chris Bowen, who led negotiations at the talks, said he “would have been happy with transition away from fossil fuels being in the text, but that was not going to get support.” Those opposed to the wording included Saudi Arabia, Russia and other oil-rich states. Earlier this year, nearly 60 national governments were represented at a separate conference in Colombia focused on how to reduce reliance on fossil fuels.

Some longtime observers of UN climate meetings said the results of this year’s Cop31 were a sign that countries were prepared to stand up to fossil fuel interests and block a consensus unless it acknowledged action was needed to reduce on coal, oil and gas, the main causes of the climate crisis.

The Australian Conservation Foundation said it would be “reckless” for Watt to approve the proposal. “Australia is already one of the world’s largest exporters of climate-heating fossil fuels. What we export as coal and gas comes back as bushfires, floods, heatwaves and droughts,” ACF’s head of advocacy, Gavan McFadzean, said.

Georgina Woods, head of research and investigations at the Lock the Gate Alliance, said the outcome of the UN talks was “a global reflection of a dynamic that we will see coming to a head politically here in Australia.” She said the Hunter Valley Operations coalmine was “a huge coalmine with enormous global warming consequences, and it should be refused.”

“Leaders have preferred to avoid getting stuck into the heart of the issue, that global warming is being driven by the burning of coal, oil, and gas,” Woods said. “There is a use-by date on that tactic and we’re living through it.” She added: “As people everywhere suffer mounting impacts from the global warming that is caused by mining and burning coal, oil and gas, they will become less and less willing to accept that those industries should keep on being protected when everyone else is paying the price for their pollution.”

Naomi Hodgson, from Rising Tide, a grassroots climate group that holds an annual “people’s blockade” at the coal port of Newcastle, said “to make true on nice words about Pacific solidarity and climate action, the Albanese government must immediately reject” the Hunter Valley development, which she described as a “climate bomb.”

The expansion remains before Watt under federal nature laws, with the national environmental protection agency’s report completed earlier this month and still unreleased, and the government has said it will not comment further on the process.
