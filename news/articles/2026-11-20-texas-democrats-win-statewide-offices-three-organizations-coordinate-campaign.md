## Texas Together shared quantitative voter data and split specialized work

On Nov. 3, 2026, Texas Democrats won a U.S. Senate seat, the governor’s office and a broad slate of down-ballot offices. Their 2026 operation was Texas Together, a $30 million coordinated campaign involving the Texas Democratic Party, Texas Majority Pac and Powered by People. Texas Together supported Democratic candidacies by sharing resources and coordinating work while the three organizations continued to operate independently in tandem.

James Talarico defeated Texas Attorney General Ken Paxton to become the state’s first elected Democratic U.S. senator in nearly 30 years. Gina Hinojosa was set to become Texas’s first Latina governor and its first Democratic governor since 1995. Texas Democrats flipped 20 state House seats, three state Board of Education seats, all four Texas Supreme Court seats and all three Texas Court of Criminal Appeals seats up for election.

Wendy Davis, a former state senator and the 2014 Democratic gubernatorial candidate, compared managing the result to trying to drink out of a firehose and said, “Who knew that winning was such hard work?” At a midterm election-night watch party at the Long Center for the Performing Arts in Austin on Nov. 3, Talarico spoke after winning the Texas Senate race. (Photograph: Ronaldo Schemidt/AFP/Getty Images.)

The three groups shared access to quantitative voter data and discounts for mail advertising. The Texas Democratic Party focused on growing internally. Texas Majority Pac connected candidates with voter research and fundraising and hosted paid virtual civic classes explaining how policies such as the minimum wage affected the ability to afford groceries. Powered by People, O’Rourke’s youth voter engagement organization, focused on mobilizing people aged 18 to 29 to vote for Democrats despite their resistance to identifying with either major party.

Organizing and delegating more than 30,000 volunteers to campaigns and other projects became a shared effort. Aimee Prudhomme said, “We agreed as different entities, different bodies, that we all had the same goal.” She added, “Working with these groups, it’s very apparent what’s possible when we can truly coordinate together.” Kendall Scudder said the groups should play to their strengths, remain in their designated roles and avoid duplicating work, disputes over strategy and stepping on one another’s efforts. “We knew we were going to be out-raised and out-spent, so how do we get as efficient as possible?” he said. On Nov. 4, Senator-elect Talarico and Governor-elect Hinojosa, both Democrats, were photographed with drinks among supporters at Whataburger in Austin. (Photograph: James Talarico/X/Reuters.)

## Recruitment drive expanded the candidate field

As part of the coordinated effort, Texas Democrats worked to find candidates for every state and federal race for the first time. With help from Wendy Davis, Beto O’Rourke and other politicians, Texas Together hosted 40 recruitment rallies, interviewed 1,385 candidates in target districts and recruited 104 candidates for the 2026 election. Ninety percent of the recruits were running for public office for the first time.

The scale of the field differed sharply from 2024, when Republicans won five U.S. House seats and 22 state House seats without Democratic opposition. Democrats won at least 29 state House seats in 2026, including the 20 they flipped, and many of the winners faced Republican incumbents. At the recruitment rallies, Democrats encouraged attendees to nominate leaders within their communities who might otherwise not nominate themselves. Davis worked with prospective candidates, particularly working mothers who were anxious about running but spoke passionately about affordability and education. Senate candidates Talarico and Paxton also campaigned at the University of North Texas on the first day of early voting. (Photograph: Bloomberg/Getty Images.)

Among the recruited candidates was Stefanie Bord, a former science teacher and active volunteer with Moms Demand Action, an anti-gun violence organization. Bord ran for a state House district in Houston. Her experience as a Harris County precinct chair gave her an advantage over other first-time candidates. The Abbott campaign sent AI-generated mailers alleging that Bord had “failed to support” his so-called affordability plan. She challenged the flyer’s inaccuracies and told voters, “Just in case everybody doesn’t know it, I am not an elected official – I am a mom!” Bord, the first Democrat to run in her district since 2020, defeated her Republican opponent by 290 votes.

## Voter-contact methods shaped turnout efforts

Prudhomme said she believed the down-ballot races were where Powered by People made the greatest impact. In Tarrant County, where Democrats flipped three state House seats, voters in the organization’s network accounted for more than one-third of the winning margin. In Brazos County, Democrat Janet Dudding defeated the Republican incumbent by 309 votes, a narrow margin measured against the 1,438 network voters who participated. Davis said, “I believe that James and Gina wouldn’t have won as resoundingly as they did [nor] flip Tarrant county as decisively as we did” without the down-ballot races.

In 2025, Democrat Taylor Rehmet flipped his House seat in north Fort Worth, on the outskirts of Dallas. Texas Majority Pac used the race to test broader-targeting strategies. After Rehmet advanced to the runoffs, the group targeted every primary-election voter regardless of previous voting history. Rather than trying to persuade voters to support Rehmet, Texas Majority Pac surveyed them about what would influence them to vote for him.

Texas Majority Pac also piloted paid virtual civic classes among Texans considered less likely to vote. Fischer said the classes were 10 times more effective at turning Texans out to vote than traditional methods such as phone banking and mailers.

## Context: Earlier losses and a 2024 Senate defeat

The Guardian reported that organizers had spent more than a decade trying, without success, to mobilize voters in Republican-led Texas. The Guardian said a statewide Democratic victory had seemed remote, while liberal Texans had become cynical after repeated disappointments, and described the down-ballot flips as a surprise.

Davis, who rose to national prominence through her filibuster of the state Senate’s 20-week abortion ban, lost the 2014 governor’s race to Greg Abbott, then the state attorney general, by more than 20 percentage points.

Four years later, in 2018, O’Rourke came within just under 215,000 votes of defeating Senator Ted Cruz after a grassroots statewide tour of all 254 counties during his Senate campaign. The Guardian said O’Rourke’s tour and his volunteer-led voter engagement organization, Powered by People, gave hope to a new generation of Texans. He launched the organization in 2020. Davis now supports down-ballot Democrats through Pac Blueprint Texas.

Aimee Prudhomme, 39, executive director of Powered by People, said, “A lot of us have not known a world where that was possible.” In January, she was set to live in a Democratic-led Texas for the first time since childhood; for many members of the organization’s staff, it would be the first. “We kept trying to fight for it and then kept having our hearts broken, time and time again in this state,” she said.

The party had hoped Colin Allred could defeat Cruz in the 2024 Senate race. George Soros provided county-level financial support through Texas Majority Pac, but Allred lost to Cruz by over eight points. Texas Majority Pac executive director Katherine Fischer, a former campaign staffer for both of O’Rourke’s statewide races, described the loss as a moment of intense scrutiny and reflection between Texas Majority Pac and the Texas Democratic Party: “It was sort of like a slap in the face.” She said the loss “pushed us in Texas to run a bunch of experiments, test new ways of talking to voters, that produced a lot of innovative techniques”.

## Context: A party-reform letter changed operations

Pressure for sweeping organizational changes grew after O’Rourke’s 2018 loss and the Texas Democratic Party’s failure to produce wins for down-ballot candidates in 2020. Dozens of the party’s executive committee members signed a letter demanding that chair Gilberto Hinojosa, the father of governor-elect Gina Hinojosa, initiate sweeping changes to the party’s direction.

Among those leading the effort was Scudder, a former Democratic state Senate candidate from religious, rural east Texas, home to some of the “saltiest, grittiest Democrats out there”, as he put it. When Democrats failed to win again in 2024, Hinojosa resigned and Scudder ran to finish the remainder of his term and won. Scudder’s subsequent changes echoed the demands in the party members’ letter. Scudder spoke at the Texas Democrats watch party at the Hyatt Regency in Austin on Nov. 3. (Photograph: Sara Diggins/The San Antonio Express-News/AP.)

One change under Scudder was a task force to reconsider the location of the party’s headquarters. Scudder said organizers outside Austin had a harder time getting party leaders to pay attention to their issues without knowing someone inside the capital who could help. Texas Democrats moved the headquarters from Austin to Dallas, maintained an office in the capital city and opened offices in Amarillo, Eagle Pass and Houston. The stated purpose of the move was to break up the saturation.

The 2020 party letter also called for an overhaul of the approved vendor list. Scudder said the previous list consisted of “holdovers from old-guard Democratic operatives,” which made it harder for newer, innovative talent to be considered for services such as ready-to-use digital branding. A new, transparent request-for-proposal process resulted in all vendors being owned by at least one person of color.

Scudder described the party’s earlier approach this way: “We became this corporate party for defending institutions, and I’m sorry, defending institutions is not standing for something.” He added, “So if those institutions are gonna burn to the ground, let’s not try to rebuild them the way they were – let’s build them the way they should be built.”

## Context: Democrats emphasized affordability and healthcare

The messaging was intended for Texans across party lines. The Guardian reported that Democrats shifted their emphasis to issues affecting people daily, including affordability and healthcare, over “culture war” rhetoric. Hinojosa campaigned on ending the state’s interference in local public school systems rather than explicitly discussing bans on books with queer or racial themes. Talarico campaigned on ending corruption in government and state overreach in private healthcare decisions of all Texans.

The Guardian wrote that Democrats won on “a populist message of economic relief that echoed the exhaustion that voters had begun to externalize.” David Villalobos, statewide political director of the Texas Organizing Project, said, “I was helping [voters] connect the dots, and man, they were connecting the dots with us, or for us, too.”

Scudder, who began his political career defending his lesbian parents against Republican attacks, said the party needed to broaden the issues it addressed to bring in more people. “Sometimes in our efforts to be inclusive, we have put people in boxes. Yes, suicide rates are through the roof in trans and LGBT communities. And a lot of that ties into things like being unhoused; a lot of that ties into economics, right?” Scudder said. “If we can focus on these things we all have in common, we can not allow communities to bear the brunt of a lot of these attacks, but face it together.”
