## Challenges say rule reverses nearly 30 years of federal policy

State attorneys general, public housing authorities and tenant groups have sued the U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development over a rule that would require each member of a household receiving federal housing assistance to prove citizenship or eligible immigration status. The cases are before a Massachusetts federal judge as the rule is scheduled to take effect December 14, and affected families would have until March 14 to verify every member's status after housing authorities begin notifying them.

The dispute centers on whether HUD may require every person in an assisted household to prove immigration status. HUD says the rule implements the 1980 Housing and Community Development Act, while challengers say it reverses congressional policy dating to 1996 and violates the Administrative Procedure Act.

A coalition of 22 state attorneys general filed its federal suit Monday, days after public tenant advocates and residents filed a class-action complaint for mixed-status families. The tenant groups said the rule “leaves families no real choice” because an assisted household would otherwise have to break apart or leave assisted housing. The complaint also said families forced out would enter a housing market already desperately short of affordable units.

Both the tenant and state cases argue the rule is arbitrary and capricious, exceeds HUD's authority and violates the Administrative Procedure Act. Tenants also claim the rule and its documentation requirement violate the equal protection and due process guarantees of the Fifth Amendment. The attorneys general wrote that “Federal financial assistance is not a slush fund that executive agencies can redirect at will to the groups they favor,” and argued that Congress's definition of eligible families includes mixed-status households.

HUD Secretary Scott Turner said in a statement Monday evening that the final rule “fully implements the 1980 Housing and Community Development Act and ends the exploitation of taxpayer dollars.” He said the change represents a reallocation of up to $218 million to eligible Americans on housing waitlists. Turner said the rule guarantees that only American citizens and certain eligible noncitizens will reside in HUD-funded housing. He also said earlier this month that “the federal government is not good at providing housing” and that long-term subsidies are “not the way that it’s intended to be.”

Since 1996, Congress has allowed families whose members have different legal immigration statuses to live in federally funded housing, provided assistance is prorated so only eligible household members receive support. The state and tenant plaintiffs say the rule reverses that policy, while HUD says it is carrying out the congressional mandate in the 1980 law.

The state prosecutors estimate that about 20,000 mixed-status families, totaling 78,100 people, could lose housing assistance under the rule. Their estimate includes 55,000 people who are fully eligible for federal housing assistance, many of them children and seniors. In Massachusetts, about 910 households would likely be affected.

One class-action plaintiff is a Massachusetts mother of four who has lived in the country for 15 years and is authorized to live and work in Massachusetts, although her immigration status is not among those deemed eligible under the rule. She lives in a Section 8 subsidized apartment with her four children, all 12 or younger, three of whom are U.S. citizens. The suit asserts that the family would lose its subsidy entirely and face eviction and homelessness because the citizen children cannot live independently from their mother.

The City of Boston and the Boston Housing Authority also sued HUD, with city officials saying more than 500 Boston families could have federal housing assistance stripped away. Kenzie Bok, the housing authority's administrator, said, “Almost every family we house at the Boston Housing Authority has previously suffered through a period of homelessness.” She said, “These are Boston kids, they are eligible for federal housing in their own right, and they deserve to stay safely in their homes.” The litigation also includes a case brought by public housing authorities including Boston, New York, San Francisco and Chicago, filed in the District of Massachusetts. Those authorities call the rule “callous, ill-conceived, and illegal” and say it departs from statutory authority, congressional intent and prior regulations.

Lawyers and advocates cautioned that the December 14 effective date would not mean benefits were withdrawn immediately. Housing authorities would have about a month to begin sending notices to affected families, which would then have until March 14 to verify the eligibility of every member.

Zafiro Patiño, director of organizing for the Massachusetts Union of Public Housing Tenants, urged families to prepare but remain patient. “We are basically saying to people, ‘Do not sign anything yet.’ The law still is the same, so do not panic,” she said. Her group had already heard that some housing authorities were sending notices, which Patiño expected would alarm many households. She said housing authorities already faced substantial work and would face more under the rule.

Annette Duke, a senior housing attorney at the Mass Law Reform Institute, said the rule would affect about 8.8 million tenants nationwide because every tenant would have to verify status. She said the burden would fall disproportionately on people of color, women, children and people with disabilities. “The rule really seeks to divide the communities,” she said, “and what’s great is this lawsuit shows that people are standing together to fight this.”

HUD's impact review estimates that sheltering people displaced by the rule could cost state and local governments between $20,000 and $50,000 per person each year.

The tenant and state cases ask a Massachusetts federal judge to declare the rule unlawful, strike it down and block HUD from implementing or enforcing it against states, local governments and public housing authorities. HUD says the rule carries out Congress's housing mandate and redirects aid to eligible Americans on waitlists; the plaintiffs say it exceeds HUD's authority and puts families at risk of losing housing.
