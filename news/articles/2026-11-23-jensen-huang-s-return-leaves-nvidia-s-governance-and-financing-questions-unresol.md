## Commerce Department restrictions and congressional scrutiny remain in view

Jensen Huang returned as Nvidia’s chief executive on Monday, and the chipmaker’s shares rose 31.48%, adding $1 trillion to its market value. The Nasdaq gained 7%, while more than four in five members of the S&P 500 advanced as traders focused on the return and government support for the company.

Nvidia said Huang had been confirmed as “not a national security risk” according to “senior levels of the United States government.” The Wall Street Journal columnist James Mackintosh said that removed a major risk for Nvidia and avoided the disruption of losing the chief executive who built the company. Huang’s 60 direct reports, along with his daughter and son-in-law, could carry on functioning under his return, Mackintosh reported.

Nvidia also said it was carrying out an independent investigation into Huang’s entanglement with an alleged Chinese spy, Mackintosh reported. The return eased a separate leadership risk but did not end the investigation or the broader questions surrounding Huang’s position at the company.

Mackintosh framed the market question as whether the surge would become an epic version of what traders call a “dead cat bounce,” meaning a temporary rebound after a plunging market. He wrote that traders were focused on the short term and that support from President Trump had become central to the reaction.

On China, Mackintosh said it was hard to see the U.S. government going further with serious investigations into how Nvidia chips ended up in China after the White House pressed for Huang’s return. During Huang’s interregnum, the Commerce Department issued a rule restricting Chinese access to U.S. chips in offshore data centers.

The return also made it less likely that Nvidia would abandon what Mackintosh described as its strategy of acting as a bank for weak customers, giving hope to companies that rely on circular financing. CoreWeave, which Mackintosh called one of the weakest “neoclouds,” jumped 23%. Micron and SK Hynix, suppliers of the expensive memory chips needed in data centers, rose by almost as much, and most of the rest of the AI supply chain gained double-digit percentages.

Mackintosh’s governance criticism focused on Nvidia’s board and the sequence of decisions surrounding Huang’s return. Chairman Harvey Jones told investors the previous week that Huang would not return, even as President Trump called for his reinstatement. Nvidia still insists that its board is independent. Mackintosh asked whether Trump had become the board’s “shadow chairman” and argued that Huang was too powerful to fire, making Nvidia’s code of conduct appear meaningless.

The columnist also cited what he described as conflicts of interest arising from Huang’s affairs with a customer and an Nvidia executive. Mackintosh wrote that opponents of unfettered capitalism had gained a powerful new marketing tool in Huang’s return, while even centrists jaded by corporate scandals should be outraged by the conflicts he had raised.

Investor concerns about capital spending remain another unresolved issue, Mackintosh wrote. More than half of fund managers expected a Big Tech company to cut capital expenditures in the next six months, according to a Bank of America survey. Nvidia can finance customers who buy its chips for a while, but Mackintosh said the model eventually requires outside investors to supply the trillions of dollars forecast for data centers.

Nvidia also faces intense competition from AMD, Broadcom, Alphabet and Amazon. Mackintosh said Huang’s return might help Nvidia innovate, but it did not remove that pressure, which he expected to reduce the company’s margins eventually.

Congressional scrutiny may continue, Mackintosh said, with China hawks in both parties likely to keep pressing investigations into Nvidia and Huang. He noted that if investigators found anything embarrassing, Nvidia could no longer pass the blame to a former CEO.

Together, the developments left Huang back at Nvidia but did not resolve the questions Mackintosh identified around governance, conflicts, financing, competition and congressional oversight. The market’s immediate response was strong; his analysis focused on the longer-term exposure that remained.
