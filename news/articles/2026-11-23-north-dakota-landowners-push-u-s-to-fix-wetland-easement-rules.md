## U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service pilot would remap easements with 18 landowners

About 200 people filled a room at the North Dakota Farmers Union headquarters in Jamestown on Monday to discuss how to correct a history of poorly documented conservation agreements and how to ensure ducks and other wildlife can thrive on North Dakota wetlands. The agreements paid landowners in return for agreeing not to disturb the wetlands. Many people in the room were descendants of North Dakota landowners who signed agreements in the 1960s.

Most of the people who spoke identified themselves as landowners dealing with restrictions on drain tile and vague records of what their families had agreed to in the 1960s. Ryan Pederson, who farms in Rolette County, called signing a conservation agreement with the federal government the "greatest single mistake" that ancestors of North Dakota landowners ever made. Pederson told Interior Secretary Doug Burgum, a former North Dakota governor, "it is your mistake to have to deal with." The Interior Department oversees the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, which holds the agreements.

Burgum called the dispute a national issue but said a disproportionate share of the problem falls on North Dakota, which has prime habitat for breeding ducks. Sen. Kevin Cramer, R-N.D., who organized the meeting, said more than 50% of the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service's easements are in North Dakota and that the state has more than 16,000 such agreements. Another 30% of the easements are in South Dakota, Cramer said.

Cramer said the problem of poorly mapped easements has been exacerbated by a setback rule enacted by the Biden administration. The rule expands the area around wetlands where farmers cannot install drain tile, or plastic tubing buried in the ground that drains excess moisture. Violating the rules can result in criminal charges.

Landowners showed maps with wetlands as small as less than 1 acre, and sometimes less than one-tenth of 1 acre. Burgum said that under the setback rule, the areas taken out of agricultural production are sometimes more than five times the size of the original easement.

Cramer said the small wetlands often no longer hold water and instead have become heavy with salt deposits, making the area poor for wildlife and farming. "There are no ducks on saline divots," he said.

Drew Courtney, who farms in Dickey County near the South Dakota state line, said most farmers agree on being good stewards of the land. "We love our land, we love wildlife habitat, and we love hunting, too," Courtney said. He showed a map of small wetlands on his farm and how the setback rules limit his ability to use drain tile.

Others at the meeting spoke in favor of preserving small wetlands. Jill Shaffer, a retired ecologist from the Northern Prairie Wildlife Research Center who lives south of Jamestown, said habitat areas of less than 1 acre and seasonal wetlands are important to ducks and other wildlife species. "They are your everything," she said.

The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service has started a pilot program to address the mapping issue. The agency has met with 18 landowners — 17 in North Dakota and one in South Dakota — to try to agree on new maps that protect a similar number of acres to the original agreements, though the areas protected may shift to create larger protection areas.

Changing the setback rule itself is a separate challenge. In August, the agency said it would pause a change to the rule while it reviewed more than 200,000 comments. Sen. John Hoeven, R-N.D., thanked Cramer for leading the North Dakota effort to change the rule but said it may have to go beyond administrative rule changes and include action by Congress or a legal challenge.

Cramer called the rule "theft of property" and a breach of the contract signed by landowners who have lost trust in the federal government. "We're here to rebuild that trust," he said.

Cramer had put a hold on Trump administration confirmations in the Senate while advocating for the administration to change the wetlands rule. He said he has been releasing those confirmations as there have been more conversations about the rule change, including visits to North Dakota by Fish and Wildlife officials.
