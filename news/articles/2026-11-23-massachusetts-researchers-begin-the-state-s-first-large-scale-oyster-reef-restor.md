## Westport survey counted hundreds of oysters ahead of spring seeding

On a boat in the neck of the Westport River, Massachusetts Division of Marine Fisheries researchers and conservation nonprofit The Nature Conservancy are conducting Massachusetts’s first large-scale oyster reef restoration. The partners received a five-year federal grant in 2024 to try to restore the reefs, and Massachusetts Division of Marine Fisheries Habitat Restoration Specialist Sean Terrill calls the proposed 60-acre Westport site the flagship among the project’s locations.

Summer O’Rourke, a DMF seasonal technician, spent five hours counting oysters without taking a lunch break. She used a pair of pricey digital calipers she brought from home to measure each bump and cluster, brushing away muck, sand and the occasional mud crab to get an accurate reading of the shellfish’s growth. The work is slow and meticulous, but it is integral to determining whether the restoration succeeds.

O’Rourke wears gloves, though she does not appear to need them. She grew up playing with the lobsters her father brought home. “He’d yell at me to put the rubber bands on (their claws), but I knew how to hold them,” O’Rourke said. “And I still have all my fingers.”

Her work takes place on a boat in the neck of the Westport River. Hundreds of years ago, oyster reefs dominated the Eastern Seaboard and provided critical habitat for coastal species at a scale rivaling coral reefs. Thousands of acres were destroyed over the past 500 years through overharvesting and the effects of fertilizer, sewage and sediment.

Large-scale restorations have proved successful in the Chesapeake Bay region and are underway in states like New York and Rhode Island, making the Massachusetts project the first of its kind in the state.

In October, O’Rourke and a team of scientists and volunteers canvassed the bottom of the future restoration sites to establish a baseline of the oysters already there. On Oct. 15, Terrill and his team, accompanied by O’Rourke, Westport River Watershed Alliance Science Director Roberta Carvalho and others, surveyed the Westport River. Members of DMF’s habitat team suited up in scuba gear to retrieve oysters from the riverbed, while O’Rourke and Carvalho counted and measured each one.

After seven hours on the water, O’Rourke and Carvalho had counted hundreds of oysters. Terrill said his team was still finalizing the data from that day, but he was pleasantly surprised to see the oysters thriving even in areas open to recreational shellfishing.

No statewide regulatory body manages shellfishing grounds in Massachusetts. To obtain permission to seed wild oysters in the state’s waterways, Terrill and his partners at The Nature Conservancy went town to town for approval, pitching restoration sites in Westport, Fairhaven, Mashpee and Bourne. The 60-acre Westport River proposal is the largest of the proposed sites and, according to Terrill, the flagship for oyster restoration.

The Westport River’s wide mouth and brackish waters, shielded by harsh currents, present ideal conditions for oyster growth. If currents become too strong, oyster larvae cannot establish on the river floor; if the bottom is too muddy, as in some freshwater areas, oysters could be buried. “There used to be hundreds of acres of oyster reef habitat there,” Terrill said. “It just meets a lot of criteria for being a really good place for restoration.”

In spring, Terrill and his team plan to seed the future reef sites with cultch, or discarded oyster shells, to create a rocky environment where oyster larvae can attach. Shells are not always easy to obtain. Nonprofits such as the Massachusetts Oyster Project help fish markets and seafood restaurants recycle their oyster shells for aquaculture and projects like this restoration, but Terrill said the vast majority of shells still end up in landfills.

Once the cultch is set, scientists will lay down spat-on-shell, a mixture of oyster larvae and shells. With any luck, the reefs will self-populate over time, and the new larvae could travel to and from neighboring reefs. “We’re going to be kind of connecting the dots,” Terrill said. “The reefs that we’re restoring are going to start producing larvae, and so then we could restore even further downstream, and hopefully just create a meta population within the system that are all exchanging larvae and increasing genetic diversity.”

Terrill is still determining what true success will look like. The standard for a successful oyster reef in the Chesapeake Bay region is 50 oysters per square meter, though some areas Terrill’s team surveyed in Massachusetts in October already have hundreds of oysters per square meter. In the long term, Terrill hopes to restore 2,500 acres of oyster reefs by 2050. The goal is a small step toward rectifying the thousands of acres lost over five centuries.

Restored reefs could house fish and other species, while oysters could improve water quality by pulling nitrogen out of the river and driving excess carbon into the soil. “The need is to restore a severely degraded wild species, and by doing that, bring back all of the ecosystem benefits that it provides,” Terrill said. The next phase is planned for spring, but the outcome remains uncertain. Terrill acknowledged that the effort involves guesswork: “We’ll just have to see what happens.”
