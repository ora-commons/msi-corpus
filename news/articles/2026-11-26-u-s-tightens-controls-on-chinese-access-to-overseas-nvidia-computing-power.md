## U.S. tightens controls on Chinese access to overseas Nvidia computing power

## Overseas data centers face a 90-day licensing deadline

The Wall Street Journal reported that the Commerce Department rolled out the rule on Nov. 19, requiring overseas companies with Nvidia hardware to obtain a license before leasing significant computing power to Chinese firms. Providers already leasing restricted Nvidia computing power to Chinese clients have 90 days, until February, to secure a U.S. license or end those relationships. Noncompliance can bring fines, criminal charges or placement on a U.S. trade blacklist.

The rule also imposed stricter controls on exports of advanced Nvidia chips to five countries, including Malaysia. Unlike export rules that ordinarily govern future shipments, the measure reaches existing leases. Its stated goal is to prevent Chinese companies from using Nvidia’s top chips to develop advanced AI models that could pose a national-security risk.

Washington spent years trying to restrict China’s access to cutting-edge AI semiconductors by blocking exports of chipmaking machines and direct sales of Nvidia hardware. But the Trump administration shelved a Biden-era export rule in May 2025, allowing Chinese firms to rent Nvidia computing power from overseas data centers. The new licensing regime tightens that remote-access route and could redirect restricted chips toward American companies that have faced logistical and political challenges building more data centers at home. The biggest hub for that remote access is Southeast Asia, especially Malaysia, which The Wall Street Journal noted is friendly with Beijing, has a large Chinese-speaking population and is welcoming to data centers.

The Institute for Progress estimated in an October report that remote access supplied 24% of China’s AI computing power. The think tank also estimated that smuggling supplied about 22% this year, nearly as much as remote access. Researchers in the United States and China estimated that American AI models lead Chinese models by roughly six months. The Wall Street Journal said the new policy was set to widen that gap.

The United States is also expanding its overseas enforcement staff, adding embassy-based export-control officers to help curb the smuggling of advanced chips into China. Some U.S. officials have long complained there were too few regional officers, essentially cops stationed at embassies to sniff out smuggling. Just eight operated worldwide last year, periodically visiting sites to verify that Nvidia chips were accounted for. After Congress boosted funding, that number is set to reach 20 by year’s end, according to a U.S. official, and could grow to 65 by the end of 2027 if Congress approves the president’s budget request. Southeast Asia has had only one officer, based in Singapore, and the Commerce Department is adding officers in both Malaysia and Thailand, according to people familiar with the matter.

U.S. officials will review license applications case by case. The rule includes an exemption for chips used for social media and other commercial purposes. A U.S. official said the goal was to avoid hindering legitimate business and that applicants must show that a data center is used exclusively for commercial work that does not threaten national security. The Wall Street Journal reported that answers will emerge over the next three months in Malaysia, as U.S. officials begin vetting Nvidia chip owners.

Some Chinese companies leasing Nvidia power in Malaysia, including Alibaba and ByteDance, build advanced AI models alongside their commercial businesses. Southeast Asian data centers with Chinese clients expect licenses will be difficult to obtain.

The rule already affected one infrastructure deal. The Wall Street Journal reported that a day after the rule took effect, the Malaysian arm of Dubai-based data-center operator Damac forfeited a deposit by terminating a $49 million land deal in Malaysia, just four days after its announcement. Damac provides Alibaba with infrastructure for Nvidia computing, according to a person familiar with the matter. Damac and Alibaba did not respond to requests for comment.

Companies have invested more than $30 billion to build the data-center industry in Malaysia and neighboring countries. Much of that infrastructure was built for Chinese technology companies such as Alibaba, ByteDance and Tencent, which operates WeChat. With licenses expected to be difficult to obtain, operators leasing capacity to Chinese firms will need to find non-Chinese customers.

“Only the U.S. firms would be able to match the demand appetite that was provided by Chinese clients in the past,” said James Chai, a researcher at Iseas-Yusof Ishak Institute in Singapore. People in the industry said Southeast Asian operators may even have to cut prices to lure new customers.

Some Southeast Asian AI operators anticipated tighter controls by writing contracts with Chinese buyers that allowed them to suspend service when U.S. export controls changed, easing the way for American companies to become customers. Southeast Asian companies are working to build their roster of American clients. Singapore-based cloud company Aolani courted investors last year by pitching to connect Chinese customers with advanced chips. Now it likely needs a U.S. license to continue leasing access to Nvidia chips to its major Chinese customer, ByteDance. Aolani, which had some $2.5 billion in hardware at the beginning of the year, is working to diversify its client roster. In September it signed San Francisco-based AI company FriendliAI, and aims to have American companies make up at least 70% of its client base by mid-2027.

Aolani said it had always intended to build a global customer base and welcomed the new regulatory framework. “We expect many more U.S.-based companies to recognize that Southeast Asia can offer economical access to compute in a compliant way,” a company spokesman said.

Export-control officers often arrange visits to data centers and other sites, giving operators an incentive to cooperate to avoid blacklisting or other U.S. penalties. Local governments also generally cooperate to avoid losing American business. One such check aided a major smuggling prosecution. In December 2025, an officer visited a Southeast Asian warehouse to verify a company’s Nvidia-powered servers and, according to an indictment, was shown dummy servers fitted with stickers peeled from the real ones. An indictment said the company had bought at least $510 million worth of servers and sent them to China. The case is continuing.

The Wall Street Journal said enforcement will be especially important because President Trump had helped reinstate Nvidia chief Jensen Huang after lawmakers from both parties questioned whether he helped supply Chinese customers. The report also said Huang’s affair with a suspected Chinese intelligence asset and chip smuggler had drawn congressional scrutiny, and that U.S. officials would likely focus on closing the loophole.

“So long as there’s at least half-hearted enforcement of the rules, this is a pretty big shift,” said Chris Miller, a Tufts University professor and author of “Chip War.” Kevin Kurland, a former official at the Commerce Department arm enforcing the policy, said: “This is a significant action that fills the loophole that Chinese companies used to circumvent chip controls through remote access of AI chips.”

Industry officials expect some smaller companies serving only Chinese customers may find it more lucrative to disappear from the U.S. government’s radar and smuggle chips into China. At the same time, the restrictions could shift computing capacity toward American customers and spark a rush to smuggle the chips into China, making enforcement and license reviews the central tests of the policy.
