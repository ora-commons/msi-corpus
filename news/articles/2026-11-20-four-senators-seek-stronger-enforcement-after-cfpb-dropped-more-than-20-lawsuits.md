## Complaints against firms involved in dropped cases rose by an average of more than 75%

Four US Senate Democrats said Friday they would work to revive the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau after a Guardian investigation documented a sharp reduction in the watchdog's public enforcement. Elizabeth Warren, Chris Van Hollen, Chuck Schumer and Angela Alsobrooks backed stronger agency action, with Schumer tying restoration of the bureau's full capacity to Democrats gaining control of Congress.

The Guardian's investigation found that the CFPB dropped more than 20 lawsuits against banks and corporations accused of a range of financial misconduct. Complaints against those firms rose by an average of more than 75% after the cases were dropped. In many of the recent complaints, consumers described the same issues that the CFPB's lawsuits had sought to address.

That shift came as the agency dramatically scaled back public enforcement under the second Trump administration, even while Americans submitted record numbers of complaints. After averaging roughly 30 public enforcement actions each year for more than a decade, the CFPB had initiated just one since Director Rohit Chopra, a Joe Biden appointee, was fired on 30 January 2025.

Elizabeth Warren, the Massachusetts senator who first proposed creating the CFPB after the 2008 financial crash and is set to become chair of the Senate Banking Committee in January, promised to investigate the administration's attempts to weaken the bureau. “Donald Trump has sidelined the financial cop on the beat that has returned more than $21bn directly to Americans who were cheated by big banks and giant corporations,” Warren said in a statement.

Warren said The Guardian's reporting “shows what came next: Trump’s billionaire friends and Wall Street donors getting away with scamming Americans.” She added: “The next Congress will defend the CFPB, investigate how the Trump administration has abandoned cheated Americans, and put forward legislation to protect consumers and bring down costs.”

Chuck Schumer, the Senate minority leader, vowed to “make sure that the CFPB is operating at its full capacity” once Democrats gain control of Congress. “The Trump administration has done everything in its power to bow down to special interests and billionaires at the expense of American families,” Schumer said. “The CFPB was created to make sure that everyday folks could be protected from predatory actors, but Republicans have undermined it at every turn.”

Chris Van Hollen, who announced on 12 November that he would challenge Schumer for the role of Senate majority leader when the new Congress convenes, said the CFPB's rollback demanded greater focus on working Americans. “The CFPB returned billions of dollars to Americans who were conned and scammed by big corporations,” Van Hollen said. “Naturally, many billionaires didn’t like that, so they had Trump and his cronies use every tool they could to dismantle an agency whose job is to stand up for working families.”

Angela Alsobrooks, Van Hollen's fellow senator from Maryland, also defended the bureau. “The CFPB works for the American people,” she said. “The CFPB is worth fighting for.”

The CFPB's leadership under the second Trump administration has claimed that the agency has long been harmful to companies and consumers alike. A spokesperson said the CFPB had cost Americans hundreds of billions of dollars, “making the American Dream, like homeownership, more costly by the thousands for each borrower.”

The spokesperson also said: “This Weaponized agency attacked political enemies and disfavored industries and used novel legal theories and secret justifications to ruin lives and businesses.” The statement added: “We are fixing that and bringing the agency back to operating within statute and away from breaking the law and helping all business and Americans who were victims of thuggery.”

The Guardian's investigation came after midterm elections in which the economy and cost of living were at the top of most voters' minds. Across their statements, the senators linked the CFPB debate to working families, consumer protection and lower costs, while the agency's leadership defended its reduced enforcement posture as a return to statutory operation.

The steps named by the senators were prospective: Warren promised an investigation and legislation for the next Congress, while Schumer tied restoration of full capacity to Democratic control. The CFPB's leadership said it was already correcting what it called unlawful agency practices. Their competing accounts left the bureau's future enforcement role unresolved.
