## Authors say White House mapped out two years of governing without Congress

Congressman Steve Cohen and Michael Shank said in a Guardian opinion column published on November 19 that the Democratic gains in the midterms did not end the contest over American institutions. Cohen has represented Tennessee's ninth district since 2006, and Shank is an adjunct professor at New York University's Center for Global Affairs and George Mason University's Carter School for Peace and Conflict Resolution.

Cohen and Shank argued that state and local officials, community organizations and the incoming Congress each face a test beginning with the January swearing-in, and they urged local governments to prepare emergency-management plans and protocols before then. The column described the midterms as a "blue tsunami" and listed what the authors called historic firsts: the country's first female Black governor, the first female Native American governor and the first Muslim US senator.

Cohen and Shank wrote that Democrats had seized the majority in both chambers of Congress, which they described as a clear repudiation of Donald Trump's agenda. The authors said the work of defending democratic institutions would not ease overnight even after the election result.

Cohen and Shank said the White House had already mapped out a plan for the next two years to govern without Congress, relying on more executive orders, actions and overreach, more vetoes, and more legal delays and obfuscation. The authors also said Republicans in the lame-duck Congress were preparing retribution, including one last reconciliation bill to enact their agenda without Democratic input, and they expected the executive branch to lash out further.

Cohen and Shank said the administration spent the weeks before the vote sowing doubt about the legitimacy of the midterms. The authors said it offered $1 million for reports of voter fraud and $5,000 in bribes tied to midterm results, fearmongered about Chinese involvement in elections-tinkering, attempted to thwart mail-in voting, and pressured US states to segregate ballots. Cohen and Shank said those actions would not disappear quickly.

The authors said the pre-election conduct could escalate in the coming months, potentially under the fog of an escalated war on Iran. Cohen and Shank set out two conditions for the scenario they describe: that January's swearing-in proceeds smoothly and that no escalation of the war on Iran by the White House leads to emergency declarations, power outages or martial law.

State and local officials have spent recent months preparing for worst-case scenarios, and Cohen and Shank said those officials should not let down their guard. The Mayors Innovation Project and other democracy-defending institutions have prepared local leaders with emergency-management plans and protocols, with the stated caveat that the work is just a starting point for protecting democracy far into the future.

A report titled The American Crisis made a related argument. The report called local communities the “central locus of control in stabilizing America’s democratic transition, as republican power has always ultimately remained with the people.” The report said Congress cannot leave local communities to fight alone and must assert itself as the primary branch of the federal government, taking its oath to uphold the Constitution seriously by ensuring a successful democratic transition of power.

Cohen and Shank said the new Congress would need to meet the coming challenge with more than oversight hearings and proceedings to hold the White House accountable for past and present corruption. The authors urged the new Congress to work with local movements, saying democratic infrastructure would be only as strong as the link between local, state and federal leadership.

Defend 612 in Minneapolis has an 80,000-strong network of concerned and activated individuals ready to rally in defense of democracy, Cohen and Shank wrote. Free DC in Washington DC has thousands of well-trained members, the authors said, ready to defend the nation's capital from further federal overreach and military occupation.

Cohen and Shank described the rapid-response networks that popped up in US cities, including Free DC and Defend 612, as new social infrastructure and human capital forming in response to government repression. The authors said the groups would evolve to meet the democratic needs of the day and would provide an essential complement and accountability check going forward.

The two authors closed by arguing that the mobilization showed a powerful resurgence of democratic engagement that was only beginning. They wrote: "It took a serious threat to realize what’s at stake, but the masses mobilizing have never been mightier." Cohen and Shank said the resulting momentum may save the country, and they described the moment as a possible rebirth at 250 years old.
