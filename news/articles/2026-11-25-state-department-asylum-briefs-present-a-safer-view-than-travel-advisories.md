## Six-page briefs omit full human-rights analysis, former official says

The U.S. State Department prepared six-page country reports for immigration courts that describe selected parts of Honduras, Guatemala, Mexico and El Salvador as having low crime, good schools, quality hospitals and constitutions that protect individual rights. A State Department travel advisory tells Americans not to visit Honduras’ Gracias a Dios region because of violent crime, armed robbery, murder and active terrorist groups. Yet a State Department background report obtained by The Associated Press says, “Overall, Gracias a Dios is a low-crime, functioning department.”

The Associated Press found that the documents use “cherry-picked data,” careful phrasing and, in some cases, facts that do not withstand scrutiny. The review said they create misleading depictions of countries often plagued by violence, government corruption and police inaction. The documents appear designed to make it easier for judges to deny asylum to people from those nations fleeing domestic violence and criminal gangs by painting pictures of life in their home countries that often clash with reality, the AP reported.

Elliot Young, a professor at Lewis & Clark College who analyzed the documents for the college’s Migration and Asylum Lab, called the reports “reverse-engineered.” Young, a scholar of Latin America and asylum law who has testified as an expert witness in more than 700 asylum cases, said: “They’re trying to hit the most frequent kinds of (asylum) cases that come up and provide counterevidence to the overwhelming evidence that these are dangerous places.”

The documents were prepared for use in immigration courts, where asylum denial rates have reached 94% during President Donald Trump’s second term. Data from the research nonprofit Transactional Records Access Clearinghouse show the rate rose from roughly 72% at the end of President Joe Biden’s administration to 94% in June under Trump.

Cutting back on asylum has been a centerpiece of Trump’s immigration agenda since his first term, when he dismissed migrants waiting in Mexico to enter the U.S. as “not legitimate asylum-seekers.” Trump, the Republican president, also said most asylum seekers were fleeing poverty. In a 2018 White House appearance, he said: “Asylum is a very special protection intended only for those fleeing government persecution based on race, religion and other protected status.”

The Associated Press said Trump’s description was years out of date. Asylum was largely seen after World War II as protection for people fleeing political persecution, but the definition was expanded long ago to encompass some people fleeing domestic and criminal violence.

The Associated Press also noted that Trump had a point about poverty. Legal and illegal migration, particularly from Latin America, has grown sharply over the past two decades as people have sought economic opportunity, while increasing numbers of asylum claims have been based on safety fears involving domestic partners and criminal gangs. Even during President Joe Biden’s years, most asylum claims were rejected.

Trump was elected to a second term partly by focusing on frustrated voters who had seen illegal immigration soar under Biden, the AP reported. Border arrests from Mexico reached a record 250,000 in December 2023, while the asylum system faced a backlog of millions of cases. The Trump administration has reshaped immigration courts as part of its push to increase deportations, organizing mass hearings, authorizing military lawyers as immigration judges and giving judges greater latitude to dismiss claims summarily.

Immigration judges must weigh whether a person seeking asylum based on domestic violence or criminal threats has a reasonable fear of persecution because of race, religion, nationality, political views or the often-debated catch-all category, “membership in a particular social group.” The person must also show that the government cannot or will not protect the applicant and that the applicant cannot move elsewhere in the home country to escape persecution.

The reports offer what can sometimes seem like travel-brochure descriptions. They mention good schools, low crime, quality hospitals and constitutions that protect individual rights, with brief notes that “the availability of services” can vary across each country. They highlight specific places, including an off-the-beaten track tourist destination in Guatemala and a province in El Salvador with low crime and 24-hour emergency medical care.

For El Salvador, whose government under President Nayib Bukele has long faced accusations of widespread human rights abuses, including torture, the State Department report says the government approach to rights abuses “is no different than that of developed countries in Europe and North America, including the United States itself.” The report highlights a handful of prosecutions tied to the civil war that ended in 1992 and adds, “In any country, a rogue official can mistreat those under their power.”

For Honduras’ Gracias a Dios province, the report says violent crime rates “compare favorably with rates of violent crime in many parts of the United States.”

Scott Busby, a former deputy assistant secretary in the State Department’s Bureau of Democracy, Human Rights and Labor who is now a senior adviser at Human Rights First, said: “These shorter reports clearly don’t address the full range of human rights issues that the country reports have traditionally addressed.” Traditional country reports have provided lengthy dissections of more than 100 nations around the world, but they have been truncated and delayed under the second Trump administration. Busby called the change “especially problematic” because the previous year’s country reports had not yet been issued.

In many ways, the reports appear aimed most directly at asylum regulations around a person’s ability to escape persecution by simply moving. Those regulations are key for anyone seeking asylum based on domestic or criminal violence. The reports highlight specific parts of each country, mentioning places like an off-the-beaten track tourist destination in Guatemala and a province in El Salvador that has low crime and 24-hour emergency medical care. But those mentions only tell part of the story.

Neela Chakravartula, an asylum attorney with the Center for Gender & Refugee Studies at the University of California College of the Law, San Francisco, said references to safe areas do not mean everyone can move there. “The fact that there may be pockets of a country that are relatively safe for some people doesn’t mean that everybody can just move to that part of the country,” she said. “We’d all be living in Malibu.”

The State Department did not respond to an Associated Press request for comment on the reports. The Justice Department, which oversees immigration courts, also did not respond to a request for comment on what role the reports play in influencing asylum decisions.
