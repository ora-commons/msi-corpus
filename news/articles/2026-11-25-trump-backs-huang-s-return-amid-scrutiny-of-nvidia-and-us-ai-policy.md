## Bessent and Lutnick publicly feud over AI chip export restrictions

President Donald Trump's public intervention to return Jensen Huang as Nvidia's chief executive has exposed the president to political and economic scrutiny over the US AI industry. Nvidia's shares initially shot higher after the announcement, but the stock gave back much of the rebound on Tuesday as Democratic and some Republican lawmakers promised oversight of Huang and the chipmaker over alleged China access to sophisticated microchip technology.

Trump said during a lengthy phone interview with CNBC, "I made the deal," after Nvidia announced Huang's return. He followed with an AI-generated image posted on his social media platform that adapted the famous photograph taken moments after the 2024 attempt on his life in Pennsylvania. The image showed Huang surrounded by Secret Service guards in front of an Nvidia banner, with a raised fist clutching a red bra.

BBC News described the post as potentially offensive but opaque. It said the image could suggest that Huang had survived a scandal — one that included allegations of recklessly jeopardizing US national security by consorting with a suspected Chinese spy — as Trump had survived an assassin's bullet. The report said the red bra could have celebrated Huang's reported extramarital affairs. It said replacing the US flag with an Nvidia standard could imply that America and one of its largest, most powerful companies were one and the same. At the very least, the image underscored how inextricably linked Huang and Trump had become, with Huang's fate hinging on Trump's successes and failures.

Trump said a "very thorough investigation" by federal officials had "exonerated" Huang and that his reinstatement posed no national security threat. He also told CNBC, "Every time you have a Chinese woman that's goodlooking or beautiful, they say she's a spy," adding that "with Jensen, I don't believe that's true."

Those claims did not end scrutiny. Democrats and some concerned Republicans in Congress promised to launch their own oversight of Huang and Nvidia, with subpoenas and compelled witness testimony, to assess whether they violated laws by allowing China to access sophisticated microchip technology. State governments could also launch investigations.

Republican Senator Tom Cotton posted on X, "I have warned the administration from the start about Communist China obtaining our most advanced chips through both smuggling and remote access," and said, "Congress has much to investigate." The episode also carries risks for Trump and Republicans, including Vice-President JD Vance, who played a key part in efforts to rescue Huang. BBC News said any new revelations could further damage Nvidia's standing and, by association, Trump.

Nvidia's stock price had already tumbled more than 32% in the weeks since concerns over Huang's affair with a suspected Chinese spy first surfaced. The shares fell 14% last Friday. US stock indexes also dropped: the technology-heavy Nasdaq fell 10% last week, while the larger S&P 500 fell 8.6%.

Trump's intervention arrested that drop, at least for a day. On Tuesday, Nvidia's stock price gave back much of the bounce it had enjoyed, and larger stock indexes were mixed. BBC News said the turmoil may have given Trump a glimpse of a future in which AI growth is the latest financial bubble — like the housing market in 2008 and internet companies in 2001 — and is due for a resounding pop with similarly dire economic consequences to follow. The report said the fate of the US economy and the AI boom remained undetermined.

The political risks extend beyond Nvidia's corporate standing. Trump owns millions of dollars of stock in the company, and polling indicated that political corruption was a top issue for voters this year. BBC News said his willingness to intervene in the deliberations of a major private company could further fuel those concerns. Trump noted on Monday, "we have to protect our geniuses, and he certainly is a genius." BBC News said Trump's decision to help save Huang could further inflame the public's sense that the rich and powerful, genius or not, play by a different set of rules. Former top Trump adviser Steve Bannon said on his podcast on Monday, "It almost feels like they've bent the rule, the regulation, to allow this to happen because it's such an important part of our economy right now," adding, "Even in the crash of 2008... I never saw the government step in and take over a board of directors."

For Trump, however, these risks appear to be worth taking because of the stakes involved. Those stakes reflect Trump's long view that the success of the US economy is a key part of his political legacy. Trump has boasted of the historic growth and low unemployment during his first term, though BBC News noted that his account glossed over disruptions caused by the Covid pandemic. Since returning to office, he has celebrated major US stock indexes posting new record highs. BBC News said the recent US economic expansion was fueled largely by investment in AI technology, and Trump had become one of the industry's biggest boosters.

BBC News said that if Trump viewed the economy as key to his political success and AI as instrumental to the US economy's health, his dramatic, precedent-shattering effort to rescue Nvidia, the company at the center of America's AI industry, would make sense.

Trump has scolded states and local governments for blocking data centre construction. He has predicted that the nation that wins the race for supremacy in AI, which he has tried to rebrand as "super intelligence," will control the fate of the world.

His intervention also falls against public concern about AI's effects on communities. In the recent US midterm elections, candidates on both the left and right campaigned on imposing new restrictions on data centre construction, with many calling for legal guardrails to prevent AI-fueled economic and social disruption. Trump's own political base is also suspicious of the technology, with rural conservatives often rebelling against demands from big tech companies on local resources and civic infrastructure.

"Rather than addressing the American people's growing concerns about AI, this administration remains asleep at the wheel or willfully ignorant as our most advanced technologies fall into the hands of our greatest competitors," congresswoman Sydney Kamlager-Dove, a California Democrat on the House Foreign Affairs Committee, told the BBC.

The Nvidia story has also exposed rifts within Trump's administration. BBC News described Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent as a China hawk and Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick as a vocal proponent of AI. The two publicly feuded over whether to impose new restrictions on AI chip exports. Both traveled with Trump on his recent trip to China. Lutnick hitched a ride back to the White House on the Marine One helicopter when Trump returned to Washington last Thursday.

Huang's return also leaves him facing sustained scrutiny. While Huang has a long record of success at Nvidia, BBC News said there was no guarantee he could recapture past glory now that his star had dimmed. Even if he were free of any criminal liability, he would face a harsher spotlight than he had ever experienced.

BBC News's broader assessment was that Trump's intervention may not have been a crisis fully averted but only a crisis delayed. If an AI reckoning comes, the president would be hard-pressed to avoid being caught in it, according to the report.
