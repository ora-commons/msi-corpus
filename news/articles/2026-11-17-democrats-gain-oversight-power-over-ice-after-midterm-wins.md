## Congress could theoretically restrict ICE funding and detention centers, but barriers remain

The Guardian reported on Nov. 17 that Democrats who won strong House and Senate majorities in the 2026 midterms are preparing to use their new congressional control to examine the Trump administration’s immigration enforcement. The outlet said the election also brought new governorships and power in some unexpected places, and that Trump’s deportation campaign and ICE raids were among the key issues that cost Republicans support. One of their clearest unilateral tools is oversight: Democratic-led committees can issue subpoenas, hold hearings and visit detention facilities, while legislative changes remain constrained by Republican opposition, the Senate filibuster and Trump’s veto power.

Mississippi Representative Bennie Thompson, likely to chair the House homeland security committee, said, “We have to rein ICE in because its illegal, out-of-control activities [have] killed individuals … assaulted individuals… incarcerated individuals”. Incoming Minnesota Senator Peggy Flanagan said, “We will rip ICE apart”. The Guardian noted that not everyone in the Democratic caucus was on the same page. Initial proposals from Democratic leaders are more modest guardrails, including bans on masks for ICE officers and requirements for body-worn cameras in the field.

Democratic-led congressional committees will be able to issue subpoenas and already plan to call in Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin, White House chief of staff Stephen Miller, Tom Homan and former Border Patrol commander-at-large Gregory Bovino for questioning in public. The committees may hold hearings in cities where they can present evidence of agency misconduct and may continue visits to ICE detention facilities to examine conditions and increase public exposure. The Guardian described greater oversight as one of the clearest unilateral tools Democrats will have next year.

Congressional Democrats may also consider impeachment, although The Guardian said it is unclear whether the Constitution permits a chief of staff such as Miller to be impeached. If Democrats lead a second attempt to impeach Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth, the attempt could curtail Miller’s authority, especially if Democrats succeed in removing Hegseth from office. The effort could do so by putting other impeachable officials directing immigration enforcement, including Mullin, on notice that they could lose their jobs if they followed White House advice to act lawlessly.

Legislation presents a harder path, although The Guardian said Congress theoretically has the ability to force many changes on ICE through legislation, such as taking away funds, restricting detention centers and mandating a focus on migrants with violent records. Trump pledged to target “the worst of the worst,” but the outlet reported that the administration’s campaign produced mass arrests of people without criminal records and even people with legal papers. Democrats, both sitting and incoming, are pressing for comprehensive changes to an immigration system that both parties have long agreed is “broken,” while seeking to balance border security with assistance for immigrants already in the United States. The Guardian said Congress has not had the will to reform the system from top to bottom.

Even with Democratic majorities, enactment faces steep odds without Republican support because of presidential veto power and the Senate filibuster, which requires at least 60 votes to move a bill forward. The Guardian said sufficient bipartisan unity is hard to come by. Republicans largely oppose what they call “amnesty” — giving people in the country unlawfully the chance to legalize their status. The outlet said some progressive Democrats denounce border crackdowns and restrictions on seeking asylum that conflict with international law. Trump has threatened to become “Mr Veto,” making his and Senate Republicans’ next steps uncertain.

Targeted changes may have more room. According to a recent Gallup poll cited by The Guardian, some 81% of Americans endorse a citizenship pathway for undocumented immigrants who came to the United States as children, also known as “Dreamers”. The Guardian said even the Trump administration had indicated renewed openness to the idea since the midterm drubbing. Lawmakers may be able to reach agreement to reinstate temporary protected status, a program providing work permits and protection from deportation for people in the United States from nationalities that cannot safely return to their home countries, especially Haitians and Afghans. The administration has ended that status for roughly a million people across more than a dozen nationalities, and legislation could create a green-card pathway for holders, which does not exist now.

DHS, which oversees immigration enforcement and many immigration benefits, received about a quarter of a trillion dollars during Trump’s second term alone, according to The Guardian. Taxpayers’ money designated for ICE soared, and ICE is now the highest federally funded US law enforcement agency. A roughly $70bn cash infusion in June was intended to bankroll the department through the end of Trump’s presidency. Democrats say they plan to seek the clawback or withholding of at least some of that funding through legislation once they control Congress.

The Guardian described abolishing ICE as very unlikely. The outlet said the “abolish ICE” slogan had become a political liability for Democrats as unauthorized border crossings rose sharply under the Biden administration, Americans turned on undocumented immigrants, and Trump and what the outlet called his “Maga white nationalist ranks” started describing the increase as an “invasion”. Even to many mainstream US voters, abolishing ICE entirely — like defunding the police — stood for the left being too soft on law and order.

The Guardian said Republicans are feeling backlash over the perceived cruelty of Trump’s mass deportation campaign and ICE violence on the streets. Despite that backlash, the outlet said the ideological divide among progressive and more moderate Democrats would probably preclude closing the agency. Last Wednesday, re-elected centrist Democratic Congressman Henry Cuellar, who represents a Texas-Mexico border district, said, “I’m not going to abolish ICE.”

Although immigration enforcement is broadly a federal responsibility, state and local governments have become deeply involved in recent years through police and sheriff cooperation with ICE, including 287(g) agreements and other collaborative policies. In Texas, Democrats flipped a US Senate seat, the governorship, the attorney general’s office and the state House after decades of Republican dominance. Texas laws and policies have required local law enforcement to work with ICE, and state penalties have criminalized people who crossed the border with Mexico unlawfully.

Democratic leaders in states that shifted heavily blue now have an opportunity to pull back from such tactics. The Guardian said backing off would likely lead to fewer ICE arrests, less depletion of immigrant-heavy workforces and the potential for communities to rebuild trust with local law enforcement. Democrats are set to hold all 10 House seats along the almost 3,000 miles of the US-Mexico border and the four border governorships in California, Arizona, New Mexico and Texas for the first time since 1979, as well as the top legal offices in four states.

Texas Governor Greg Abbott is outgoing after 12 years; The Guardian said he unilaterally bussed migrants to Democratic-led cities and spent an estimated $11bn militarizing the Tex-Mex border through “Operation Lone Star”. New Democratic state leaders will examine what they can and wish to unwind. They could restore access to in-state college tuition for undocumented students. The Guardian said such policies had been gutted in some states amid what it called federal collusion during Trump’s second term. The outlet said cities, counties and states newly aligned on the issue could join lawsuits challenging Trump-era policies and pool resources for legal battles against the federal government instead of each other. It also noted that the “sanctuary city” label had somewhat gone out of vogue in recent years, but said Democratic city and county leaders and state legislatures might devise new laws and strategies to block cooperation with ICE, at least when immigrants do not pose a public safety risk.

Flipped congressional offices may provide a new point of contact for immigrants, including noncitizens. Such offices often help constituents contact federal agencies, get answers from the government and sometimes find solutions. That can mean finally receiving a work permit stuck in limbo, helping a family member overseas with visa processing or getting a loved one out of detention.

At the same time, Trump and his subordinates have given no sign that immigration policies will ease, and the administration plans to continue deportations. After the midterm losses, Trump told 60 Minutes that immigration enforcement may have been “too tough,” but shortly after the interview was recorded, he appointed longtime ally Stephen Miller as chief of staff. The Guardian said Democrats can spotlight enforcement excesses and racist attitudes and act as a buffer, while more transformational change would probably have to wait for a Democrat, particularly a progressive, to win the White House in 2028.
