## Justice Department alleges OSU Med admissions favored Black, Hispanic applicants

**Subtype:** fact

- The Justice Department alleges that OSU Med’s admissions process discriminated against White and Asian applicants in the 2023, 2024 and 2025 incoming classes.
- The Justice Department alleges that OSU Med used four mechanisms to favor Black and Hispanic applicants: a holistic approach, Super Screeners who could see race and ethnicity, a side-door pathway and socioeconomic disadvantage scores.
- The Justice Department says Black applicants with similar academic credentials had 7.7 times the odds of advancing to an interview as Asian applicants and 4.6 times the odds as White applicants in 2025.
- The Justice Department says Black applicants using the side-door pathway were four times as likely to be admitted as White applicants with comparable credentials in 2024 and 2025.
- Ohio State says its medical school does not consider race, ethnicity or any other protected class during admissions.
- The Justice Department is seeking a voluntary resolution agreement with Ohio State to bring OSU Med’s practices into legal compliance.

## Four admissions mechanisms are at issue in the federal findings

On Wednesday, Oct. 21, the Justice Department’s Civil Rights Division announced findings that four parts of OSU Med’s admissions process favored Black and Hispanic applicants: a holistic approach, admissions reviewers known as “Super Screeners,” a side-door pathway and socioeconomic disadvantage scores. The department alleges the process discriminated against White and Asian applicants in the 2023, 2024 and 2025 incoming classes. Ohio State disputes the findings and says it will contest them.

According to the findings, OSU Med has practiced a “holistic approach” to admissions since 2011 to increase the admission of underrepresented minorities. Ohio State says it weighs an applicant’s attributes, experiences and metrics, including GPA and MCAT scores, equally. The Justice Department alleges that the school instead gave greater weight to attributes and experiences to favor Black and Hispanic applicants.

The department also alleges that OSU Med used the so-called Super Screeners, admissions reviewers who could see applicants’ race and ethnicity, to favor Black and Hispanic applicants. According to the findings, Black applicants with similar academic credentials had 7.7 times the odds of advancing to the interview stage as Asian applicants and 4.6 times the odds as White applicants in the 2025 admissions cycle. Hispanic applicants had 2.3 times the odds of being interviewed as White or Asian applicants with similar credentials, the department said.

The Justice Department further alleges that OSU Med operated a “side door” admissions process that allowed some applicants to bypass initial screening and standard admissions committee review. Black applicants who used that pathway were four times as likely to be admitted as White applicants with comparable credentials during the 2024 and 2025 admissions cycles, according to the findings. No Asian applicants were admitted through the pathway, the department said.

The fourth alleged mechanism was the use of socioeconomic disadvantage scores as a substitute for race to favor underrepresented minority applicants.

## Legal findings and a proposed resolution agreement

The Civil Rights Division said its investigation found that OSU Med violated Title VI of the Civil Rights Act of 1964, as interpreted by the Supreme Court’s 2023 ruling. The department described the ruling as prohibiting discrimination based on race or national origin in federally funded programs, and said the findings applied to admission of the 2023, 2024 and 2025 incoming classes.

In a letter to the university, the Justice Department said Ohio State’s medical school intentionally discriminated in its admissions process while pursuing its stated goal of including students from “historically excluded and underserved groups.” The department said the result was discrimination “against certain applicants, particularly Asians, to benefit preferred race classes of Black and Hispanic.”

Assistant Attorney General Harmeet Dhillon of the Justice Department’s Civil Rights Division said, “A university’s admissions process, and all its stages, should apply equally to all applicants regardless of race.” He also said, “The use of a separate admissions process based on race is plainly illegal under federal law, and this department will not allow these appalling practices to continue at OSU Med or any other federally funded higher ed institution.”

The Justice Department said it was seeking to enter a voluntary resolution agreement with Ohio State to ensure the medical school’s admissions practices were brought into legal compliance.

## Ohio State says it will contest the findings

Ohio State strongly disagreed with the findings, according to its emailed statement to UPI. The university said it was in full compliance with the Supreme Court’s 2023 ruling and all federal and state laws and regulations governing admissions.

“The College of Medicine does not consider race, ethnicity or any other protected class during the admissions process,” Ohio State said. “Ohio State follows the law and will contest these findings.”

## The inquiry extends across higher education

UPI described the OSU Med finding as the latest investigative finding in the Trump administration’s campaign to eliminate diversity, equity and inclusion policies at institutions of higher learning. The Civil Rights Division has opened dozens of investigations into federally funded universities’ compliance with the 2023 Supreme Court ruling, which restricted race-conscious admissions practices.

The division has launched more than 100 investigations into the admissions processes of federally funded universities, in line with executive orders signed by President Donald Trump that sought to eliminate DEI policies. The Justice Department said the broader effort seeks to reestablish merit-based practices in higher education. The Trump administration has announced similar findings involving at least seven other medical schools and at least three law schools.
