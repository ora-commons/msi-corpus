## BTP officer numbers fall more than 10% as union labels fines inadequate

The Guardian's First Edition briefing on Tuesday turned to the question of whether the campaign can work. Its subheading said the prime minister's campaign against noisy passengers "has struck a chord with commuters" — "whether anyone hears him remains to be seen" — and the article about the campaign launch had been most-read across the Guardian website throughout the morning, the briefing noted. To find out more, the author spoke to a bus driver, a transport expert and a cultural critic.

Burnham launched the crusade with a chatty Instagram post recorded on a morning train into Manchester Piccadilly, setting his sights on the phenomenon of "loudcasting" — or "headphone dodging" — which he called "one of the most annoying things on public transport." Alongside a nationwide campaign encouraging people to plug in their headphones and "share the carriage, not the noise," Burnham announced new guidance to help local transport authorities introduce fines of up to £100 for nuisance behaviour like vaping, littering and playing loud music or videos on buses. Train operating companies already hold powers to impose fines of up to £1,000 for antisocial behaviour. "It's a simple thing, but one that will make journeys that little bit better for all of us," Burnham said. About 88% of Britons, according to recent polling cited by the briefing, consider it unacceptable to play music out loud on a train.

It remains unclear how the rules would be enforced, the briefing said. Government officials have reassured bus drivers that they will not be expected to police passengers themselves, with the job falling to transport safety officers or police officers. Mark, a bus driver in central London, said he and his colleagues simply don't have the capacity to help. He spends his working life in the glass cabinet at the front of the vehicle and said he can't hear every noise or spot every puff of vape smoke on the top deck while navigating city traffic and watching for fare-dodging. His route has become noisier in recent years, though he was keen to stress that it is "an all-ages" issue and not to lay the blame solely with younger passengers. He doubts Burnham can isolate the problem to public transport: "It's a whole society thing: being kinder and more aware of their environment." "It's not workable without resources," Mark said. "I don't really see how it would be enforceable: you can't have someone on every bus and train."

The briefing noted that fines for nuisance noise on trains are already vanishingly rare in practice, with offenders able to shut up or leave the carriage before enforcement arrives. British Transport Police officer numbers have fallen by more than 10% over the past two years, leaving passengers themselves to challenge the "noisy doom-scroller." Transport for London has done work on bystander intervention, suggesting straightforward ways to interrupt bad behaviour without putting yourself in danger, though many would think twice about confronting a stranger in the confines of a moving vehicle, the briefing said.

Not everyone is impressed by the scale of the response. Sharon Graham, general secretary of Unite, described the policy as "well-meaning but wholly inadequate" to tackle the "avalanche of escalating abuse" faced by the transport workers her union represents. Guardian transport correspondent Gwyn Topham said there has been "a massive escalation in reported assaults on public transport across the country" and reported earlier this month on the growing number of verbal and physical assaults faced by transport workers and passengers. The briefing also recalled the death of bus driver Keith Rollinson at Elgin bus station after a violent assault by a teenage passenger.

The briefing acknowledged the argument that antisocial behaviour exists on a continuum, and that a "nip it in the bud" approach might prevent more serious problems from escalating. Its author wrote that zero-tolerance policing has a mixed record — "if you detect Tony Blair vibes here, so do I" — and that "dropping a grand for loudcasting your tunes has more than a whiff of New Labour's respect agenda and the classist asbo culture it ushered in, which singularly failed to tackle antisocial behaviour at its roots."

Still, the briefing said, there is much to be said for elevating the issue from shared eye-rolls to official tannoy announcements. Topham predicted the policy will appeal hugely to commuters. "What he's done well is to acknowledge an everyday irritation, that doesn't cost a lot of money to fix, but can hopefully alter public behaviour," he said.

On whether telling people to behave better can work, writer Dan Hancox, whose latest book, Multitudes, is about crowd behaviour, told the briefing that gentle nudges towards more respectful behaviour, rather than sledgehammer fines, have made a difference historically. He recalled "a real moral panic" in the early 2010s over teenagers playing tinny MP3s aloud from basic mobile phones, behaviour he defended at the time: "It was a rare example of sharing of music as a social adhesive, at a moment when David Cameron was introducing austerity and the spaces in which young people could gather together were being shut down." Within a couple of years, the behaviour had died down again, as often happens after the initial buzz around new technology and the social pressure points they create.

The phenomenon of people playing video out loud on their phones has got noticeably worse in the last couple of years, Hancox believes, and it is not all about the young this time — younger travellers seem more likely to rely on noise-cancelling headphones, which some audiologists have raised concerns over for potential links to auditory processing problems. He refuted the notion that people have "forgotten how to behave" since the Covid lockdown: "It feels like an easy answer for something that is much more complex. But it didn't go on for that long, and the really remarkable thing about human beings is how quickly and elastically they bounce back to their previous sets of behaviours." The more boring answer, he suggested from his own research, is that as 5G makes watching videos easier to do wherever we are, people are simply seizing that opportunity.

Crowd psychology tells us that other people's propensity to broadcast noise has a minimal effect on our own behaviour, Hancox said: "People's pre-existing values and social mores are incredibly robust and are unlikely to change based on the behaviour of a complete stranger." That, he said, makes behaviour much more susceptible to the kind of nudge messaging over the train tannoy that Burnham is proposing.

Anxiety about strangers and the malign influence of the crowd tends to arise at moments of wider pressures, Hancox said: "When people are worried about paying the rent or feeding their families, as wars in the wider world rock our sense of stability and faith in human beings, our suspicion of the random person on the bus grows. But my day-to-day experience of life in a big and supposedly alienating city like London tells me people want this kind of spontaneous connection more than ever, and it's a joy when it happens. Even if it's just a few warm words exchanged with a stranger at the bus stop, on the train or in the shops – this is what it means to live in a society."

The author wrote that few voices have suggested Burnham should be concentrating on more important matters, arguing that other people's noise and inconsiderate behaviour on public transport "has become shorthand for wider concerns about social cohesion, generational conflict and political fragmentation": "we are all making much more noise, and have become much worse at listening to what anyone else is actually saying." In the author's own experience, two things can be true at once — a growing anxiety that this indicates an unravelling of the social contract, and interactions with fellow passengers that in the main reassure us this is not, in fact, the case.
