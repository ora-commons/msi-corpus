## Ranchers say Trump's import plan rattled markets without cutting beef prices

President Donald Trump angered ranchers in August when he announced a plan to allow 661 million pounds of lean beef trimmings into the country at a lower tariff rate, saying it would lower beef prices for consumers. But the United States was importing beef before he began his second stay in the White House, and the volume had already been climbing.

In 2024, during the final year of President Joe Biden's administration, U.S. beef imports reached a then-record 4.64 billion pounds, and they have continued to go up.

Falling U.S. cattle numbers are part of the reason. The nation's cattle numbers have declined from a recent peak of about 95 million cattle in 2019 to an estimated 86 million, a 75-year low, according to the U.S. Department of Agriculture. Meanwhile, the number of U.S. farms or ranches with cattle has declined from more than a million in the 1970s to about 600,000.

Brett Kenzy, a rancher from Gregory, said the reasons for those declines are many, and so are the ideas to reverse the trends. He said abrupt changes in import policies are not helping.

“What's required to build our cattle herd up is confidence — confidence in the market, and we don't have that right now,” Kenzy said.

Since the import plan took effect, the targeted shipments have been slow to arrive and beef prices have remained high. Trump's announcement also sent the cattle futures market lower, according to Scott Kolousek, a farmer and rancher from Wessington Springs.

“When this news comes out of Washington, the markets are up and down, up and down,” Kolousek said.

Kolousek said his operation feeds calves to roughly 750 to 800 pounds. That means a $10-per-hundredweight decline in the market reduces an 800-pound calf's value by about $80. For a producer selling 200 calves, that is a $16,000 difference that “you can lose in a matter of minutes,” Kolousek said.

Producers say raising cattle takes years of planning, while government policy and market expectations can change overnight. A rancher deciding whether to retain heifers for breeding must invest in raising those animals before they can produce calves. Those calves then need additional time to reach market weight. Kenzy said that makes it difficult for ranchers to invest in expanding their herds if they fear future policy decisions could undermine their operations.

After blowback about the administration's move to allow more beef imports, U.S. Secretary of Agriculture Brooke Rollins announced the Ranchers First Initiative on Aug. 31. One part of the proposal is an insurance option to encourage ranchers to keep young heifers for breeding. It would provide financial protection over a two-year period if a heifer's slaughter value exceeds the value of retaining the heifer for breeding. The administration also announced proposals including allowing disaster recovery assistance on some federally protected grasslands, expanding support for beginning ranchers and prioritizing American beef in government programs.

Some cattle producers want a further step, called Mandatory Country of Origin Labeling, which would require labels identifying where an animal was born, raised and slaughtered. Supporters say it would give consumers more transparency about where their meat comes from and encourage them to buy U.S. beef, which could motivate ranchers to grow their herds. The policy was implemented in 2009, but after Canada and Mexico threatened tariffs on U.S. exports in response, Congress removed the labeling requirements in 2016.

The U.S. Senate Agriculture Committee approved Majority Leader John Thune's American Beef Labeling Act amendment in August, which includes a pathway to reinstate labeling in the pending farm bill. The bill passed out of the committee in September and is awaiting further action in the Senate, where Thune, a South Dakota Republican, will soon become minority leader instead of majority leader as Democrats take control of Congress following their wins in the general election.

Kenzy said that even if cattle numbers grow and the market becomes more stable, the lack of competitive buyers for cattle would remain a significant problem. Only four companies control 85% of the U.S. beef processing market, he said. The country has spent decades becoming more concentrated in cattle feeding and meatpacking, and “we've lost a huge number of independent packing plants and feeders in this country,” Kenzy said. That has meant more producers have to take whatever is offered.

“Anybody who's thoughtful would know that herd building without a long-term fair price mechanism is not going to work,” Kenzy said.

Kolousek said the lack of fair markets is contributing to the decline in ranches and cattle numbers. “People are getting out of the cattle business,” he said. “The government doesn't have our backs. The markets don't work the way they should.”

Federal officials have made some efforts to address concentration in the meatpacking industry. Under Biden in 2022, the U.S. Department of Agriculture invested in expanding independent meat processing via grants to help small facilities increase capacity. Trump's administration also announced $500 million in assistance for smaller and midsize beef processors this year, and the Department of Justice has been investigating potentially anticompetitive practices in the meatpacking and retail industries. Kenzy is among the cattle producers who advocate requiring major packers to purchase more cattle through negotiated cash sales and further strengthening antitrust enforcement.

Competing land use is another pressure. Across the Great Plains, about 36 million acres of grassland were converted from 2012 through 2023, with row-crop expansion identified as the leading cause, according to a World Wildlife Fund analysis. Federal crop insurance subsidies and other federal agricultural programs can reduce the financial risks of growing crops, Kenzy said, while cattle producers remain more exposed to market swings — a difference he argued can make crop production more attractive than raising cattle. Crop farmers benefit from subsidized crop insurance that protects against losses from drought, flooding and other natural disasters, from direct payments when crop prices or revenues fall below certain levels, and from government-backed loans; the federal government also mandates a minimum amount of biofuels in the nation's fuel supply, incentivizing crops such as corn for ethanol.

Republican U.S. Sen. Mike Rounds of South Dakota has proposed legislation to make cattle production more attractive. The Rebuilding America's National Cow Herd Act would pay farmers to convert less-productive cropland back to grass and allow livestock to graze the acres all season, with an enrollment cap of 20 million acres.

Kenzy also pointed to ranching's physical demands, compared with the increasingly automated nature of crop farming, as another factor drawing people away. “Raising cattle is physical,” Kenzy said. “My shoulders are shot.”

Darrell Raschke has spent his life in the cattle industry. He grew up on a ranch and still has some cattle on his Beadle County farm, where he primarily raises crops. He said automation in farming has allowed farmers to do a lot more with less labor. “One man, two guys can farm an awful lot of acres with the equipment we have today,” Raschke said.

Cattle demand year-round attention, including feeding, calving and maintaining water supplies through harsh winters, Raschke said. Producers may need herds of 500 to 700 cattle to earn a living, requiring more workers than many operations can afford. “People used to have eight, nine kids to help out with all those chores,” he said. Raschke said he is skeptical there is a solution.

Trevor Olson, a 27-year-old who raises cattle near Letcher, said he sticks with ranching because “it just feels like the way of life.” “I've grown up doing it,” he said. “I've always found that it's those certain moments throughout a year that really make you appreciate it. Those moments that you feel nothing but pride in knowing the hard work was worth it.”

Olson said if political leaders can figure out how to help grow American cattle numbers, the customers will be there. “At the end of the day, ranchers work for the consumers, and they have shown us their demand for beef is real,” he said.
