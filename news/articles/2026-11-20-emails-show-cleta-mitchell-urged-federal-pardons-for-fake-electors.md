## Emails show Cleta Mitchell urged federal pardons for fake electors

**Subtype:** fact

- Cleta Mitchell, a former Trump lawyer and a key lawyer in Donald Trump's efforts to overturn the 2020 presidential election, urged Justice Department pardon attorney Ed Martin to help the department seek presidential pardons for fake electors.
- American Oversight obtained the fall 2025 email exchange through a Freedom of Information Act request and provided it to The Guardian.
- Mitchell argued that a federal pardon for the fake-elector scheme could wipe out state charges.

## Georgia charges were dismissed for unrelated reasons; Wisconsin case remains pending

Emails obtained by American Oversight through a Freedom of Information Act request and provided to The Guardian show that Cleta Mitchell contacted Justice Department pardon attorney Ed Martin in fall 2025 with an urgent request. Mitchell, a former Trump lawyer and a key lawyer in Donald Trump's efforts to overturn the 2020 presidential election, knew Martin was working on a memo outlining why those involved in the fake-elector scheme should receive a presidential pardon. She wanted the Justice Department to move forward with the pardons immediately before any of the cases went to trial.

In one message, Mitchell wrote, “Time is of the essence.” She warned, “If trials (and in the Michigan case, an appeal of the dismissal) are allowed to occur, the additional financial and emotional costs to these loyal Trump supporters will be enormous,” and added: “It is essential that the pardons move forward as quickly as possible before trials are scheduled and before these defendants are subjected to further expense and mental anguish. Please let me know what else I can do to help get this done.” Martin's response was brief: “Thank you for this,” he wrote. “Very helpful.” A person close to the pardon effort said Martin had been pressing and working on the issue for months. Mitchell did not respond to a request for comment.

The requests concerned criminal cases against people who signed certificates falsely claiming that Trump won the presidential election in their state, a group widely known as fake electors. Cases were pending in Georgia, Michigan, Arizona, Nevada and Wisconsin. In Georgia and other states, slates of Republican electors met privately and signed such certificates, and Republicans sought to have the certificates recognized during the counting of the vote on 6 January 2021 — an effort that was unsuccessful.

In Georgia, the criminal case against Trump and the fake electors was pending before the prosecuting attorneys council after Fulton County District Attorney Fani Willis was disqualified. Mitchell suggested that a federal pardon could lead to dismissal and wrote to Martin: “If the pardons are issued promptly, before a new prosecutor is appointed, the Council can move for dismissal of the only remaining charges in the case, being the charges against President Trump.”

Writing about the Arizona case, Mitchell urged a pardon before a grand jury could file charges, saying it would “be optimum to stop the persecution of these defendants, which include two of Charlie Kirk’s top lieutenants at TPUSA [Turning Point USA].” Kirk had been slain in Utah only weeks earlier. Summarizing the Michigan and Wisconsin cases, Mitchell wrote that a presidential pardon could spare defendants further financial burdens: “Some of them have already spent their retirement funds, mortgaged their homes to pay legal fees, and suffered significant emotional hardship over the past four years. Allowing a trial to proceed is simply unacceptable.”

By the time The Guardian published its report, the five cases stood in different postures. The prosecutor who eventually took over Georgia's case had dismissed the charges for reasons unrelated to the pardons. Wisconsin's criminal case against three architects of the fake-elector scheme remained pending, while the cases in Arizona, Michigan and Nevada had stalled for reasons unrelated to the pardons.

The U.S. Constitution gives the president broad power to pardon people convicted of federal crimes, but not state crimes. Mitchell nevertheless advanced a novel legal argument that a federal pardon for the fake-elector scheme could wipe out state charges. In a November 2025 op-ed in the Federalist, she wrote, “The U.S. Constitution created the presidential electors and the federal Electoral Count Act controls the proceedings by which the electors conduct their federal responsibilities,” and, in a second passage, “We can reach only one conclusion: These are not true state charges, these are not state actors, and the entire scheme to concoct some odd collection of purported violations of state law are a nullity.” Mitchell also advocated for the pardons on X.

The Oversight Project, a rightwing group, published a memo in November 2025 making similar arguments. Trump issued the blanket pardons on 10 November 2025, weeks after Mitchell's requests, though they did not legally affect the state-level cases. That day, Martin received an email congratulating him on the pardons. “It was a long project,” Martin wrote. “But I am glad we stuck the landing.”

Mitchell's interest in Georgia was notable because she was on the telephone call when Trump put pressure on Georgia Secretary of State Brad Raffensperger to find enough votes to overturn the election. A Georgia special-purpose grand jury recommended criminally charging Mitchell for her involvement in that effort, though she was never charged. The Guardian described Mitchell as a key figure in the election-denial movement, which it said had spread since the 2020 election. The Guardian also said she had helped organize a network of local activists who spread false and misleading information about elections and was linked to organizations that provided funding for groups that spread misleading information about elections.

Before joining the Justice Department, Martin had participated in efforts to overturn the 2020 election and spoke at a rally in Washington on 5 January 2021. “What they’re stealing is not just an election. It’s our future, and it’s our republic,” he said during the speech. On January 6, he posted on X: “I’m at the Capitol right now. Abd [sic] I was at the POTUS speech earlier. Rowdy crowd but nothing out of hand. Ignore the #FakeNews.” Martin represented some of those charged over January 6 and served on the board of an organization that provided financial assistance to their families. Before becoming pardon attorney, he served as the interim U.S. attorney for the District of Columbia, a position from which he fired and demoted prosecutors who worked on January 6 cases.

Chioma Chukwu, executive director of American Oversight, said: “While the pardons were federal, not for the ongoing state cases, the documents shed light on the extent to which Mitchell and other Trump allies were using their access to get President Trump to act. Protecting our democratic system requires ensuring that no one, regardless of political influence or proximity to power, is placed above the law.”
