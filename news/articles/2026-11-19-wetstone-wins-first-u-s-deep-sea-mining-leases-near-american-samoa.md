## Wetstone won each lease area for $3 million

The Trump administration on Thursday auctioned two 20-year deep-sea mining leases covering millions of acres of federal seafloor near American Samoa. The leases are the first of their kind in U.S. waters and followed President Donald Trump's directive the previous year to leverage deep-ocean minerals to counter growing competition for resources from China.

There were two bidders at the auction, but only Wetstone U.S. Ventures placed bids. Each of the two lease areas sold to Wetstone for the minimum bid amount of $3 million, according to the Marine Minerals Administration. Wetstone did not immediately respond to a request for comment.

Wetstone can use the leases to begin initial seafloor surveys, explore for minerals and prepare testing or mining plans. The company also receives an exclusive right to mine those areas commercially if the plans clear further environmental reviews. The leases do not authorize commercial mining on their own.

The auction proceeded despite opposition from locals in American Samoa and three lawsuits seeking to halt the sales. It was held at the Ventura County Fire Department Headquarters in Southern California, where a few dozen protesters gathered outside. Sabrina Suluai-Mahuka, an American Samoa resident who has fought the auction for more than a year, said in a statement Thursday: “We had hoped it would never come to this.”

Globally, deep-sea mining remains at an experimental stage. A handful of other countries have allowed companies to begin exploring their waters for deep-sea minerals, but no company has started commercial operations. The United States has never issued a lease for deep-sea minerals beyond a short-lived mining effort in California waters more than 60 years ago.

The administration's interest in seafloor minerals stems from an April 2025 executive order that directed the administration to search for and responsibly develop critical minerals on the ocean floor, both within and beyond U.S. borders. The Marine Minerals Administration is considering further auctions of the seabed near the Northern Mariana Islands, Virginia and Alaska, while the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration is reviewing permit applications from companies seeking to mine in international waters.

It remains unclear whether the leases will withstand legal challenge. On Monday, a court ordered the National Marine Fisheries Service to more thoroughly assess the impacts mining could have on endangered species. The court said the agency's “cramped, expedited, informal” review violated the Endangered Species Act.

The Marine Minerals Administration said it had addressed the court's opinion by starting to consult with the National Marine Fisheries Service on another environmental assessment. The agency did not cancel or delay the auction.

David Henkin, deputy managing attorney at Earthjustice, said the ruling made Thursday's auction unlawful. He said the government needed to comply with the Endangered Species Act before issuing the notice of sale. Earthjustice is representing Fa’asao Amerika Samoa, a group dedicated to safeguarding the territory's culture, and the Conservation Council of Hawaii in the suit.

“We will use the full extent of the law to vigorously push for the leases to be voided,” Earthjustice attorney Chris Eaton said in a statement.

Another lawsuit filed Wednesday by the Natural Resources Defense Council and Finafinau argues that the environmental review process was rushed and asks the court to strike down the leases. Finafinau is an environmental group founded by Suluai-Mahuka. A third lawsuit, brought by the Faleula o le Manu’atele, a council of local chiefs in American Samoa, challenges the federal government's authority to auction waters near the islands.

Tony Romeo, head of the deep-sea mining startup Eco Minerals, which did not participate in the auction, said any company going into deep-sea mining needs to be prepared for pushback from environmental groups. “If they can halt it through legal action, they will,” he said. “We’ll see how it ultimately plays out.”

Erik Milito, president of the National Ocean Industries Association, said in a statement that he expects industry interest and investment to grow in the coming years. “Decades of environmental research and extensive assessments have helped lay the groundwork for responsible ocean mineral development,” he said.

The auction assigned the leases, but commercial mining remains contingent on further environmental reviews. Three lawsuits also leave the sale's legal status unsettled, with the disputes targeting the federal review process or the government's authority to auction waters near the islands.
