## UKMTO reports tanker fire after second strike near Khasab

Oil prices fell Wednesday as traders assessed the prospect of a global supply surplus even as renewed Middle East tensions and continued threats to regional oil shipments kept risks to crude flows in view. The session unfolded alongside new maritime incidents and renewed military exchanges between the U.S. and Iran.

Farhan Rafid reported from Doha, Qatar, that front-month Brent crude futures fell 1.8% to $91.49 a barrel. Front-month West Texas Intermediate crude futures declined 2.5% to $83.86 a barrel.

Thadeu Dos Santos, an analyst at online trading provider Infinox, said expectations of a global supply surplus and efforts to maintain crude flows could limit further price gains. Dos Santos made the assessment in a note Tuesday. He also said crude stockpiles at decade lows had left the market with a thinner buffer against fresh supply disruptions.

U.K. Maritime Trade Operations said a tanker transiting the Strait of Hormuz was struck for a second time near Oman's Khasab, causing a fire onboard. The vessel had initially been struck Monday, leaving it disabled after an engine-room fire.

UKMTO also reported an attempted boarding of a cargo vessel southwest of Al Mukalla, Yemen, on Tuesday. The report came as security risks remained elevated across the region.

The latest incidents occurred amid renewed military exchanges between the U.S. and Iran. Those exchanges included attacks targeting American military facilities in Bahrain and a U.S. strike on an Iranian missile launch site on Qeshm Island near the Strait of Hormuz.

On the supply side, analysts at ANZ Research said recovering Middle Eastern production was improving global oil availability. The analysts also said an increasing amount of oil had to move via alternative export routes that were highly exposed to disruptions from attacks from Iran or constraints on logistics.

The warning left Wednesday's price decline alongside a broader supply picture in which recovering output improved availability, but increased reliance on alternative routes left shipments vulnerable to further disruption.
