## Frozen-asset reparations loan could fund Kyiv for at least two years

There is every reason to fear a "great betrayal" of Ukraine in the coming weeks — one that would reward Vladimir Putin for Russia's war of aggression against Kyiv and also do lasting damage to the European security order, Paul Taylor wrote in The Guardian. Taylor, a senior visiting fellow at the European Policy Centre, argued that Donald Trump has lost substantial power over domestic policy after last week's devastating midterm losses, but that his ability to cement his legacy by upending US foreign policy remains largely intact. Trump will want to make advances before the new Democratic-controlled Congress takes office in January, Taylor wrote.

Trump has said he is working with Moscow on a "big, beautiful deal" that is "going to involve more than Ukraine, and I could even say it might involve all of Europe." Jared Kushner, described by Taylor as Trump's seemingly Kremlin-friendly negotiator and son-in-law, said Trump had discussed with several European leaders the need for "a broader European security architecture that can help establish conditions for lasting peace."

Strip away the rhetoric, Taylor wrote, and both appear to be talking about cutting a deal with Putin behind the backs of Volodymyr Zelenskyy and other European leaders to carve up eastern Europe. Such a deal might hand Moscow permanent control of occupied Ukrainian territory — possibly more than it currently holds — and relief from western economic sanctions. It could also deny the countries of eastern Europe the right to choose their own security arrangements, by ruling out any further enlargement of NATO and possibly rolling back some of NATO's eastern front deployments.

Taylor speculated that, in return, Trump's family and business associates would secure lucrative energy, commodity and perhaps real-estate deals with Russia.

Such a carve-up may be only a few weeks away, Taylor wrote, since Trump has accepted the principle of a meeting with Putin, and a possible three-way meeting with Chinese President Xi Jinping could take place on the sidelines of an Asia-Pacific summit in Shenzhen on November 18-19. Even if a deal is not "oven-ready" by then, Taylor said, the opportunity to shape the future of European security in the absence of Ukrainian or other European leaders must look inviting to Trump after his electoral drubbing.

To force Kyiv to accept what he called a humiliating partition, Taylor said, Trump has already shown he is willing to effectively blind Ukraine by withholding crucial satellite intelligence and missile interceptors. Elon Musk, he wrote, is hobbling Ukraine's drone defence by imposing crippling speed limits on his Starshield satellite network, preventing Ukrainian drones from shooting down Russia's jet-powered kamikaze drones.

Trump could also try to bludgeon European allies into acquiescing to a deal by threatening US troop withdrawals and base closures, under a Pentagon review that is nearing completion, Taylor said. He noted that Trump and Musk have already used most of these tactics recently, albeit in short, deniable bursts, as an apparent warning to Zelenskyy. Zelenskyy has made clear he will not surrender to such coercion, Taylor wrote, while Putin's relentless destruction of Ukraine's energy and transport infrastructure is, in his assessment, a drive to put Kyiv in the weakest possible position before diplomatic talks proceed.

The warning comes as three-way US-Russia-Ukraine negotiations continue this week in Abu Dhabi. Despite fundamental European interests being at stake, Taylor wrote, Europe is relegated to a side room, represented by officials from the UK, France, Germany, the EU and NATO, while what he called "lilliputian" European leaders are too busy squabbling over who should represent them in future peace talks to focus on how to prevent a sellout.

Europe is not without cards of its own, Taylor wrote. Europeans control hundreds of billions of dollars in frozen Russian assets and have the power to refuse any lifting of economic and financial sanctions against Moscow. The EU could tighten the screws, he argued, by transferring impounded Russian central bank funds from the Belgian clearing house, Euroclear, to an EU-controlled entity, and leveraging those holdings to raise a reparations loan for Ukraine.

A group of sovereign-debt experts has argued that such a loan would keep Ukraine in the fight by meeting Kyiv's financing needs for at least two years, removing the need for increasingly fraught EU funding negotiations every few months. It would also ensure Russia is held financially accountable for its destruction of Ukraine and made to contribute to the reconstruction, the experts argued.

Yet, Taylor wrote, beggar-thy-neighbour European politics is preventing the EU from strengthening Zelenskyy's hand here too. Belgian Prime Minister Bart de Wever has refused to allow the impounded Russian funds to be used to support Ukraine, winning domestic popularity for supposedly standing up to Europe. As a result, the EU is likely to have to provide Kyiv with another loan to help it through next year. Taylor warned that elections in France and beyond could bring to power Eurosceptic nationalists who argue that their countries cannot afford to go on funding Ukraine while imposing spending cuts on their own citizens.

It is not too late, Taylor wrote, for Europe in the broadest sense — including the UK, Norway, Canada and Turkey — to act to counter a great betrayal by Trump. Beyond leveraging the frozen assets, he urged tighter sanctions on the Kremlin's shadow fleet, choking its exports by interdicting unsafe and uninsured tankers transporting Russian oil; the transfer of more of European countries' own stocks of Patriot missile interceptors to Ukraine, which he called Europe's frontline of defence by any definition; and joint diplomacy making clear to Trump that Europeans will reject any deal reached without their participation and assent.

Europeans have headed off Trump once before, Taylor noted: after his summit with Putin in Alaska last year, they accompanied Zelenskyy to the White House as "bodyguards" and clearly spelled out their red lines. This time, he wrote, the weakened US president is more desperate for a deal, and the Europeans are more divided and distracted by their own electoral politics. There can be no greater priority for the future of European security, Taylor concluded, than to prevent a sellout of Ukraine.
