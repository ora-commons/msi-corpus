## Idaho has 21 dairy facilities under quarantine amid 180 reported livestock cases

A U.S. Department of Agriculture genetic-sequencing analysis indicates that the bird flu virus infecting Washington dairy herds and workers likely came from Idaho through cattle movement, according to federal officials. Earlier this month, a Yakima County farmworker with underlying health conditions died after exposure to an infected dairy animal, the Yakima Health District said. USDA spokesperson Tanya Espinosa said officials cannot determine the date of transmission through genetic sequencing.

USDA announced this month that its sequencing analysis indicated the virus likely came from Idaho through cattle movement. Espinosa said in an email that officials cannot determine the date of transmission through genetic sequencing. She said Idaho was aware of the analysis and its indication that the virus originated in the Gem State.

The federal analysis identifies interstate cattle movement as the likely link between the states, while leaving the timing of transmission unresolved. Idaho officials have continued surveillance, monitoring and testing for more than two years, and the state has reported no human infections with the virus.

The Yakima County farmworker became ill after exposure to an infected dairy animal and had underlying health conditions, according to the Yakima Health District. CDC data listed 15 human bird flu cases in Washington, the second-highest total among states. Most of those cases came from poultry farms and culling operations, while three came from exposure to dairy herds.

Idaho reported its first detection of highly pathogenic avian influenza, known as bird flu, in dairy cattle in April 2024. The USDA counted 180 reported cases in Idaho livestock since then, including two confirmed cases in the last 30 days. As of Nov. 17, 21 dairy facilities in Idaho were under quarantine after detections, according to the Idaho Department of Agriculture.

Elizabeth Fisher, a spokesperson for the Idaho Department of Agriculture, said the agency has continued its response to bird flu detections for more than two years. The department has conducted surveillance, monitoring and testing to track the disease, and it has urged producers to continue testing and monitoring since the disease was first detected in the state. Fisher said many recent detections have involved asymptomatic cattle.

The Idaho Press reported that many livestock bird flu cases in Idaho are self-reported. The Idaho Department of Agriculture recommends that producers with suspected cases contact their veterinarians; the veterinarian then contacts the agency to set up testing. When a case is confirmed, state officials provide oversight and ensure the virus is cleared from the cow before quarantine is lifted, the state veterinarian said.

The Idaho Department of Health and Welfare has monitored potential human cases since Idaho's first dairy cattle detection. Spokesperson AJ McWhorter said the department works with local public health districts, the Idaho Department of Agriculture, the Dairymen's Association and facility owners to educate workers and monitor health status.

Health officials say the risk to people in the U.S. is low. State agricultural officials monitor confirmed cases in Idaho dairies and ensure that milk from infected cows does not get sold. The CDC says pasteurization inactivates the virus in infected cattle's milk, and health officials do not recommend drinking raw milk, which is unpasteurized.

Since the virus was first found in dairy cattle in Texas in 2024, 74 people nationwide had reported contracting bird flu, the CDC said. Most of those infections followed exposure to dairy cattle. Three deaths have been reported, and Idaho has had no reported human cases. The CDC said human infection with the virus is rare.

The CDC says avian influenza typically infects wild aquatic birds worldwide and domestic poultry. After the first dairy cattle cases were detected, experts noted that it was unusual for the virus to infect mammals, University of Idaho veterinary medicine assistant professor Lauren Christensen told the Idaho Capital Sun in 2024.

As cattle move between states, federal requirements require milk testing of lactating dairy cattle, Fisher said. The Idaho Department of Agriculture's Animal Health Lab has received and tested thousands of milk samples to support the Federal Movement Order and safe transport of livestock. The department said its partnership with Idaho's dairy industry continues to emphasize early detection and reducing risks from interstate movement of lactating dairy cattle.
