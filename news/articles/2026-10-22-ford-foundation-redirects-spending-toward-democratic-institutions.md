## Ford Foundation redirects spending toward democratic institutions

**Subtype:** fact

## Seven new programs will guide broader social grants

The Ford Foundation will overhaul its spending to direct more money toward the basic building blocks of democracy. President Heather Gerken, in a report to trustees, described the rationale as addressing the linked pressures of autocracy and inequality and framed the changes in the context of an “emboldened strain of 21st-century authoritarianism.”

The New York-based philanthropy, one of the world’s largest private grantmakers, made the commitment less than one year into Gerken’s tenure leading the social justice funder’s $18 billion endowment. Gerken is widely recognized as a leading expert on constitutional law and elections.

“If you don’t have a rule of law, if you don’t have an election system that functions, all the other values that Ford cares about, all the other projects that Ford has done over the years, fall apart,” Gerken told The Associated Press. “So, protecting marginalized communities, pushing forward on equality, protecting the climate, all of these depend on those basic building blocks.”

The overhaul is the foundation’s largest strategic refresh since immediate past president Darren Walker prioritized inequality in a 2015 overhaul. Already the nation’s leading democracy grantor by some counts, the Ford Foundation views the new approach as a necessary extension of that earlier focus.

Seven new programs — focused on democratic governance, inclusive economies, trustworthy information, public safety, gender equality, global cooperation and racial justice — will be detailed throughout next year. Gerken suggested the changes will reach beyond issues of democracy alone.

Gerken also emphasized that the foundation’s work is nonpartisan. She pointed to $80 million this year for nonprofits working to safeguard election results, including Pillars of the Community. Co-led by a former White House counsel to President Barack Obama and an adviser to multiple Republican presidential campaigns, the nonprofit seeks to build public confidence in voting systems.

Where the philanthropic sector has invested heavily in reducing polarization, Gerken plans to focus on another possibility: that polarization will persist well into the future. Without sharing details, she said her team was “thinking hard” about how to bolster democratic institutions “in a world where we’re completely polarized.”

## Grantmaking mechanics and January 2027 reorganization

In response to the funding constraints facing the philanthropic sector, the Ford Foundation vowed to deliver more large-sized, multiyear general operating support. Gerken said the most resilient grantees have been those with more flexible funding. “The last year has confirmed how extraordinarily important it is for philanthropy to work in this way,” she said.

That commitment follows the foundation’s discontinuation of a program that offered flexible funding for nonprofits’ general operations. Nick Turner, the executive vice president for programs, said the foundation is incorporating that effort, known as Building Institutions and Networks, into “the DNA of all of our grantmaking.”

Sanjiv Rao, a vice president of the Democracy Fund, said pro-democracy nonprofits need more sustained, generalized funding “that is less subject to the boom and bust cycle that we know will happen during election years.” The Democracy Fund, established by eBay founder Pierre Omidyar, has recently led efforts to support U.S. organizations that knock doors, run hotlines and challenge voting restrictions well in advance of Election Day.

Rao also stressed the need for philanthropy to adopt an expansive definition of pro-democracy work. “Groups that are galvanizing their communities around issues of shared concern, regardless of what those issues are, are all pro-democracy groups,” he said.

Beginning January 2027, Ford Foundation staff will shift to three temporary units to maintain existing grants while simultaneously transitioning new ones. To pause grantmaking “would be a sin at a moment like this,” Gerken said.

## 2027 grant estimate and program sunsets

A foundation spokesperson estimated the organization will move $700 million in grants for 2027, though that figure could increase based on new needs and endowment growth.

The changes do mean other programs will eventually be sunset. Foundation officials declined to identify which of the foundation’s more than 2300 grantees will lose funding; Turner said it is “too early to say.” He pledged to exit fields responsibly “with respect and grace and care and support.”

## Funding pressure, political criticism and historical commitments

The changes arrive during a midterm election cycle. The Associated Press reported that Republicans drove unusual levels of partisan redistricting this year and that the Supreme Court weakened the landmark Voting Rights Act. An April survey from The Associated Press-NORC Center for Public Affairs Research found that fewer U.S. adults said democracy is central to the country’s identity, while international monitors have warned of deteriorating political rights and civil liberties worldwide.

Foundations have responded to those pressures by increasing support for democracy work. According to Candid, foundation funding for democracy broadly tripled from 2016 to 2020 and has climbed slightly since a 2021 dip. George Soros’ Open Society Foundations pledged $300 million in May toward initiatives intended to defend democratic rights.

According to The Associated Press, President Donald Trump’s administration has sought to influence nonprofits by withholding funding or threatening investigations. The report said the president at times directed those efforts toward advocacy groups that support civil society, naming billionaire philanthropists George Soros and Reid Hoffman last year while calling for action against what he called “left-wing terrorism.” His allies in Congress have pushed for federal probes into nonprofits they oppose.

Gerken is also standing firm on the foundation’s support for LGBTQ causes, racial justice and other “work for marginalized communities.” She said she used her presidential funds to double the foundation’s immigration budget this year. “Those are places where not everyone is staying,” she said. “We are staying.”

Those commitments have put the foundation at odds with conservative policymakers in recent years, including Vice President JD Vance. Speaking as a 2021 U.S. Senate candidate, Vance described the Ford Foundation and others as “cancers on American society” while decrying that “we are actively subsidizing the people who are destroying this country.”

Progressive nonprofits had questioned whether major foundations would maintain their support once such critics entered the White House last year. Gerken emphasized that “the most important thing in a moment like this is not to be buffeted,” and pointed to the Ford Foundation’s defense of free speech during the era of McCarthyism and its support for civil rights litigation in the 1960s.

“The protection of marginalized communities and their basic rights is central to what we do,” she said. “While I recognize that that work isn’t always a source of common ground, it is incredibly important and it is the right thing to do.”
