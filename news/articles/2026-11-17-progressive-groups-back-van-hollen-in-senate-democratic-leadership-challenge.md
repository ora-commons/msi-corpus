## Schumer holds 15 public endorsements before Dec. 1 vote

Sen. Chris Van Hollen of Maryland entered the race for Senate Democratic leader as a long shot, but progressive organizations are backing his bid against Chuck Schumer of New York. Democrats will vote on the matter Dec. 1. Schumer, 75, says he has enough votes locked down and has received 15 public endorsements from Democratic senators. Van Hollen, 67, has received endorsements from only a few senators, and most senators and senators-elect have not said how they would vote. The contest is the Senate Democrats’ first leadership fight since 1994.

At a Saturday rally outside Van Hollen’s offices in Baltimore — an hourlong train ride from the U.S. Capitol — about 100 people gathered to support his bid to run the Senate majority next year and push out long-serving Democratic Leader Sen. Chuck Schumer. Activists who knocked on doors during the midterm election campaign said the Democratic Party’s big pickups were partly their doing and that their work should be rewarded with a leader who understands the call for change.

Elisabeth Kato, who attended the rally in support of Van Hollen, said, “So many people worked so hard, and so long, over the past two years to bring about the result that we saw on Tuesday, Nov. 3.” She added, “For Chuck Schumer to waltz in and claim credit for it really hurts.”

Van Hollen insists he has a path to becoming leader and has called the race wide open. Activists’ pressure campaign around his candidacy risks turning off some on-the-fence Democratic senators who will vote on the matter Dec. 1. “We need to be able to pull the caucus together and deliver,” he said Monday night on MS NOW. “And I do think that means turning the page for new leadership.”

The Senate Democratic caucus is bracing for a bumpy few weeks as outside groups, activists and voters weigh in through the Capitol switchboard, social media and petitions. About 50 protesters gathered Monday in Augusta, Maine, outside an office of Sen. Angus King, an independent who caucuses with Democrats, to press him to reconsider his support for Schumer.

King said, “I don’t have any problem with people disagreeing with me.” He added, “I’ve been surprised in this case by the volume, the vehemence.” One Senate office on Friday reported more than 280 calls urging Schumer’s ouster after Van Hollen’s announcement. Many callers were not from the state of the senator they were trying to convince, an aide said.

Democratic senators and staffers who support Schumer told The Wall Street Journal that they did not view the contest as truly competitive, despite their own dissatisfaction at times with the Senate’s functioning. They said they are frustrated by the leadership fight because it would focus on Democratic divisions when the party should be advancing its agenda and taking aim at President Trump.

As Democrats returned to Capitol Hill, most avoided saying how they would vote. Sen. Andy Kim said he had not made an announcement but was talking with both candidates. “I certainly am raising with them what I hope we can do better with the caucus,” he said.

Sen. Michael Bennet, who had not endorsed either candidate, praised Schumer for “doing a tremendous job raising the resources that he raised” during the midterms and said Schumer always does a good job recruiting candidates. “It doesn’t mean he’s perfect,” Bennet said, citing past disagreements.

Democrats are set to have a 56-44 majority in the new Senate if Mary Peltola wins in Alaska. Schumer’s supporters argue that he was a key architect of the midterm victories, recruiting winning candidates and raising a record $553 million through a super political-action committee run by his allies and affiliated groups. Those groups directed money into races in 22 battleground states, including contests where the Democratic candidate was opposing Schumer as leader. The spending included $67 million to help Abdul El Sayed in Michigan and $65 million to back Troy Jackson in Maine.

Schumer “did so much to lift up these incredible candidates, gave them the resources and the support they needed across this country to be these messengers of change,” said Sen. Kirsten Gillibrand, chair of Senate Democrats’ campaign arm, on MS Now. Gillibrand also said Schumer was best positioned to deliver on Democrats’ affordability agenda as leader.

Progressive activists criticized Schumer’s handling of a government funding showdown with Republicans last year. Some Democrats across the caucus have also criticized his leadership style, saying Schumer tells individual senators what they want to hear even if that means making contradictory commitments.

Many progressive voters view Van Hollen as a champion for the left’s agenda. They point to his visits to the Gaza border and his meeting with Kilmar Armando Abrego Garcia, a migrant deported to a Salvadoran prison.

Van Hollen is now threatening to trigger a government shutdown as leverage to end the Iran war. Such a move would up the ante for Schumer, who has not made such a threat, although he has promised that Democrats will end the war using Congress’s “power of the purse” if necessary.

Putting Schumer back in charge would be “a terrible message to send at a time when we need to be closing the credibility gap” with voters who want a change to the status quo, said Ezra Levin, co-founder of the progressive grassroots group Indivisible.

Indivisible endorsed Van Hollen on Monday and urged its members to contact Democratic senators to make their preferences clear. National Nurses United, which represents more than 225,000 registered nurses, also endorsed him. The Sunrise Movement and other progressive groups established a website urging voters to call their senators.

The International Association of Machinists and Aerospace Workers, or IAM, which represents about 600,000 members, touched off the drive by calling on Schumer not to run and endorsing Van Hollen.

IAM members “are hungry for change in the Senate—they just don’t feel they’re going to get that from Sen. Schumer,” said union president Brian Bryant.

North America’s Building Trades Unions backed Schumer. “Our unions have always believed leadership should be measured by what gets done, not by who generates the most headlines,” said the union’s president, Sean McGarvey.

Schumer continues to say he has enough votes locked down, while Van Hollen maintains that he has a path to the leadership. The Dec. 1 vote will determine the party’s choice, with most senators and senators-elect still withholding their public preferences.
