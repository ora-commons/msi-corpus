## Estonia's foreign minister says EU and NATO membership protect its independence

Steve Bloomfield, an international affairs commentator for The Guardian, argues in a column published on 22 November that the alignment between Donald Trump and Vladimir Putin threatens every European country — and that the continent's own voters may prove the greater danger.

Bloomfield anchors the argument in the Shenzhen summit, where Trump greeted Putin on the sidelines of the Apec meeting on 19 November. "It's my friend!" Trump said, in the columnist's account. A trilateral session alongside China's Xi Jinping, Bloomfield writes, was partly aimed at cementing the idea that great powers should control their own back yards: the western hemisphere for Trump, central and eastern Europe off limits to outside interference for Putin. He writes that both men share a view of Europe as a once-great Christian civilisation that has damaged itself through migration, particularly from Muslim-majority countries.

The columnist notes that long before Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine in February 2022, Putin was arguing that former Soviet or Warsaw Pact states seeking sanctuary in NATO were committing an act of aggression — an argument Bloomfield says denies those countries the basic right to decide their own security arrangements. Until recently, he writes, that case gained little support among mainstream western politicians; since Trump's return to office in January 2025, Putin has found a valuable ally in high office.

On the substance of the negotiations, Bloomfield writes that Putin has pushed throughout the stop-start peace talks led by Trump's son-in-law Jared Kushner and Steve Witkoff to broaden the discussion from Ukraine to Europe as a whole. Putin wants NATO to withdraw its troops and bases to the alliance's 1997 borders, which Bloomfield says would leave 16 alliance members without outside support and, for instance, Estonia and the other Baltic states without air defences. Putin has also demanded that Britain and France rule out using their nuclear weapons to protect any other European nation.

Trump has embraced the prospect of a wider settlement. Bloomfield quotes him telling reporters in the Oval Office that the deal "is going to be very big. It's going to involve more than Ukraine, and I could even say it might involve all of Europe. It's going to be a big, beautiful agreement, and everyone's going to be thrilled."

Europe was largely excluded from the Ukraine talks for more than a year, Bloomfield writes. In Abu Dhabi, where a group of European national security advisers had come to meet on the sidelines, Kushner was upbeat about the continent finally having a formal role in future talks, the columnist reports. Europe has now found a seat at the table, he writes, "only to discover that Europe itself is on the menu."

Bloomfield grounds the argument in Tallinn, where a plaque on the facade of Stenbock House, the Estonian prime minister's neoclassical office building, commemorates the murder of 10 heads of government killed by Soviet forces after the USSR's invasion of Estonia in 1940. Estonia was occupied for 51 years, he writes, and has lived independently in its neighbour's shadow for the past 25. The country has a population of 1.4 million and a neighbour 100 times its size.

Over the past decade, Bloomfield writes, there has been interference in Estonian politics, propaganda targeting the country's Russian-speaking minority, and a host of cyber and hybrid attacks — the most recent an attempted arson attack targeting an Estonian defence company.

"The goal is to create fear in our society," Margus Tsahkna, Estonia's foreign minister, told the columnist by phone from Tallinn. Tsahkna said the only thing protecting Estonia's independence was membership of the EU and, most importantly, NATO. "Without Nato membership we couldn't have this conversation," he said. "Estonia would have been 100% occupied."

Bloomfield writes that the danger is not only external. Within three years, he argues, far-right or hard-right parties could win power — or at least join the governing coalition — in western Europe's four biggest nations: the UK, France, Germany and Italy. The leaders of all four parties, he writes, are either openly sympathetic to Putin or at the very least critical of Ukraine's president, Volodymyr Zelenskyy.

Marine Le Pen, who Bloomfield says remains favourite to win the French presidency next spring, has vowed to "end financial aid" to Ukraine within six months of taking office and has refused an offer from Zelenskyy to visit him in Kyiv. The columnist notes that a photograph of her shaking hands with Putin featured prominently on her election leaflets in 2022, until the war in Ukraine made the image electoral poison.

In Germany, Alice Weidel, co-leader of the Alternative für Deutschland, does not attempt to hide her contempt for Ukraine, Bloomfield writes. He notes that the AfD currently leads Friedrich Merz's Christian Democrats by eight percentage points, and that in an interview with the Economist she questioned Ukraine's democracy, argued it was "impossible" for Ukraine to win and, in an echo of Putin, said "Russia's security interest" must be taken into account.

Italy's prime minister, Giorgia Meloni, has been one of Ukraine's staunchest allies, Bloomfield writes, but now faces a new far-right challenger, Roberto Vannacci, whose policy on Ukraine echoes the AfD's. Vannacci has questioned whether Alexei Navalny was killed by the Russian state, claims that Russia is a democracy and, asked whether he preferred Putin or Zelenskyy, chose the Russian leader.

In Britain, Bloomfield writes that Nigel Farage is smart enough to realise Putin is deeply unpopular, but has repeatedly expressed admiration for the Russian leader in the past. Following the full-scale invasion of Ukraine, Farage claimed the west had "provoked the war" by "poking the bear", and he blamed Zelenskyy for Trump's Oval Office ambush, accusing him of being "rude". Last month, Farage claimed Ukraine was "one of the most corrupt countries on earth" and said a Reform government would take a "keen look" at British aid to the country.

Kyiv's biggest supporters have taken notice, according to the columnist. Merz has promised to block the next seven-year EU budget unless there is a substantial aid package for Ukraine, and has proposed making future lending subject to qualified majority voting rather than unanimity — which would mean a President Le Pen seeking to block an EU-wide aid package would need several other countries to join her.

Support for Ukraine may be slipping, Bloomfield writes, not helped by a continent-wide cost of living crisis. "It is easy to manipulate people with easy solutions," Tsahkna told the columnist, saying he feared the elections in France and Germany could fundamentally reshape the continent.

Zelenskyy has long argued that Ukraine's frontline is Europe's frontline, Bloomfield notes. The columnist concludes that the continent's future is being fought in the Donbas, at the negotiating table and at the ballot box — and that while the Trump-Putin alliance threatens every European nation, the greatest danger is that those nations elect leaders who support it.
