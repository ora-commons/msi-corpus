## Jensen Huang’s ouster accelerates push for tighter U.S. chip controls on China

**Subtype:** fact

- Nvidia co-founder and CEO Jensen Huang’s ouster has accelerated Washington efforts to regulate AI and restrict U.S. business with China.
- The Trump administration’s new chip-export rule took effect immediately and restricts Chinese access to computing power powered by U.S. chips in Malaysia, Thailand, Indonesia and Singapore.
- Oracle canceled a $7 billion, five-year agreement to lease AI computing capacity to Tencent in Southeast Asia after U.S. officials raised concerns, according to people familiar with the matter.
- Joseph Hoefer, chief AI officer at lobbying firm Monument Advocacy, which represents companies including Netflix and Waymo, said companies expecting a light-touch China policy through 2028 must recalibrate.
- The Trump administration is considering requiring U.S. companies to disclose which Chinese AI models they deploy, according to people familiar with the matter.

## New export rule restricts Chinese access to U.S.-chip computing power

The Trump administration’s new chip-export rule took effect immediately and leaves companies about 60 days to offer comments. The rule makes Chinese access to U.S.-chip computing power more difficult in Malaysia, Thailand, Indonesia and Singapore, tightens restrictions on Chinese chip designers and is intended to prevent China’s AI industry from accessing computing power running on U.S. chips. It follows Jensen Huang’s ouster from Nvidia, giving momentum to Washington policymakers seeking tighter AI controls and more guardrails around U.S. business with China. The dispute comes amid long-simmering worries about AI dominance.

Oracle canceled a $7 billion, five-year agreement to lease AI computing capacity to Chinese technology giant Tencent in Southeast Asia after administration officials raised concerns, according to people familiar with the matter. The cloud-computing company did not respond to a request for comment.

The administration is also weighing a rule that would require U.S. companies to disclose which Chinese AI models they deploy, according to people familiar with the matter. The proposal would discourage use of the tools without banning them outright. Airbnb and crypto firm Coinbase are among the prominent companies using Chinese AI tools, which are often less expensive and easier to customize than models from OpenAI and Anthropic.

Business advisers said many businesses that see a relationship with China, or an intermediary, as a political liability are paying more attention to new policies and threats from the incoming Democratic-run Congress than to Trump’s words. Joseph Hoefer, chief AI officer at lobbying firm Monument Advocacy, which represents companies including Netflix and Waymo, said, “Companies that assumed the light-touch approach would hold through 2028 are going to have to recalibrate.” He added, “National security gives all sides cover to deal in a way that just wasn’t there before.”

Saif Khan, founding director of the Center for Technology & Statecraft who worked on technology policy in the Biden administration, said, “The more that changes, the less there’s going to be an appetite to sell it. The whole Nvidia incident was an accelerant of this.”

Corporate compliance work has expanded in parallel. H.K. Park, who leads the national-security practice at Washington advisory firm Crumpton Global, said he had received triple the number of requests since the Nvidia scandal broke from companies seeking checks on suppliers, business partners and customers in Southeast Asia for any threats to national security. Corporate boards, largely from the technology industry, hired the firm to determine whether they had exposure to a China-linked security risk, and some companies worried they could become the next target of a U.S. government investigation.

The policy change also reflects faster action inside the Trump administration. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent, Secretary of State Marco Rubio and other administration players have advanced proposed rules in recent weeks that had been languishing, including the chip-export restrictions announced Monday. On Monday, before President Trump boarded Air Force One for the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation meetings in Shenzhen, where he was scheduled to meet China’s Xi Jinping, Trump defended Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick. Lutnick had resisted restrictions that might anger China despite opposition from Bessent and other officials.

Much of the administration’s preceding-year approach to China emphasized maintaining a detente with Beijing. According to The Wall Street Journal, steps viewed as antagonistic toward China were mostly put on hold, abandoned or softened after Beijing imposed export controls on rare earths. The new controls mark a break from an administration approach that had mostly relied on companies to balance commercial interests against national security when selling sensitive technology.

The policy shift followed Huang’s own opposition to tighter controls. A year ago, he called the term China hawk “a badge of shame” and “unpatriotic.” Before his departure, Huang denied that Nvidia had a diversion problem and lobbied President Trump directly for fewer regulations and greater access to China.

China-focused lawmakers in both parties had pressed for investigations into alleged chip smuggling in Southeast Asia. Lawmakers and Commerce Department staffers wanted the department to close overseas loopholes that allowed Chinese companies to access computing power in other countries, and they blamed the agency for not doing more, faster. Some Commerce Department staffers were forced out after advocating for greater restrictions.

Claims that Nvidia customers had funneled sophisticated chips worth billions of dollars to Chinese AI companies ran into a string of technological breakdowns in recent days. Those breakdowns included a nationwide ground stop for airlines, bank-account glitches and money-transfer delays.

Sen. Pete Ricketts (R., Neb.), one of the lawmakers pushing for more chip-export restrictions, said, “There are those of us who wanted to have this regulation and now it’s becoming obvious why we need to have it,” adding, “This gives us more momentum to go back and do more restrictions.”

The controversy has breathed new life into Democratic demands for AI regulation and strengthened pro-regulation voices inside the Trump administration and around Washington. Democrats who promised to regulate AI, including Sen. Elizabeth Warren (D., Mass) and Rep. Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez (D., N.Y.), were expected to keep raising criticism and questions about the close relationships Jensen Huang and other tech executives have with Trump and his advisers. A senior Democratic staffer said Democrats who might not have taken such hawkish stances will now adopt them on many issues.

Washington’s tougher posture could also revive the prospects of the House Select Committee on China. Created by the Republican-controlled House in 2023, the committee had called for restrictions on companies including Megaspeed, a Singapore-based Nvidia business partner accused of smuggling Nvidia chips to China. The Wall Street Journal reported that Alice Huang, a former top Megaspeed executive suspected of being a Chinese intelligence asset, had an affair with Jensen Huang. For much of the previous two years, the committee had been sidelined as Trump stressed friendly relations with China, and it was at risk of being disbanded by the incoming Democratic Congress. Its prospects looked better after the Nvidia controversy, and Rep. Hakeem Jeffries (D., N.Y.), the presumptive House speaker, was expected to make a determination after Thanksgiving. Huang and Nvidia have been called to testify before congressional committees in the coming weeks.
