## Baca links Leo’s apology to Afro-Peruvian history and forgiveness

At 82, Susana Baca is a Latin Grammy-winning Afro-Peruvian singer and Peru’s first Black culture minister. When she was asked to sing for Leo during his visit home to Peru, she vowed to let the music speak for itself and declined all interview requests. She broke that silence in an exclusive written interview with The Associated Press. AP characterized the moment as a watershed for Baca and Peru’s African-descended community, while she feared it could be trivialized. As culture minister, she remains a leader in the campaign against the long-standing, second-class treatment of Indigenous and Black Peruvians.

Baca reflected on Leo’s message, its meaning for Peruvians and the music she chose to sing for him. Her comments followed Leo’s historic apology for the Catholic Church’s role in facilitating slavery and his visit to a UNESCO-recognized memorial for slavery in Peru. AP noted that Leo’s family tree includes enslaved people and slave owners.

Baca said the poem and song she performed before Leo were rooted in her heart. She said singing for the pope was a way to accompany him in his message and the preaching of faith. She described “Canción de Fe” (“Song of Faith”) as “a song of hope at a difficult moment for Peru with politicians of low caliber and little moral quality.”

For “Si me quitaran totalmente todo” (“If they took absolutely everything from me”), Baca said she sang an intense poem as an act of solidarity with those suffering in a violent world. She said the song appears on her album “Espíritu Vivo” (“Living Spirit”), which she recorded in New York City on Sept. 11, 2001. Baca said she had managed to move people living through great desperation and war because the poem brings life and death together.

She then linked the song to Gaza and the Middle East: “I feel that this is Gaza today … . It’s that same pain, which persists today in the Middle East and that can’t be silenced.”

Baca said Leo’s humility “opens the door to music and to communicating with young people.” She compared the visit to the return of the prodigal son, which she said every culture celebrates. For Baca, Leo was that son visiting, and she described the welcome through music, dance and “flavors for the palate.” Such a welcome, she said, could happen only when the bond between the people and the pope was that of family.

Baca said she felt “very good and happy” when Leo denounced the “suffering and humiliation” endured by African enslaved people at the Sanctuary of St. Turibius of Mogrovejo. AP described St. Turibius as revered for denouncing abuses against Indigenous and enslaved people by Spanish colonizers. She said Leo’s act of asking forgiveness and reflecting on St. Turibius’s history so long afterward was very important.

She called it a gesture the Afro-Peruvian community was grateful for and said it addressed “this dark chapter of history” with what she described as a clear vision of light, forgiveness and recognition that people can fail and also heal their mistakes.

Baca said an important part of Afro-Peruvian history was that Afro-Peruvians did not remain submerged despite their history of suffering. She described them as a people who had learned to forgive and said injustice in many moments of history became less painful with that capacity. She connected that history to Leo’s homilies and teachings, which she said emphasized asking for forgiveness, forgiving and being forgiven. “Finally, it’s forgiveness. And that’s faith,” she said.

She said her singing sought to be “a reflective singing” linked to the same notion of faith she believed Leo had and to a historical process belonging to the Afro community. Baca said Peru’s people have “a beautiful diversity,” a point she said Leo also made in his speech. She said his presence among Afro-Peruvians and Indigenous and Amazonian peoples condensed his rich human nature.

On the visit itself, Baca said, “For me, the pope himself was a revelation. In his gaze of a good man, I was able to find a deeper meaning in his ecclesiastical commitment.” She added, “He draws closer to his religious foundation where he grows, which is the people.” Baca said Leo conveyed a vision of a church renewed in its social dimension, one that highlighted Catholicism’s core principles: “the struggle for, and the commitment to, the men and women who are most vulnerable.”

For young people, Baca said, the message was “very clear.” She said young people who understood Leo’s words as part of their own “language of rebellion today” were speaking a truth that Francis had covered. She said that before Francis, the Church was distant from young people and did not represent a sense of the future; Leo placed his words in a context that spoke to young people. “The pope moves beyond the conservative rhetoric of the old church and speaks in a context that is youthful, vital and grounded in day-to-day life,” Baca said. She called his words “an embrace for the young people.”
