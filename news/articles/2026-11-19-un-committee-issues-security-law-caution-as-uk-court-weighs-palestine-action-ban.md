## Committee says defending human rights cannot itself breach public order

The UN human rights committee warned on Thursday that governments misuse counter-terrorism and national security laws to silence associations and their members, publishing its first comprehensive guidance on freedom of association as the UK Supreme Court considers the lawfulness of the Palestine Action ban. The guidance does not explicitly refer to Palestine Action. Hélène Tigroudja, the committee's vice-chair, said the decision to issue it was spurred by events in the UK, North Korea, Russia, China and Israel.

The committee's first comprehensive guidance on freedom of association criticizes vaguely worded laws targeting "extremism" or "undesirable" organizations. Tigroudja said there were accepted grounds for restricting the exercise of freedom of association, such as national security and the fight against terrorism.

"These legitimate purposes are too often so vaguely defined by domestic legislation, and more importantly, so extensively misused by authorities to silence by way of criminalising associations such as climate activists, children and young-led movements, human rights defenders, journalists, trade unionists, that these aims must be used with a heightened caution," Tigroudja said.

The guidance also states: "The right to defend human rights, the reporting of human rights violations, or the advocacy for accountability, for instance, cannot as such be labelled as a threat or breach of public order." Tigroudja said the committee had observed a global pattern in which 'terrorism' rhetoric was used to target associations and their members, exposing them not only to public opinion's criticism but also to sanctions, including heavy fines, arrest and detention.

The UK Supreme Court heard the challenge to the proscription at a two-day hearing in early November and has yet to rule on whether the ban was lawful. Palestine Action's legal challenge argues that the proscription disproportionately interfered with the rights to freedom of speech and protest.

Palestine Action was the first direct action group to be banned under the UK Terrorism Act, placing it alongside the likes of al-Qaida and Boko Haram. Last year's decision to ban the group has been criticized by civil liberties groups, politicians of different stripes, a former director of public prosecutions and a former director of the security services.

At the hearing, a lawyer for the group described its aims as the "disruption of the supply of weapons from the United Kingdom to Israel" at a time when the ICJ had found "a real and imminent risk [of] irreparable prejudice" to the rights of Palestinians in Gaza not to be subjected to acts of genocide. Israel denies committing war crimes or genocide.

Huda Ammori, a co-founder of Palestine Action who brought the legal challenge, said the committee's report "goes to the heart of the Palestine Action case." She argued that the government cannot simply label a movement 'terrorist' and use that designation to suppress political dissent by extinguishing the fundamental rights of its members and supporters. Ammori urged the Supreme Court to heed the committee's warning and overturn what she called an "unjust, dangerous and disproportionate" ban. If the courts fail to uphold those freedoms, she said, the group would take its case to the European Court of Human Rights, adding that "we will not stop fighting until this Orwellian proscription is overturned."

A Home Office spokesperson said: "As the court of appeal found, Palestine Action is not a civil disobedience protest group but one that carried out acts of terrorism, celebrated those who took part in them and promoted the use of violence." The spokesperson added that the "proscription followed a rigorous and evidence-based decision-making process, endorsed by parliament" and said: "Free speech is a fundamental democratic right; the proscription of Palestine Action does not affect lawful protest in support of the Palestinian cause."

The committee's guidance does not name Palestine Action, and the Supreme Court's decision remains awaited after the early-November hearing. The ruling will determine whether the proscription survives the challenge that it disproportionately interfered with the rights to freedom of speech and protest.
