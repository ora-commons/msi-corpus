## Peltola details fish racks and housing; Sullivan campaign, NRSC give no response

In Anchorage, Alaska Native Sam Schimmel said, "People across the country don't understand what it means to grow up in a place where there isn't running water, where there's only one store, where gas costs $17 a gallon, where heating fuel is not a guaranteed thing, which means that your house isn't necessarily going to be warm when it's 50 below zero." He added, "Having somebody who really understands what that means in D.C., representing us in the Senate, is an enormous achievement," and noted the significance of Peltola's election as the first Alaska Native in the Senate.

Michael Dunn, also in Anchorage, said he voted for Republican Sen. Dan Sullivan but hoped Peltola would do well. "She put in the effort," he said of her campaign. "So hopefully she puts in the effort in D.C. for us."

In her statement, Peltola said voters sent a clear message that "the status quo in D.C. is not working for Alaska." She added: "Over the course of this campaign I've spoken with elders in tears about empty fish racks; families struggling to find housing they can afford; and parents in the Bush faced with the impossible choice of heating their homes or putting food on the table." "Today we take a monumental step forward in addressing those issues and putting Alaska first."

Republican Sen. Dan Sullivan's campaign spokesperson Nate Adams did not respond to a request for comment. Adams had said Monday that the campaign would let the process "run its course until every legal vote is counted and certified." A spokesperson for the National Republican Senatorial Committee also did not respond.

Peltola's victory over two-term Republican Sen. Dan Sullivan came as Alaska's last U.S. Senate contest to be resolved in the 2026 midterms. It gave Democrats a 56-44 majority after they flipped nine Republican-held seats to retake the chamber. Democrats also won campaigns in South Carolina, Texas, Kansas and Mississippi.

The victory completed a political comeback for Peltola, who previously represented Alaska's lone House seat and became the first Alaska Native to serve in Congress. She lost her bid for a second full term to Republican Rep. Nick Begich in 2024 but became central to Democrats' plans to retake the Senate in 2026. The result came in a state President Donald Trump repeatedly won by wide margins. Peltola's election made her the first Alaska Native to serve in the Senate, and she will be the fifth Democrat since statehood to represent Alaska in the chamber. The most recent was Mark Begich, whom Sullivan defeated in 2014.

During the campaign, Peltola tapped into Alaskans' frustrations with high fuel prices and other costs. Those concerns hit especially hard in remote communities that rely on diesel for electricity and heat and are generally accessible only by air or water. Her campaign posted social media photos of her travels around the state as she sought to separate herself from national Democrats and build a coalition around a platform of "Alaska first."

The strategy resonated with voters who felt they could relate to Peltola, who is Yup'ik and often spoke about growing up in western Alaska. "Alaskans who know me know that I'm not liberal or radical," Peltola said. "I am only interested in working for Alaska and working for the best interest of Alaskans, in as nonpartisan of a way as I possibly can."

Emphasizing her Alaska roots, Peltola spoke about fishing for salmon on the Kuskokwim River with her father and spending parts of the summer at fish camp with her children and late husband, who died in September 2023. She said the fish and wildlife Alaskans long relied on to fill their freezers for winter were becoming harder to find. The changes were forcing people to buy more food and other staples at the store, with supplies that must be barged or flown in, and to deal with "crushing prices." Peltola also said members of Congress were "more focused on their stock portfolios" than helping ease those costs, and she vowed to push back against the billionaire class.

In the campaign's final stretch, Peltola faced allegations that her team helped recruit retired teacher Dan J. Sullivan to run in order to siphon votes from the incumbent. President Donald Trump called for Peltola's prosecution after The New York Times first reported the allegation less than a week before the Nov. 3 general election. Peltola denied any involvement. "I knew nothing about any other candidates on the ballot except for me and the incumbent," she told supporters in Juneau days before the election.

Dan J. Sullivan has denied any contact with Peltola's campaign or running at the Democrats' behest. He had not responded to messages from The Associated Press since The Times' article was published. The Times report was based partly on text messages and other documents and interviews with a former Peltola campaign manager, who was fired shortly before the open primary and later sought to recant her statements. Trump had already referred to Dan J. Sullivan multiple times during a rally supporting the senator in mid-October.

The Department of Justice filed a lawsuit that President Donald Trump said he ordered. It sought to block certification of the race if the incumbent lost, pending an investigation into possible voter confusion. Peltola called the lawsuit "flagrant corruption." A federal judge dismissed the case, and the Justice Department appealed. Attorneys for the state sought dismissal, saying officials should be able to conduct the election "without unwarranted interference by the United States." They also argued that the ballot was designed to distinguish the two Sullivans as much as possible and that Alaska's ranked-choice voting process would allow voters who might be confused to rank them.

The Justice Department also confirmed the existence of a criminal investigation, but Alaska's U.S. attorney would not say what potential crimes authorities were looking into. Peltola finished first in the primary with 49.5% of the vote, followed by the incumbent with 41.4%. Dan J. Sullivan finished a distant third with about 2.5% but advanced to the general election as one of the top four finishers. He was listed on both ballots as Daniel J. Sullivan Jr., with no party affiliation, while the senator was listed as Dan S. Sullivan, Republican and incumbent.

"Lower 48" became an insult bandied by both sides. Sullivan tried to align Peltola with "Lower 48 liberals" who wanted to restrict oil and gas drilling, mining and other resource development in Alaska. Peltola said she would work with Trump to get things done for Alaska but, "I will never put any Lower 48 politician before Alaskans. That's the difference between Dan and me."

As part of the Nov. 3 general election, Alaskans also elected a Democrat as governor for the first time since 1998, as former state lawmaker Jonathan Kreiss-Tomkins defeated three Republicans, including one endorsed by Trump. The Associated Press had not yet called the U.S. House race, where independent candidate Bill Hill was challenging Republican Rep. Nick Begich. Associated Press journalist Mark Thiessen in Anchorage contributed to the report.
