## Corporate governance expert calls Albertsons selection 'odd'

Albertsons CEO Susan Morris opened her latest earnings call by telling analysts it "is going to be a little different" — because she was about to introduce Meg Whitman, the grocery and pharmacy company's new executive chair, to "say a few words about the business."

Charles Elson, founding director of the Weinberg Center for Corporate Governance at the University of Delaware, called the selection "odd." "There's no doubt that she's extremely talented," Elson said. But the grocery business, he added, is one that "leaders usually grow up in." "Between the logistics, the real-estate concerns, the labor questions," Elson said, "it's complex."

Creating an executive chair position and placing someone in it who can advise a CEO is not unusual on its own. The chair joining an earnings call is also standard. The role, however, is normally reserved for founders or former CEOs — insiders who have moved on from the corner office and can provide stability during a transitional or turbulent period. Comparable examples include Starbucks, Disney, and Amazon, where the benefits and drawbacks — including the risk of confusion over who's really in charge — have been widely explored. Naming an outsider as a public company executive chair — and someone who doesn't have direct industry experience — is a less intuitive strategy, even if that outsider is as decorated as Whitman.

Albertsons' September press release announcing the role described Whitman as an "operations and strategic adviser" to Morris and her senior team. The release said Whitman's "experience will be particularly relevant as Morris continues to streamline the operating structure around AI technologies" and that she would help "refine the company's long-term strategy."

On the earnings call, Whitman laid out her priorities as Albertsons attempts a turnaround. She told analysts the chain has strong local brands and a powerful e-commerce platform, but acknowledged its problems. "[W]e must move faster to reduce costs, reinvest much of those savings into lower prices, improve the store experience for customers, grow traffic and share in core markets," she said.

Whitman's prior executive experience includes serving as chief executive of eBay and HP. She has since led the short-lived Quibi and represented the U.S. as an ambassador to Kenya. She also serves as a director at Coreweave and other companies.

Appearing at the Leadership Institute's WSJ Board of Directors Council Summit last year, Whitman told Council Chair Poppy Harlow she missed parts of her former working life. "I am a big person who loves my team, who loves doing things with other people, who loves a place to go, who wants to feel part of something," Whitman said. "And I'm very busy, but I don't feel part of something. And that's been a real challenge."
