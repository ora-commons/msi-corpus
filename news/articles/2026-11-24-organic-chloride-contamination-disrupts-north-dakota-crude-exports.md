## Organic chloride contamination disrupts North Dakota crude exports

## Testing backlogs and tank limits could accelerate production losses

Organic chloride contamination in North Dakota crude oil bound for export has prompted the Dakota Access Pipeline and Enbridge's pipeline from the Bakken oil field to Minnesota to require testing of every delivery before entry. Enbridge's system has operated at reduced capacity as producers send samples to overwhelmed commercial laboratories and search for storage space while they wait for results.

The contamination was first detected at Enbridge's hub in Minnesota, according to media reports. At least Enbridge's pipeline was operating below capacity as oil companies rushed samples to commercial labs and sought storage for crude awaiting test results.

Enbridge confirmed that its pipeline system, which crosses North Dakota from the Bakken oil field to Minnesota, is experiencing delays because of the contamination. “We expect delays to continue in the short term, until feeders deliver product clean of organic chlorides continuously,” the company said in a statement. “Enbridge is working with shippers to return service to normal operations as quickly as possible.”

A spokesperson for Dakota Access Pipeline developer Energy Transfer did not respond to a request for comment.

North Dakota Pipeline Authority Director Justin Kringstad estimated that the disruption has lowered state oil production by between 40,000 and 60,000 barrels per day. That is a small share of North Dakota's 1.1 million barrels of daily production, and the reduction could last only while companies have storage for oil awaiting testing.

During a Monday meeting of the North Dakota Industrial Commission, Kringstad said, “We’re probably getting to the point where tanks are full.” He also said, “This could cascade quickly.” Storage capacity varies by company, Kringstad said, and predicting when space would run out was “totally speculative.”

Kringstad said the organic chloride contamination does not pose a public safety risk. He said it is corrosive and can damage equipment including pipelines and refineries. Media reports said two refineries had stopped accepting deliveries of North Dakota crude oil, but Kringstad said he had not independently verified those reports.

Kringstad said even a few gallons of the contaminant can contaminate tens of thousands of barrels of crude oil. Under the current requirements, producers must wait for test results before major pipelines accept their oil.

Those samples are being sent to commercial laboratories, including facilities in Minot and Bismarck, that are experiencing a backlog. Kringstad said he was told the testing facilities are bringing in additional staff and resources to increase the speed of processing samples.

North Dakota's state government is heavily reliant on tax revenue generated by crude-oil production, making the estimated production decline a concern for a major source of public revenue.

Nathan Anderson, director of the Department of Mineral Resources, said organic chloride does not naturally occur in the Bakken formation, meaning it must have been introduced by an external source. Anderson said the contaminant could have been carried in chemicals used at a well site for tank cleaning or well servicing.

Identifying what introduced the contaminant is more difficult, Anderson said: “You’re looking for a needle in a haystack.”

The issue has not affected the refinery in Mandan or other retail products sold in North Dakota, Kringstad said. Anderson does not expect the contamination problem to last long.
