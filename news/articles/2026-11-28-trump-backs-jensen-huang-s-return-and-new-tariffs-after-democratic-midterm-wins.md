## Cost-of-living concerns remain without a formal policy response

During Thanksgiving week, President Donald Trump announced a new round of tariffs, defended his friendship with Russian leader Vladimir Putin and backed the return of billionaire Jensen Huang as Nvidia CEO after Democrats gained majorities in the House and Senate. At his Mar-a-Lago estate in Florida, Trump also accused his own government of releasing “fake” economic data and ordered an investigation into a revised GDP report.

The Associated Press reported that Trump appeared to reject the kind of post-election pivot presidents have used after midterm losses. Instead, the report said, he recommitted himself to tariffs, technology billionaires and autocrats without offering a course correction on affordability pressures.

The tariffs announced Wednesday applied to China and traditional partners including Japan, South Korea and the European Union. Two-thirds of voters said Trump’s import taxes had already hurt the economy as inflation worsened. Trump has called “tariff” one of his favorite words in the English language, but he did not personally promote the higher rates.

Trump had successfully pushed for Huang to be reinstalled as Nvidia CEO after Huang was ousted following his admission of an extramarital affair with Alice Huang. The Associated Press described Alice Huang as connected to the Chinese government; Beijing denies that she was a spy. Trump said Thursday, “He is so happy to be going back. He calls it his second term.” The Associated Press Voter Poll found that 7 in 10 voters said the economy had become rigged in favor of the wealthy.

In a Thanksgiving Day message on social media, Trump defended his focus on renovating the White House and accused his own government of releasing fake economic data. In the run-up to the holiday week, he also defended calling Russian leader Vladimir Putin a friend and offered no assurance about his next steps in the Iran war.

The Associated Press characterized Trump’s response to the midterms as historically unique. Matthew Dallek, a professor and political historian at George Washington University, said past presidents changed course after midterm losses. Democrat Bill Clinton overhauled welfare after the 1994 elections; Republican George W. Bush surged troops into Iraq after his party’s 2006 setback; and Democrat Barack Obama reached a bipartisan deficit-reduction deal after electoral losses in 2010.

Dallek said Trump’s seeming unwillingness to adapt to his unpopularity and a Democratic Congress was a sign that the White House might be embarking on a path of self-destruction. “If you’re going to commit to political hari-kari, Trump seems to be doing that on behalf of his party,” Dallek said. He called the midterm results a “deeper repudiation” of Trump on a national scale that “potentially shakes the bedrock assumption of American politics that there are red and blue states and a handful of purple states.”

The White House did not respond to questions about whether the Election Day outcome was prompting a serious reconsideration of its policies or whether it believed the public might eventually come around to Trump’s agenda. Trump suggested willingness to work with Democrats, even as he posted a cartoon of himself online as the mafia boss from “The Godfather.” The meme of Trump as Vito Corleone played on the joke that he would freely “veto” what Democrats sent him.

At cameras Thursday, Trump claimed he expected a “very bipartisan relationship” because Democrats promising to hold him accountable would be intimidated by his ability to veto legislation. “So I’m hearing from Democrats,” Trump said. “They’re calling because I have the power of veto, and I’m not looking to use it, but it’s a very strong power.”

Sen. Tim Kaine, D-Va., posted on X that the president had responded to a “historic repudiation” by digging in on policies such as deporting immigrants, aligning himself with technology billionaires and pursuing tariffs. “Read the room!” Kaine said.

Voters sent a message in the midterms that they expected Washington to help make necessities more affordable, but Trump had yet to present any concrete fixes. On Wednesday, he did not focus on a government report showing that the main personal consumption expenditures price index, the key inflation gauge tracked by the Federal Reserve, rose 3.8% over the past 12 months.

Instead, Trump focused on the recent decline in gasoline prices, implying that prices had been artificially high before the Nov. 3 election as a form of political pressure on Republicans. “Gasoline is coming way down,” Trump said Thursday. “That happened right after the election was over.”

The American Petroleum Institute attributed the decline in part to colder weather, saying “as Americans drive less, refiners switch to less expensive gasoline blends and fuel inventories rebuild.” Energy costs initially climbed after Trump launched the war with Iran in February.

Yuval Levin, a senior fellow at the conservative American Enterprise Institute, said Trump had not pursued “any organized policy response to the election” and did not expect one. Trump’s hints about expanding access to healthcare and pursuing an immigration overhaul had not been matched by any formal presidential decisions. “I’d expect him to continue to play a more reactive and inconsistent role, keeping people inside and outside the administration off balance but not taking any steady course,” Levin said.

On Thanksgiving, Trump accused the Commerce Department of releasing a “fake” gross domestic product report before the election. The department had revised third-quarter GDP upward on Wednesday to an annual rate of 4.3%, well above the 3.4% advance estimate released at the end of October. The Associated Press said the revision was part of a standard procedure for updating GDP figures. Trump claimed without evidence that the change was a “purposeful” error and ordered an investigation on social media. When a reporter asked who would lead the investigation, Trump changed the subject.
