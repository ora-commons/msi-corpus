## Acting Army secretary calls operation a ‘huge success’

The U.S. Army carried out the controlled detonation of nearly five tonnes of degraded lead azide near Middletown on Wednesday, sending smoke into the sky shortly after 09:00 local time (14:00 GMT). Acting Army Secretary Adam Telle called the mitigated blast a success, and the Army said the risk was “as close to zero as is absolutely humanly possible.”

The operation had sparked widespread fears of health and environmental problems across the region. The explosives had been stored for around 20 years and had degraded through a process called recrystallisation, which made them more responsive to accidental detonation from friction or other movement. Lead azide was described as highly volatile and reportedly at risk of exploding on its own. Officials said the Army needed to remove the stockpile to prevent a possible chain-reaction explosion.

Plans had been paused multiple times before Wednesday’s detonation as residents and politicians opposed the operation and legal challenges proceeded. On Monday, an appeals court overturned a decision to halt the blast. The court said the environmental and health concerns were valid but that the risk from an unplanned explosion was greater.

Before the operation, the Army warned locals of “potential exposure to toxic lead dust,” and the warning led to some panic. The Army put in place an optional evacuation zone and a shelter-in-place advisory. It also rescheduled the operation ahead of colder winter temperatures.

Telle wrote on social media that “The Army's controlled, mitigated detonation exceeded all expectations and was a HUGE success.” He said the Army was closely watching the Environmental Protection Agency’s sensor array throughout the area and that no readings so far were above 0.0.

President Donald Trump said the mitigation efforts “have far exceeded expectations.” Despite widespread concern from residents of Middletown, Iowa, ahead of the blast, officials said the lead readings in the area so far were promising. The BBC report also noted that no level of lead exposure is considered safe, with children and pregnant women particularly vulnerable to harmful effects.

Middletown Mayor Rodney Johnson told the BBC he felt “relieved” and “vindicated.” He watched from his job at a manufacturing plant eight miles (12 km) from the detonation site and monitored a camera at his two-bedroom home one mile away. “We did not feel anything. We did not see anything,” Johnson said.

Sandy Mabeus, whose home was eight miles from Middletown, closed the windows and garage, brought their outdoor cat inside and hunkered down earlier that day. Her family watched three separate live streams on iPads and televisions. She said, “We did not feel or hear a thing here,” adding that she checked the videos to confirm the detonation had actually happened. Mabeus said, “The waiting and wondering has not been a good thing for anybody in the area.”

Residents’ accounts described what they felt and heard, while the Army’s monitoring claim addressed whether lead readings had risen above 0.0. Mabeus said, “Hopefully they had a very good result and we can just get on with things.”
