## Recommendations target training, communication and firefighting strategy

A multiagency team of experts has found that leadership failures, inadequate preparedness and poor communication were driving factors in the 2025 Dragon Bravo Fire, which destroyed the Grand Canyon Lodge and dozens of structures on the North Rim of Grand Canyon National Park. The federal review, recently made public, says the fire should be considered a warning for agencies managing complex wildfire landscapes.

According to the review, lightning ignited the fire in July 2025, and the National Park Service allowed it to burn for days before it grew into a fast-moving conflagration. The blaze forced evacuations and consumed the Grand Canyon Lodge, historic cabins, employee housing and other critical infrastructure. Suppression costs topped $141 million over three months.

The National Park Service initially defended its handling of the fire, saying a sudden and extreme shift in the wind far exceeded forecasts. The review said drought conditions and extreme fire behavior contributed, but that systemic weaknesses within the National Park Service — including problems involving authority and qualifications — hindered decision-making at critical moments.

Park officials knew the region was experiencing persistent drought and dense fuels lay close to nationally significant historic structures. Fire planning and policy documents existed, but the review stated those plans “were not fully translated into effective preseason preparedness actions or operational readiness.”

“The findings indicated many of the challenges observed during the fire were foreseeable, preventable, and reflective of broader institutional issues that extend beyond a single park, region, or agency,” the report stated.

Ordered by the Interior Department's deputy secretary, the review starts with the day of ignition and reconstructs the weather conditions. It includes photographs of the towering plume that could be seen from the South Rim, the lodge burning and the charred remains of the historic building.

The review also highlighted a breakdown in communication that produced a multiday delay in using fire retardant as the flames intensified. The decision to use retardant came only after several structures, including the lodge, had been lost, the findings said. The continued authorization of a confine-and-contain strategy also did not reflect awareness of changing conditions or escalating risk.

Among its recommendations, the review calls for requiring agency administrators to undergo the training needed to handle complex incidents. It also calls for communication to flow through proper channels so decision-makers receive firsthand, accurate and timely information. Fire leadership should reevaluate strategies regularly and reconsider weather conditions, fire behavior and the availability of resources.

The review team described the Dragon Bravo Fire as a defining event for Grand Canyon National Park and a consequential case study for the Interior Department and U.S. Wildland Fire Service. It said the loss of historic structures, displacement of employees, risks to firefighters and significant suppression costs underscored the stakes of managing wildfires in complex landscapes.

“What began as a small, naturally ignited fire rapidly evolved into a high-consequence, cross-boundary incident that tested leadership, governance, preparedness, decision quality, and execution across multiple programs and agencies,” the team stated.

Grand Canyon officials referred questions about the findings to the Interior Department, which oversees the National Park Service. The Interior Department did not immediately answer emails sent by the Associated Press.
