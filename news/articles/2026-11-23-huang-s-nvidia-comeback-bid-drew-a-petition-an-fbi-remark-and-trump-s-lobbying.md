## Huang's Nov. 17 statement met a unanimous rejection on the earnings call

The bid to return Jensen Huang to Nvidia's top job ran through a board investigation, a petition drive and sustained lobbying from the White House, and it was rejected at a quarterly earnings call before the president said he had been working behind the scenes to change the board's decision. Nvidia's board announced its own investigation into the allegations against Huang while saying it remained confident in his leadership. President Donald Trump said the FBI was investigating but remained complimentary of the chief executive, saying Huang “has done a fantastic job.”

Pressure on the company compounded late Wednesday, after reports surfaced of another alleged affair by Huang, this time involving an Nvidia employee who reported to him. Nvidia had announced Huang's resignation on Nov. 12, effective immediately, replacing him in the interim with Chief Financial Officer and Executive Vice President Colette Kress and naming investor and entrepreneur Harvey C. Jones as its first-ever chairman. Huang, a company co-founder, stepped down after reports of extramarital affairs, including one involving a Chinese businesswoman suspected of working as a spy for Beijing.

The day after the resignation announcement, a “Stand with Jensen” petition drive began and picked up steam, gaining signatures from many current and former Nvidia staff, investors and other Silicon Valley supporters, including SpaceX founder Elon Musk. The campaign called on the board to bring Huang back while an independent investigation proceeded or until there was “compelling evidence” that his departure was in the company's best interests.

Trump weighed in on Nov. 16, saying Huang “learned a big lesson” and he “would certainly not mind” if he was reinstated. In his first public statements since his departure, Huang posted on Nov. 17 on social media that he would be “honored” to return as chief executive if the board decided it was in the company's best interests, and he acknowledged making “serious mistakes” in his personal life.

Later that day, during Nvidia's quarterly earnings call, the board rejected Huang's offer to return as chief executive. Jones said the board of directors “unanimously accepted his resignation and does not intend to reinstate Jensen as CEO.”

Also on Nov. 17, key House lawmakers said Huang had voluntarily agreed to appear for transcribed interviews, as the company faced intensifying scrutiny over sales of its advanced computer chips and the implications for national security.

Trump continued lobbying publicly and behind the scenes. Over the weekend, he posted on social media that he hoped the board would rehire Huang, writing, “It would be the best thing for the Company, and also, for the U.S.A.” After Huang was reinstated, the president disclosed that he had been working behind the scenes as well. He said he spoke with Huang about the reinstatement on Sunday, and that the chief executive assured him he would work harder than before his fall. Trump also said his administration had assured Nvidia that there were no national security concerns about Huang's return.

The board's Nov. 23 announcement said Huang had returned as chief executive, effective immediately, 11 days after the company announced his resignation and six days after the board rejected his offer to return. Nvidia's statement said: “In the process of reaching its decision, the Board received input from senior levels of the United States government, and based on that input, the Board is confident that Mr. Huang's reinstatement poses no national security risk.”

Trump claimed credit for the outcome, saying in an interview Monday with CNBC: “The board decided to do what we wanted them to do.”

Huang's return ended Colette Kress's interim leadership and placed him back at the center of Nvidia, a company at the heart of the artificial intelligence boom. The 11-day sequence ran from Nvidia accepting his resignation, through the board's rejection of his offer, to his reinstatement. The company remained under scrutiny over advanced-chip sales and their national-security implications.
