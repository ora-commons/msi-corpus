## Sentence awaits full adjudication after defense promises appeal

Hamilton Republican state Sen. Jason Ellsworth entered a no-contest plea on Tuesday to a misdemeanor official-misconduct charge before Lewis and Clark County District Court Judge Christopher Abbott. Abbott immediately imposed six months of probation, a $500 fine and permanent forfeiture of Ellsworth’s Senate seat.

The sentence’s effect remains contested. Defense attorney Joan Mell said it would not take effect until the case was fully adjudicated, and she said the defense would promptly appeal to the Montana Supreme Court despite the plea agreement.

The agreement replaced a trial set for next month. By pleading no contest, Ellsworth does not admit or dispute the charges against him, but he agreed to accept the conviction. Montana Department of Justice prosecutors had charged the former Montana Senate president with a misdemeanor offense of official misconduct almost a year earlier.

Dan Guzynski, a prosecutor with the Montana Department of Justice, said the defense’s plan to appeal came as a surprise because the defense had not discussed an appeal during plea negotiations. “The idea of this plea agreement was to have finality,” Guzynski said in court on Tuesday.

When asked by Montana Free Press what arguments the defense planned to make before the Montana Supreme Court, Mell said, “every one of the issues from the inception.” She did not spell out the arguments in that response.

The case stems from late 2024, when Ellsworth attempted to contract with a longtime friend’s company for $170,100 in state funds over which he had discretion at the time. The company had been formed only weeks earlier. Ellsworth did not go through the public bidding process typically required for state-funded contracts of that amount.

The money was left over from a special legislative committee on judicial reform that Ellsworth formed in 2024. Under the contract, Bryce Eggleston was to track the progress and implementation of 27 bills drafted by the special judicial committee. Eggleston had no relevant expertise in that area.

Before the final contract was approved, emails between Ellsworth and state staff showed that he sent two nearly identical contracts totaling $170,100. Each contract was worth less than $100,000, the threshold at which the work had to be posted publicly for bids. Legislative staff rejected the two-contract approach, the emails showed.

The case had already reached a jury once. A jury found Ellsworth guilty following a criminal trial this summer, but Abbott later threw out the verdict after determining that the jury had been inadvertently tainted. After that verdict was thrown out, the case had been set for a new trial next month before the plea agreement.

Ellsworth criticized the state for spending public funds on a misdemeanor case in a statement released through Montana Senate staff. “Continuing to burn through Montana taxpayers’ money over a misdemeanor is unconscionable, and stepping off this path is the righteous thing to do for the people footing the bill,” Ellsworth said in the written statement. “It has been an honor to represent Senate District 43, and I thank you for the trust you placed in me.”

The Montana Department of Justice did not immediately respond on Tuesday to questions about why prosecutors chose a plea deal rather than take the case to trial next month.

The permanent office forfeiture was nearly moot before the end of Ellsworth’s term, which ends in January when the new Legislature gavels in. It would matter if he chose to run for reelection. This year, Ellsworth sought a seat in the Montana House of Representatives but lost the Republican primary by nearly 77 points, or 2,468 votes.

During the 2025 legislative session, the Senate banned Ellsworth for life from its floor, but his colleagues stopped short of expelling him. Mell said the sentence would not take effect until the case is fully adjudicated, and the defense has promised to promptly appeal to the Montana Supreme Court.
