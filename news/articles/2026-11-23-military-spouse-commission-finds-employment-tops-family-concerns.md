## Family policy changes span childcare, toll relief and health records

The President's Military Spouse Commission has held three meetings since President Donald Trump established it by executive order in August and has begun meeting monthly to identify military-family quality-of-life challenges in areas such as employment, education and deployment-related support. The broader population includes more than 1.4 million spouses and dependents of active-duty U.S. military service members around the world. The two-year advisory commission is chaired by Jennifer Hegseth, wife of Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth, and all members are spouses of senior leaders from each military branch and the Department of Homeland Security.

At its Nov. 16 meeting, the commission said employment was the top issue across the hundreds of submissions in its online feedback portal, accounting for 45%. Childcare and education were also frequently cited. PBS News conducted nearly a dozen interviews with military spouses and received more than 230 responses to a callout; more than half of those responses cited employment.

Respondents also described difficulties navigating the Exceptional Family Members Program, which supports family members with special medical or educational needs, and the financial burdens of permanent change of station moves. A recent community survey by the National Military Family Association also ranked employment and health care among the top issues. Military families have long raised these concerns, and legislative efforts have addressed some of them, though spouses told PBS News that more work remained.

Crystle Ardoin, a military spouse for nearly 15 years, quit her corporate job just shy of a year into her husband's Army career because the family could not afford childcare. She later obtained credentials as a school librarian but said reentering the workforce remained difficult; she has quit multiple jobs in that field as the Army moved the family. A 2024 federal survey of active-duty spouses found that civilian spouses who had made a PCS move within the previous year had about a 136% higher chance of being unemployed than spouses who had not. Retired Air Force Lt. Col. Doug Lindsay said 20% unemployment among military spouses has a direct effect on a service member's ability to serve. He said the concern becomes a force-readiness consideration.

Food access also shapes quality of life on overseas bases. Haley Harman, whose four-year marriage has included stations in South Korea and Japan, said the commissary "is not the place to go for produce." She also said "a lot of the items that are sent to the base arrive expired since it's being shipped from the states." Many families seek produce from local farmers off base, but transportation was limited and Harman's base authorized only one vehicle per family. WIC, the USDA's Supplemental Nutrition Program for Women, Infants, and Children, cannot be redeemed at off-base markets or stores, she said. Her husband is an E6, a midlevel enlisted position, and is 10 years into his career, yet they still qualify for assistance. Harman said, "I can't think of any other career where you work for 10 years and you still qualify for food assistance."

The cost of daycare was the main factor in Brooke Freeman's decision to leave the workforce and care for her children. Freeman, stationed in Tennessee with her husband, has moved five times in eight years of marriage. After her second child was born, she applied for jobs for two years but said employers would not hire her because she had children at home and described herself as a military spouse who would operate like a single parent. Freeman said many employers did not want to take her on as a liability for their business. Despite her husband's advancement in rank, she said they still qualify for WIC.

Freeman's family lives off base because of housing conditions on military installations. In one relocation, Freeman said asbestos and mold were confirmed during a walkthrough of military housing; because one child had respiratory issues, she was told "it's this or nothing." The family receives a monthly basic allowance for housing, calculated to cover 95% of housing costs based on the service member's rank, local rental market rates, utilities and number of dependents. Freeman said her family pays significantly more than the allowance.

Relocation costs also reach National Guard families. Chelsea Macias, married for 14 years to an Active Guard Reserve soldier who serves full time in the Texas National Guard, said the family stayed in Texas but made several PCS moves. Some AGR soldiers whose units are restationed within the state or nearby commute two hours each day because their families lack the resources to move or live closer to work, Macias said. Her unit headquarters is in a large, expensive city, and her family moved about every three years. For people from smaller areas with a lower cost of living, Macias said, "it doesn't make sense to sell your house and dump all your money into a volatile housing market."

Each self-procured move cost $3,000 to $10,000, Macias said. A self-procured move is one option the government offers for a PCS: the family moves its belongings instead of using government-arranged movers, pays upfront and is later reimbursed only up to a certain point. One move cost $15,000 before the government reimbursed $11,250, leaving $4,000 out of pocket, she said. Macias said people in the civilian world generally do not spend $4,000 to move and then do it again.

Questions about representation follow the commission's senior-leader membership. Harman asked members to "remember their roots" and "Think back to when every paycheck mattered." Junior-grade officers make up about 56% of the officer corps, while junior enlisted personnel make up about 50% of the enlisted workforce. Lt. Col. Kaylee Bandy, program manager of the Department of the Army Career Engagement Survey, told Stars and Stripes in July that effects on family were the second-highest reason for soldiers leaving the Army. Lindsay said family employment or employability problems could have a "really drastic effect" on a soldier who might otherwise want to stay after 10 years. Lindsay said experiences can differ greatly by rank and said the commission needed due diligence.

Past commissions and advisory boards have operated under the Pentagon and advised the acting secretary, but the current group has closer presidential access, Lindsay said. He said being "really proximal to the president and advising directly to the president gives them a visibility and insight that they haven't had before." The commission explicitly places family quality-of-life issues within military readiness and retention, he said. Lindsay suspected the state of the economy was the catalyst for the new commission, saying, "Childcare, it's not cheap, right? Food is not getting any cheaper." He said unemployment, PCS moves and their associated costs were becoming more salient than they had been in the past.

Commission members serve unpaid but may receive travel reimbursement. The One Big Beautiful Bill Act designated roughly $9 billion for military quality-of-life issues, but the commission will not direct that money. Lindsay said some recommendations could be pursued directly by Trump, while those requiring resources would need to move through Congress as a new Congress prepares to take office.

A White House spokeswoman said military spouses are "essential to the strength, readiness, and retention of our military." She also said Trump had rebuilt America's military and was ensuring military families received support. Retired U.S. Navy Rear Adm. Mark Montgomery, a senior fellow at the Foundation for Defense of Democracies who ran a federal cybersecurity commission, was more skeptical. He said "Most commissions produce wonderful summaries of the challenges; few are effective at implementing the results impactfully." His own panel succeeded, he said, because it included four serving congressmen. Montgomery said changing laws requires congressional buy-in from the start and that recommendations might not have an effect before the current administration leaves office, adding that administrations can ignore predecessor initiatives that remain in process.

The commission has also advanced specific proposals. At its September meeting, Jennifer Hegseth announced that at the commission's urging, the Pentagon would roll out a five-location pilot allowing families to use Military Child Care in Your Neighborhood assistance at certain faith-based, licensed-exempt daycares in their communities. Eligible providers must have legally recognized, licensed-exempt status and comply with local health and safety laws. The Pentagon announced Nov. 12 that the pilot would expand to include certain school-operated public and private childcare programs.

In October, the commission said it was working with Florida and toll operators on a relief pilot for families commuting to MacDill Air Force Base in Tampa. Active-duty service members and spouses currently receive a 50% discount on tolls; the commission said it was seeking a 100% discount and a nationwide model.

The commission also said it had worked with the Defense Health Agency to overturn its "Mature Minor Policy." Under the former policy, parents lost access to a child's electronic health records and protected health information when the child turned 13, although a portal allowed them to view appointments, messaging, immunizations and allergy care. Starting Nov. 18, the commission said minors ages 13 to 17 can grant parents access to their medical records through an online portal unless state law prevents it.

A "state of the spouses" report expected in November had not received an update in the commission's latest meeting or in an emailed response to PBS News. The National Military Family Association, which has worked with military families for more than 50 years, said it was hopeful the commission would lead to "actionable solutions on issues such as spouse employment, childcare, and food insecurity." The association also said the commission members' lived experience could inform its work. Montgomery said he was glad the administration was tackling military-family issues but would need to "readjust the mission to the timetable they have remaining."
