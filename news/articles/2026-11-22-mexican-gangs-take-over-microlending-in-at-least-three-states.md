## Borrowers pay 60% interest and loan officers face abduction

The call reached the office of a small lender in Zacapu, a rural community in the mountains of southwestern Mexico, in the late afternoon. The managers had five minutes to get to a nearby parking lot, where the local drug-gang boss was waiting.

When they arrived, the managers said, the crime boss told them they could no longer operate in the area and that he would be taking full control of their loan operations.

Mexican criminal groups are now targeting small financial institutions that provide microloans, a shift that security experts say is part of a search for alternative revenue streams to diversify from drug smuggling.

The industry sits at what its executives describe as the base of an economic pyramid. Microlenders provide $3.5 billion in loans — usually in increments of $300 up to $3,500 — to nearly 11 million clients across Mexico, according to The Wall Street Journal. The recipients, who usually do not qualify for retail bank loans, use the money to start or expand small businesses: taco and market stands, shops selling everything from flowers to piñatas, in a vast informal, cash-based economy.

Gangs are trying to take over lending to small businesses in at least three Mexican states, said Claudia Revilla, the director general of ProDesarrollo, a national network for microfinance. Taking control of the loan book strengthens a group's hold on territory and on local economies that run on cash payments: access to capital must pass through the gang and be repaid at rates often significantly above market levels.

"This tactic of trying to take over businesses is something new," Revilla said. "And now it's spreading."

Revilla said microlenders are watching organized crime spread across a corridor in southwestern Mexico that stretches from Guerrero and Michoacán through the state of Mexico, which neighbors the capital. Microfinance organizations and clients who have been threatened are often hesitant to go to authorities, she added, given the criminal infiltration of local law enforcement in some states.

For one firm with a loan portfolio of around $50 million, the trouble began last year in and around Acapulco, on Mexico's Pacific coast — once a destination for Hollywood celebrities like John Wayne. The working-class barrios in the hills surrounding the bay are now plagued by drug addiction, domestic and criminal violence and extortion by organized crime.

Many microlenders operate in those impoverished communities around Acapulco, such as Coyuca and Atoyac. Some of the companies lend to groups of around a dozen women, who repay in 16 weekly installments, usually in cash; the group structure, in which borrowers back one another against default, reduces credit risk for the lender.

Once a criminal group takes over microlending, access to capital becomes far more expensive and more dangerous. In Zacapu, the gang tripled the interest rate loan recipients paid, to 60% over 14 weeks. In the Acapulco area, one manager said the cartel takeover caused some business owners to close their shops rather than deal with organized crime.

Some academic studies have found that microfinance does not improve the economic conditions of most borrowers. Many small-business owners in Acapulco say the small loans are essential anyway.

Susy Lira lost everything when Hurricane Otis devastated Acapulco in 2023, including her tiny Popsicle business in a barrio on the hills overlooking the tropical bay. She started from scratch with a loan of about $200 to open a gift shop, then added perfumes, cosmetics, children's clothing and toys bought at wholesale markets in Mexico City as her recurring credit line grew. Lira says the main risk to her business is the deteriorating security situation in her working-class community, a site of frequent shootings and murders.

In recent months, criminal groups began approaching employees at multiple offices of a microlending firm in the area. At one branch, gang members told staffers they could no longer work in the area because the group was taking over the loan business.

The branch manager there said a loan officer's motorcycle was stolen by a criminal group that had earlier warned him to get out of the area. The group later abducted two more staffers and beat one of them with a metal cable, landing him in the hospital.

The lending firm had to close one branch in the Acapulco area and relocate another. Many clients in the area, knowing they would get no more credit, defaulted on their loans. The microlender lost around 500 clients.

Across the country, branches that account for 15% of the company's total portfolio have faced threats from criminal groups, microlending executives said.

Lenders have also faced extortion threats elsewhere. In October, a criminal group held hostage six employees of a microlending firm in western Jalisco state, according to an internal security report viewed by The Wall Street Journal. The employees were blindfolded and transported to a safe house, where members of the group accused them of operating illegally and demanded a payment of roughly $27,000. The employees were eventually released without paying.

In February, a criminal group contacted a loan officer in Cuautla, in southern Morelos state, and told the officer that keeping several nearby communities would cost around $4,000 for each company vehicle used. The firm declined to pay and stopped renewing loans in the area. Since then the Cuautla branch has lost about 40% of its portfolio, or about $115,000. The gang took over lending to former clients and warned them that if they were caught taking loans from the firm, their children could be kidnapped.

"They are charging more," one employee said of the gang, adding that people took the loans anyway. "It was the only option people in town had."
