## Schwartz details measles death-count review and vaccine outreach strategy

CDC scientists are turning to outside medical journals to publish urgent health information because staff losses have slowed the agency's internal process for sharing information, CDC Director Dr. Erica Schwartz said in an Associated Press interview on Tuesday.

Schwartz said the CDC asked scientists to submit a warning about measles in newborns to an external journal rather than go through internal clearance. Three months into her tenure, Schwartz is being investigated by House Democrats, has faced a call to resign and is drawing criticism from inside and outside the agency, and employees told the Associated Press that staff morale is as bad as ever.

The CDC has historically used health alerts and the Morbidity and Mortality Weekly Report to share information about urgent public health matters quickly. Schwartz said the slow review process was the reason the newborn measles warning appeared in a medical journal: “We wanted to make sure that this information got out very quickly. So we asked them to push it to an external journal so that they can get that information out quickly rather than go through” an internal CDC process for information release, she said.

Public health leaders reacted angrily after reports emerged that, amid the worst U.S. measles epidemic in more than three decades, the CDC was slow to provide guidance to doctors on how to recognize and treat measles in unvaccinated women of childbearing age. In September, CDC scientists submitted a proposed health alert to Schwartz's office, and administrators did not reply for more than a month, according to a CDC scientist with direct knowledge of the situation who spoke to the Associated Press on condition of anonymity and fears retribution for publicly revealing what happened. The Associated Press confirmed the delay, which was first reported by The Washington Post.

“The idea that CDC scientists (were) not being able to give guidance to American doctors? I mean, like, steam coming out of my ears,” said Dr. Tom Frieden, who was the CDC director during the Obama administration.

HHS officials had attributed the delay to the CDC's scientific review and clearance process. Schwartz said it is faster for CDC scientists to submit articles to external publications and go through peer review than for the CDC to release information directly.

Staffing losses compound the process. The CDC has lost about 30% of its staff since President Donald Trump took office early last year, largely through layoffs, retirements and resignations. “We're operating under a situation where we do not have the same resources that we once did, prior to my coming here,” she said. The office that clears direct releases “do not have the amount of personnel that they had in prior times,” she said. A CDC scientist noted that other health alerts this year have come out fairly quickly, including one about hantavirus.

Schwartz's approach to transparency is also under scrutiny. At her nomination hearing in July, she pledged to use “radical transparency” to rebuild public trust in the agency, but the CDC has repeatedly been criticized for growing less transparent. In late August, shortly after Schwartz started, the CDC posted about two deaths in Pennsylvania on its website, then deleted them and said it was doing further investigation.

Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. initially said the Pennsylvania deaths may have been “altogether fabricated,” and he has also questioned the reported number of U.S. COVID-19 deaths. Schwartz said Kennedy played no part in the decision to take the Pennsylvania deaths off the CDC's tally. She said “the CDC did not do a great job during COVID of defining what a COVID death is” and wanted a nationwide, uniform definition of what should be counted as a measles death. That definition is still being developed, and the agency is reviewing death certificates before updating its count.

Three Democrats on the U.S. House of Representatives Energy and Commerce Committee disagreed with that explanation in a recent letter to Schwartz. “As the (measles) crisis worsens, we are concerned that you continue to direct an effort at CDC to downplay the outbreak and artificially reduce the number of measles deaths that CDC reports to the public,” they said.

Schwartz said she came to the job with three priorities: rebuilding trust in the CDC, modernizing the agency and strengthening its relationships with state and territorial partners. She has made a number of trips to meet leaders of state and tribal agencies and is pushing development of an early-warning Biothreat Radar program that she calls “the future of CDC.”

She acknowledged that she has not been a highly visible advocate for vaccines. The measles outbreak has especially hit the “Plain communities” of Amish and conservative Mennonites, groups named for their modest dress and limited use of technology, where vaccination rates range widely but are lower overall than in the general population. Schwartz said members of these close-knit religious communities “made decisions about their vaccine status 10, 20, 30, 40 years ago. So our approach is not to get on the bully pulpit and say ‘Verily, thou shalt get the vaccine,’” she said.

The CDC is supporting efforts to find trusted messengers to work on the ground in affected communities, rather than having a CDC official appear “on a TV show on the weekend when these people are not even watching this TV show,” she said.

The Associated Press asked why Schwartz was not promoting flu, COVID-19 and other vaccinations, as previous CDC directors have. She said a lawsuit filed by the American Academy of Pediatrics, the Infectious Diseases Society of America and other organizations constrains her while the case is ongoing. “I'm sort of constrained in what I can and what I cannot say,” she said.


In [MSI's earlier report on the federal measles response](/articles/2026-11-17-some-experts-say-no-kennedy-or-cdc-measles-briefing-is-scheduled/), some experts noted that neither Kennedy nor the CDC had scheduled a news conference as confirmed U.S. cases exceeded 5,400, and state and local health officials said the moment demanded a louder federal voice and a bigger push on vaccination. The interview supplied a federal account of that gap: Schwartz said CDC staff losses have slowed internal guidance releases, and she said the CDC is supporting trusted messengers in affected communities rather than sending an official onto a weekend television show.
