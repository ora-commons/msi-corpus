## Court filing cites hundreds of arrests in three states since May 2025

The ACLU filed a lawsuit last week in the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of Georgia challenging the Department of Homeland Security's decision to reverse an ICE practice that had generally kept immigrants from being re-detained after release. The suit names four plaintiffs, including North Carolina residents Livinton Sosa Martinez and Raquel Mariona Ebora and two residents of Georgia. It says Sosa Martinez's case is among hundreds of re-detentions in North Carolina, South Carolina and Georgia since DHS abandoned the practice in May 2025.

Under the previous policy, ICE generally did not re-detain immigrants who had been released from custody while awaiting the outcome of their immigration cases, provided they complied with release conditions such as attending scheduled check-ins and court hearings. Officers had to show that a person's circumstances had changed in a major way, such as a new criminal charge, before arresting the person again. The ICE Atlanta office had followed the practice for more than four decades, according to the complaint.

The ACLU alleges that DHS abandoned the policy without explanation and adopted an approach that allows immigration officers to re-arrest previously released immigrants without determining whether their circumstances have materially changed. The lawsuit says Sosa Martinez's case is among hundreds of re-detentions across North Carolina, South Carolina and Georgia since the May 2025 reversal.

Livinton Sosa Martinez, a 41-year-old Nicaraguan man living in Greensboro, fled political persecution in his home country in 2022. According to the lawsuit, Sosa Martinez had attended his annual ICE check-ins and had no criminal history before he was detained on March 19 during a routine check-in in Charlotte.

He was transferred to Stewart Detention Center in Georgia, where he remained for several weeks before a federal judge ordered a bond hearing. Sosa Martinez was subsequently released on a $10,000 bond. He remains under ICE supervision and is required to report for in-person appointments every four weeks in Charlotte, according to the lawsuit.

Sosa Martinez described the uncertainty that followed in a statement provided by the ACLU. “When I have to go to work, especially in Raleigh or Charlotte, my children cry because they are worried I will be re-detained again,” he said. “I will continue to pursue my asylum case, but my life has a lot more uncertainty now. I know that I could be detained by ICE at any time even though I follow all the rules.”

Raquel Mariona Ebora, a 39-year-old Honduran woman in Dunn, was not detained, but her husband, Kelvin Lopez, was arrested during a routine ICE check-in in Charlotte on April 28. The couple, who fled Honduras in 2022, live in Dunn with their 3-year-old U.S. citizen son, who has autism. Lopez was released on bond a few weeks later after a federal judge ordered a hearing, but his arrest disrupted the family's routine and made it harder to care for their son, according to the lawsuit.

According to the complaint, both families were taken into immigration custody after crossing the border illegally in 2022 and later released on humanitarian parole.

Jacqueline Landry, a legal fellow at the ACLU of North Carolina, said the policy shift has created constant fear and uncertainty for people who did what they were told. “These are people that have been following all the rules that the government set for them,” Landry said. “They’re going about their lives based on an understanding that as long as they follow the rules, they’ll be able to continue living in the community.”

In their complaint, the plaintiffs argue DHS violated the Administrative Procedure Act by discarding a decades-old practice without explaining why or considering how the shift would affect people who relied on it. They also contend the arrests violate the Fourth Amendment because ICE is re-detaining people on the same immigration charges without showing anything has changed.

The plaintiffs are asking the judge to put the policy on hold while the case plays out in court. Earlier this week, they filed a motion asking the Georgia court to certify the lawsuit as a class action.

The ACLU says DHS abandoned the policy without explanation. DHS did not respond to Newsline's request for comment by publication time.

The suit places the re-detention practice before a federal court in Georgia, where four plaintiffs are asking the judge to halt the policy and have the case certified as a class action. Under the practice the plaintiffs are challenging, officers had to identify a major change in a person's circumstances before rearresting someone released while that person's case was pending; the plaintiffs say that step is no longer required.
